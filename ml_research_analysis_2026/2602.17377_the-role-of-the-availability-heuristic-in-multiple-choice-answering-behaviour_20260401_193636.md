# The Role of the Availability Heuristic in Multiple-Choice Answering Behaviour

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.17377
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17377
- **Reference count**: 19
- **Primary result**: Corpus‑based prevalence (availability heuristic) correctly predicts MCQ answers with accuracy 13.5%–32.9% above random baseline.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether the availability heuristic—choosing the option that comes most readily to mind—serves as an effective guessing strategy for multiple‑choice questions. Operationalizing cognitive availability through the prevalence of answer options in large text corpora (Wikipedia, BEIR), the authors find that correct answers exhibit significantly higher out‑of‑context availability than distractors across three datasets (effect sizes 0.30–0.62). A simple strategy of always selecting the most available option yields scores 13.5%–32.9% above random‑guess baselines. The effect holds for expert‑, crowd‑, and LLM‑generated distractors, but vanishes with the BEIR corpus, highlighting the influence of corpus structure on modeling expert versus novice knowledge.

## Method Summary
The method transforms each MCQ into a semantic query by concatenating its option texts (without the stem). Using Cohere’s Embed v3, the query is encoded and used to retrieve the top *N* most similar passages from a pre‑indexed corpus (Wikipedia or BEIR). Each retrieved passage is then assigned to the option whose embedding has the highest cosine similarity to the passage embedding. An option’s availability score is the proportion of passages assigned to it. The option with the highest score is selected as the answer. This pipeline is evaluated on Biopsychology (776 MCQs), Immunopharmacology (639 MCQs), and SciQ (1,000 MCQs). The authors also test an in‑context variant where the question stem is included in the query. Statistical analysis includes Friedman tests, Wilcoxon post‑hoc with Holm‑Bonferroni correction, rank‑biserial effect sizes, and Bayesian Dirichlet‑Multinomial models for comparing distractor sources.

## Key Results
- Correct answers have significantly higher out‑of‑context availability than distractors on Wikipedia, with rank‑biserial effect sizes between 0.30 and 0.62.
- Always choosing the most available option yields absolute accuracy gains of 13.5%–32.9% over random guessing (e.g., 48.5% vs. 33.3% for 3‑option Biopsychology questions).
- The availability advantage persists across human‑written and LLM‑generated distractors; however, the BEIR corpus shows no significant differences, indicating corpus‑dependency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Operationalizing Cognitive Availability via Corpus Prevalence
- **Claim**: The study operationalizes the availability heuristic by measuring the prevalence of MCQ option texts in large corpora, using semantic similarity to retrieve and assign passages.
- **Mechanism**: For each MCQ, the concatenated options are embedded (Cohere Embed v3) and used as a query to retrieve the top‑*N* semantically similar passages from a pre‑indexed corpus (Wikipedia or BEIR). Each passage is then assigned to the option whose embedding has the highest cosine similarity with the passage embedding. The availability score for an option is the proportion of retrieved passages assigned to it.
- **Core assumption**: The frequency of a concept in a broad, factual text corpus approximates how readily that concept comes to mind (i.e., its cognitive availability) for individuals with at least novice domain knowledge.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "We propose a computational method of assessing the cognitive availability of MCQ options operationalised by concepts’ prevalence in large corpora."
  - [section 2.1] The section details the embedding, retrieval, and assignment pipeline, including the use of combined options as the retrieval query to measure out‑of‑context availability.
  - [corpus] The neighboring corpus contains many papers on MCQ evaluation but none directly validate this specific operationalization; evidence is indirect and based on the paper’s own experiments.
- **Break condition**: The mechanism breaks down if the corpus lacks adequate coverage of the question domain (e.g., BEIR for specialized topics), leading to uninformative or noisy availability scores. Additionally, highly similar options can cause ambiguous assignments, reducing reliability.

### Mechanism 2: Correct Answers Exhibit Higher Availability, Providing a Guessing Advantage
- **Claim**: Across multiple datasets, correct answers have significantly higher out‑of‑context availability than distractors, enabling a simple “always pick the most available option” strategy to perform well above chance.
- **Mechanism**: After computing per‑option availability, the option with the maximum score is selected as the answer. Because correct answers tend to be more prevalent in the corpus, they are more frequently the most available option.
- **Core assumption**: The availability gap between correct and incorrect options is sufficiently consistent that the correct answer is statistically more likely to be the highest‑availability option across a wide range of MCQs.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "correct answers exhibit significantly higher out‑of‑context availability than distractors... Always selecting the most available option yields scores 13.5% to 32.9% above the random‑guess baselines."
  - [section 3] Friedman tests followed by Wilcoxon post‑hoc comparisons (α=0.01) show correct > distractors with medium‑to‑large effect sizes (rank‑biserial 0.30–0.62) when using Wikipedia.
  - [section 3.3] Table 4 confirms that selecting the most available option yields accuracy gains, e.g., 48.5% for Biopsychology (3 options) vs. 33.3% chance.
  - [corpus] The corpus of neighboring papers discusses overall MCQ performance with LLMs but does not provide direct evidence for the availability‑based guessing advantage; the paper’s own data is the primary source.
- **Break condition**: The guessing strategy fails to outperform chance when the corpus does not reflect the domain (e.g., BEIR yields negligible effects) or when distractors are crafted to be more frequent than the correct answer. The effect is also weaker for reasoning‑heavy domains not well captured by corpus frequency.

### Mechanism 3: Availability Patterns Generalize Across Distractor Sources
- **Claim**: The availability advantage of correct answers is robust across expert‑written, crowdsourced, and LLM‑generated distractors, indicating that the heuristic is not limited to high‑quality distractors.
- **Mechanism**: For each MCQ, the researchers replace original distractors with those produced by three LLMs (Qwen3‑8B/30B/80B). They then compare the correct answer’s availability to the average distractor availability using a Bayesian Dirichlet‑Multinomial model (uniform prior).
- **Core assumption**: LLM‑generated distractors, despite being sampled from a frequentist model, have corpus prevalence distributions similar to those created by human experts, thereby preserving the availability gap.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "LLM‑generated MCQ options show similar patterns of availability compared to expert‑created options, despite the LLMs’ frequentist nature..."
  - [section 3.1] Figure 4 and Bayesian analysis (probability of direction *pd* > 95%) show the correct answer is more available than distractors for all LLM sizes and for the crowdsourced SciQ set.
  - [section 3.1] The authors note that increased LLM capability does not shrink the gap: "LLMs of different capabilities… do not lead to the generation of distractors with more similar availability to the correct answer."
  - [corpus] The neighboring corpus includes work on distractor generation and MCQ quality, but none directly address availability; thus external corroboration is limited.
- **Break condition**: The generalization may not hold if LLMs are prompted explicitly to produce rare or unusual terms, or if future LLMs are trained on corpora with drastically different distributions that alter option frequencies. Domain‑specific fine‑tuning could also change patterns.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Availability Heuristic**
  - Why needed here: The paper’s central premise is that test‑takers may rely on the availability heuristic when guessing. Understanding its definition and dual interpretations (frequency vs. ease of recall) is crucial for interpreting the operationalization.
  - Quick check question: How did Tversky & Kahneman (1973) define the availability heuristic, and what are the two main ways it can be cued?
- **Concept: Semantic Similarity via Text Embeddings**
  - Why needed here: The method relies on embedding models to measure conceptual overlap between MCQ options and corpus passages. Without grasping how embeddings represent meaning, one cannot evaluate the validity of the retrieval‑assignment pipeline.
  - Quick check question: Why are dense vector embeddings preferred over traditional keyword matching for assigning passages to MCQ options?
- **Concept: Effect Size and Bayesian Comparison for Repeated Measures**
  - Why needed here: The paper uses rank‑biserial correlations (effect sizes) and Bayesian posterior probabilities (*pd*) to interpret differences in availability. These metrics quantify the robustness of the observed effects beyond p‑values.
  - Quick check question: What does a rank‑biserial correlation of 0.55 indicate about the magnitude of the availability difference between correct and incorrect options?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input (MCQ) -> Preprocessing -> Embedding module -> Retrieval module -> Assignment module -> Scoring module -> Evaluation module
- **Critical path**:
  1. Embed combined options → query corpus.
  2. Retrieve top *N* passages.
  3. Embed each option individually.
  4. Assign each passage to the nearest option.
  5. Compute availability proportions; choose the highest.
  6. Record correctness; repeat for all questions.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Corpus choice**: Wikipedia offers high‑quality, broad factual coverage, approximating an expert’s knowledge; BEIR is more diverse but shallower, modeling a novice perspective but yielding weaker signals.
  - **Number of passages (*N*)**: Larger *N* improves statistical stability but risks incorporating irrelevant passages, especially for niche topics. The paper found minimal difference between *N*=20 and *N*=60.
  - **Embedding model**: Using a proprietary model like Cohere Embed v3 may yield better performance but raises reproducibility and cost concerns; open‑source alternatives could be substituted but require re‑validation.
  - **Retrieval query**: Out‑of‑context (options only) isolates the heuristic; in‑context (including stem) boosts correct answer availability but conflates the heuristic with question‑specific reasoning.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Corpus coverage gap**: For specialized domains (e.g., Immunopharmacology with BEIR), the availability difference disappears → the corpus lacks relevant content.
  - **Assignment ambiguity**: When options are semantically very close, many passages have near‑equal similarity, causing random assignment and high variance in availability scores.
  - **Embedding drift**: Upgrading or switching the embedding model without re‑calibrating may flip similarity rankings, breaking reproducibility.
  - **Tie handling**: If multiple options have equal maximum similarity for a passage, the assignment rule must be deterministic; otherwise, scores become biased.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Sanity‑check retrieval**: Select a single Biopsychology MCQ, run the pipeline with Wikipedia (*N*=20), and print the per‑option availability distribution. Verify that the correct answer receives the highest score.
  2. **Replicate RQ1**: Process the entire Biopsychology dataset (3‑option subset). Compute the mean availability for correct vs. distractors, run a Friedman test, and check that the effect size for the correct answer is around 0.55–0.62 (as in Figure 3).
  3. **Test guessing strategy**: Implement the “always pick the most available option” rule on the same dataset. Confirm that accuracy exceeds the random baseline (33.3%) by ~15% (i.e., reaching ~48.5%).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: What factors explain the stark contrast in availability effects between Wikipedia (expert‑like) and BEIR (novice‑like) corpora, and how do corpus characteristics modulate the heuristic's applicability?
  - *Basis in paper*: The authors note Wikipedia yields robust availability differences while BEIR does not, hypothesizing Wikipedia approximates expert knowledge and BEIR mirrors novice perspective (Section 4, Figure 3).
  - *Why unresolved*: The hypothesis is based on corpus scope but lacks empirical validation of how specific corpus attributes (domain specificity, text genre) impact the operationalization of availability.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Systematic analysis of corpus linguistic properties (term frequency distributions, topical coherence) combined with human studies comparing availability judgments of experts and novices on identical items.

- **Open Question 2**: Why does out‑of‑context availability fail to predict student distractor selection rates, despite correct answers having higher availability?
  - *Basis in paper*: RQ2 found no significant differences in availability between distractors ordered by student selection rates, leading to the conclusion that "availability is not sufficient to explain" distractor choice (Section 4).
  - *Why unresolved*: The authors suggest other distractor qualities (plausibility, common misconceptions) may override availability, but these are not quantified or tested against availability.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: A multivariate analysis correlating student selection rates with multiple distractor attributes—availability, semantic plausibility, thematic similarity to the stem—to identify dominant predictors.

- **Open Question 3**: Does corpus‑based frequency genuinely capture the cognitive availability heuristic, or does the operationalization conflate frequency with related constructs like general familiarity or topical prominence?
  - *Basis in paper*: The method equates "prevalence in large corpora" with "ease of cognitive retrieval" (Section 2.1) without direct validation that these measures align psychologically.
  - *Why unresolved*: The proxy assumes textual frequency mirrors mental accessibility, but cognitive availability may involve salience, recency, or personal experience not reflected in corpora.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Human experiments measuring retrieval latency or confidence for MCQ options, then correlating these behavioral indices with the corpus‑derived availability scores.

- **Open Question 4**: To what extent do availability effects generalize beyond factual recall domains to reasoning‑intensive MCQ formats (e.g., mathematical or logical problems)?
  - *Basis in paper*: The authors state findings likely become "more subtle in reasoning‑heavy domains like mathematics" due to the factual focus of their datasets (Section 5, Limitations).
  - *Why unresolved*: All tested datasets (Biopsychology, Immunopharmacology, SciQ) emphasize declarative knowledge; no evidence from domains requiring applied inference or procedural knowledge.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Replication using reasoning‑centric question sets (e.g., standardized test quant/logic sections) to assess whether correct answers still exhibit higher out‑of‑context availability and whether the heuristic remains a viable guessing strategy.

## Limitations
- Unspecified Wikipedia passage segmentation and preprocessing details affect retrieval content and similarity patterns.
- Use of the proprietary Cohere Embed v3 model hinders exact replication; alternative embeddings may yield different results.
- Private datasets (Biopsychology, Immunopharmacology) are unavailable, preventing full validation of all claims and analyses involving student response data.
