# Learning Query-Specific Rubrics from Human Preferences for DeepResearch Report Generation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03619
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03619
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Rubric generator achieves 65.16% preference accuracy and 0.366 paired Cohen's d; integrated into MaMs workflow to score 49.3 on DeepResearch Bench, outperforming open-source baselines and matching closed-source models.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the challenge of designing verifiable rewards for DeepResearch report generation by learning query-specific evaluation rubrics directly from human preferences. It constructs a dataset of over 5,000 research queries, each paired with two candidate reports annotated by human experts to indicate which is more useful, coherent, complete, and aligned. A rubric generator is trained via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) using a hybrid reward that combines preference consistency, LLM-based rubric quality assessment, and format validity. The resulting rubrics are more discriminative and human-aligned than static or pointwise baselines. When integrated into a Multi-agent Markov-state (MaMs) workflow—which structures research into search, state (memory/plan), and report agents with chunk-based processing—these learned rubrics guide agents to produce higher-quality reports. On DeepResearch Bench, the MaMs system attains an overall score of 49.3, surpassing all open-source baselines and matching leading closed-source models, demonstrating effective long-context handling and human-aligned supervision.

## Method Summary
The method unfolds in three stages. First, a preference dataset is constructed: queries are auto-generated from a knowledge graph, candidate reports are produced via an initial search-based workflow, and human experts provide pairwise preferences based on multiple quality dimensions. Second, a rubric generator (an LLM) is trained with GRPO to output a JSON rubric for any given query. The training reward hybridizes three signals: preference consistency (ensuring the rubric scores the preferred report higher), LLM-judged rubric quality (coherence, coverage, relevance), and a format validity check. Third, the trained rubric generator is frozen and integrated into a Multi-agent Markov-state (MaMs) workflow for report generation. In MaMs, three specialized agents (Search, State, Report) operate on a shared abstract state comprising memory, plan, and partial report, with the State Agent processing long observations in chunks to manage context limits. The final report is scored by the rubric, and the entire agent policy is optimized via GRPO using these rubric-based rewards.

## Key Results
- Rubric generator validation: 65.16% preference accuracy and 0.366 paired Cohen's d on a held-out human preference test set, significantly outperforming human-defined general rubrics, pointwise scoring, and pairwise judgment baselines.
- End-to-end performance: MaMs with learned rubrics achieves an overall score of 49.3 on DeepResearch Bench, exceeding all open-source baselines and matching leading closed-source models.
- Ablation evidence: The hybrid reward (preference + LLM quality) is crucial; single-reward variants and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) yield inferior rubrics. MaMs with learned rubrics also outperforms ReAct with identical rubrics, validating the architectural benefits.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Human pairwise preferences on report pairs provide a scalable, discriminative supervision signal for learning query-specific evaluation rubrics, avoiding costly per-query rubric authoring.  
**Mechanism:** A dataset of 5,000+ queries is built, each with two candidate reports annotated by experts on usefulness, coherence, completeness, and alignment. During rubric training, generated rubrics score both reports via an LLM-as-judge; the preference reward encourages the rubric to assign higher scores to the preferred report. This directly optimizes for human-aligned discriminative power.  
**Core assumption:** Comparative judgments are more reliable and scalable than absolute scores or manual rubrics, and can be effectively modeled via learned rubrics.  
**Evidence:** Dataset construction described in Section 3.2; Table 1 shows rubric generator trained with preference data outperforms alternatives.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Training the rubric generator via GRPO with a hybrid reward (preference consistency, LLM rubric quality, format validity) yields rubrics that are both discriminative and well-designed.  
**Mechanism:** The total reward combines: (1) `R_pref` from human preferences, (2) `R_llm` assessing rubric quality via an LLM judge, and (3) `R_fmt` for valid JSON. GRPO optimizes the generator to increase relative advantage of higher-reward groups, aligning rubrics with human preferences and intrinsic quality.  
**Core assumption:** Hybrid reward effectively guides the generator toward rubrics that are human-aligned and coherent, avoiding pitfalls of using either signal alone.  
**Evidence:** Section 3.3 defines rewards; Table 1 shows hybrid reward variant achieves best performance; ablations confirm complementarity.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The Multi-agent Markov-state (MaMs) workflow decouples search, state compression, and report generation, enabling effective long-horizon reasoning and report synthesis compared to ReAct.  
**Mechanism:** MaMs models research as a Markov Decision Process over abstract state `s_t = <m_t, p_t, r_t>`. The Search Agent decides actions, the State Agent processes raw observations in chunks to update memory and plan, and the Report Agent incrementally refines the report. This separation and chunking address context limits and error accumulation in long ReAct chains.  
**Core assumption:** Specialized agents with a compact state representation lead to more coherent, complete reports than a monolithic ReAct loop.  
**Evidence:** Abstract definition in Section 3.4; Table 2 shows MaMs with learned rubrics (49.3) significantly outperforms ReAct with same rubrics (45.2) for Tongyi backbone; also matches closed-source models.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Human Preference Collection for LLM Alignment**

  *Quick check:* What are the key challenges in collecting high-quality, discriminative pairwise preferences for long-form text, and how does this paper address them (e.g., expert selection, filtering candidates)?

- **Concept: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**  
  *Why needed:* GRPO is the core reinforcement learning algorithm used to train both the rubric generator and the DeepResearch agent. It’s adapted from RLHF for this setting, using group-wise relative advantages instead of a separate reward model.  
  *Quick check:* How does GRPO differ from standard PPO in this paper's context, and why might it be preferred for training the rubric generator (considering the discussion about GSPO vs GRPO in Section 4.4)?

- **Concept: State Abstraction in Agent Systems**  
  *Why needed:* The MaMs workflow fundamentally relies on a compressed state representation (`s_t = <m_t, p_t, r_t>`) to manage long-horizon reasoning. This core design choice differentiates it from ReAct, which carries a full history.  
  *Quick check:* What are the three components of the abstract state in MaMs, and what specific problem does this abstraction solve compared to passing raw conversation history?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Preference Dataset Creator -> Rubric Generator -> Reward Scorer; DeepResearch Agent (executing MaMs Workflow) -> GRPO Trainer. The Rubric Generator outputs query-specific rubrics that the Reward Scorer uses to score agent rollouts; GRPO then optimizes the agent policy based on these scores.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Build human preference dataset D_pref from queries and candidate reports.  
  2. Train Rubric Generator on D_pref via GRPO with hybrid reward.  
  3. Freeze Rubric Generator.  
  4. Train DeepResearch Agent with MaMs, where each rollout's reward is computed by: query -> Rubric Generator (outputs rubric y) -> Reward Scorer (scores final report r using y) -> score as reward for GRPO.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *GRPO vs. GSPO for Rubric Generator:* GRPO chosen over GSPO for stability and consistency in rubric outputs (Section 4.4).  
  - *MaMs vs. ReAct:* MaMs adds architectural complexity (three agents, state chunking) but explicitly addresses long-context limits and error propagation; tradeoff is increased latency vs improved quality.  
  - *Rubric Generator vs. Human/Static Rubrics:* Learned generator is query-adaptive but requires large preference data and RL training; static rubrics are simpler but less discriminative.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Preference Dataset: Low inter-annotator agreement; filtered reports still contain subtle errors.  
  - Rubric Generator: High invalid JSON rate; consistently low LLM quality scores; poor preference ranking on validation set (<50%).  
  - MaMs Workflow: State Agent forgetting key info from early chunks; Report Agent producing repetitive content; Search Agent failing to terminate properly.  
  - Overall Training: Reward hacking (e.g., keyword stuffing without coherence); no improvement on DeepResearch Bench after several GRPO steps.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Rubric Generator Validation:** Evaluate trained generator on held-out test set; report Preference Accuracy and paired Cohen's d against baselines (human-defined general rubrics, pointwise, pairwise, SFT). Success: >65% Acc, high d. (Anchored by Table 1)  
  2. **Ablation on Reward Components:** Train rubric generators with only R_pref, only R_llm, and hybrid; validate on D_pref. Expected: Hybrid outperforms single-reward variants.  
  3. **End-to-End Agent Integration:** Integrate best rubric generator into MaMs; train DeepResearch agent (e.g., Tongyi-DeepResearch) via GRPO. Compare on DeepResearch Bench against: ReAct with same rubric, MaMs with other rubrics, ReAct with no rubric. Success: Significant gain over ReAct with same rubric and over MaMs with other rubrics. (Anchored by Table 2)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Can rubric generators trained with pairwise preferences benefit from richer preference signals such as rankings or graded scores?**  
  *Basis:* Future work explicitly mentions extending beyond pairwise to rankings or graded scores.  
  *Why unresolved:* Current method uses binary preferences only; unknown if multi-level signals improve alignment or stability.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Build a dataset with ranked/scored report pairs, train a rubric generator with these signals, and evaluate preference accuracy and Cohen's d against binary baseline.

- **How can we reliably incorporate assessments of novelty, creativity, and reasoning depth into automatically generated rubrics?**  
  *Basis:* Future work focuses on improving assessment of novelty, creativity, reasoning depth.  
  *Why unresolved:* Existing rubrics emphasize structure and factual coverage; these subjective aspects are underexplored and hard to measure.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Design prompts explicitly targeting these dimensions, add them to hybrid reward, test if agent performance or human agreement improves.

- **Can dependence on LLM-as-a-judge components in rubric training be reduced without sacrificing human preference alignment?**  
  *Basis:* Future work suggests reducing dependence via self-consistency checks or hybrid human-LLM validation.  
  *Why unresolved:* LLM judge may introduce bias and instability; alternatives untested.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Replace LLM judge with self-consistency (multiple LLMs, voting) or small human feedback loops; compare rubric quality and training stability.

- **Do query-specific rubrics learned on a knowledge‑graph‑derived dataset generalize to entirely new domains or report formats?**  
  *Basis:* Limitations note generalization to different formats/domains not extensively explored.  
  *Why unresolved:* Training queries limited to certain topic categories; rubrics may overfit.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Evaluate trained rubric generator on out‑of‑domain queries (e.g., legal opinions, scientific reviews); measure preference accuracy and Cohen's d compared to in‑domain performance.

## Limitations
- **Dataset construction:** Exact knowledge graph source, query rewrite prompts, and filtering criteria (including LLM verifier details) are unspecified, potentially affecting reproducibility and dataset diversity.
- **Training details:** Hyperparameter choices (λ_pref, λ_llm), judge LLM identity for R_llm, and GRPO group sampling specifics are not fully disclosed, hindering precise replication.
- **Implementation gaps:** Tool APIs for MaMs search, exact chunking strategy, and inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ) for the preference dataset are unreported, introducing uncertainty in workflow fidelity.
- **Reproducibility:** Target model weights (Qwen3-30B-A3B, Tongyi-DeepResearch) are not publicly released, forcing proxy models that may underperform and limit direct comparison.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Preference supervision):** Medium. Strong empirical gains with 5k+ queries, but reliability depends on unreported annotator agreement and filtering quality.
- **Mechanism 2 (Hybrid reward GRPO):** High. Ablations (Table 1) clearly isolate hybrid reward benefits; algorithm is specified, though λ values are not tuned.
- **Mechanism 3 (MaMs workflow):** Medium. End-to-end gains over ReAct validated (Table 2), but exact state update logic and chunking implementation details are only partially described.
