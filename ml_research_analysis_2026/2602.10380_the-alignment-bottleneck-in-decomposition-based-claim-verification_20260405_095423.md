# The Alignment Bottleneck in Decomposition-Based Claim Verification
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10380
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10380
- Reference count: 35
- Primary result: Claim decomposition improves verification performance only when sub-claims are paired with granular, aligned evidence (SAE); using repeated claim-level evidence (SRE) fails or degrades performance.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates why decomposition-based claim verification yields inconsistent results, identifying evidence alignment and sub-claim error profiles as key bottlenecks. Using a new dataset with human-annotated sub-claim evidence spans, they compare Sub-claim Aligned Evidence (SAE) and Repeated Claim-level Evidence (SRE). Results show decomposition significantly improves performance (e.g., +6.25 F1 on PHEMEPlus) under SAE with oracle labels, while SRE fails to improve and often degrades performance on other datasets. Under noisy sub-claim labels, conservative abstention greatly reduces error propagation compared to aggressive predictions. The primary outcome is that decomposition’s benefit is conditional on granular, aligned evidence and calibrated sub-claim label bias.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a dataset with human-annotated evidence spans for each sub-claim, enabling two evidence configurations: SAE (each sub-claim receives its own aligned evidence) and SRE (all sub-claims share the full claim’s evidence). They evaluate claim-level verification using oracle sub-claim labels and predicted sub-claim labels (via GNN, BERT, or LLM). An LLM aggregator combines sub-claim veracity predictions (T/F/U) into a final claim verdict. Ablations remove sub-claim labels to isolate the effect of evidence granularity. Noise simulations explore error propagation by replacing gold sub-claim labels with model predictions.

## Key Results
- Oracle_SAE significantly outperforms Vanilla and Oracle_SRE on PHEMEPlus (+6.25 F1), while SRE often underperforms or degrades on MMM-Fact and COVID-Fact.
- Without sub-claim labels (Ablation_SAE), performance drops below the SRE baseline, indicating granular evidence alone is insufficient and may be detrimental.
- Under noisy sub-claim predictions, conservative sub-claim predictors (e.g., GNN with 35% abstention) maintain stability in SAE, whereas aggressive predictors (e.g., Qwen with 15.8% abstention) cause severe SRE collapse.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Decomposition yields gains only with granular, aligned evidence (SAE), not with repeated claim-level evidence (SRE).
- **Mechanism:** SAE provides each sub-claim specific evidence spans, reducing irrelevant context. SRE repeats the same full claim evidence for all sub-claims, introducing substantial irrelevant content that confuses the verifier.
- **Core assumption:** Evidence relevance is inherently sub-claim-specific; coarse evidence bundles contain many irrelevant fragments.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract statement on granular alignment; formal definitions of SAE/SRE; new dataset provides annotated spans for SAE, while other corpora lack these, forcing SRE.
- **Break condition:** Failure occurs when evidence cannot be aligned (no annotated spans) or when the evidence set is too sparse to provide distinct spans for multiple sub-claims.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The error profile of sub-claim predictors (abstention vs. aggression) determines downstream robustness; high abstention mitigates error propagation.
- **Mechanism:** Models predicting *Unverified* (abstain) avoid incorrect polar predictions that poison aggregation. Aggressive models commit more false *True/False* errors, which compound during claim inference, especially under SRE where evidence is already noisy.
- **Core assumption:** Not all errors are equal; false *True/False* predictions are more damaging than *Unverified* abstentions in the aggregation step.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.4 on noise and stability; GNN’s conservative behavior; Table 5 error profiles (GNN 35% *U*, Qwen 15.8% *U*).
- **Break condition:** When sub-claim predictors have high precision on polar labels but low coverage, or when the aggregation rule does not treat *Unverified* as a safe neutral signal.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Granular evidence without reliable sub-claim labels is more detrimental than coarse evidence without labels.
- **Mechanism:** Providing sub-claim-specific evidence (SAE) without correct sub-claim veracity supervision confuses the claim-level model, leading to worse performance than using coarse evidence (SRE) or omitting sub-claim labels.
- **Core assumption:** Models need correct supervision at the sub-claim level to effectively utilize granular evidence; otherwise granularity becomes noise.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.2 ablations; Table 3 showing Ablation_SAE (0.5485 F1) < Ablation_SRE (0.5808) on PHEMEPlus.
- **Break condition:** When sub-claim label accuracy is very low (< ~60%) and evidence alignment is granular (SAE), performance may degrade below simpler baselines.

## Foundational Learning
- **Claim Decomposition:** Splitting complex claims into atomic sub-claims. *Why needed:* Entire study hinges on decomposition; poor sub-claims invalidate SAE/SRE comparison. *Quick check:* "The plane crashed" and "There were 150 fatalities" as sub-claims of a crash claim—are they atomic and independent?
- **Evidence Granularity:** Matching evidence specificity to sub-claim specificity. *Why needed:* Core bottleneck is aligning evidence granularity to sub-claim; misalignment leads to flawed experiments. *Quick check:* From a long article, extract different evidence spans for different sub-claims.
- **Error Propagation in Multi-step Pipelines:** Errors in sub-claim verification affect final claim verdict. *Why needed:* Decomposition creates a pipeline; understanding the causal chain is essential to interpret noise experiments. *Quick check:* If sub-claim accuracy drops from 90% to 70%, how might claim accuracy change? Possibly non-linear.
- **Conservative Classification (Abstention):** Predicting *Unverified* to avoid false polar predictions. *Why needed:* Paper shows when to abstain is as important as what to predict; key design choice. *Quick check:* Compare a model with 40% abstention, 90% accuracy on remaining 60% vs. 10% abstention, 85% accuracy on remaining 90%.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** 
  Decomposition Module (LLM) -> Sub-claims -> Evidence Aligner (human) -> SAE evidence spans OR SRE repeated claim evidence -> Sub-claim Verifier (GNN/BERT/LLM) -> Sub-claim labels (T/F/U) -> Claim Aggregator (LLM) -> Final claim verdict. Evidence Store provides pre-linked documents.
- **Critical path:** Claim → Decomposition → (SAE: alignment → sub-claim verification → aggregation) OR (SRE: direct aggregation with repeated evidence).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - SAE vs SRE: SAE needs costly human annotation but yields accurate evaluation; SRE uses existing claim evidence but misaligns context.
  - Oracle vs Noisy: Oracle uses gold sub-claim labels (upper bound); Noisy uses predicted labels (realistic); gap shows intolerance to sub-claim errors.
  - Model selection: GNN captures interactions but over-abstains; LLMs reason well but are aggressive; trade-off depends on desired error profile.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - SRE performance < Vanilla on MMM-Fact/COVID-Fact.
  - Ablation_SAE < Ablation_SRE.
  - Large performance drop from Oracle_SAE to Qwen_Noisy_SRE (~0.19 F1 drop).
  - High false positive rate on *False* (low PF) in aggressive models like Qwen.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Claim-level oracle: Run Vanilla, Oracle_SRE, Oracle_SAE on PHEMEPlus with gold labels to establish best-case decomposition benefit.
  2. Ablation study: Remove sub-claim labels from SAE and SRE to isolate evidence granularity effect.
  3. Sub-claim veracity benchmark: Evaluate GNN, BERT, LLM on sub-claim classification to characterize error profiles (abstention, refutation sensitivity) that propagate downstream.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Does claim decomposition improve human fact-checkers' accuracy and efficiency? *Basis:* Paper mentions evaluating whether sub-claim rationales assist humans but no user study. *Resolution:* Controlled experiments with journalists comparing decomposition vs. monolithic interfaces.
- **Open Question 2:** How can sub-claim predictors learn a calibrated bias toward abstention to minimise error propagation? *Basis:* Paper suggests optimizing label bias rather than macro-F1. *Resolution:* Develop training objectives (e.g., cost‑sensitive losses) that trade off abstention vs. polarity errors and test downstream claim verification.
- **Open Question 3:** Can we automate the creation of sub‑claim‑aligned evidence spans, and does this substitution retain the performance gains? *Basis:* Paper notes need for advancements in evidence synthesis. *Resolution:* Build span extraction models trained on provided spans; test if predicted spans yield similar improvements.

## Limitations
- Reproducibility gaps: Aggregation prompt unspecified; GNN graph construction details missing; temporal filtering for MMM-Fact/COVID-Fact unclear.
- Generalizability: Results rely on manually annotated sub-claim evidence spans (SAE) not available for most datasets; SRE degrades on other datasets but SAE untested there.
- Decomposition quality: Fixed decomposition schema (FactScore) assumed; variability in sub-claim atomicity could alter results but not deeply analyzed.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (SAE benefit):** High confidence. Strong empirical support on PHEMEPlus with oracle labels, consistent SRE failure on other datasets.
- **Mechanism 2 (abstention mitigates noise):** Medium confidence. Supported by error profiling and noise experiments but may be model-specific.
- **Mechanism 3 (granular evidence harms with noisy labels):** Medium confidence. Ablation results clear on one dataset; external validity uncertain.
