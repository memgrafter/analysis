# UniPACT: A Multimodal Framework for Prognostic Question Answering on Raw ECG and Structured EHR
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17916
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17916
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Achieved state-of-the-art mean AUROC of 89.37% on the MDS-ED benchmark for multimodal prognostic question answering, outperforming prior specialized methods.

## Executive Summary
UniPACT addresses the challenge of integrating raw 12‑lead ECG waveforms and structured electronic health record (EHR) data for clinical prognosis using large language models (LLMs), which natively process only text. The framework employs a structured prompting mechanism to convert numerical EHR into semantic text and a trainable multimodal projector to fuse ECG features with text embeddings, enabling holistic LLM reasoning across both modalities. Evaluated on the MDS‑ED benchmark, UniPACT achieves a mean AUROC of 89.37% across diagnosis, deterioration, ICU admission, and mortality tasks, surpassing the previous best (88.90%). The approach demonstrates significant gains from multimodal fusion (+8.54% AUROC versus EHR‑only) and maintains robustness in missing‑data and multi‑task settings.

## Method Summary
UniPACT processes two input streams: raw ECG waveforms and structured EHR tables. EHR data (demographics, biometrics, vital parameters) is converted into natural language sentences via predefined templates, making it directly tokenizable by the LLM. The ECG signal is encoded by a pre‑trained D‑BETA Transformer into a sequence of feature embeddings, which are then projected into the LLM’s token embedding space using a two‑layer MLP (MM‑Projector). These aligned ECG tokens are concatenated with the EHR text tokens and the question tokens to form a unified input sequence for the MedGemma‑4B LLM. Training proceeds in two stages: first, the ECG encoder and LLM are frozen while only the MM‑Projector is trained; second, LoRA adapters are added to the ECG encoder and LLM for end‑to‑end fine‑tuning. The model is trained to generate “Yes”/“No” answers for a diverse set of prognostic tasks using a standard language modeling objective.

## Key Results
- Mean AUROC of 89.37% across 1443 sub‑tasks on MDS‑ED, setting a new state‑of‑the‑art.
- Multimodal fusion improves AUROC by +8.54% compared to an EHR‑only model.
- Strong multi‑task performance, with notable gains in ICU admission (+8.56%) and mortality (+1.56%) over single‑task models, and robustness to missing data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Structured Prompting for EHR Conversion
- **Claim:** Converting numerical EHR data into semantic text sentences via structured prompting makes it natively comprehensible to an LLM, preserving clinical context without information loss.
- **Mechanism:** Predefined templates transform raw numerical values (e.g., heart rate = 88.0) into grammatically correct, clinically meaningful sentences (e.g., "The vital parameters: heartrate 88.0"). These sentences are tokenized and embedded using the LLM’s native embedding layer, allowing the LLM to interpret clinical variables with inherent semantics rather than arbitrary numbers.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM’s pretrained knowledge can effectively reason over clinical concepts when presented in natural linguistic form, and this format better retains relational meaning (e.g., “abnormal potassium” vs. just “5.2”) than raw tables.
- **Evidence:** The paper details the template‑based conversion and states it maintains numerical precision while providing contextual cues LLMs require. Direct comparative evidence is limited but the approach is novel relative to other ECG‑QA works.
- **Break condition:** If the fixed templates are too rigid to capture complex, non‑linear interactions between EHR variables that are meaningful in clinical reasoning but not expressible in simple sentences, the textualized EHR may fail to convey critical multivariate relationships.

### Mechanism 2: Multimodal Projector for ECG‑Text Alignment
- **Claim:** A trainable multimodal projector (MM‑Projector) aligns raw ECG waveform embeddings from a specialized encoder into the LLM’s text embedding space, enabling fusion without altering the LLM’s weights during initial stages.
- **Mechanism:** A pre‑trained ECG encoder (D‑BETA) processes the raw 12‑lead signal into a sequence of feature embeddings (H_ecg). A two‑layer MLP maps these embeddings from the ECG encoder’s dimension (d_ecg) to the LLM’s token embedding dimension (d_llm). The aligned ECG sequence is concatenated with the tokenized EHR text embeddings to form a unified input sequence [H_ecg′, H_prompt, H_question] for the LLM.
- **Core assumption:** The manifold of ECG features can be linearly/non‑linearly projected into the LLM’s semantic space without losing discriminative information, and the LLM can then attend to both modalities as a single token stream.
- **Evidence:** The paper describes the projection network and reports that training first freezes the ECG encoder and LLM, updating only the MM‑Projector, supporting that projection is the primary fusion mechanism. The architecture is distinct from neighbors like HeartLLM that discretize ECG into tokens.
- **Break condition:** If the ECG feature space and LLM text embedding space are inherently misaligned (e.g., ECG features represent low‑level morphological patterns while LLM embeddings represent high‑level semantics), the projection may create representations the LLM cannot meaningfully integrate, leading to modality interference.

### Mechanism 3: Multi‑Task Learning for Generalizable Representations
- **Claim:** Multi‑task learning across diverse prognostic tasks (diagnosis, deterioration, ICU, mortality) within a single unified prompt‑based framework forces the model to learn generalizable patient state representations, improving overall robustness and performance.
- **Mechanism:** All tasks are framed as question‑answering with a consistent prompt template instructing role, task, and specific query. The model is trained end‑to‑end to generate “Yes”/“No” answers for all tasks simultaneously. Shared layers (LLM, ECG encoder with LoRA) must encode a holistic representation of patient state useful for multiple downstream predictions.
- **Core assumption:** Different prognostic tasks share underlying patterns of patient physiology and pathology; a single model trained on all tasks learns a more complete and robust internal representation than isolated single‑task models.
- **Evidence:** The paper reports that the multi‑task model (mean AUROC 89.37%) outperforms the average of single‑task models (86.63%), with especially large gains in ICU (+8.56%) and mortality (+1.56%) tasks.
- **Break condition:** If the tasks have fundamentally conflicting gradients or represent mutually exclusive clinical pathways (e.g., one task requires focusing on acute waveform changes while another requires longitudinal EHR trends), multi‑task training could cause negative transfer, degrading performance on some tasks.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multimodal Alignment**
  - **Why needed here:** The core challenge is fusing two heterogeneous data types (continuous ECG signals, discrete numerical EHR) into a space the LLM can process. Understanding alignment as a projection problem, not just concatenation, is key.
  - **Quick check question:** "Why not simply concatenate raw ECG values with EHR numbers and feed to a single model? What breaks in that approach?"
- **Concept: Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning (LoRA)**
  - **Why needed here:** Fine‑tuning the entire 4B‑parameter MedGemma LLM and ECG encoder is computationally prohibitive. LoRA adapters allow task‑specific learning with minimal new parameters, enabling end‑to‑end training of the multimodal system.
  - **Quick check question:** "If we only train the MM‑Projector and keep the LLM frozen, what capability are we likely missing? Why add LoRA to the LLM in stage 2?"
- **Concept: Structured Prompting for Tabular Data**
  - **Why needed here:** LLMs are text‑native; representing structured EHR as text is non‑trivial. This concept covers how to template relational data into a sequential format that preserves meaning without overwhelming the context window.
  - **Quick check question:** "How does converting `{"heart_rate": 88, "temp": 36.1}` to 'The heart rate is 88 beats per minute and temperature is 36.1 Celsius' affect what the LLM can 'see' compared to a JSON string?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Raw 12‑lead ECG waveform -> D‑BETA ECG Encoder -> H_ecg -> MM‑Projector (2‑layer MLP) -> H_ecg′; Structured EHR table -> Structured Prompter (templates) -> T_EHR string -> LLM tokenizer -> H_EHR; Concatenation [H_ecg′, H_EHR, H_question] -> MedGemma‑4B LLM -> Yes/No tokens.
- **Critical path:** ECG waveform is encoded to features and projected; EHR is textualized and tokenized; both modalities are concatenated with the question and processed by the LLM to generate an answer.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Complexity vs. Fidelity:* Using a pre‑trained ECG encoder preserves signal fidelity but adds a modality‑specific component. An alternative (tokenizing ECG like text) would simplify architecture but lose information.
  - *Frozen vs. Fine‑tuned:* Freezing the LLM/ECG encoder in Stage 1 speeds training and stabilizes but limits adaptation. Unfreezing with LoRA later captures task specifics at low cost.
  - *Prompt Engineering:* Template design is manual. Too simplistic prompts lose nuance; too complex prompts exceed context limits. Current design is a heuristic balance.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **MM‑Projector failure:** If H_ecg′ vectors are poorly aligned (e.g., norm outliers), LLM attention to ECG tokens becomes noise, performance drops to EHR‑only levels (~80% AUROC).
  - **Prompt brittleness:** Changing template wording (e.g., “BP is” vs. “blood pressure is”) can cause significant performance variance if the LLM’s internal knowledge is sensitive to phrasing.
  - **Modality imbalance:** If ECG embedding dimension N >> text token length M, ECG tokens may dominate attention, causing the model to ignore EHR. Monitor attention weights.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Uni‑modal ablations:** Train ECG‑only (MM‑Projector + LLM on [H_ecg′, Question]) and EHR‑only (no ECG path). Compare AUROC to full multimodal. Expected: Large drop for multimodal removal (+8.54% gain vs. EHR‑only per paper).
  2. **Projector capacity test:** Replace 2‑layer MLP with a linear layer. If performance drops significantly, non‑linearity in MM‑Projector is critical for alignment.
  3. **Prompt sensitivity test:** Systematically vary EHR template phrasing (e.g., “HR:88” vs. “The heart rate is 88”). Measure AUROC change. Expected: Large variance indicates fragility; small variance supports robustness of textualization approach.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does UniPACT generalize to external patient cohorts? Only validated on MIMIC‑IV; needs testing on other datasets (e.g., eICU, UK Biobank).
- Can temporal EHR data (e.g., vitals trends) be incorporated? Current prompting uses static values; temporal patterns may improve prognosis but are not handled.
- Does more sophisticated fusion (e.g., cross‑attention) improve results? Simple MLP + concatenation is used; advanced fusion might better exploit cross‑modal interactions.

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details: The D‑BETA ECG encoder is not publicly described; exact EHR templates and the full list of vital parameters are incomplete.
- Dataset specificity: All results are on MIMIC‑IV‑ECG; generalizability to other populations or data sources is unknown.
- Prompt engineering risks: Sensitivity to template wording is not systematically evaluated; brittle prompts could degrade performance.
- Training hyperparameters unspecified: Learning rates, batch sizes, and exact epoch counts are not provided, hindering full reproducibility.

## Confidence

- Claim 2 (MM‑Projector aligns ECG features into LLM space): Medium
- Claim 3 (Multi‑task learning improves robustness): High

## Next Checks
1. **Alternative ECG encoder test:** Replace D‑BETA with a publicly available encoder (e.g., PhysNet) and compare AUROC to quantify encoder specificity.
2. **Prompt sensitivity analysis:** Systematically vary EHR template phrasing and order; measure AUROC changes to assess robustness.
3. **Gradient conflict measurement:** During multi‑task training, compute gradient similarity across tasks; high negative correlation would indicate potential negative transfer in new settings.
