# Generative clinical time series models trained on moderate amounts of patient data are privacy preserving

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10631  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10631  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Membership inference attacks fail (AUROC ≈ 0.5) against synthetic clinical time series when generators are trained on ≥ 500 patient records.

## Executive Summary
This study audits the privacy of four state-of-the-art generative models for synthetic clinical time series (GHOSTS, HALO, KoVAE, Diffusion-TS) using a comprehensive suite of membership inference attacks (MIAs). Models were trained on the MIMIC‑IV ICU dataset, with eICU as external auxiliary data. The experiments show that all tested attacks become ineffective (AUROC ~0.5, chance level) once the generator’s training dataset exceeds approximately 500 patient records. This threshold holds across model architectures and attack types, and also for static patient attributes. The authors conclude that moderate training cohort sizes inherently provide strong empirical privacy protection, making differential privacy mechanisms—which typically degrade data utility—unnecessary in this regime. The findings highlight the dominant role of sample size in governing vulnerability to membership inference.

## Method Summary
The authors implemented a standardized privacy audit pipeline. For each generative model and training size (from 25 to 21 057 ICU stays), they trained the model on a MIMIC‑IV subset and generated 20 000 synthetic records. Synthetic time series were reduced to 40 principal components (PCA fit on the synthetic data) to enable feasible attack computation. Nine MIAs were then applied: Monte‑Carlo, three DOMIAS variants (density only, KDE ratio, BNAF ratio), five GANLeak variants (distance‑based and calibrated), and Logan‑pb (trainable discriminator). Attacks used the synthetic data to learn a scoring function and were evaluated on a balanced test set mixing equal numbers of real training and holdout records. Performance was measured as AUROC via 100 bootstrap samples. Overfitting was quantified by comparing nearest‑neighbor distances between synthetic data and training vs. holdout sets (NRMSEₘᵢₙ). Sanity checks (using real training data as synthetic) validated attack correctness, and cross‑dataset attacks with eICU auxiliary data assessed robustness to distribution shift.

## Key Results
- All membership inference attacks achieve chance‑level performance (AUROC ≈ 0.5) when generators are trained on ≥ 500 patient records.
- The sample‑size threshold is consistent across four generative models and nine attack types for both multivariate time series and static patient attributes.
- Overfitting (positive NRMSEₘᵢₙ) correlates with MIA success only below the threshold; beyond ~500 records, overfitting present no longer yields detectable signals.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Three mechanisms explain the findings:

1. **Overfitting as necessary but insufficient.** MIAs rely on the generator overfitting to its training data, creating synthetic samples that are statistically closer to training records than to holdout ones. However, even when overfitting occurs (measured by NRMSEₘᵢₙ > 0), attacks still fail for training sizes ≥ 500. This indicates that the residual overfitting signal is too weak or too dissimilar to the assumptions of existing attacks to be exploitable.

2. **Population size induces statistical indistinguishability.** With moderate cohorts (~500+ records), the generative model learns a distribution that well approximates the true population distribution. Synthetic samples from this learned distribution become indistinguishable from real samples drawn from the same underlying distribution, leaving no systematic difference for an MIA to detect whether a specific record was used in training.

3. **Limitations of current attack models.** Many MIAs adapted from static data (images, tables) are unsuitable for high‑dimensional, temporally dependent clinical time series. Attacks such as DOMIAS‑BNAF and Logan‑pb fail even in sanity checks (real training data as synthetic), suggesting that density estimation or discrimination assumptions break down in this domain. The chance‑level results at large N may partly reflect these attack limitations rather than perfect privacy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Membership Inference Attack (MIA)**  
  *Why needed:* It is the core empirical tool used to quantify privacy risk; the entire study is an audit using a battery of MIAs.  
  *Quick check:* If a synthetic generator perfectly mimics overall statistics but occasionally replicates exact patient trajectories, would an MIA looking for exact matches be more or less effective than one looking for statistical proximity?

- **Overfitting vs. Memorization**  
  *Why needed:* The paper distinguishes overfitting (capturing distributional nuances) from exact memorization; the NRMSEₘᵢₙ metric quantifies overfitting and correlates with MIA success only at small training sizes.  
  *Quick check:* Can a model have high utility (e.g., good SOFA prediction) but still be vulnerable to MIAs? What about a model with low utility?

- **Differential Privacy (DP)**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s claim that DP is unnecessary for moderate sample sizes contrasts theoretical guarantees with empirical results; understanding DP’s worst‑case guarantees and utility cost is essential.  
  *Quick check:* If an audit shows no MIA success at ε ≈ 10 (no DP), does that mean DP is broken or that the MIA threat model is too weak for this dataset?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
MIMIC‑IV/eICU preprocessing → PCA (40 components) → Synthetic Generator (GHOSTS, HALO, KoVAE, Diffusion‑TS) → Synthetic dataset → MIA Engine (9 attacks) → Evaluation Harness (AUROC, NRMSEₘᵢₙ).

**Critical path**  
For each (Generator, N_train):  
1. Subsample N_train stays from MIMIC‑IV train split.  
2. Train generator, produce 20 000 synthetic records.  
3. Fit PCA on synthetic data, transform all real sets (train, holdout, auxiliary).  
4. Build D_test = (N_train holdout records ∪ N_train training records).  
5. Train each attack on synthetic (and auxiliary if required).  
6. Compute attack scores for D_test, set threshold = median score, compute AUROC via 100 bootstrap resamples.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- PCA reduces dimensionality for attacks but may discard subtle discriminative patterns.  
- Attack suite breadth improves robustness but includes methods (e.g., DOMIAS‑BNAF) that fail on this data, adding noise to conclusions.  
- Threshold selection assumes the attacker knows the exact member proportion; real‑world conditions could differ.

**Failure signatures**  
- Generator failure: Synthetic data loses fidelity (e.g., poor downstream task performance).  
- MIA “success”: AUROC significantly > 0.5; if this occurs at N≥500, likely an implementation flaw.  
- Sanity check failure: Attack cannot distinguish real training data from holdout (e.g., DOMIAS‑BNAF, Logan‑pb on time series), rendering it invalid for this domain.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. *Sanity Check:* Replace synthetic data with real training data; expect high AUROC (≥ 0.8) on small N, except for known weak attacks.  
2. *Main Audit:* Vary N_train (25 to 21 057) and plot AUROC for each generator/attack; identify convergence to chance level around N=500.  
3. *Cross‑Dataset Attack:* Use eICU as auxiliary dataset (instead of MIMIC holdout) to attack MIMIC‑trained generators; test robustness to data‑distribution shift.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Q1:** Is the ~500‑sample privacy threshold generalizable to other clinical datasets (e.g., outpatient, pediatric, non‑US systems)?  
- **Q2:** How do generative models that jointly produce time series and static attributes compare to those handling modalities separately in terms of privacy protection?  
- **Q3:** Can purpose‑built MIAs that exploit temporal dynamics (periodicity, autocorrelation) succeed even when N≥500?  
- **Q4:** Can modern differential privacy techniques (e.g., DP‑SGD with privacy amplification) provide theoretical guarantees for generative clinical time‑series models without catastrophic utility loss?

## Limitations
- Attack implementations remain underspecified; several attacks (DOMIAS‑BNAF, Logan‑pb) fail sanity checks even on real data, casting doubt on their suitability for time‑series MIAs.  
- PCA preprocessing may distort distance metrics and discard signals relevant to membership inference, potentially inflating the observed privacy threshold.  
- Results are limited to two ICU datasets (MIMIC‑IV/eICU) and a single preprocessing pipeline; generalizability to other EHRs and data representations is unknown.

## Next Checks
- **Attack sensitivity test:** For each attack, confirm AUROC > 0.7 on the smallest training size (N=25) where overfitting is evident; discard attacks that fail.  
- **PCA ablation:** Repeat the full audit without PCA or with an alternative dimensionality reduction (e.g., autoencoder) to assess impact on the N‑threshold.  
- **External replication:** Conduct the same audit on a different clinical dataset (e.g., MIMIC‑III) to evaluate whether the ~500‑sample threshold holds.
