# Improving Risk Stratification in Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy: A Novel Score Combining Echocardiography, Clinical, and Medication Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26254
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26254
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** A Random Forest model integrating echocardiographic, clinical, and medication data achieved an AUC of 0.85±0.02 for 5-year composite cardiovascular outcome prediction, significantly outperforming the ESC score (AUC 0.56±0.03) and showing superior risk separation on external validation (log-rank p=8.62×10⁻⁴).

## Executive Summary
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) patients require accurate risk stratification to guide therapy. Current models, such as the Executive Summary Society of Cardiology (ESC) score, have only moderate predictive performance. In this study, the authors developed an explainable machine learning risk score using echocardiographic, clinical, and medication data from electronic health records. A Random Forest model trained on 1,201 patients achieved a high internal AUC of 0.85, far surpassing the ESC score (0.56). External validation on an independent cohort (N=382) confirmed robust risk separation (log-rank p=8.62×10⁻⁴ vs 0.0559 for ESC). The model's predictions increased longitudinally for patients who later experienced arrhythmic events or heart failure progression, demonstrating its ability to track dynamic risk. Interpretability was enhanced through SHAP analysis, which highlighted both known and novel predictors, supporting clinical translation.

## Method Summary
The study used two cohorts: Florence (N=1,201) for training/internal validation and Rennes (N=382) for external validation. Eighty-two features were initially considered: 63 echocardiographic, 14 clinical, and 5 medication variables. Preprocessing involved median imputation, standardization, and random undersampling to address class imbalance. Feature selection comprised two steps: first, removing pairwise highly correlated features (|ρ|>0.75) guided by clinical relevance; second, sequential floating forward selection (SFFS) within a nested cross-validation framework to optimize the feature subset and hyperparameters simultaneously. Classifiers evaluated included Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Gradient Boosting (GB). Nested cross-validation used an outer 5-fold stratified split and an inner 3-fold stratified split for hyperparameter tuning and feature selection. The best model from each outer fold (selected by AUC) was retained, and the five outer-fold models were ensembled by averaging predicted probabilities. Performance was assessed using AUC, sensitivity, specificity, balanced accuracy, and F1-score. Survival analysis used Kaplan‑Meier curves and log‑rank tests after dichotomizing predictions at an optimal threshold derived from the Florence training set. Longitudinal analysis applied each outer‑fold model to all prior examinations of its test‑set patients, computing a linear slope of predicted probability over time per patient and comparing slopes between event and event‑free groups. SHAP values were computed for each submodel and averaged across folds to provide global feature importance and local interpretability.

## Key Results
- **Internal validation:** Random Forest achieved AUC 0.85 ± 0.02, compared to ESC score AUC 0.56 ± 0.03 on the Florence cohort.
- **External validation:** On the Rennes cohort, the model showed superior risk separation with log‑rank p = 8.62×10⁻⁴; ESC score separation was non‑significant (p = 0.0559).
- **Longitudinal dynamics:** Predicted 5‑year probability increased in patients who experienced composite events (mean slope +0.0431/year) but remained stable in event‑free patients (slope −0.0027/year), with a statistically significant difference (T‑test).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The model outperforms traditional risk scores through a combination of comprehensive data integration, nonlinear modeling, and rigorous validation. By incorporating echocardiographic, clinical, and medication variables, it captures a broader spectrum of risk factors than the ESC score, which relies mainly on clinical and echocardiographic parameters without medication data. The Random Forest algorithm effectively models complex interactions among features and is robust to overfitting when coupled with nested cross‑validation and careful feature selection. The use of SHAP analysis yields both global and individual explanations, highlighting established predictors (e.g., left ventricular ejection fraction, left atrial diameter) and uncovering novel associations (e.g., specific drug effects), thus maintaining clinical interpretability. Longitudinal probability trends reflect true underlying disease progression, enabling dynamic risk monitoring and potentially earlier intervention.

- **Nested Cross‑Validation**: Outer loop estimates performance; inner loop selects features/hyperparameters; why needed: prevents data leakage and optimistic bias; quick check: Ensure test folds from outer loop are never used in feature selection or tuning.
- **Sequential Floating Forward Selection (SFFS)**: Greedy feature subset selection; why needed: identify compact, high‑performing feature set from many candidates; quick check: Track selection frequency of features across CV folds.
- **SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)**: Game‑theoretic approach to attribute predictions to features; why needed: provide interpretable, locally accurate explanations; quick check: Compare SHAP global importance with known clinical predictors for consistency.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Acquisition -> Preprocessing (Imputation, Standardization, Undersampling) -> Feature Selection (Correlation Filter + SFFS) -> Model Training (Nested CV) -> Ensemble -> Evaluation (Internal/External Metrics) -> SHAP & Longitudinal Analysis

**Critical path:** 
1. Load Florence and Rennes datasets with consistent feature definitions.
2. Perform median imputation (store medians from Florence training) and z‑score scaling.
3. Apply random undersampling to balance classes in training sets.
4. For each outer 5‑fold split: 
   - Compute pairwise correlations; remove one of each highly correlated pair per clinical guidance.
   - Run SFFS with inner 3‑fold CV to select optimal feature subset and tune hyperparameters (optimizing AUC).
   - Train final model on full outer‑training data using selected features and best hyperparameters.
   - Predict on outer test fold.
5. Average probabilities from the five outer‑fold models to produce final ensemble.
6. Compute internal performance metrics on pooled outer predictions.
7. Apply ensemble to Rennes cohort, dichotomize at ESC‑derived threshold, and run survival analysis.
8. Compute SHAP values (averaged across folds) and longitudinal slopes.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Undersampling** balances classes but discards data; alternatives like SMOTE could preserve information at the cost of synthetic sample complexity.
- **SFFS** is computationally expensive but yields a compact, high‑performing subset; alternatives include L1‑regularized models or tree‑based importance.
- **Random Forest** chosen for robustness and SHAP compatibility; Gradient Boosting might achieve higher accuracy but can be less stable.
- **Ensemble via probability averaging** reduces variance from single‑fold models; could also use weighted averaging based on AUC.
- **SHAP background**: Using a fixed subset of training data for all models ensures consistency but may not represent full distribution; larger background samples improve fidelity but increase compute.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Covariate shift** between Florence and Rennes cohorts leading to performance degradation; indicated by mismatched feature distributions (e.g., KS tests).
- **Data leakage** if feature selection or hyperparameter tuning touches outer test data; would manifest as unrealistically high internal AUC that does not replicate externally.
- **Unstable feature selection**: low Jaccard index of selected features across outer folds suggests overfitting.
- **Noisy longitudinal slopes** when few time points are available, producing unreliable trends.
- **SHAP inconsistency**: top features vary widely across submodels, indicating lack of robust explanations.

**First experiments:**
1. Replicate internal performance: Implement the described pipeline on the Florence cohort and confirm that the ensemble Random Forest achieves an AUC > 0.80 with nested CV.
2. External validation: Apply the trained ensemble to the Rennes cohort, dichotomize predictions at the threshold identified from the Florence training ROC, generate Kaplan‑Meier curves, and verify that the log‑rank p‑value is < 0.001.
3. SHAP analysis: Compute averaged SHAP values across the five outer‑fold models and check that known HCM risk markers (e.g., left atrial size, LVOT gradient) appear among the top ten global features.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly discuss open questions or future work. The authors focus on presenting the model development and validation without a dedicated section outlining unresolved issues or next steps.

## Limitations
- Critical implementation details are missing: exact hyperparameter grids, SFFS stopping criterion, undersampling ratio, ESC threshold value, SHAP background size, and precise composite outcome definition.
- Feature selection stability across outer folds is unknown; high variability could undermine model reliability.
- External validation was performed on a single site, raising concerns about covariate shift and generalizability; no calibration or decision‑curve analysis was reported.
- Longitudinal analysis assumes linear probability trends, which may not hold for all patients; effect sizes are modest and depend on consistent follow-up.

## Confidence
- **Performance superiority claim:** Medium – internal AUC is high but ESC threshold for external survival analysis is unspecified; external validation limited to one cohort.
- **Longitudinal slope claim:** Medium – significant differences observed but effect sizes are modest and dependent on probability calibration and number of exams.
- **Interpretability claim:** Medium – SHAP analysis is standard, but results may vary with background data choice; no clinical panel validated the identified predictors.

## Next Checks
1. Obtain or recompute the ESC score’s optimal threshold from the Florence training set (point on ROC closest to upper‑left corner), then re‑run the external survival analysis to confirm the reported log‑rank p‑value.
2. Evaluate feature selection stability by recording the frequency of each feature’s inclusion across the five outer‑fold SFFS runs; compute Jaccard index of final feature sets to assess consistency.
3. Diagnose covariate shift by comparing distributions of key variables (e.g., age, LVEF, LA diameter) between Florence and Rennes using histograms and KS tests; if shift exists, apply Platt scaling recalibration on a Rennes holdout set and re‑assess performance.
