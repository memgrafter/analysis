# CausalRM: Causal-Theoretic Reward Modeling for RLHF from Observational User Feedbacks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18736  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18736  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** CausalRM combats annotation errors and selection bias, achieving up to 49.2% improvement on WildGuardMix and 32.7% on HarmBench for safety alignment.

## Executive Summary
CausalRM introduces a causal framework to address two fundamental challenges in reward modeling from observational user feedback: annotation errors and selection bias. By modeling annotation errors via a noise-aware surrogate loss and correcting selection bias through propensity score reweighting (IPS or doubly robust), CausalRM yields an unbiased estimator of the ideal reward modeling objective. Theoretical guarantees are provided, and the method is validated on multiple benchmarks.

Experiments on HelpSteer, UltraFeedback, and PKU-SafeRLHF demonstrate that CausalRM significantly outperforms strong baselines, achieving lower MSE and higher R² across various LLM backbones. In downstream RLHF, it delivers substantial safety improvements, including a 49.2% gain on WildGuardMix and 32.7% on HarmBench, confirming its effectiveness in learning accurate reward signals from noisy, biased observational data.

## Method Summary
CausalRM employs a two-stage training procedure. First, nuisance models are learned: a propensity estimator \(p_\phi\) predicts the probability that a user provides feedback on a given response, and a proxy reward model \(r_\zeta\) is trained on the observed subset using inverse propensity weighting to mitigate selection bias. The proxy is used to identify anchor points (minimum and maximum preference responses) under a weak separability assumption, enabling estimation of false positive and false negative rates \(\rho_{10}\) and \(\rho_{01}\). Second, the final reward model \(r_\theta\) is trained using a causally-corrected loss that combines the noise-aware surrogate (based on estimated \(\rho\)) with propensity reweighting (IPS or doubly robust). This yields an unbiased estimator of the ideal objective under the assumed causal model. The overall pipeline allows reward models to be trained from realistic observational data that is both noisy and biased, without requiring ground‑truth preferences.

## Key Results
- CausalRM combines a noise‑aware surrogate loss (correcting false positive/negative rates) with propensity score reweighting (IPS or doubly robust) to yield an unbiased estimator of the ideal reward modeling objective.
- Empirically, CausalRM outperforms strong baselines on three benchmarks (HelpSteer, UltraFeedback, PKU‑SafeRLHF), achieving lower MSE and higher R² across multiple LLM backbones.
- In downstream RLHF, CausalRM improves safety alignment by 49.2% on WildGuardMix and 32.7% on HarmBench.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** CausalRM corrects annotation errors through a noise‑aware surrogate loss that is unbiased under the true noise model.
- **Mechanism:** Model annotation errors as class‑conditional noise with false positive rate \(\rho_{10}\) and false negative rate \(\rho_{01}\). Derive a surrogate loss \(\tilde{\ell}(\cdot,\cdot)\) such that \(\mathbb{E}_{r|r^*}[\tilde{\ell}(\hat{r},r)] = \ell(\hat{r},r^*)\). This yields specific forms for \(\tilde{\ell}(\cdot,1)\) and \(\tilde{\ell}(\cdot,0)\) (Eq. 7). During training, replace the primal loss with \(\tilde{\ell}\) computed on observed noisy feedback \(r_i\).
- **Core assumption:** Annotation errors follow a stationary noise model (same \(\rho\) for all \(x\)) and \(\rho_{01}+\rho_{10} < 1\). The true preference \(r^*\) exists and the loss \(\ell\) is standard (e.g., squared error).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "noise‑aware surrogate loss term that is provably equivalent to the primal loss under noise‑free conditions by explicitly modeling the annotation error generation process."  
  [section] Section 3.2 (Eqs. 6–8) provides the derivation and form of the surrogate loss.  
  [corpus] Weak; related work does not combine noise modeling with bias correction.
- **Break condition:** If noise rates vary across \(x\), or if \(\rho_{01}+\rho_{10}\) approaches 1, the surrogate becomes unstable. Misestimation of \(\rho\) (e.g., due to lack of anchor units) leads to biased gradients.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** CausalRM eliminates user preference bias by reweighting observed samples using estimated propensity scores, via IPS or doubly robust (DR) estimators.
- **Mechanism:** Define propensity \(p_i = P(o_i=1|x_i)\). Form an unbiased estimator \(L_{\text{IPS}} = \frac{1}{|D|} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{o_i}{\hat{p}_i} \tilde{\ell}(\hat{r}(x_i), r_i)\). Alternatively, \(L_{\text{DR}}\) adds an imputation model \(\hat{\varepsilon}(x_i)\) to reduce variance: \(L_{\text{DR}} = \frac{1}{|D|} \sum \left[ (1 - \frac{o_i}{\hat{p}_i})\hat{\varepsilon}(x_i) + \frac{o_i}{\hat{p}_i}\tilde{\ell}(\hat{r}(x_i), r_i) \right]\).
- **Core assumption:** The propensity model accurately captures the conditional observation probability; selection is ignorable given \(x\) (no unobserved confounding affecting both \(r^*\) and \(o\)). For DR, either \(\hat{p}\) or \(\hat{\varepsilon}\) must be accurate.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "propensity score reweighting (IPS or doubly robust) to eliminate selection bias."  
  [section] Section 3.3 defines \(L_{\text{IPS}}\) (Eq. 14) and \(L_{\text{DR}}\) (Eq. 15) and proves unbiasedness (Theorems 1,2).  
  [corpus] Weak; existing debiasing methods (IPS, DR) are applied separately from noise correction.
- **Break condition:** Poor propensity estimation (e.g., extremely small \(\hat{p}\)) causes high variance in IPS; DR mitigates but requires a well‑specified imputation model. If ignorability assumption fails (unobserved confounders), reweighting cannot recover the full distribution.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** CausalRM estimates the unknown noise rates \(\rho_{01}\) and \(\rho_{10}\) without ground‑truth preferences by exploiting weakly separable data and a proxy reward model.
- **Mechanism:** Under weak separability (\(\exists\) units with \(r^*\approx0\) and \(r^*\approx1\)), identify anchor units that minimize and maximize true preference via a proxy reward model trained on debiased observational data. Approximate \(P(r_i=1|x_{\text{anchor}})\) with the proxy’s prediction. Then \(\rho_{01} = 1 - P(r=1|x_{\text{max}})\) and \(\rho_{10} = P(r=1|x_{\text{min}})\).
- **Core assumption:** Weak separability holds: \(\inf_x P(r^*=1|x)=0\) and \(\sup_x P(r^*=1|x)=1\). The proxy model’s predictions are monotonic with true preferences, and noise rates are constant across \(x\).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] Not explicitly mentioned; but the overall framework includes "noise‑aware surrogate loss" that requires \(\rho\), and the method estimates them.  
  [section] Section 3.2 (Eqs. 10–13) states the weak separability assumption and describes anchor identification and estimation of \(\rho_{01}, \rho_{10}\).  
  [corpus] No direct evidence; related work often assumes noise rates are known or estimated from a separate clean set.
- **Break condition:** If anchor units do not exist (e.g., all responses have intermediate true preference) or the proxy model fails to rank them correctly due to high noise, estimated \(\rho\) become biased, compromising the surrogate loss unbiasedness.

## Foundational Learning
- **RLHF reward modeling:**  
  *Why needed here:* CausalRM operates in the reward modeling stage of RLHF; understanding standard objectives (MSE for point‑wise, BT for pairwise) clarifies what is being corrected.  
  *Quick check:* What loss is typically minimized for point‑wise reward modeling?  
  (Answer: Mean squared error between predicted reward and human rating.)

- **Selection bias and propensity scores:**  
  *Why needed:* The debiasing via IPS/DR relies on correctly modeling the probability of feedback observation; grasping the role of propensity scores is essential to understand the reweighting mechanism.  
  *Quick check:* What does a propensity score represent in observational reward modeling?  
  (Answer: The conditional probability \(P(o_i=1|x_i)\) that a user provides feedback on response \(x_i\).)

- **Label noise correction via transition matrix:**  
  *Why needed:* The derivation of the noise‑aware surrogate loss treats annotation errors as a probabilistic flipping process; this concept underpins the mathematical correction.  
  *Quick check:* Define false positive and false negative rates in the context of annotation errors.  
  (Answer: False positive: \(r_i=1\) while true preference \(r_i^*=0\); False negative: \(r_i=0\) while true preference \(r_i^*=1\).)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Frozen LLM backbone (e.g., Qwen, LLaMA) encodes prompt‑response pairs into embeddings -> MLP head (\(\hat{r}_\theta\)) predicts scalar reward -> MLP propensity estimator (\(\hat{p}_\phi\)) predicts feedback probability -> Proxy reward model (\(\hat{r}_\zeta\)) trained on debiased observational data -> (Optional for DR) MLP error imputation model (\(\hat{\varepsilon}_\psi\))

**Critical path:**  
1. Train propensity model \(\hat{p}_\phi\) on full dataset \(D\) to predict observability \(o_i\) (binary).  
2. Train proxy reward model \(\hat{r}_\zeta\) on observed set \(O\), weighting each sample by \(1/\hat{p}_\phi(x_i)\) to mitigate selection bias.  
3. Identify anchor units: find \(x_{\text{min}}\) and \(x_{\text{max}}\) in \(D\) that minimize and maximize \(\hat{r}_\zeta(x)\).  
4. Estimate noise rates: \(\hat{\rho}_{01} = 1 - \hat{r}_\zeta(x_{\text{max}})\), \(\hat{\rho}_{10} = \hat{r}_\zeta(x_{\text{min}})\).  
5. Train final reward model \(\hat{r}_\theta\) using CausalRM‑IPS (\(L_{\text{IPS}}\) with estimated \(\rho\), \(\hat{p}\)) or CausalRM‑DR (\(L_{\text{DR}}\), also train \(\hat{\varepsilon}_\psi\)).  
6. Deploy trained \(\hat{r}_\theta\) for RLHF policy optimization.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- IPS vs DR: IPS is simpler but can have high variance when propensities are small; DR reduces variance at the cost of an additional imputation model and potential bias if imputation is poor.  
- Noise rate estimation relies on weak separability; if the data lacks extreme examples, \(\rho\) estimates become unreliable.  
- Proxy model quality: using a weakly trained proxy may misidentify anchors; consider training proxy with sufficient capacity and early stopping.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Training loss diverges or plateaus at high value: likely misestimated \(\rho\) or poor propensity model.  
- High gradient noise (spikes) in IPS training indicates extremely small \(\hat{p}\); switch to DR or apply propensity clipping.  
- Validation MSE worse than naive baseline: assumptions violated (e.g., noise not class‑conditional, anchors missing) or implementation bug (e.g., forgetting to detach gradients).  
- Downstream RLHF shows no improvement or reward hacking: check whether reward model generalizes to unobserved data; verify that reweighting and noise correction are active.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Compare CausalRM against strong baselines (Naive, IPS, SDR, ILDE) on three benchmark datasets (HelpSteer, UltraFeedback, PKU‑SafeRLHF) under simulated noise and bias, reporting MSE, MAE, R².  
2. Ablation study: remove debiasing (IPS only) or denoising (noise‑aware loss only) to show both components are necessary for optimal performance.  
3. Synthetic/semi‑synthetic experiments with known true preferences to empirically validate that CausalRM yields an unbiased estimator of the ideal objective, while naive and partial methods remain biased.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can observational and experimental feedback be integrated in CausalRM's framework?**  
   - *Basis:* The limitations section states that "an important future direction is therefore to synergistically combine the scalability of observational feedback data with the quality of experimental feedback data to further enhance RM performance."  
   - *Why unresolved:* The current CausalRM framework relies solely on observational feedback. Experimental feedback, though costly, provides high‑quality preference signals. A principled method to fuse both data types within the causal debiasing and denoising framework is unexplored.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Develop a hybrid learning objective that combines observational and experimental data, possibly with a weighting scheme. Empirically validate on mixed datasets to assess whether integration improves reward model accuracy and downstream RLHF performance compared to using either source alone.

2. **Is the weak separability assumption for noise rate estimation realistic across diverse LLM tasks?**  
   - *Basis:* Section 3.2 uses the "weak separability assumption (Menon et al., 2015): \(\inf P(r^*=1|x)=0\) and \(\sup P(r^*=1|x)=1\)" to identify anchor units for estimating annotation error rates.  
   - *Why unresolved:* This assumption requires the existence of responses with near‑certain positive or negative ground‑truth preferences. In nuanced tasks (e.g., creative writing, subjective preferences), such anchor units may not exist, potentially leading to biased noise rate estimates and flawed surrogate loss correction.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct controlled studies across diverse tasks (e.g., coding, dialogue, summarization) to evaluate anchor unit identification success rates. Compare estimated noise rates against ground truth from small experimental datasets to measure estimation bias under varying degrees of separability.

3. **How does CausalRM handle extreme user preference bias and near‑zero propensity scores?**  
   - *Basis:* The IPS estimator is known for high variance when propensities are small, and while DR reduces variance, the theoretical variance reduction condition (Theorem .3) requires the imputation error to be no more than twice the ground‑truth error, which may not hold under extreme bias.  
   - *Why unresolved:* In practice, users may provide feedback only on a tiny subset of responses (e.g., \(\alpha\to0\) in simulations), yielding extremely low propensities. This could destabilize training despite DR's double robustness, especially if propensity model misspecification occurs.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Perform stress tests with simulated data having extreme bias (\(\alpha\) values close to 0) and analyze variance, convergence stability, and RM accuracy across propensity distributions. Compare CausalRM‑DR against alternative variance‑reduced estimators in such regimes.

4. **Can CausalRM's causal framework be extended to non‑binary or implicit user feedback formats?**  
   - *Basis:* The problem formalization (Section 2.2) and loss functions assume binary point‑wise feedback (\(r_i \in \{0,1\}\)). Multi‑level ratings (e.g., 1‑5) or implicit signals (e.g., dwell time, scroll depth) are not addressed.  
   - *Why unresolved:* Real‑world user feedback is often ordinal or continuous. The noise model (false positive/negative rates) and surrogate loss derivation (Equation 7) are specific to binary outcomes. Generalizing to multi‑class or continuous feedback requires new causal assumptions and mathematical formulations.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Reformulate the noise‑aware surrogate loss for multi‑class classification (e.g., extending to categorical cross‑entropy) or regression (e.g., with Gaussian noise). Derive unbiased estimators and validate on datasets with multi‑level annotations (e.g., original HelpSteer attributes) against appropriate baselines.

## Limitations
- Core assumptions (stationary annotation noise, weak separability, selection ignorability) may not hold in real observational settings; violations can lead to biased reward models.
- Anchor‑based noise‑rate estimation relies on proxy monotonicity and presence of true extreme examples; failure here yields unreliable \(\rho\) estimates and unstable training.
- Propensity scoring is error‑prone: IPS suffers from high variance when propensities are small, while doubly robust introduces imputation model bias if mis‑specified.

## Confidence
- Noise‑aware surrogate loss corrects annotation errors: Medium  
- Propensity‑based reweighting (IPS/DR) eliminates selection bias: Medium  
- Anchor‑based \(\rho\) estimation: Low  
- Empirical superiority over baselines: High (on reported datasets) / Medium (generalization)
