# On Representation Redundancy in Large-Scale Instruction Tuning Data Selection

## Quick Facts
- *arXiv ID*: 2602.13773  
- *Source URL*: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13773  
- *Reference count*: 40  
- *Primary result*: CRDS-W selects only 3.5% of a 2M-example instruction-tuning pool and outperforms the full-data baseline by an average of 0.71% across four benchmarks (GSM8K, MMLU, MBPP, BBH).  

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the challenge of selecting high-quality data for instruction tuning at an industrial scale, where millions of examples render exhaustive evaluation infeasible. It reveals that semantic embeddings produced by LLM encoders are highly redundant, which undermines the effectiveness of standard nearest‑neighbor selection based on cosine similarity. To overcome this, the authors propose Compressed Representation Data Selection (CRDS), featuring two variants: CRDS‑R, which compresses and concatenates hidden states from multiple transformer layers via Rademacher random projection; and CRDS‑W, which applies whitening‑based dimensionality reduction to decorrelate embeddings. Both methods substantially improve data quality, with CRDS‑W achieving state‑of‑the‑art results—using merely 3.5% of the data, it surpasses the full‑data baseline by 0.71% on average and exceeds the prior representation‑based SOTA RDS+ by up to 1.54% absolute at 5% data scale. The work demonstrates that careful representation compression can dramatically enhance the efficiency and efficacy of large‑scale data selection.

## Method Summary
CRDS operates on a large pool of instruction examples and a small set of representative test tasks. First, a pretrained LLM encoder (e.g., Llama‑2‑7B or Qwen‑3‑8B) extracts hidden‑state embeddings for each example. For CRDS‑R, the last *H* hidden layers are retained; each layer’s representation is independently projected to a lower dimension using a Rademacher random matrix, and the projected vectors are concatenated to form a compact yet informative embedding. For CRDS‑W, a whitening transform is fitted on a large random subset of pool embeddings: it centers the data and decorrelates dimensions (optionally reducing dimensionality to *β*), yielding an isotropic space where cosine similarity better reflects semantic proximity. After preprocessing, the cosine similarity between every test embedding and every pool embedding is computed (using a distributed sharding scheme to handle scale). A round‑robin top‑*k* retriever then cycles through the test examples, selecting the most similar pool examples in a balanced manner to maintain diversity. The chosen subset is used to fine‑tune a target LLM (e.g., a 16B model) via standard supervised fine‑tuning. The entire pipeline is designed for scalability, storing embeddings on disk to avoid GPU memory overflow.

## Key Results
- CRDS‑W achieves superior performance using only 3.5% (70k) of a 2M‑example dataset, surpassing the full‑data baseline by an average of 0.71% across GSM8K, MMLU, MBPP, and BBH.
- CRDS‑W outperforms the state‑of‑the‑art representation‑based method RDS+ by up to 1.54% absolute gain at the 5% (100k) data scale.
- CRDS‑R also consistently beats RDS+, though its performance depends on the number of concatenated layers (optimal *H*=18 for Qwen‑3‑8B).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method’s gains stem from addressing two interrelated problems: the redundancy of LLM encoder embeddings and the anisotropy that distorts cosine similarity.

1. **Redundancy in raw embeddings** hides discriminative signal. The binarization experiment—keeping only the sign of each dimension—produced nearly identical data selections and downstream accuracy as full‑precision embeddings, indicating that magnitude variations carry little useful information and may even introduce noise. This suggests that many dimensions are collinear or irrelevant for distinguishing instruction examples. Consequently, a naive cosine similarity over the original high‑dimensional space is an ineffective selector.

2. **CRDS‑R mitigates redundancy while preserving complementary semantics** by drawing from multiple transformer layers. Different layers encode distinct abstractions (e.g., syntactic vs. conceptual). Applying a separate Rademacher random projection to each layer’s hidden state (a Johnson–Lindenstrauss transform) compresses each representation while approximately preserving inner products. Concatenating these compressed layers integrates orthogonalizable information without blowing up dimensionality. This multi‑layer design yields a richer, less redundant representation space for retrieval, as evidenced by performance peaks when increasing *H* beyond a single layer.

3. **CRDS‑W corrects anisotropy to make cosine similarity geometrically faithful**. LLM embeddings often exhibit a cone‑like structure (non‑zero mean, correlated dimensions), causing certain points to dominate similarity rankings (the “hubness” problem). Whitening centers the embeddings and decorrelates dimensions, producing an isotropic space where cosine similarity aligns with Euclidean distance and vector angles are unbiased. This reduces artificial similarity inflation and yields more accurate nearest‑neighbor retrieval. The optimal whitening dimensionality *β* (∼20% of original) reflects a trade‑off: enough dimensions to retain task‑relevant signal while discarding noise and anisotropic bias.

Together, these mechanisms explain why compressed, de‑correlated representations lead to markedly better data selection.

## Foundational Learning
- **Semantic embeddings from LLM encoders**  
  *Why needed*: CRDS uses hidden‑state vectors as proxies for the semantic content of instruction examples. Understanding that these vectors are often anisotropic and redundant is crucial to motivate compression.  
  *Quick check*: If two sentences are semantically similar, should their embeddings have a high cosine similarity? What could cause high‑similarity pairs to be unrelated?

- **Cosine similarity and its geometric interpretation**  
  *Why needed*: The selection criterion is cosine similarity. Its sensitivity to vector direction vs. magnitude, and how whitening changes the relationship between cosine and Euclidean distance, is central to the method.  
  *Quick check*: How does whitening affect the distribution of vector norms? After whitening, does a high cosine similarity always imply a small Euclidean distance?

- **Dimensionality reduction: random projection vs. whitening**  
  *Why needed*: CRDS‑R and CRDS‑W employ different reduction techniques with distinct properties (inner‑product preservation vs. de‑correlation). Knowing their trade‑offs explains their design choices.  
  *Quick check*: What does the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma guarantee about random projections? Does whitening preserve pairwise distances or inner products?

## Architecture Onboarding
The CRDS pipeline comprises the following components in sequence:

**Encoder** (LLM) → **Preprocessor** (CRDS‑R: multi‑layer Rademacher projection & concatenation; CRDS‑W: whitening transform) → **Similarity engine** (cosine similarity computation) → **Selector** (round‑robin top‑*k* retriever) → **Distributed coordinator** (sharding, disk I/O management).

**Critical path**: Encode the data pool → preprocess pool embeddings → encode the test set → preprocess test embeddings → compute the full asymmetric similarity matrix → run the round‑robin selection → output the selected indices.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *CRDS‑R vs. CRDS‑W*: CRDS‑R is online (no fitting required), leverages multi‑layer information, but may not fully remove anisotropy; CRDS‑W needs a fitting pass (SVD on a large subset) but explicitly decorrelates and can reduce noise.
- *Memory vs. time*: The framework stores embeddings on disk to avoid GPU OOM, trading I/O bandwidth for scalability; whitening fitting is a one‑time cost.
- *Encoder choice*: Performance is encoder‑dependent; multi‑layer CRDS‑R works best with encoders whose layers are diverse (e.g., Qwen), while CRDS‑W is more robust across encoders.
- *Failure modes*: Poor whitening (too small/biased fitting set), layer mis‑selection for CRDS‑R, distribution shift between test and pool, and round‑robin implementation bugs can degrade selection.

**Failure signatures**:
- Whitening yields NaNs or extreme norm variance → indicate singular covariance or insufficient fitting samples.
- CRDS‑R performance drops when *H* is too small or too large → suggests under‑ or over‑integration of layer information.
- Selected data heavily biased toward one test task → round‑robin logic is broken.
- Binarization experiment fails to replicate original selections → redundancy assumption violated or similarity mis‑computed.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Binarization sanity check**: Apply sign‑only binarization to pool and test embeddings; confirm that selection overlap and downstream accuracy remain nearly identical to full‑precision. If not, the redundancy premise is unsound.
2. **CRDS‑R hyperparameter sweep**: Vary the number of layers *H* (e.g., 1–32) and measure downstream accuracy on a validation set; expect a peak (e.g., *H*≈18 for Qwen‑3‑8B).
3. **CRDS‑W hyperparameter sweep**: Vary the whitening output dimension *β* (e.g., 64–2048) and track validation performance; anticipate an inverted‑U curve with optimal *β* around 20% of original dimension. Also verify that whitening reduces norm variance of embeddings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to smaller models**: Does CRDS work for base models below 7B parameters? The paper notes unknown behavior for small models, as they may have fundamentally different representations. Evidence from a single 7B model shows mixed results, necessitating systematic evaluation across scales and architectures.
2. **Mechanism of compression success**: Why do Rademacher projection and whitening improve selection? The paper acknowledges a lack of theoretical understanding of how compression affects the relationship between representation similarity and data utility.
3. **Representation vs. gradient methods**: Are representation‑based selectors like CRDS fundamentally more scalable and effective than gradient‑based approaches for industrial‑scale instruction tuning? The paper did not compare due to computational limits; developing scalable gradient approximations could resolve this.
4. **Root causes of redundancy**: What makes LLM encoder embeddings so redundant? The phenomenon is observed but not dissected; investigating intrinsic dimensionality, eigenvalue spectra, or training objectives could explain its origins.

## Limitations
- **Proprietary resources**: The core data pool (Ling 2.0) and base model (Ling‑mini‑2.0) are internal, so exact replication is impossible; public substitutes may not capture the same distribution.
- **Incomplete specifications**: Training hyperparameters, prompts, and evaluation details are not fully disclosed, leading to implementation variance.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and scalability**: Optimal CRDS settings (*H*, *β*, random seeds) are not robustly justified, and the distributed framework for handling 2M examples is custom and not easily reproduced with standard tools.

## Confidence
- Embedding redundancy: **High** (binarization experiment shows almost unchanged selection).
- CRDS‑R (multi‑layer random projection): **Medium** (consistent gains but encoder‑specific layer selection).
- CRDS‑W (whitening): **Medium** (outperforms RDS+ reliably, yet optimal *β* varies and risk of over‑reduction exists).
- Overall claim (3.5% beats full baseline): **Medium‑High** (validated on four benchmarks but depends on internal data/model and noted instability).
