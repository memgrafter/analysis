# Implementation of Quantum Implicit Neural Representation in Deterministic and Probabilistic Autoencoders for Image Reconstruction/Generation Tasks

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.06755
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06755
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** QINR‑VAE generates sharper, more diverse images than PQWGAN, Quantum AnoGAN, and QINR‑QGAN, achieving FID scores in the 110–200 range and higher SSIM/PSNR on MNIST, E‑MNIST, and Fashion‑MNIST.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles low diversity and instability in quantum generative models (QGANs) by introducing quantum implicit neural representation (QINR) into autoencoder (AE) and variational autoencoder (VAE) frameworks. The hybrid architecture combines a classical CNN encoder with a quantum decoder built from a parameterized quantum circuit that employs data reuploading and learnable angle‑scaling to map latent vectors into a high‑frequency feature space. Evaluated on MNIST, E‑MNIST, and Fashion‑MNIST (500 samples per class), QINR‑VAE produces significantly sharper and more diverse images than baseline QGANs, with lower FID, higher SSIM/PSNR, and stable training despite modest quantum resources (6 qubits, ~120 parameters). These results show that QINR can mitigate mode collapse and offer a promising path for quantum‑enhanced generative modeling under constrained data and hardware.

## Method Summary
The method uses a classical convolutional encoder to compress a binarized 28×28 image into a low‑dimensional latent vector (d_z = 8). For the VAE, the latent is sampled from a Gaussian parameterized by the encoder. The latent is then linearly transformed and batch‑normalized to yield rotation angles for a quantum circuit implementing the QINR decoder. The quantum block consists of alternating **encoding layers** (RZ rotations scaled by learnable parameters ξ) and **parameterized layers** (single‑qubit rotations and CZ entangling gates). Measurements (Pauli Z on all qubits) pass through shallow classical linear layers (128→512→784) to produce image logits. Training employs separate learning rates for classical and quantum parameters, gradient clipping, and a reconstruction loss (binary cross‑entropy) together with a KL‑divergence term (VAE only) applied with a β‑warm‑up or capacity schedule. This hybrid design yields stable optimization and high‑quality generation.

## Key Results
- **Lower FID:** QINR‑VAE achieves FID scores in the 110–200 range on MNIST, E‑MNIST, and Fashion‑MNIST, outperforming PQWGAN, Quantum AnoGAN, and QINR‑QGAN.
- **Sharper & more diverse outputs:** Visual assessment confirms mitigation of mode collapse; generated images exhibit greater intra‑class diversity and sharpness.
- **Stable training under limited resources:** Smoothly decreasing BCEWithLogits and KL losses are observed using only 6 qubits and ~120 quantum parameters.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Data Reuploading Enables High‑Frequency Feature Space
The QINR decoder transforms latent vectors into a rich, high‑frequency feature space via data reuploading in a parameterized quantum circuit. The latent h is used as rotation angles (RZ) in multiple encoding layers `Sℓ(h)`. The circuit’s layered structure and entangling gates (CZ) allow it to approximate Fourier series‑like functions, where the output f(h) acts as a spectral decomposition. This superposition of frequency components yields varied outputs even from similar latents, boosting diversity. *Evidence:* [abstract] mentions “highly rich, periodic, and high‑frequency features”; [section 3.2] links data reuploading to Fourier series; [section 4.1] shows visually greater intraclass diversity versus baselines that suffer mode collapse.

### Mechanism 2: Learnable Angle‑Scaling Stabilizes Optimization
Learnable per‑reupload scaling parameters ξℓ adapt the effective input magnitude to the quantum circuit, improving gradient flow and preventing vanishing/exploding gradients. The angles h are multiplied element‑wise by ξ before RZ rotations. This autonomy lets the model find an optimal input range for the nonlinear PQC transformation, reducing manual tuning. *Evidence:* [abstract] cites addressing optimization challenges; [section 3.2] explains that trainable scales enhance expressiveness and reduce hyperparameter tuning; [section 4.2] displays stable, monotonically decreasing loss curves for QINR‑VAE, contrasting with the instability typical of unmodified QGAN training.

### Mechanism 3: Hybrid Classical‑Quantum Architecture Balances Capacity and Efficiency
The classical CNN encoder provides robust hierarchical compression, while the quantum decoder—with very few parameters (≈120)—offers a highly expressive nonlinear mapping from the small latent (d_z=8) to the full image. This contrasts with QGANs that must learn the full data distribution adversarially with many quantum parameters (2016–3600), often leading to instability and mode collapse. *Evidence:* [abstract] highlights the hybrid model’s advantage in “taking a point in latent space and converting it into an instance in image space with a compact but highly expressive decoder”; [section 1] frames this as a key motivation; [section 4.1] shows QINR‑VAE outputs are cleaner and more diverse than PQWGAN, Quantum AnoGAN, and QINR‑QGAN.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input (28×28 binary image) → CNN Encoder → Latent z → Linear + BatchNorm → Quantum core (L=2 encoding, K=2 parameter layers) → Pauli‑Z measurement on 6 qubits → Linear (128→512→784) → Output logits.

**Critical path:** Image → CNN Encoder → Latent z → Linear(h) → [Encoding(L1) → Param(L1) → Encoding(L2) → Param(L2)] → Measure → Linear Output → Logits.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Quantum vs. classical parameters: The QINR decoder uses very few quantum parameters (≈120) and relies on classical pre/post‑layers for most capacity, trading quantum expressiveness per parameter for overall efficiency.
- Measurement basis: Single‑basis (Z) readout is simple but discards phase information; multi‑observable (X,Y,Z,ZZ) readouts improve metrics at the cost of more circuit executions.
- Latent dimension: d_z=8 forces aggressive compression; success implies the quantum decoder can unpack substantial structure from minimal information.

**Failure signatures:**
- Training instability: oscillating or diverging loss curves (common in QGANs). QINR‑VAE shows smooth decay.
- Posterior collapse (VAE): KL loss drops to zero early; reconstruction stagnates. Prevented by β/C scheduling.
- Mode collapse (GANs): generated samples within a class appear nearly identical.
- Blurry/regular outputs (e.g., on CelebA): indicate insufficient model capacity or data.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Train separate QINR‑AE/VAE per digit on MNIST (500 samples/class). Monitor BCEWithLogits loss (and KL for VAE) over epochs to verify stable convergence.
2. Sample 10–20 latents from the prior (VAE) or random z (AE) for a chosen digit and generate images. Compare visually to PQWGAN, Quantum AnoGAN, and QINR‑QGAN outputs, focusing on diversity and sharpness.
3. Compute SSIM, PSNR, and cosine similarity for reconstructions, and FID for prior samples (VAE). Compare averages to the paper’s reported ranges (FID ≈ 110–200, SSIM ≈ 0.6–0.8 on MNIST) to reproduce the quantitative advantage.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization to unseen distributions:** How do QINR‑AE/VAE perform on full datasets, out‑of‑distribution samples, or higher‑resolution images? The authors explicitly state that generalization “is left for future research.” (Evidence: Section 1, last paragraph). Resolution would require testing on larger, more varied datasets and measuring metric degradation.
- **Robustness to quantum hardware noise:** The paper evaluates only noiseless simulations; it remains unknown how realistic noise (decoherence, gate errors, finite sampling) affects quality and stability. (Evidence: Section 1, last paragraph). Addressing this would involve simulating noise models or running on quantum devices.
- **Optimal QINR decoder architecture:** Which design choices (number of reuploading layers, qubit connectivity, multi‑observable readouts) maximize diversity and sharpness without harming stability? The paper uses a fixed architecture due to resource limits and does not systematically explore alternatives. (Evidence: Section 4.1 and Appendix B hint at the impact of multi‑basis readouts). A thorough ablation study is needed.

## Limitations
- **Incomplete architectural details:** The exact specification of post‑quantum linear layers (activation functions, layer sizes) and key hyperparameters (gradient clipping threshold, Adam β₁/β₂, β/C scheduling details) are missing, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **Scalability beyond small images:** Experiments rely on binarized 28×28 images with only 500 samples per class. It is unclear whether the approach extends to higher‑resolution or natural images, where modest performance on CelebA suggests potential limitations.
- **Lack of mechanism isolation:** The three proposed mechanisms (data reuploading, learnable scaling, hybrid architecture) are not disentangled via ablations; alternative factors (e.g., encoder capacity, VAE regularization) could contribute to the observed improvements.
