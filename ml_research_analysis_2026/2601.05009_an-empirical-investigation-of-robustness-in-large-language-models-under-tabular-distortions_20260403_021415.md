# An Empirical Investigation of Robustness in Large Language Models under Tabular Distortions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05009  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05009  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** All tested LLMs exhibit substantial accuracy declines on distorted tables, with GPT-5.2 showing the smallest drop at 22%, and distortion‑aware prompting yielding inconsistent improvements.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the robustness of large language models (LLMs) when processing tabular data that contains semantic and structural distortions. The authors construct an expert‑curated benchmark comprising 50 canonical tables and 100 distorted variants (50 semantic, 50 structural). They evaluate a diverse set of LLMs across multiple input modalities (text, image, file upload) and with or without distortion‑aware prompting. The results reveal that all models suffer significant accuracy degradation on distorted tables, with vertical column shifts being particularly challenging. Distortion‑aware prompting helps only selectively and can even Harm performance for some fine‑tuned models. The study concludes that current LLMs lack an intrinsic ability to detect or correct layout distortions; they instead treat them as preprocessing noise.

## Method Summary
The core methodology revolves around a controlled evaluation framework. Experts first created 50 canonical tables covering realistic domains. They then applied 22 semantic distortions (e.g., swapped units, mismatched symbols) and 28 structural distortions (e.g., column shifts, row misplacements) to generate distorted variants. For each table, the authors prepared up to three input representations: raw file upload, Markdown text, and PNG image. Models were queried with a fixed set of table‑centric questions under two system‑prompt conditions: distortion‑unaware (baseline) and distortion‑aware (explicitly instructing the model to check for and correct distortions). Some configurations allowed the model to generate and execute Python code in a sandbox to perform computations. Primary metrics were exact‑match accuracy on canonical and distorted tables, and robustness (ratio of distorted to canonical accuracy). The setup was run with temperature 0.1 and three independent trials for pass@3 estimation.

## Key Results
- All models showed meaningful accuracy drops on distorted tables; GPT‑5.2 ’s minimum drop was 22% under distortion.
- Distortion‑aware prompting produced inconsistent gains; for vertical structural shifts, GPT‑5’s best accuracy was only 45.45%.
- LLMs do not proactively detect distortions; they often treat them as noise and fail to realign columns/rows before reasoning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1  
- **Claim:** LLMs default to assuming tables are correctly formatted, leading to failures when encountering distortions.  
- **Mechanism:** Models rely on a heuristic focusing on headers and the first few rows, ignoring global layout constraints; this works for canonical tables but breaks when rows or columns are displaced.  
- **Core assumption:** Training data predominantly consists of well‑formed tables, creating an inductive bias toward canonical layouts.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “LLMs lack an inherent ability to detect and correct subtle distortions … Only when provided with an explicit prior … do models partially adjust.”  
  - Section 5.2: “Models are encouraged to focus primarily on table headers and the first few rows … ignoring the full table layout where such shifts become evident.”  
- **Break condition:** Exposure to diverse distorted tables during pre‑training or incorporation of a preprocessing module that validates global table structure before reasoning.

### Mechanism 2  
- **Claim:** LLMs often detect distortions only after execution feedback, not proactively.  
- **Mechanism:** Models generate reasoning or code under the assumption that the table is correct; when execution fails or yields inconsistent results, they may retrospectively adjust the table layout.  
- **Core assumption:** LLMs lack an internal validation loop for input structure; they rely on downstream errors as a signal.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section 5.3: “For GPT‑5.2 … the model detects and corrects distortions before producing the final answer in at least 80% of cases. In contrast, for image‑based inputs … about 70% of cases are handled retrospectively.”  
  - Section 5.1 and Figure 4 show that only with explicit prompting does the model repair the table before answering.  
- **Break condition:** Introduction of a pre‑execution structural consistency check that forces validation of column/row alignment before processing.

### Mechanism 3  
- **Claim:** Distortion‑aware prompting yields inconsistent improvement because it conflicts with fine‑tuning objectives in some models.  
- **Mechanism:** Models fine‑tuned for direct table operations (e.g., Mistral, Deepseek‑R1‑Distill) are biased toward executing operations without questioning input validity; an error‑aware prompt may confuse this bias, causing hesitation or ineffective checks.  
- **Core assumption:** A model’s response to system prompts is shaped by its fine‑tuning data and loss function, which may prioritize execution fidelity over verification.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section 5.1: “We don’t find a similar trend holding in Mistral and Deepseek‑R1‑Distill. Their inductive bias favors executing operations directly … introducing an explicit error‑aware prompt may conflict … leading to hesitation or ineffective checks.”  
  - Table 1 shows Mistral‑7B‑Instruct‑v0.2 (Text) robustness: Dist. Unaware 10%, Dist. Aware 4%.  
- **Break condition:** Re‑fine‑tuning models on tasks that require distortion detection and correction, aligning the instruction‑following objective with structural validation.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Dataset module -> Representation generator -> Prompt manager -> Model registry -> Inference loop (with optional Execution sandbox) -> Evaluation engine -> Metrics aggregator

**Critical path:**  
1. Dataset preparation: experts create canonical tables and apply single‑level distortions.  
2. Representation generation: produce Markdown and PNG for each table.  
3. Prompt construction: choose aware/unaware prompt per config.  
4. Inference loop: load model, provide table context, inject prompt and query, optionally allow Python generation, record output.  
5. Scoring: compare answer to ground truth, aggregate over three runs.  
6. Analysis: compute robustness ratios and breakdowns by distortion subtype.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Dataset size vs. control: expert‑curated 50 tables ensure quality and precise distortion control but limit diversity; larger synthetic datasets risk uncontrolled noise.  
- Markdown vs. CSV: Markdown preserves visual alignment cues needed for structural distortions; CSV loses those cues but is more canonical.  
- Sandbox vs. direct reasoning: sandbox isolates distortion recovery from arithmetic errors; however, models may bypass detection by writing code that naively operates on misaligned columns, inflating false robustness.  
- Distortion‑aware prompting: probes instruction‑following adaptation but may interact with fine‑tuning objectives.  
- Vertical vs. horizontal focus: highlights a specific weakness but may overlook other structural issues.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Large accuracy drop on distorted tables (e.g., GPT‑5.1 shows 48% drop in Text mode).  
- Very low robustness on vertical column shifts (best observed 45.45% for GPT‑5 Text).  
- No improvement or regression with distortion‑aware prompting for some models (Mistral, Deepseek‑R1‑Distill).  
- Modality‑dependent detection: image inputs lead to late distortion handling (70% of successful cases corrected after execution).  
- Systematic behavior: models treat distortions as preprocessing noise (e.g., dropping misaligned columns) rather than repairing layout.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Canonical vs. distorted accuracy sweep: run all models on canonical and all distorted tables to measure overall robustness and identify worst‑case degradation.  
2. Distortion‑aware vs. unaware comparison: for each model, evaluate with both prompt types; compute accuracy delta and stratify by distortion type.  
3. Structural sub‑analysis: isolate horizontal and vertical structural distortions; test across input modalities (None/Text/Image) with and without code sandbox to pinpoint why vertical shifts are especially challenging.

## Limitations
- Small expert dataset (50 tables) limits diversity and statistical power.  
- Distortions are single‑level only; multi‑level or combined distortions are not examined.  
- Exact‑match metric may not capture partial correctness; evaluation covers a limited set of model families.

## Confidence
- Overall robustness drop: **High** (consistent across all tested models and modalities).  
- Distortion‑aware prompting yields inconsistent improvement: **Medium** (observed pattern but limited per‑model replication).  
- No autonomous detection/correction of distortions: **High** (clear output evidence).  
- Heuristic focus on headers/first rows: **Medium** (requires internal probing).  
- Reactive detection after execution: **Medium** (needs finer‑grained analysis).  
- Prompt vs. fine‑tuning conflict: **Low** (only two models, no causal manipulation).

## Next Checks
1. Scale benchmark: create a larger, diverse tabular dataset with multi‑level distortions and test on all major LLMs (GPT‑4, Claude, Llama 3, etc.).  
2. Causal fine‑tuning test: fine‑tune a base model on distorted tables; measure whether distortion‑aware prompting improves, directly probing the conflict hypothesis.  
3. Internal analysis: use attention roll‑outs or probing to verify whether models encode global layout constraints and whether they detect misalignments before generating reasoning steps.
