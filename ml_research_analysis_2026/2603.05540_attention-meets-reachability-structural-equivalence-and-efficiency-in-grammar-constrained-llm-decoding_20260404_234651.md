# Attention Meets Reachability: Structural Equivalence and Efficiency in Grammar-Constrained LLM Decoding

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05540
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05540
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Language-equivalent CFGs induce identical admissible token sets during grammar-constrained decoding, but structural differences can cause polynomial vs. constant per-token engine costs.

## Executive Summary
Grammar-constrained decoding (GCD) uses formal grammars to restrict the token choices of large language models. This paper shows that while logically equivalent grammars produce the same admissible tokens (ensuring semantic invariance), they can lead to vastly different computational costs during decoding. The authors formalize GCD as pushdown reachability, introduce Structural Ambiguity Cost (SAC) to quantify incremental parse-forest growth, and prove that any sound, retrieval-efficient masking engine must incur quadratic per-token work for certain constant-size CFGs. Thus, grammar design becomes a critical performance lever, separate from expressiveness.

## Method Summary
The method compiles a CFG into a pushdown automaton (PDA) that tracks reachable configurations for each generated prefix. Admissible tokens are those for which the extended prefix remains in the language's prefix closure. Structural Ambiguity Cost (SAC) measures the worst-case increase in the size of the packed parse forest (representing ambiguous derivations) per token. For empirical analysis, specific CFG families (e.g., Σ* and aⁿbⁿ) are instrumented to compare control-state counts and split-node growth under different equivalent formulations.

## Key Results
- Oracle invariance: Language-equivalent grammars induce identical admissible next-token sets for every prefix.
- SAC separation: For Σ*, a right-recursive grammar yields O(1) SAC per token, while a concatenative grammar yields Θ(t²) SAC (cumulative Θ(n³)).
- Engine lower bound: Any sound, retrieval-efficient, parse-preserving online masking engine must incur Ω(t²) per-token work on a specific constant-size CFG family.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Language-equivalent CFGs induce identical admissible token sets (logit masks) during GCD.
- **Mechanism:** GCD reduces to pushdown reachability: a token v is admissible at prefix u iff uτ(v) remains in the prefix closure of L(G). Since prefix closure depends only on the language, equivalent grammars yield identical masks.
- **Core assumption:** The compiled PDA correctly recognizes L(G).
- **Evidence anchors:** Theorem 1 formalizes the reduction; corollary establishes invariance.
- **Break condition:** If the tokenizer τ is not a homomorphism (e.g., subword tokens split across terminals), equivalence may fail.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Structurally different but language-equivalent grammars cause radically different per-token engine costs, measurable by SAC.
- **Mechanism:** SAC counts incremental packed parse forest nodes (e.g., split triples). For Σ*, right-recursion keeps splits per token constant; concatenation forces quadratic growth because each new token creates splits with all prior boundaries.
- **Core assumption:** The engine maintains a fully ambiguous packed forest (SPPF-style).
- **Evidence anchors:** Theorem 2 proves Θ(t²) SAC for G₄; empirical instrumentation in Section 5.4.
- **Break condition:** If the engine prunes or aggregates ambiguities, SAC may underestimate true work but then violates parse preservation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Any sound, retrieval-efficient, parse-preserving online masking engine must incur Ω(t²) per-token work for certain CFGs.
- **Mechanism:** For G₄, the set of parse structures P_G(u) includes all triples (i,k,j) with 0≤i<k<j≤t. At step t, Ω(t²) new triples appear. Retrieval efficiency requires the engine to support enumeration in time linear to |P_G(u)|, forcing Ω(t²) memory writes per token.
- **Core assumption:** The engine explicitly stores all splits to enable output-sensitive enumeration (Definition 16).
- **Evidence anchors:** Theorem 3 establishes the lower bound via split-complete representation.
- **Break condition:** If the engine relaxes retrieval efficiency (e.g., approximates parses), the bound does not apply.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Pushdown Reachability  
  **Why needed:** GCD requires checking whether a prefix remains in Pref(L(G)), formalized as non-emptiness of CoReach_G in the compiled PDA. Distinguishing Reach (all configurations) from CoReach (live configurations) ensures sound masking.  
  **Quick check:** How does CoReach differ from Reach in Definition 6? Why must the engine filter to live configurations to avoid blocking valid continuations?

- **Concept:** Packed Parse Forests  
  **Why needed:** SAC quantifies overhead by counting symbol and packed nodes in the forest. Ambiguity causes polynomial blowup; engineers must instrument these counts or proxies.  
  **Quick check:** For G₄ and length-n string, why does Lemma 6 yield (n+1 choose 3) packed nodes? Relate to Catalan parse count and binary tree splits.

- **Concept:** Structural Ambiguity Cost (SAC)  
  **Why needed:** SAC isolates the incremental cost of grammar structure independent of language. It predicts decoding latency and guides grammar design.  
  **Quick check:** For a right-recursive grammar, why does SAC stay O(1) per token? What structural property prevents combinatorial explosion?

- **Concept:** Grammar-Constrained Decoding (GCD)  
  **Why needed:** GCD couples neural logits with a grammar oracle to enforce syntactic validity. Understanding its formalization as reachability is key to correct implementation.  
  **Quick check:** What is the difference between a hard mask (binary) and grammar-conditioned logits (modulated)? When would each be used?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Neural core (Transformer computes logits) -> Masking engine (compiles CFG to PDA, maintains live configuration set C_t) -> Integration layer (applies mask m_{A_t} to logits, optionally adds grammar-conditioned vectors).
- **Critical path:** The masking engine update (computing C_t and Ω_G) must finish before next-token sampling. Under high SAC, this can exceed Transformer forward time, becoming the bottleneck. Overlap is critical: while GPU runs forward pass, CPU updates engine state.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  Grammar shape: Prefer right-recursive over concatenative forms to keep SAC O(1).  
  Engine type: General CFG (Earley) vs. deterministic PDA (LR(1)). Determinism can reduce active configuration fanout.  
  Vocabulary alignment: Ensure tokenizer's terminal mapping aligns with grammar to avoid spurious ambiguity.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  Latency increasing super-linearly with generated length t (sign of Θ(t²) SAC).  
  Memory bloat due to packed forest growth; out-of-memory in beam search as beam width B multiplies independent state growth.  
  Cache thrashing from scanning large bitsets (state count κ(G) inflation per Lemma 2).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Grammar equivalence stress test:** Implement G₃ (right-recursive) and G₄ (concatenative) for Σ*. Generate 256 tokens, profile per-token engine CPU time. Expect G₄ time ∼ t², G₃ constant.
  2. **Instrumentation validation:** Using an Earley engine, instrument creation of split nodes and active items. Correlate SAC proxy (split count per token) with wall-clock time. Fit affine model T_mask(t) ≈ a·SE_t + b.
  3. **Beam amplification:** Run beam search with widths B=1,4,16 on aⁿbⁿ grammar. Total engine time should scale roughly linearly with B if engine states are independent across hypotheses. Deviations indicate state sharing overhead.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** What is the computational complexity of finding a grammar with minimal structural ambiguity cost (SAC) for a given context-free language? The paper proves existence of minimal-SAC representatives within bounded rewrite families but notes that full pushdown automaton minimization is NP-hard, suggesting global minimal SAC may be intractable.
- **Question 2:** Does SAC reliably predict actual decoding latency across diverse hardware, engines, and model architectures? SAC is proposed as a theoretical performance surrogate, but mapping from packed-parse-forest growth to wall-clock time depends on implementation details, cache effects, and GPU–CPU overlap, which are not captured by asymptotic SAC alone.
- **Question 3:** Can grammar-conditioned logits meaningfully reduce the KL distortion of hard masking relative to true conditional sampling? The theoretical distortion bound depends solely on survival-probability spread Γ; it is unclear whether grammar-conditioned logits systematically reduce Γ in practice.
- **Question 4:** For arbitrary CFGs, what is the maximum possible SAC growth rate? Is Θ(t²) per token the worst-case, or can it be super-quadratic? The paper separates SAC O(1) and Θ(t²) for two Σ* grammars but does not classify the full spectrum for general CFGs.

## Limitations
- Tokenizer alignment: Invariance assumes τ is a homomorphism; subword tokenizers violate this.
- Parse preservation: Ω(t²) lower bound requires storing all splits; real engines often prune, violating retrieval efficiency.
- Asymptotic effects: SAC differences manifest only at larger t; constant factors may dominate in practice.

## Confidence
- Oracle invariance: High (proven, but tokenizer-dependent).
- SAC separation (Σ*): High (proven for specific grammars).
- Engine lower bound: Medium (requires strict retrieval-efficiency; many engines relax it).
- Hard-masking KL bound: Medium (theoretical, needs empirical validation).
- Minimal-SAC existence: High (proven within bounded families).
- SAC as latency predictor: Low (no empirical validation provided).
