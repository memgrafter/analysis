# Stylometry Analysis of Human and Machine Text for Academic Integrity
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2601.01225
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01225
*   **Reference Count:** 39
*   **Primary Result:** Transformer models achieve near-perfect F1-scores (~0.9998) on human vs. machine text classification with "normal" prompts, but performance degrades (~0.9463) with "strict" prompts designed to mimic human style; tasks involving fine-grained author identification remain challenging (F1 ~0.68, ~0.51).

## Executive Summary
This study presents a stylometry-based NLP framework using transformer models (BERT, RoBERTa) to address academic integrity threats. It introduces a dataset generated with Gemini, where machine text is embedded into human-authored multi-author documents under "normal" and "strict" prompting conditions. The framework tackles four tasks: distinguishing human from machine text, classifying single vs. multi-authored documents, detecting author changes at the paragraph level, and recognizing specific authors. Results show exceptional performance on the first two tasks, a significant drop for the more nuanced author change detection, and poor results for specific author recognition, highlighting both the potential and current limits of stylometric analysis for collaborative document forensics.

## Method Summary
The method involves generating two variants of a dataset based on an existing human-authored corpus of four authors. Machine text is created using the Gemini API with either a "normal" prompt (topic-aligned) or a "strict" prompt (context/style-mimicking). These machine paragraphs are randomly inserted into multi-author documents, simulating a fifth author. For each of the four tasks, the text is formatted (e.g., document-level or paragraph pairs) and used to fine-tune pre-trained transformer encoders (BERT, RoBERTa, ALBERT, DistilBERT). Training uses a weighted loss function to handle class imbalance, a fixed learning rate (1e-5), and a cosine scheduler. Models are evaluated primarily on F1-score, with threshold tuning for the multi-label author recognition task.

## Key Results
*   **Task 1 (Human/Machine):** Near-perfect F1-score of ~0.9998 on "normal" prompt data, dropping significantly to ~0.9463 on the more challenging "strict" prompt data.
*   **Task 2 (Single/Multi-author):** Consistently high F1-score of ~0.986 across both prompt types, indicating document-level stylistic heterogeneity is a strong signal.
*   **Tasks 3 & 4 (Fine-grained analysis):** Substantially lower performance, with F1-scores of ~0.68 for author change detection and ~0.51 for author recognition, underscoring the complexity of localized style shifts and distinguishing similar human experts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Transformer models can distinguish human-written from machine-generated text with near-perfect accuracy by capturing distinctive stylistic fingerprints in their lower/intermediate layers.
*   **Mechanism:** Lower transformer layers extract traditional stylometric features (function words, punctuation, sentence rhythm) that differ systematically between human and AI prose. These features are more stable than semantic content, which can be prompted to mimic.
*   **Core assumption:** A generative model (Gemini) under a consistent prompting strategy produces text with high intra-class cohesion and low inter-class separation from human text.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] High F1-scores (~0.9998) on Task 1.
    *   [section 3.3] Lower/intermediate layers extract traditional stylometry features.
    *   [section 4.2.1] Sentence-BERT embeddings showed higher intra-class cohesion for machine text from the normal prompt (0.29) vs. strict prompt (0.14).
*   **Break condition:** Performance degrades significantly (F1 ~0.946) with "strict" prompts that force the AI to mimic surrounding context, reducing inter-class separation and increasing machine text's intra-class diversity.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Classification of single vs. multi-authored documents works well because multi-author documents exhibit greater overall stylistic heterogeneity at the document level.
*   **Mechanism:** The model learns to recognize the overall "consistency score" of a document's writing style. Single-author documents show higher uniformity, while multi-author documents (with embedded machine text) show lower uniformity.
*   **Core assumption:** The combination of multiple human authors plus machine paragraphs creates a detectable "composite origin" signal at the document level.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] Task 2 F1 remained high (~0.986).
    *   [section 4.2.2] Performance improvement attributed to lower complexity and lower involvement of AI-generated text compared to Task 1.
    *   [section 3.1] Machine text was "randomly embedded," disrupting stylistic coherence.
*   **Break condition:** If all authors in a multi-author document produce highly uniform outputs under homogenizing prompts, document-level stylistic variance may not exceed that of a single-author document.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Fine-grained author change detection and specific author recognition are substantially harder because they require detecting subtle, localized style shifts and distinguishing between several similar human authors.
*   **Mechanism:** These tasks demand paragraph-level sensitivity. Transformers may be dominated by semantic topical coherence (shared technical topic), overshadowing subtle lexical/syntactic cues. Author recognition is a multi-label problem with difficult class distinctions.
*   **Core assumption:** Style change signals are weak relative to semantic content at the paragraph level, and human authors within the same domain have more similar styles than a human vs. machine comparison.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] Tasks 3 and 4 F1-scores were substantially lower (~0.68, ~0.51).
    *   [section 4.2.3] Lower performance due to task complexity.
    *   [section 4.2.4] Very low precision vs. recall, and poor performance on certain classes due to low sample counts (data sparsity, class imbalance).
*   **Break condition:** Performance plateaus at low F1-scores (~0.5-0.7) because discriminative stylistic signals for individual human authors or paragraph boundaries are too faint or inconsistent for current architectures on this dataset.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Stylometry & Style Change Detection (SCD)**
    *   **Why needed here:** The entire framework relies on stylometry—analyzing authorial style via intrinsic textual features (lexical richness, punctuation, syntax) rather than content. SCD is the specific task of detecting where an author changes within a document.
    *   **Quick check:** What are three example stylometric features that might remain stable even when an AI is prompted to mimic a human's topic? (e.g., function word frequency, punctuation distribution, average sentence length).
*   **Concept: Transformer Model Layer Specialization**
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper's key mechanism posits that lower/intermediate layers capture "shallow" stylometric signals while higher layers capture semantic content. This explains why models work for human/machine classification but struggle with fine-grained tasks.
    *   **Quick check:** If a model is achieving high accuracy on a stylometry task, would you expect the learned representations to be more dependent on the first 4 layers or the last 4 layers of a 12-layer BERT? Why? (First 4 layers, as they capture more superficial, style-related features).
*   **Concept: Imbalanced Classification & Class-Weighting**
    *   **Why needed here:** The datasets, especially for author recognition, are highly imbalanced. The paper uses class weights in the loss function instead of resampling techniques like SMOTE.
    *   **Quick check:** When using class weights in a loss function, what is the effect on the gradient for a misclassified example from the minority class compared to the majority class? (The gradient is scaled up, increasing the update step for minority class errors).

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Dataset Generator (Gemini API) -> Pre-processor (Cleaning, Class Weight Calculation) -> Classifier Network (Fine-tuned Transformer) -> Evaluator (F1, Precision, Recall, Threshold Search)

Critical path: Dataset Generation dictates prompt type and insertion logic -> Pre-processing applies cleaning and calculates class weights -> Model Fine-tuning uses weighted loss with specific hyperparameters -> Evaluation computes metrics, with grid search for optimal multi-label thresholds. Dataset generation is the primary determinant of downstream task difficulty.

Design tradeoffs:
*   Model size vs. speed: DistilBERT is smaller/faster but may sacrifice nuanced style capture compared to RoBERTa/BERT; paper shows minor performance differences.
*   Loss function: Class weighting was chosen over SMOTE/undersampling. This retains all samples but risks the model being dominated by majority class patterns if weights are insufficient.
*   Prompt engineering: The "strict" prompt creates a more challenging, realistic scenario but hurts performance. Using only "normal" prompts yields artificially high metrics.

Failure signatures:
*   Task 1: Large drop in F1 (>0.05) between normal and strict prompt datasets indicates prompt stealthiness is effective.
*   Task 3 & 4: F1 scores below 0.70, especially low precision in multi-label task, indicate inability to reliably identify fine-grained author boundaries or distinguish similar humans. Per-class analysis (Table 9) shows catastrophic failure on minority human authors (Authors 2,3,4).
*   Intra-class cohesion: Low similarity among human text samples is expected. Abnormally low intra-class cohesion for machine text in the strict prompt dataset (0.14 vs 0.29) correlates with poorer classification performance.

First 3 experiments:
1.  Replicate Dataset Generation: Implement the flowchart from Section 3.1. Generate small samples with both prompt types. Verify paragraph insertion logic and ground truth (author change list) updates. Check that machine text topics align with provided list for normal prompt.
2.  Baseline Task 1 Replication: Fine-tune `distilbert-base-uncased` on the normal prompt dataset for human/machine classification. Reproduce Table 4 results (>0.999 F1). Then, test on the strict prompt dataset to observe the performance drop (~0.946 F1).
3.  Task 4 Failure Diagnosis: Using the trained author recognition model, analyze per-class precision/recall (like Table 9). Confirm catastrophic failure on minority human authors (Author 4). Experiment with adjusting the decision threshold for weak classes (grid search) to see if recall improves at the cost of precision.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** What specifications should a large-scale benchmark dataset for academic integrity tasks include to effectively cover diverse educational content (e.g., project reports, theses) and support multi-task evaluation (human/machine, single/multi-author, author change)?
*   **Open Question 2:** Can incorporating multi-modal features (linguistic, semantic, contextual, metadata) significantly improve the performance of author change detection (Task 3) and author recognition (Task 4) over text-only transformer models?
*   **Open Question 3:** How can stylometry models be made robust against adversarial prompts that reduce machine-text detectability, as demonstrated by the performance drop from normal (~0.9998) to strict prompts (~0.9463) in Task 1?

## Limitations
*   Dataset generation specifics (Gemini API usage, rate limits) not fully documented, hindering exact replication.
*   Preprocessing pipeline details (text normalization, handling of long documents) remain ambiguous.
*   Performance on tasks 3 & 4 is low (F1 ~0.68, ~0.51), limiting practical utility.
*   Findings may be dataset-specific (Gemini, one human corpus); generalizability to other LLMs or domains is uncertain.
*   Severe class imbalance in author recognition causes catastrophic failure on minority authors (Table 9).

## Confidence
*   **High:** Core empirical results (Task1 normal F1~0.9998, strict F1~0.9463; Task2 F1~0.986; Task3/4 low scores). Directly measurable from released data/code.
*   **Medium:** Mechanistic claim that lower transformer layers capture stylometric features. Supported by analysis but lacks causal ablation.
*   **Low:** Extrapolation to unseen LLMs or real-world plagiarism scenarios due to artificial dataset construction and single-model focus.
