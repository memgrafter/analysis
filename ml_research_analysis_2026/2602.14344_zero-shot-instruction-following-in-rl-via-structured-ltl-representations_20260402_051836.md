# Zero-Shot Instruction Following in RL via Structured LTL Representations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.14344
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14344
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: StructLTL achieves state-of-the-art zero-shot instruction following on LTL-specified tasks, with >95% success on complex Warehouse tasks where competitors fall below 70%.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles zero-shot instruction following in multi-task reinforcement learning (RL) using Linear Temporal Logic (LTL). Existing methods fail to capture LTL's rich logical and temporal structure, leading to poor generalization. The authors propose **StructLTL**, which conditions the policy on sequences of Boolean formulae derived from the Büchi automaton of the LTL task. A hierarchical neural architecture encodes the formulae's logical structure, and a temporal attention mechanism allows non-myopic reasoning about future subgoals. Experiments on ZoneEnv and a new Warehouse environment show that StructLTL significantly outperforms baselines in success rates and sample efficiency, especially on tasks requiring compositional reasoning with many propositions.

## Method Summary
StructLTL converts an LTL specification φ into a limit-deterministic Büchi automaton (LDBA). From the current automaton state, it enumerates all accepting runs via depth-first search. Each transition in a run is translated into a pair of Boolean formulae: β⁺ (assignments enabling the desired transition) and β⁻ (assignments to avoid). These sets are minimized to disjunctive normal form (DNF) using Quine-McCluskey. The policy π(a|s, ζ) conditions on a sequence ζ of these formula pairs (or ε-transition tokens). A hierarchical encoder embeds each formula by aggregating literals within clauses (conjunction) and then clauses across disjunctions via DeepSets. A temporal attention layer (with ALiBi bias) contextualizes the current step's representation by attending to all future steps. The state of the environment is encoded by an MLP or CNN, and the combined representation feeds actor and critic heads. Training uses PPO with a curriculum that gradually increases sequence length and formula complexity. At test time, for a new φ, candidate sequences are generated, and the one with the highest value function output is selected and followed until the automaton state changes.

## Key Results
- StructLTL achieves state-of-the-art success rates on both ZoneEnv and Warehouse environments.
- On complex Warehouse tasks (involving many propositions and composite actions), StructLTL reaches >95% success while LTL2Action and DeepLTL fall below 70%.
- The method demonstrates superior sample efficiency and compositional generalization, particularly on tasks requiring handling numerous atomic propositions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Structured Representation via Boolean Formulae
**Claim**: Representing LDBA states as sequences of Boolean formulae enables compositional generalization and mitigates combinatorial explosion from raw assignment sets.  
**Mechanism**: For each transition in an accepting run, the algorithm constructs β⁺ (assignments that move to the desired next automaton state) and β⁻ (assignments to avoid, e.g., sink states). Quine-McCluskey minimizes these sets to succinct DNF. The ordered sequence of such pairs describes high-level subgoals with explicit temporal ordering.  
**Evidence**: The abstract states that the policy conditions on "sequences of Boolean formulae constructed from a finite automaton." Section 4.1 explains the construction. In Warehouse (many propositions), StructLTL's >95% success vs DeepLTL's <70% (Table 1) shows the structured representation scales better with proposition count.

### Mechanism 2: Hierarchical DNF Encoder
**Claim**: A hierarchical DeepSets encoder that respects DNF structure (conjunction then disjunction) allows the policy to generalize compositionally across formulae.  
**Mechanism**: Atomic propositions have learned embeddings. Within a clause, literals are aggregated via a permutation-invariant DeepSets encoder (sum + MLP). Clause embeddings are then aggregated across the disjunction via another DeepSets, yielding a step embedding that captures the formula's semantics.  
**Evidence**: Section 4.2 details the hierarchical processing. An ablation in Appendix D.1 (Figure 5a) replaces this with a flat GRU encoder; the flat version fails to progress beyond early curriculum stages in Warehouse, while the hierarchical version succeeds.

### Mechanism 3: Temporal Attention with ALiBi
**Claim**: A single-head attention layer with ALiBi biases enables non-myopic reasoning about future subgoals, improving sample efficiency and avoiding dead ends.  
**Mechanism**: The sequence of step embeddings (from β⁺/β⁻ pairs) is processed with attention where the first step's embedding acts as the query. ALiBi subtracts a linear penalty based on step index, biasing the model toward nearer future steps while maintaining access to all. This contextualizes the current subgoal.  
**Evidence**: The abstract mentions an "attention mechanism that enables the policy to reason about future subgoals." Section 4.2 elaborates. Appendix D.2 (Figure 5b) shows attention outperforms both a myopic baseline (using only first step) and a GRU sequence encoder in ZoneEnv on success rate and steps to completion.

## Foundational Learning
- **Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) and automata semantics**  
  Why needed: The entire pipeline starts from an LTL formula, which is converted to an LDBA. The automaton's states and transitions encode the task's temporal requirements, and the run extraction produces the structured input to the policy.  
  Quick check: For "F a ∧ G b", the formula requires that 'a' eventually holds and that from that point onward 'b' always holds. An accepting run in the LDBA would transition to a state where 'a' is true, then move to a cycle where 'b' is always true.

- **Goal-conditioned reinforcement learning**  
  Why needed: StructLTL trains a single policy π(a|s, ζ) conditioned on a task representation ζ (the formula sequence). This is a form of multi-task RL where the goal is implicit in ζ. The reward shaping approximates LTL satisfaction.  
  Quick check: In a standard MDP, the policy maps states to actions. In a goal-conditioned MDP, the policy also takes a goal as input, and the reward depends on both state and goal.

- **Set encoders (DeepSets) and attention mechanisms**  
  Why needed: DeepSets enables permutation-invariant aggregation of literals within clauses and clauses within disjunctions, critical for processing the hierarchical DNF structure. Attention allows the policy to directly attend to all future subgoals in the sequence, overcoming the sequential bottleneck of RNNs.  
  Quick check: Why DeepSets for clause literals? Because the conjunction "a ∧ b" is equivalent to "b ∧ a"; aggregating literals as a set respects this symmetry. What does attention solve for variable-length sequences? It provides direct access to all positions, avoiding signal degradation in long sequences.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
LTL-to-Automata (Spot) → Run Extraction (DFS + Quine-McCluskey) → Formula Encoder (proposition embeddings, hierarchical DeepSets) → Temporal Attention (ALiBi) → State Encoder (MLP/CNN) → Policy Head (actor + critic)

**Critical path**:  
1. Test time: Given new LTL φ, build LDBA B_φ.  
2. From initial automaton state q₀, run DFS to enumerate accepting runs and produce candidate formula sequences {ζ_i}.  
3. For current environment state s, compute value V^π(s, ζ_i) for each ζ_i; select ζ* = argmax.  
4. Execute a_t ~ π(·|s_t, ζ*). After environment step, update automaton state via δ(q, L(s_t)).  
5. If automaton state changed, return to step 2.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- **DNF minimization**: Quine-McCluskey yields minimal DNF but is exponential in worst-case; for many propositions, approximate minimization could reduce computational cost at extraction time.  
- **Attention vs RNN**: Attention provides direct future access and handles variable lengths with ALiBi; RNNs process sequentially and may lose early information. Simpler RNNs might suffice for short sequences but underperform on complex tasks.  
- **Curriculum**: Stage thresholds (κ) are tuned per environment. Too rapid progression risks training collapse; too slow slows convergence.

**Failure signatures**:  
- **Low success on tasks with many propositions**: Indicates formulas may be too large or the encoder cannot handle complexity; check DNF size distribution.  
- **Myopic behavior**: Agent satisfies current β⁺ but later violates β⁻ or gets stuck; inspect attention weights—if nearly uniform or only on immediate step, attention is underutilized.  
- **Curriculum stagnation**: Failure to progress past a stage may be due to insufficient network capacity, poor hyperparameters, or formula sequences too difficult relative to agent capability.  
- **High variance across seeds**: Suggests unstable PPO optimization; consider increasing batch size or adjusting entropy regularization.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Formula extraction validation**: For a simple LDBA (e.g., from LTL "F a ∧ G b"), manually compute β⁺ and β⁻ for a transition and verify the algorithm's DNF matches the intended assignment sets.  
2. **Encoder unit test**: Feed known formulae like "a ∧ b" and "a ∨ b" into the hierarchical encoder and a flat GRU encoder. The hierarchical embedding should differentiate structure, while the flat one may not capture the compositional difference.  
3. **Zero-shot compositional probe**: Train on a curriculum with at most 3 atomic propositions in conjuncts. Test on a held-out task requiring a conjunction of 4 propositions; success indicates out-of-distribution generalization.

## Limitations
- **Formula generation distribution** not fully specified (depth, literal count), impacting reproducibility and task diversity.  
- **DNF minimization** don't-care handling is unclear; implementation variations may produce different formula sizes.  
- **Scalability** beyond 7 propositions remains untested; the value function may not reliably predict success for very long sequences.

## Confidence
- **SOTA performance on tested environments**: High (clear empirical superiority across multiple seeds).  
- **Structured generalization (compositional)**: Medium (evidenced by Warehouse but not large-scale tests with many propositions).  
- **Hierarchical encoder necessity**: High (ablation shows flat encoders fail).  
- **Temporal attention benefit**: High (ablation confirms non-myopic improvement).

## Next Checks
1. Validate formula extraction: For a simple LDBA (e.g., from "F a ∧ G b"), compute β⁺/β⁻ manually and check against the algorithm's DNF output.  
2. Test hierarchical encoder: Compare embeddings of "a ∧ b" vs "a ∨ b" in hierarchical and flat encoders; hierarchical should reflect structural differences.  
3. Probe compositional zero-shot: Train on ≤3 conjuncts; test on a 4-conjunct task; success confirms the representation's compositional nature.
