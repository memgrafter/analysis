# OMGs: A multi-agent system supporting MDT decision-making across the ovarian tumour care continuum

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13793
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13793
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: OMGs achieved comparable safety‑gated overall SPEAR scores (4.45±0.30 vs 4.53±0.23) and significantly higher Evidence scores (4.57 vs 3.92) versus expert MDT consensus; prospective evaluation showed high concordance.

## Executive Summary
The OMGs system is a multi‑agent AI framework that emulates multidisciplinary tumor board (MDT) decision‑making for ovarian cancer across the care continuum. Five role‑specific agents (chair, oncology, radiology, pathology, nuclear medicine) independently analyze case data, engage in constrained deliberation to resolve conflicts, and produce traceable treatment recommendations with citations from a curated evidence‑bank. In a multicentre re‑evaluation of 100 cases, OMGs was non‑inferior to expert MDT consensus in overall safety‑gated SPEAR score (4.45±0.30 vs 4.53±0.23) and achieved significantly higher Evidence scores (4.57 vs 3.92). Prospective real‑world testing on 59 patients demonstrated high concordance with routine MDT decisions, and human‑AI collaboration further improved clinicians’ own Evidence and Robustness ratings. The work suggests that structured multi‑agent deliberation can expand access to high‑quality cancer care decision support.

## Method Summary
Raw EHR documents are parsed into a structured JSON summary. Role‑specific agents receive scoped data packages (e.g., imaging for radiology, pathology reports for pathology). Each agent independently formulates an assessment, citing only from a pre‑built evidence‑bank containing guidelines and high‑impact trial literature. If conflicts or safety gaps are detected, agents engage in limited rounds of debate, challenging each other’s interpretations. The chair synthesizes a final recommendation in a predefined JSON schema (Final Assessment, Core Treatment Strategy, Change Triggers). An offline citation audit module verifies that all evidence claims correspond to actual entries in the evidence‑bank, capping the Evidence score if unsupported. Performance is measured by the SPEAR rubric (Safety, Personalization, Evidence, Actionability, Robustness), with the overall score capped at the Safety rating if Safety < 3.

## Key Results
- Overall safety‑gated SPEAR score comparable to expert MDT (4.45±0.30 vs 4.53±0.23)
- Evidence scores significantly higher than MDT (4.57 vs 3.92)
- High concordance with routine MDT decisions in prospective evaluation (n=59)

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Multi‑agent deliberation improves evidence integration and reasoning depth
**Claim:** Role‑specific debate surfaces missing variables and resolves conflicts, producing more robust recommendations than a single agent aggregating all data.  
**Mechanism:** Five specialty agents first make independent assessments using only their scoped inputs. They then participate in a constrained dialogue triggered by conflicts or safety concerns; each can challenge, clarify, and refine others’ claims before the chair synthesizes a consensus. This mimics the push‑and‑pull of a human MDT rather than parallel monologues.  
**Core assumption:** Structured agent interaction replicates interdisciplinary negotiation, leading to deeper clinical reasoning and personalization.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section Page 6 describe multi‑round, evidence‑backed deliberation; alignment with prior multi‑agent medical decision research (e.g., UCAgents, CoMMa) that promotes debate, though OMGs uniquely ties evaluation to real‑world MDT re‑evaluation.  
**Break condition:** Inputs are too fragmented or ambiguous (e.g., unclear disease status, discordant documentation) preventing agents from forming a coherent picture; the system defaults to “Unknown” or repeatedly escalates safety issues, resulting in a capped overall score.

### Mechanism 2: Traceable, evidence‑linked outputs build trust and accountability
**Claim:** Requiring citations from a static evidence‑bank and auditing fidelity increases clinicians’ acceptance, particularly in Evidence and Robustness dimensions.  
**Mechanism:** The system retrieves guideline and trial passages into a fixed “evidence‑bank” with stable identifiers. Every claim in the final recommendation must cite a specific bank entry or a verifiable patient‑report provenance. An audit step caps the Evidence score if a citation is partially supported or hallucinated.  
**Core assumption:** Auditable, source‑grounded rationales mitigate over‑generation and provide a verifiable chain of reasoning that clinicians trust.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes on “transparent rationales” and higher Evidence scores; Section Page 32–33 on citation restrictions and audit; Phase II citation audit showing 98% of outputs passed uncapped, 2% capped at 3.  
**Break condition:** Critical guideline recommendations or trial eligibility criteria are missing from the evidence‑bank, forcing generic citations or omissions and triggering audit caps.

### Mechanism 3: Safety is a first‑class constraint via role‑bound reasoning and gating
**Claim:** Isolating specialty reasoning and enforcing a Safety‑gated overall score prevents unsafe recommendations and surfaces data‑quality limitations.  
**Mechanism:** Each agent accesses only role‑scoped data (e.g., radiology sees images, not full pathology text). The final Safety score is evaluated separately; if Safety < 3, the overall SPEAR score is capped (Overall = min(Overall_raw, Safety)). This design explicitly highlights cases where records are insufficient for a safe decision.  
**Core assumption:** Preventing cross‑contamination of assumptions and capping overall scores forces attention to data sufficiency before treatment strategy.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract reporting comparable safety‑gated scores; Section Page 9 showing Safety ≥ 3 in all clinical scenes except predefined record‑constrained failures; formulation on Page 36.  
**Break condition:** A case has conflicting or missing cornerstone data (e.g., no confirmed histology, unknown platinum sensitivity) that no agent can resolve, leading to a Safety ≤ 3 and a capped overall score—correctly flagging the case for human review.

## Foundational Learning
**Multi‑Agent Deliberation**  
*Why needed:* OMGs does not merely run five agents in parallel; it coordinates a constrained dialogue where agents intervene on predefined triggers (conflict, safety). Understanding this debate flow is essential to predict system behavior.  
*Quick check:* If two agents disagree on platinum sensitivity based on the same imaging report, deliberation triggers; they must articulate evidence and the chair reconciles or flags uncertainty.

**Evidence‑Bank & Citation Fidelity**  
*Why needed:* The Evidence score depends on a pre‑built static corpus and a strict audit; knowing how evidence is chunked, indexed, and cited is necessary to interpret score changes.  
*Quick check:* If a landmark trial (e.g., SOLO1) is missing from the evidence‑bank, OMGs may receive a lower Evidence score or an audit cap if it hallucinates a citation.

**Role‑Scoped Information Access**  
*Why needed:* Agents are deliberately blind to other specialties’ raw data to enforce perspective‑taking, affecting what each agent can plausibly claim.  
*Quick check:* Can the pathology agent directly challenge the radiologist’s interpretation of a CT scan? No—they have different scopes; conflict resolution happens through the chair.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Raw EHR -> EHR Structuring Module -> Agent Orchestrator -> Role Agents (Chair, Medical Oncology, Radiology, Pathology, Nuclear Medicine) -> (Independent Assessments) -> Deliberation Loop (triggered by conflicts/safety) -> Chair Synthesis -> Schema‑Constrained Output Generator -> Citation Audit Module. Evidence Retrieval Pipeline and Evidence Bank serve all agents during assessment.

**Critical path:** The minimal sequence from input to output: (1) Parse raw EHR into structured JSON summary, (2) Package role‑scoped data packages, (3) Run each agent’s independent assessment with evidence retrieval, (4) If triggers occur, execute constrained multi‑round deliberation, (5) Chair synthesizes final assessment into predefined JSON fields, (6) Citations are audited for fidelity.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Latency vs. thoroughness:* Full multi‑agent deliberation takes ~155 s versus a single agent; the trade‑off is justified by gains in Evidence and Robustness.  
- *Role granularity:* Five roles mirror the paper’s MDT; adding more roles could improve comprehensiveness but increases coordination complexity and latency.  
- *Evidence‑bank freshness:* A static bank ensures audit stability but may miss recent trials, requiring maintenance cycles.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *High Safety but low Robustness:* Indicates a safe but brittle recommendation, possibly overfitted to a single data slice.  
- *Frequent safety‑gated caps (S < 3):* Signals systemic input data quality issues at the deployment site.  
- *Citation audit failures (>2% caps):* Suggests evidence‑bank gaps or agent hallucination; may require prompt tuning or bank updates.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate Phase I Benchmark – Run OMGs against CHAIR‑D on 50 retrospective cases from a new site; expect largest gain in Evidence and Robustness dimensions; verify multi‑agent deliberation drives improvement (matched inputs).  
2. Cross‑Scene Stress Test – Feed 10 cases per clinical scene, including complex reassessment with incomplete data; monitor Safety scores and deliberation triggers; expect Safety ≥ 3 only when core data (histology, stage) is unambiguous.  
3. Citation Audit Drill – For 20 OMGs outputs, manually verify every citation against the evidence‑bank; calculate uncapped vs. audit‑capped Evidence score delta; a perfect system shows 0 delta; > 5% caps indicate bank or retrieval issues.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalizability to other healthcare systems:** How does OMGs perform in settings with different languages, clinical guidelines, formularies, and MDT workflows? The authors note that external validation is needed.  
2. **Impact on patient outcomes:** Does integration into routine MDT workflows improve survival, toxicity, or quality of life? Current evaluations are offline; prospective interventional studies are required.  
3. **Governance for fragmented EHRs:** What escalation strategies are needed when faced with incomplete or inconsistent EHR data? The authors highlight residual risk from real‑world data fragmentation and call for defined governance, evidence‑bank maintenance, and explicit escalation pathways.

## Limitations
- Evidence‑bank composition, chunking strategy, and retrieval parameters are not disclosed, hindering precise replication of the Evidence mechanism.  
- Deliberation trigger logic and chair arbitration algorithm are underspecified, leaving room for variability in implementation.  
- Scene assignment rules rely on implicit clinical judgment; exact boundaries for stratifying cases into the five clinical scenes are not formalized.

## Confidence
- Multi‑agent deliberation superiority (vs. single‑agent): Medium  
- Traceability‑driven trust and Evidence scores: High  
- Safety gating and constraint effectiveness: High  

## Next Checks
1. **Evidence retrieval replication:** Rebuild the evidence‑bank using the same guideline sources and MEDLINE snapshot, implementing identical chunking and embedding model; measure retrieval overlap on 20 test cases to confirm >85% match with OMGs’ top‑k citations.  
2. **Deliberation trigger stress test:** Run 10 intentionally conflicting cases (e.g., radiology vs. pathology on residual disease); verify at least two rounds of constrained debate and that the chair either cites conflicting evidence or flags uncertainty—no silent consensus.  
3. **Cross‑system ablation:** Using the same backbone LLM (e.g., GPT‑5.1) and input data, compare OMGs against a re‑implemented CHAIR‑D baseline; the multi‑agent advantage should persist primarily in Evidence and Robustness; a null gap implies flawed deliberation implementation.
