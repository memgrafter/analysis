# Riemannian MeanFlow

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07744
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07744
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Riemannian MeanFlow (RMF) learns direct flow maps between arbitrary times, enabling one-step generation; on promoter DNA design it matches Fisher FM using 10 NFE (vs 100), and on protein backbone generation it achieves 82% designability at 5 steps (vs <10% for baselines), with reward-guided design improving target alignment at minimal extra cost.

## Executive Summary
RMF addresses the high inference cost of manifold-based generative models by learning direct flow maps between any two time points, eliminating the need for many small steps. It establishes three mathematically equivalent characterizations of average velocity—Eulerian, Lagrangian, and semigroup—and identifies the semigroup objective combined with x1-prediction as the most stable for high-dimensional manifolds. Experiments on promoter DNA design and unconditional protein backbone generation show that RMF achieves comparable or superior sample quality while requiring up to 10× fewer function evaluations. The approach also facilitates efficient reward-guided design via a simple x1 look-ahead heuristic.

## Method Summary
RMF defines a family of flow maps Φ_{t,s} on a Riemannian manifold that transform samples at time s to time t. Three training objectives are considered:
- **Eulerian**: match the log-derivative of the density (instantaneous change).
- **Lagrangian**: match the integrated trajectory (requires differentiating through the flow).
- **Semigroup**: enforce the consistency condition Φ_{t,0} = Φ_{t,s} ∘ Φ_{s,0} by regressing directly to the endpoint x1.

The authors prove these objectives are equivalent under ideal conditions. For high-dimensional problems, they find the semigroup objective with x1-prediction (the network outputs a manifold point) to be most stable. Stabilization techniques include adaptive loss weighting (exponent p=0.5), low-frequency time embeddings (ω=0.02), and tailored time-sampling distributions (log-normal for DNA, beta-uniform for proteins). Architectures: DNA uses a 1D CNN (20 residual blocks, kernel 9) with Fourier time features; proteins use IPA blocks (FrameDiff base) with AdaLN. Training uses AdamW, global gradient clipping, and EMA. Inference applies the learned flow map sequentially; for proteins a low-noise trick (scaling rotations/translations) improves stability. Reward-guided generation uses x1 look-ahead: ζ_t = ∇_{x_t} r(Φθ_{t,1}(x_t)).

## Key Results
- **DNA design**: At 10 NFE, RMF (semigroup + x1) matches Fisher FM on MSE between predicted transcription profiles and k‑mer (k=6) correlation, whereas Fisher FM requires 100 NFE.
- **Protein backbone generation**: RMF achieves 82% designability (fraction of samples with scRMSD < 2Å after ProteinMPNN design and ESMFold refolding) at 5 steps; baselines (FrameDiff, FrameFlow) score <10%.
- **Reward-guided design**: x1 look‑ahead improves alignment with target rewards (e.g., desired transcription profile or secondary structure) with negligible additional computational cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The semigroup objective avoids the high variance associated with differential targets in Eulerian and Lagrangian objectives. These differential targets involve estimating velocities that depend on the manifold’s curvature; the required Jacobian-vector products amplify noise, especially in high dimensions. The semigroup objective, by regressing directly to the fixed endpoint x1, sidesteps curvature terms entirely. Moreover, x1‑prediction simplifies the network’s task: it outputs a point on the manifold rather than a tangent vector, which is less sensitive to the parameterization of the ambient space and reduces gradient instability. The combination yields a smoother optimization landscape and more reliable convergence, enabling one-step generation without sacrificing quality.

## Foundational Learning
**1. Riemannian Manifolds** – Smooth spaces equipped with a metric, defining tangent spaces, exponential maps, and geodesics. Needed because data (DNA probability simplex, protein rotations/translations) lies on curved manifolds.  
*Quick check*: Can you define the exponential map and compute a geodesic on the n‑simplex or SO(3)?

**2. Continuous Normalizing Flows (CNF)** – Generative models that define a time‑dependent velocity field v_t(x) and transform a simple prior into data via the ODE dx/dt = v_t(x). The change of variables involves the trace of the Jacobian.  
*Quick check*: Derive the instantaneous density evolution equation ∂_t log p_t(x) = –∇·v_t(x).

**3. Eulerian vs Lagrangian Velocity** – Eulerian: velocity at fixed spatial coordinates; Lagrangian: velocity along particle trajectories. In CNF, Eulerian is v_t(x) and Lagrangian is the solution x(t) of the ODE. They are linked by the flow map Φ_{t,s}.  
*Quick check*: Show that ∂_t Φ_{t,s}(x_s) = v_t(Φ_{t,s}(x_s)) with Φ_{s,s}=Id.

**4. Semigroup Property** – For a deterministic ODE, the flow maps satisfy Φ_{t,s} ∘ Φ_{s,r} = Φ_{t,r}. This consistency property can be used as a training objective that does not involve derivatives of the network.  
*Quick check*: Prove the semigroup property given the ODE definition.

**5. Parameterization Choices (v vs x1)** – In flow matching, the network can predict the velocity (v‑prediction) or the endpoint x1 (x1‑prediction). The latter leads to a simpler squared‑error loss on a static target.  
*Quick check*: Write the x1‑prediction loss for a uniform time t ∈ [0,1].

**6. Stabilization Techniques** – Time clipping, adaptive loss weighting, and low‑frequency time embeddings mitigate optimization instability in high‑dimensional Riemannian settings.  
*Quick check*: Explain why limiting the maximum frequency of the time embedding (ω) can reduce gradient noise.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Input (x_t, t, conditioning) → Time embedding (Fourier features) → Conditioning (AdaLN) → Neural network body (CNN for DNA; IPA for protein) → Output head (v or x1).  
Training loss (semigroup/Eulerian/Lagrangian) uses the network output; inference iteratively applies Φ_{t,s}.

**Critical Path**
1. Sample (x0 from prior, x1 from data).
2. Sample time(s) (t for semigroup; t and s for Eulerian/Lagrangian).
3. Compute x_t via ground‑truth flow map (e.g., geodesic interpolation on the manifold).
4. Embed t and any conditioning (DNA transcription profile).
5. Forward pass through network → prediction.
6. Compute loss (e.g., ||prediction – target||²).
7. Backpropagate, apply gradient clipping and optimizer step (with EMA).
8. Repeat for many epochs.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- *v‑prediction vs x1‑prediction*: v requires differentiating the flow and can be noisy on curved manifolds; x1 is simpler but may need careful time clipping to avoid degenerate targets near t=0.
- *Time‑sampling distribution*: Boundary‑heavy sampling (e.g., 75% at t=0 or 1) helps the network learn to map from noise to data, which is crucial for one‑step generation.
- *Adaptive weighting*: Weighting loss terms with p=0.5 reduces the influence of large targets, stabilizing training.
- *Low‑noise inference (proteins)*: Scaling rotation and translation noise (η<1) prevents overshooting when applying the flow in few steps.

**Failure Signatures**
- Training loss diverges or produces NaNs → likely Eulerian/Lagrangian without proper stabilization; try x1‑prediction and lower ω.
- Generated samples have very high entropy (e.g., DNA k‑mer distribution near uniform) → check x1‑prediction with time clipping (ε=0.1) and adaptive weighting.
- Protein designability remains low (<10%) → ensure low‑noise trick (η_rot=0.45) and sufficient model capacity (RMF/M or RMF/L).
- Cycle‑consistency breaks in Lagrangian (Φ_{t,s}(Φ_{s,t}(x)) not recovering x) → add L_cycle term and monitor.

**First Three Experiments**
1. **Baseline training**: Train semigroup RMF with x1‑prediction on DNA using the recommended hyperparameters (batch 128, AdamW lr=1e‑3, log‑normal time sampling, w_semigroup=5.0, p=0.5, ω=0.02, time clipping ε=0.1). Verify loss curves are smooth and decreasing.
2. **Evaluation**: Generate DNA sequences conditioned on test profiles at NFE=1,5,10,100. Compute MSE (Sei) and k‑mer correlation. Confirm that at NFE=10 RMF reaches the performance of Fisher FM at 100 NFE.
3. **Reward guidance**: Implement x1 look‑ahead for a simple reward (e.g., maximize GC content). Sample with guidance scale λ ∈ {10,100} and measure improvement in reward vs unguided samples; check that sample quality (k‑mer correlation) does not degrade dramatically.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What theoretical property makes the semigroup objective more stable than Eulerian/Lagrangian on high-dimensional manifolds?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors state: "We find that differential objectives (Eulerian, Lagrangian) can exhibit high variance due to differential terms related to manifold curvature in the regression targets, while the algebraic semigroup objective avoids this and provides more stable optimization in high dimensions" (Sec. 3.3). No formal analysis accompanies this.
- **Why unresolved**: Without a mathematical link between variance and curvature, we cannot predict stability on manifolds with different geometries or guarantee performance at even higher dimensions.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Derive closed-form expressions for the variance of the regression targets in terms of sectional curvature and dimension; validate these predictions experimentally on manifolds with tunable curvature (e.g., spheres of different radii).

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Why does x1‑prediction maintain stability under under‑parameterization (network width ≪ D) while v‑prediction degrades as D increases?
- **Basis in paper**: In Sec. 3.2 and Sec. 4.1 (Fig. 2): "x1‑prediction performs comparably or better than v‑prediction and scales well to high dimensions (up to D= 2048), despite under‑parameterization … v‑prediction degrades as D increases."
- **Why unresolved**: The work does not explain the mechanism; understanding could reveal implicit regularization effects and guide future parameterization design.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Compare optimization landscapes (gradient norms, loss curvature, Jacobian conditioning) for both parameterizations across dimensions; theoretical analysis showing x1‑prediction yields smoother objective surfaces in high‑D.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Are the proposed stabilization techniques (time‑sampling distributions, adaptive loss weighting, low‑frequency embeddings) universally applicable to all Riemannian manifolds, such as hyperbolic spaces, Grassmannians, or manifolds with boundaries?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper evaluates RMF only on DNA (simplex) and protein backbones (SE(3)ⁿ) (Sec. 4). The stabilization techniques are described in generic terms (Sec. 3.3) but lack validation on other manifolds. The introduction notes prior manifold methods were restricted to low‑dimensional benchmarks; RMF extends to high dimensions but still on only two families.
- **Why unresolved**: Different manifolds have distinct geometric properties (curvature, topology, boundary structure) that may invalidate the empirically chosen settings. Without broader experiments, the claimed generality remains an untested assumption.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematically apply the same RMF training pipeline to a diverse suite of manifolds (e.g., Sᵈ, ℍᵈ, Stiefel, torus) and report convergence stability and sample quality. Failures would pinpoint which stabilizations need adaptation.

## Limitations
- Conditioning details for DNA (how the target transcription profile is injected) are not described in the paper and must be inferred from code.
- Protein training lacks quantitative specifications for auxiliary loss coefficients and their activation schedule (only "t > 0.75" mentioned).
- Evaluation relies on the external Sei model for DNA, which is not provided and may be difficult to obtain.
- Ambiguity around time‑sampling definitions (e.g., "75% boundary samples") complicates exact replication.
- The Lagrangian objective may require a cycle‑consistency loss; the paper does not state whether this was used for DNA.
