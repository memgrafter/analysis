# Spatio-Temporal Forecasting of Retaining Wall Deformation: Mitigating Error Accumulation via Multi-Resolution ConvLSTM Stacking Ensemble

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10453  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10453  
- **Reference count:** 8  
- **Primary result:** The multi-resolution ConvLSTM ensemble maintains an average Index of Agreement (IoA) above 0.95 through 10 prediction steps on numerical data, and 0.90 on field data, while standalone models drop below 0.75 and 0.65 respectively.

## Executive Summary
Accurately forecasting retaining wall deformation during staged excavation is challenged by error accumulation in long-horizon recursive predictions. This study proposes a stacking ensemble of three ConvLSTM models trained on distinct temporal resolutions (3, 6, and 10 steps). A fully connected neural network meta-learner combines their multi-step forecasts. The framework is trained and validated on 2,000 synthetic profiles generated by PLAXIS2D with stochastic geotechnical parameters, then tested on field measurements. Results show the ensemble dramatically reduces error propagation, achieving consistently high agreement with ground truth across ten steps. SHAP analysis reveals the meta-learner adaptively increases the high-resolution model’s contribution over time, enhancing responsiveness to site-specific variations.

## Method Summary
The methodology comprises: (1) generating 2,000 synthetic retaining wall deformation histories using PLAXIS2D with randomized soil and structural parameters; (2) preprocessing to interpolate displacements onto 100 spatial points and create sliding windows for three temporal resolutions; (3) training three independent four‑layer ConvLSTM models (single‑step prediction) with dropout 0.5; (4) recursively rolling out each base model to produce 10‑step forecasts; (5) training a nine‑layer fully connected meta‑learner on the concatenated predictions from all base models; and (6) evaluating the ensemble on a held‑out numerical test set and two field sites, with SHAP used to interpret model contributions over the forecast horizon.

## Key Results
- On numerical data, the ensemble achieves an average IoA > 0.95 across all 10 prediction steps; standalone models fall below 0.75.
- With field measurements, the ensemble’s average IoA at step 10 is 0.90, versus < 0.65 for the best standalone model.
- SHAP analysis shows the high‑resolution model’s (3‑step) contribution grows from ~20% to ~45% by step 10, while the low‑resolution model’s influence declines, demonstrating adaptive weighting that stabilizes long‑term forecasts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Multi‑resolution input windows allow base models to capture complementary temporal patterns in deformation dynamics.
- **Mechanism:** Each ConvLSTM processes sequences of different lengths, forcing the network to learn representations at distinct temporal scales. The high‑resolution model reacts quickly to recent excavation stages, while the low‑resolution model integrates longer‑term trends. The meta‑learner then combines these scale‑specific outputs.
- **Core assumption:** Retaining wall deformation during staged excavation exhibits meaningful patterns at multiple temporal grains, and no single resolution optimally captures both short‑term responsiveness and long‑term stability.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract explicitly cites leveraging "diverse temporal input resolutions to mitigate error accumulation." Section 2.1.3 details the three resolution configurations. Neighbor papers confirm ConvLSTM handles temporal dynamics, though multi‑resolution ensembles for geotechnical forecasting are novel.
- **Break condition:** If the chosen resolutions are too proximal, the models may learn highly correlated features, reducing ensemble diversity and benefit.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The stacking ensemble (meta‑learner) actively compensates for recursive error propagation inherent in long‑horizon forecasting.
- **Mechanism:** Standalone ConvLSTM models use recursive prediction, causing small initial errors to amplify. The meta‑learner is trained on the multi‑step predictions of all base models and the ground truth, learning to weight and correct their individual error trajectories. This transforms the recursive forecasting problem into a direct multi‑output correction task at the ensemble level.
- **Core assumption:** Errors made by different base models across prediction steps are not perfectly correlated and can be statistically disentangled by a sufficiently flexible meta‑learner.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 2.1.2 describes recursive forecasting and identifies "error propagation" as the key challenge. Figure 7 shows standalone models’ MAE rising sharply with prediction step, while the ensemble’s MAE grows much slower. SHAP analysis (Section 3.2.3) reveals dynamic reweighting, indirectly aiding error correction.
- **Break condition:** If all base models share a common systematic bias, the meta‑learner may fail to correct it because it only sees outputs, not the underlying bias source.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The ensemble’s contribution weights adapt over the forecast horizon, increasing reliance on higher‑resolution models for longer‑term predictions in field conditions.
- **Mechanism:** The fully connected meta‑learner’s nonlinear transformation allows the influence of each base model’s prediction to vary with the current input context and prediction step. Training on diverse, stochastic synthetic data teaches the meta‑learner that as uncertainty grows with horizon, sharper, more responsive signals from shorter‑window models become more valuable for stabilizing forecasts.
- **Core assumption:** The relationship between base model outputs and ground truth is state‑dependent; specifically, prediction error profiles change over time, and high‑resolution models provide more useful directional cues in later, more uncertain steps.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the ensemble "adaptively increases the high‑resolution model’s contribution over time." Figure 16 shows Model A’s normalized SHAP contribution growing from ~20% to ~45% by step 10 in field data, while Model C’s declines. Analysis links this to Model A’s ability to capture sudden changes in irregular field measurements.
- **Break condition:** If the training data (synthetic PLAXIS simulations) lacks the non‑stationarity and abrupt changes present in real field data, the meta‑learner may not learn to appropriately upweight the high‑resolution model for long‑horizon field predictions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Generator → Preprocessor → Base Learners (3× ConvLSTM) → Recursive Predictor → Meta‑learner → SHAP Analyzer

**Critical path:**  
The pipeline starts with generating synthetic deformation data via PLAXIS2D, preprocessing it into sliding windows of varying resolutions, training three independent ConvLSTM models, using them to recursively generate 10‑step predictions, training a fully‑connected meta‑learner on these predictions, and finally interpreting the ensemble with SHAP.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Resolution selection: low t (3) offers high sensitivity but noisy; high t (10) is stable but sluggish. Choice affects base model diversity.  
- Meta‑learner capacity: deeper FCNN captures complex interactions but risks overfitting; dropout (0.5) is critical.  
- Training data source: using only synthetic PLAXIS data improves generalization but may not capture all real‑world irregularities.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Symptom: ensemble MAE increases with step, similar to standalone models.  
  Diagnosis: meta‑learner failed to learn corrective weights. Check: SHAP values static across steps; base model outputs highly correlated.  
- Symptom: excellent performance on synthetic test, poor on field data (IoA collapse after step 5).  
  Diagnosis: domain shift too severe. Check: visual inspection of field vs. synthetic displacement trends; consider more diverse synthetic data or domain adaptation.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Train each standalone ConvLSTM (resolutions 3, 6, 10) on synthetic data. Plot their 10‑step MAE/IoA to establish baseline error accumulation curves.  
2. Train the full ensemble. Evaluate on a held‑out synthetic test set. Compare ensemble’s 10‑step IoA (expected >0.95) against best standalone (expected <0.75 per paper). This validates the core claim.  
3. Run SHAP analysis on field data predictions. Plot normalized contribution per model per step. Expect Model A (res=3) contribution to increase over time. If not, investigate meta‑learner’s training data distribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization and real‑time integration:** How does the framework generalize to a wider variety of geotechnical conditions and excavation scenarios, and how can its robustness be enhanced through integration with real‑time in situ sensor data?  
   *Basis:* The authors explicitly state in the conclusions that "further validation on additional excavation sites and integration of direct in situ sensor data will be essential to fully establish the models’ robustness under diverse field conditions."  
   *Why unresolved:* Validation is limited to two field sites in South Korea and uses preprocessed displacement profiles; performance under highly variable soil profiles, support systems, and real‑time sensor noise remains unverified.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Comprehensive testing across multiple excavation projects with diverse geology, excavation depths, and monitoring setups; implementation with live sensor streams to evaluate adaptability to data irregularities, missing values, and drift.

2. **Optimal resolution combination:** What is the optimal combination and number of temporal resolutions for the base ConvLSTM models to maximize long‑term forecast accuracy while minimizing computational cost?  
   *Basis:* The framework employs three fixed resolutions (3, 6, 10 time steps) without justification or exploration of alternatives; the choice appears arbitrary and may not be universally optimal.  
   *Why unresolved:* No sensitivity analysis investigates how different resolution sets or a continuous spectrum affect ensemble performance; the current configuration could be suboptimal in terms of error accumulation mitigation or efficiency.  
   *Resolving evidence:* A systematic study varying resolution values (e.g., 2, 4, 6, 8, 10) and the number of base models, measuring impacts on multi‑step error metrics (e.g., IoA decay) and computational requirements; possibly deriving a data‑driven method for resolution selection.

3. **Meta‑learner complexity:** Is the complex nine‑layer deep neural network meta‑learner necessary, or can a simpler model (e.g., linear regression, gradient boosting) achieve comparable accuracy with better interpretability and efficiency?  
   *Basis:* The stacking ensemble uses a fully connected network with over 467,000 parameters, but the paper provides no comparison with simpler meta‑learners, leaving its necessity unclear.  
   *Why unresolved:* The high complexity may risk overfitting, increase computational burden, and hinder interpretability; given only three base model inputs, a parsimonious meta‑learner might suffice, making the approach more practical for engineering applications.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Benchmark the deep meta‑learner against a range of simpler models (linear regression, random forest, XGBoost) on the same prediction tasks, evaluating trade‑offs in accuracy, training time, inference speed, and model explainability.

4. **Dynamic weighting mechanism:** What causes the time‑varying shifts in base model contributions to the ensemble prediction (as revealed by SHAP analysis), and can a dynamic ensemble mechanism that explicitly reweights models over the forecast horizon further reduce error propagation?  
   *Basis:* SHAP plots show that the influence of Model A (resolution=3) grows in later steps for field data, while Model C (resolution=10) dominates early steps; however, the meta‑learner is static and trained once, so the reason for this shift and its potential exploitation are not investigated.  
   *Why unresolved:* The static meta‑learner cannot intentionally adapt weights based on prediction step or input characteristics; understanding the drivers (e.g., increasing nonlinearity, noise sensitivity) could enable a more proactive ensemble that mitigates error accumulation.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Correlate SHAP contribution trends with input dynamics (e.g., displacement rate, variance, step index); design and test a recurrent or attention‑based meta‑learner that conditions on the current step or input pattern, then compare its long‑horizon performance against the static version.

## Limitations
- Reliance on synthetic PLAXIS data may not capture full field complexity, introducing domain shift.  
- Ambiguities in dataset construction (mixed 29/41‑step sequences) and meta‑learner input flattening hinder exact replication.  
- Undisclosed random seeds and parameter sampling details limit reproducibility of the simulation database.

## Confidence
- *Ensemble outperforms standalone models*: **High** (strong numerical and field evidence).  
- *Multi‑resolution captures complementary temporal patterns*: **Medium** (plausible but resolution choice arbitrary and not ablated).  
- *Adaptive weighting increases high‑resolution model’s contribution over time*: **Medium** (SHAP trends observed but could be confounded by prediction correlations).
