# Not All Queries Need Rewriting: When Prompt-Only LLM Refinement Helps and Hurts Dense Retrieval

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13301
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13301
- **Reference count:** 5
- **Primary result:** Prompt-only LLM query rewriting significantly degrades retrieval on FiQA (−9.0% nDCG@10) but improves it on TREC-COVID (+5.1%), with no effect on SciFact; outcomes are determined by whether substitutions align query vocabulary with corpus-preferred terms.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies the impact of using large language models (LLMs) to rewrite search queries in a single step, without retrieval feedback, on dense retrieval systems. Experiments on three BEIR datasets—FiQA, TREC-COVID, and SciFact—reveal contrasting effects: rewriting harms FiQA (−9.0% nDCG@10, p<0.001), helps TREC-COVID (+5.1%, p=0.024), and leaves SciFact unchanged. The analysis shows that effectiveness hinges on lexical substitution direction: whether replaced tokens become more frequent in the corpus (higher CTF) and appear in relevant documents (higher VOR). On FiQA, which already has stable query-document alignment, rewriting causes vocabulary drift, reducing lexical overlap. On TREC-COVID, with inconsistent nomenclature, rewrites standardize queries toward dominant corpus terms, improving matching. Simple gating mechanisms based on lexical features fail to reliably decide when to rewrite, while domain-specific post-training of the retriever yields safer gains. The study thus cautions against blanket application of generic LLM query rewriting and highlights the need for corpus-aware strategies.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates prompt-only LLM query rewriting across three BEIR datasets: FiQA-2018, SciFact, and TREC-COVID. Dense retrievers based on all-mpnet-base-v2 and bge-base-en-v1.5 are used, both in their base form and after post-training on FiQA data. Rewriting is performed via a single call to Ministral 8B with the prompt “Rewrite the following search query to improve information retrieval, preserving the original intent while improving clarity and adding relevant context.” (decoding: temperature=0.7, max_tokens=100). Retrieval uses FAISS with inner product (cosine similarity) to fetch the top 100 documents. Evaluation primarily uses nDCG@10, supplemented by lexical metrics: Vocabulary Overlap Ratio (VOR) – the fraction of unique query unigrams present in relevant documents – and Corpus Term Frequency (CTF) – the ratio of geometric mean corpus frequencies of substituted tokens. Statistical significance is assessed via 10,000 bootstrap resamples and paired t-tests. The paper also tests a selective rewriting policy (gating) that predicts harm using features like ΔVOR, new-token fraction, and length ratio, trained via logistic regression. Additionally, it explores contrastive post-training of the retriever on FiQA training queries.

## Key Results
- **FiQA**: Rewriting reduces nDCG@10 by 9.0% (p<0.001), accompanied by a significant decrease in VOR (−0.014, p=0.013), indicating lexical drift away from relevant documents.
- **TREC-COVID**: Rewriting improves nDCG@10 by 5.1% (p=0.024); improved queries exhibit very high CTF (mean 1153×) on substituted terms, showing standardization toward corpus-preferred terminology.
- **SciFact**: No significant effect on retrieval effectiveness; substitution effects are balanced.
- Lexical substitution occurs in 95% of rewrites across all outcome groups, confirming that the *direction* of substitution – toward or away from corpus vocabulary – drives results.
- A feature-based gating model (logistic regression) achieves only AUC=0.593 and fails to yield statistically significant gains over never-rewriting in simulated policies (p>0.12).
- Post-training the retriever on FiQA data yields performance improvements without the risks associated with rewriting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism lies in lexical substitution: the LLM replaces original query tokens with alternatives in nearly all cases. Whether this helps or harms dense retrieval depends on whether the new tokens are more frequent in the target corpus (higher CTF) and/or appear in relevant documents (higher VOR). In FiQA, the query-document vocabulary is already stable and well-aligned; rewriting introduces terms that are less typical in relevant documents, reducing VOR and causing a notable performance drop. In TREC-COVID, the corpus suffers from inconsistent nomenclature (e.g., multiple names for the disease); rewrites often replace uncommon terms with the dominant corpus term (e.g., “2019-nCOV” → “COVID-19”), raising CTF sharply and improving the chances of matching documents that use the standard term. SciFact’s terminology stability results in no net effect, as substitution benefits and harms balance out. Thus, generic rewriting without corpus awareness is a double-edged sword: its outcome is predetermined by the alignment between the LLM’s lexical choices and the corpus’s preferred vocabulary.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Test Queries -> Gating Module (optional) -> Rewriter Service -> Dense Retriever -> Evaluation Pipeline

### Critical Path
A test query enters the system. If a gating module is enabled, a classifier decides whether to skip rewriting. Queries that proceed are sent to the Rewriter Service, which invokes the LLM with the fixed prompt to produce a rewritten query. The rewritten query is then encoded by the Dense Retriever (embedding model) and searched in the FAISS vector index to retrieve the top-k documents. Finally, the Evaluation Pipeline (offline) computes metrics such as nDCG@10, VOR, and CTF against the qrels.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Rewriting adds latency and risk:** Rewriting introduces non-trivial latency and can degrade performance on domains with stable query-document alignment (FiQA: −9% nDCG@10). Domain-specific post-training of the retriever offers safer gains but requires labeled data.
- **Gating complexity vs. benefit:** A simple logistic regression classifier using lexical features (ΔVOR, new-token fraction, length ratio) yields weak predictive power (AUC=0.593) and does not significantly outperform a never-rewrite baseline, indicating that current features are insufficient for reliable selective rewriting.
- **Rewriter choice independence:** Similar patterns of degradation/improvement are observed across different LLMs (Ministral 8B, GPT-4o-mini), suggesting that dataset properties rather than specific model artifacts drive the outcomes.

### Failure Signatures
- nDCG@10 drop exceeding 5% after enabling rewriting (especially on FiQA).
- Negative ΔVOR (lexical drift away from relevant documents).
- On TREC-COVID, substitutions with CTF < 1.5 correlate with degraded queries.
- Gating model AUC < 0.6 and no statistically significant improvement over never-rewrite (p > 0.12).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline:** Evaluate the dense retriever on the original test queries of FiQA, SciFact, and TREC-COVID to establish reference nDCG@10 scores.
2. **Always rewrite:** Apply the prompt-only rewriter to all test queries; verify FiQA degradation (~−9%) and TREC-COVID improvement (~+5%).
3. **Mechanism check:** Compute VOR and CTF on rewritten queries; confirm FiQA’s VOR decrease and TREC-COVID’s high CTF for improved queries. Additionally, train a logistic regression harm predictor on FiQA features, simulate its gating policy, and compare against never-rewrite (expect no significant gain).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Can richer predictive signals enable reliable selective rewriting?
The paper’s feature-based gating (ΔVOR, new-token fraction, length ratio) achieved only AUC=0.593 and failed to beat never-rewriting. Future work should explore richer signals (e.g., semantic similarity, retrieval confidence) to predict harm and allow safe, per-query decisions.

### Does iterative rewriting with retrieval feedback mitigate domain-dependent harms?
The study examined only single-step rewriting without retrieval feedback. Multi-step methods (e.g., HyDE, Self-RAG) that incorporate retrieved context might avoid vocabulary drift, but their impact on dense retrieval remains unknown.

### How does rewriting perform on conversational or under-specified queries?
BEIR queries are curated and well-formed; real-world queries often include conversational or ambiguous language. The paper hypothesizes that rewriting could be more beneficial on such “messy” queries, but this is untested.

### Can domain-specialized rewriters improve safety and efficacy?
The experiments used a generic off-the-shelf LLM. Fine-tuning the rewriter on domain-specific query-rewrite pairs might prevent degradation on stable domains while retaining gains on inconsistent ones.

## Limitations
- Generalizability is restricted to three BEIR datasets; corpus-specific thresholds for CTF/VOR may not hold for other domains (e.g., web search).
- The CTF computation method (token-pairing strategy) is underspecified, risking replication variance.
- Post-training hyperparameters for the retriever are only partially described, hindering full replication of all reported configurations.
- The study tested only two LLM rewriters; broader model coverage is needed to confirm pattern robustness.

## Confidence
- **Effect determined by substitution direction (CTF/VOR):** Medium (strong within-study correlations but no causal manipulation of substitution direction)
- **Dataset-specific outcomes (FiQA degradation, TREC-COVID improvement):** Medium (statistically significant but TREC-COVID’s small n limits power)
- **Gating model inefficacy (AUC=0.593, no gain over never-rewrite):** High (direct evaluation with consistent failure across tests)

## Next Checks
1. **Benchmark expansion:** Replicate the study on at least five additional IR datasets (e.g., MS MARCO, NQ, HotpotQA) to test the portability of CTF/VOR thresholds and overall effects.
2. **CTF methodology disclosure:** Release the exact implementation or algorithmic details of CTF calculation to ensure reproducible measurements of term frequency shifts.
3. **Advanced gating:** Replace logistic regression with a transformer-based classifier (e.g., BERT) using identical lexical features; compare AUC and nDCG@10 gains of the resulting selective rewriting policy against never-rewrite.
