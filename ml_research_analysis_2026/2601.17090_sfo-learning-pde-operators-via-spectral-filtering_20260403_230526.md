# SFO: Learning PDE Operators via Spectral Filtering
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17090
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17090
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** SFO introduces spectral filtering with a fixed Universal Spectral Basis (Hilbert eigenvectors) to learn PDE operators, achieving up to 40% lower relative error on six benchmarks while using far fewer parameters than baselines.

## Executive Summary
Neural operators for PDEs often struggle to capture long-range, nonlocal interactions. SFO addresses this by parameterizing integral kernels using a fixed global orthonormal basis—the Universal Spectral Basis (USB)—derived from eigenvectors of the Hilbert matrix. The method learns only a small set of spectral coefficients, leveraging the theoretical insight that discrete Green’s functions of shift-invariant, stable PDE discretizations exhibit spatial linear dynamical system structure, which ensures exponential decay and high compressibility in the USB. Across six diverse benchmarks—including reaction-diffusion, fluid dynamics, and 3D electromagnetics—SFO attains state-of-the-art accuracy, reducing relative error by up to 40% compared to strong baselines while requiring substantially fewer parameters.

## Method Summary
SFO is a neural operator that approximates the solution operator of PDEs. Its architecture comprises a lifting layer, multiple Spectral Transfer Unit (STU) layers, and a projection layer. Each STU layer applies a spectral filter expressed as a truncated expansion in the precomputed USB. Only the spectral mixing matrices (Θₗ) are learned, while the basis modes (ϕₗ) are fixed. Convolutions are evaluated efficiently in the Fourier domain by precomputing the FFT of the basis modes. A residual pointwise MLP (with GELU activation) follows the filtering to introduce nonlinearity. The fixed USB provides a universal inductive bias for long-range interactions and drastically reduces the number of parameters. Training is end‑to‑end on spatiotemporal data from PDE benchmarks, minimizing the mean relative L₂ error.

## Key Results
- Reduces relative error by up to 40% on six PDE benchmarks compared to prior neural operators (FNO, UNO, SVD‑NO).
- Achieves state‑of‑the‑art accuracy while using far fewer parameters (e.g., an order of magnitude less than SVD‑NO on several tasks).
- Demonstrates consistent effectiveness across a wide range of PDE types, including reaction‑diffusion, shallow water, and 3D Maxwell equations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** SFO efficiently captures long-range interactions in PDE solution operators by representing the integral kernel as a truncated expansion in the Universal Spectral Basis (Hilbert matrix eigenvectors).
- **Mechanism:** The kernel is constrained to a low‑rank form κ(x,x') = Σ_{l=1}^L Θₗ ϕₗ(x‑x'), where ϕₗ are fixed global orthonormal modes. Only the spectral mixing matrices Θₗ are learned. The convolution is evaluated efficiently via FFT by precomputing the transforms of the basis filters.
- **Core assumption:** The true kernel’s energy concentrates in a small number of leading USB modes; i.e., the coefficients decay rapidly with index l. This holds for kernels arising from stable, shift‑invariant discretizations with geometric decay (e.g., three‑point stencils).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “parameterizes integral kernels using the Universal Spectral Basis (USB)—a fixed, global orthonormal basis derived from the eigenmodes of the Hilbert matrix in spectral filtering theory.”
  - [section 4]: “Fast spectral decay: The eigenvalues decay rapidly; beyond roughly 20 modes they are numerically negligible.”
  - [corpus]: Neighbors (e.g., SpectraKAN, MCNO) also pursue spectral bases for PDE operators, suggesting this is a promising direction for long‑range modeling.
- **Break condition:** The kernel exhibits slow coefficient decay (e.g., highly oscillatory or non‑smooth kernels), requiring many L modes to capture its structure, which erodes the parameter efficiency.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The discrete Green’s function of a broad class of PDE discretizations (linear, stable, three‑point stencil) has a spatial Linear Dynamical System (LDS) structure that ensures exponential decay and thus high compressibility in the USB.
- **Mechanism:** For operators A of the form (Au)[i] = A₀ u[i] + A₁(u[i‑1]+u[i+1]) with stability A₀ > 2|A₁|, the inverse’s Green’s function G[t] = Σ Θₖ rₖ^{|t|} with |rₖ|<1. Exponential impulse responses correspond to finite‑horizon stable LDS (the “spectral filtering” setting). The Hankel/USB modes then approximate G with logarithmic mode complexity L = O(log(1/ε)).
- **Core assumption:** The PDE discretization is linear, translation‑invariant, locally coupled, and satisfies the stability condition. This yields exponential off‑diagonal decay in the inverse operator.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “Theoretically, discrete Green’s functions of shift‑invariant PDE discretizations exhibit spatial linear dynamical system structure, allowing highly compact approximations in the USB.”
  - [section G]: Formal proof for three‑point stencil and statement that such decay holds for any stable local stencil.
  - [section 4.1]: “Since geometric Toeplitz kernels correspond to stable 1D LDS along the spatial index, prior USB results imply their energy concentrates on low‑index USB modes.”
  - [corpus]: The theoretical result draws directly from Hazan et al. (2017) on spectral filtering for LDS, which is cited and applied to PDEs by the authors.
- **Break condition:** Discretization is unstable (|ρ|≥1) leading to polynomial or no decay; or the operator is nonlocal, nonlinear, or non‑shift‑invariant, breaking the LDS structure.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The fixed, data‑independent USB basis mitigates overfitting and provides a universal inductive bias suitable across diverse PDEs, achieving state‑of‑the‑art accuracy with far fewer parameters than learned or Fourier bases.
- **Mechanism:** Unlike learned bases (e.g., SVD‑NO) that may overfit to training data, or Fourier bases that bias toward smoothness, USB is a fixed, globally supported orthonormal basis with rapid spectral decay for a wide class of signals (including those with polynomial decay). SFO learns only the spectral coefficients Θₗ, regularizing the kernel class and reducing parameters. Empirically, the learned Θₗ concentrate on a few low‑index modes across all six benchmarks.
- **Core assumption:** The fixed USB basis is sufficiently expressive for the operator kernels encountered in practice, even beyond the theoretical LDS cases, such that truncation to a small L does not hurt expressivity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.2]: “SFO attains high expressivity by expanding the kernel in a fixed orthonormal Hilbert basis and learning only the expansion coefficients. … we find the same structure effective across a broad range of PDE operator‑learning benchmarks (Fig. 3).”
  - [section 7]: Ablation against Fourier, Chebyshev, random bases (Table 2) shows USB consistently optimal or tied on 4/6 benchmarks.
  - [section 7]: Learned coefficient plots (Fig. 5) display rapid decay across all PDEs, supporting the inductive bias.
  - [corpus]: The concurrent work on SpectraKAN (255689) also emphasizes conditioning of spectral operators, highlighting the importance of basis choice.
- **Break condition:** If the target operator’s kernel truly requires a basis adaptively learned from data (e.g., highly irregular, sparse, or data‑dependent structure), the fixed USB may underfit relative to a flexible learned basis.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Neural Operator Learning**
  - **Why needed here:** Understanding that SFO approximates an operator (mapping functions to functions) rather than a pointwise mapper; it uses lifting, integral layers, and projection.
  - **Quick check:** What is the role of the lifting map P and projection Q in a neural operator architecture?
- **Concept: Linear Dynamical Systems (LDS) and Exponential Stability**
  - **Why needed here:** The theory relies on showing that the inverse discretized operator’s impulse response (Green’s function) decays exponentially, which is equivalent to a stable LDS in space.
  - **Quick check:** For a scalar linear recurrence xₜ + a xₜ₋₁ + b xₜ₋₂ = 0, what condition on a and b ensures |xₜ| decays exponentially?
- **Concept: Universal Spectral Basis (Hilbert Eigenvectors)**
  - **Why needed here:** The kernel parameterization is the core innovation; knowing that these modes are fixed, orthogonal, and have rapidly decaying eigenvalues explains why a small L suffices.
  - **Quick check:** What is the defining property of the Hilbert matrix that makes its eigenvectors suitable for capturing long‑range dependencies?
- **Concept: FFT‑based Convolution and Circular vs. Linear Boundaries**
  - **Why needed here:** SFO evaluates convolutions via FFT, which assumes periodicity; this affects handling of boundary conditions and may introduce artifacts.
  - **Quick check:** When using FFT to compute convolution on a finite interval, what implicit assumption are we making about the signal’s extension beyond the interval?
- **Concept: Spectral Filtering and Hankel Matrix Approximation**
  - **Why needed here:** The STU layer originates from spectral filtering theory, which guarantees that exponentials (or slowly decaying signals) are well‑approximated by projections onto top Hankel singular vectors (USB modes).
  - **Quick check:** Why does an exponentially decaying signal have a low‑rank Hankel matrix?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Precomputed basis filters (discretized Hilbert eigenvectors ϕₗ and their FFTs bϕₗ for l=1..L) → Lifting layer P (linear map ℝ^{dₐ} → ℝ^{d}) → Repeated T=4 STU layers (Normalization N (LayerNorm) → Spectral operator Kθ (FFT‑based filtering: bw(ω) = Σₗ Θₗ (bϕₗ(ω) · bv(ω))) → Inverse FFT → Residual MLP: v ← v + W₂σ(W₁ w)) → Projection layer Q (linear map ℝ^{d} → ℝ^{dᵤ}) → Output.
- **Critical path:** For each resolution n, precompute Hilbert matrix H, eigen‑decompose to get top L eigenvectors ϕₗ, compute FFT(ϕₗ)=bϕₗ. During training: lift input → for t=0..T−1 compute FFT(vₜ), multiply by bϕₗ and sum over l with Θₗ, inverse FFT to get wₜ, apply residual MLP update, repeat. Project final latent to output. Loss: mean relative L₂ error.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Rank L:** Tradeoff between accuracy and cost. Gains saturate beyond L≈16–20; larger L helps for near‑marginal stability but increases O(L d² n) per layer.
  - **Latent dimension d:** Larger d increases capacity and memory; paper sweeps d∈{32,64,128} per PDE.
  - **FFT circular convolution vs. explicit kernel:** FFT gives O(n log n) but assumes periodicity; for strong boundary effects consider non‑periodic padding or explicit O(n²) evaluation.
  - **Fixed basis vs. learned basis:** Fixed USB reduces overfitting and parameters; may underfit highly irregular kernels compared to data‑dependent bases (e.g., SVD‑NO).
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **High boundary error vs. interior (e.g., Table 10):** Indicates circular convolution artifacts; consider boundary‑aware variants.
  - **No rapid decay in Θₗ Frobenius norms (Fig. 5):** Kernel energy spread over many modes; may need larger L or different inductive bias.
  - **No improvement over simpler baselines (FNO, UNO):** Possibly L or d too small; run ablations.
  - **Training instability/vanishing gradients:** Deep stacks (T large) can compound errors; paper uses T=4.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Basis ablation:** Replace USB with Fourier, Chebyshev, random orthonormal bases; verify USB wins on most benchmarks (Table 2).
  2. **Mode sweep:** Train SFO with L∈{4,8,16,32} while keeping other hyperparameters fixed; plot test error vs. L to observe saturation (Figure 4).
  3. **Resolution robustness:** Train at resolution n, evaluate on subsampled grids (factor s>1); SFO should maintain reasonable accuracy (Appendix E).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to non‑periodic boundaries and non‑uniform grids:** Current FFT‑based evaluation assumes uniform grids and periodicity. Future work could incorporate boundary constraints directly into the spectral parameterization or use alternative transforms, enabling applicability to complex geometries.
2. **Spectral decay guarantees for nonlinear PDEs:** The theoretical LDS and decay results are limited to linear, shift‑invariant discretizations. For nonlinear PDEs, the authors hypothesize that a lifting (e.g., Koopman) linearizes dynamics, but a rigorous justification is lacking. Evidence would come from theoretical results or systematic empirical studies correlating decay with kernel properties across many nonlinear PDEs.
3. **Why multi‑index separable extension degrades in 2D/3D:** Using Lᴹ separable product modes (instead of L tied‑index modes) reduces performance despite more parameters. The authors hypothesize the per‑layer MLP already captures cross‑axis interactions, making the extension redundant. Evidence could isolate the MLP’s role (e.g., remove MLP) or analyze learned kernel spectra for cross‑axis structure.

## Limitations
- FFT‑based convolution enforces periodic boundaries, potentially harming accuracy on PDEs with strong non‑periodic boundary conditions; this is not systematically evaluated.
- Theoretical LDS guarantee applies only to linear, shift‑invariant, stable discretizations; many real‑world PDEs are nonlinear or have variable coefficients, where spectral decay may be slower or absent.
- Multi‑dimensional separable basis may miss anisotropic kernel structures, limiting expressivity in higher dimensions.

## Confidence
- Spectral filtering enables long‑range capture: Medium (empirical support but relies on decay assumptions).
- LDS structure ensures compact USB representation: High (formal proof for stable local stencils).
- Fixed USB provides universal inductive bias: Medium (strong ablation results but limited to six PDEs).
