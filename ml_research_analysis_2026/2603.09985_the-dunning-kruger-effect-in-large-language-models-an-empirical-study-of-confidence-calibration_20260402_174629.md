# The Dunning-Kruger Effect in Large Language Models: An Empirical Study of Confidence Calibration

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.09985
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09985
- **Reference count**: 5
- **Primary result**: Poorly performing LLMs exhibit severe overconfidence (high calibration error), while well-calibrated models achieve high accuracy with low calibration error, revealing a Dunning-Kruger–like inverse relationship.

## Executive Summary
This study empirically examines the Dunning-Kruger effect—the inverse correlation between competence and overconfidence—in large language models. The authors evaluated four LLMs (Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Kimi K2) across 24,000 trials using standard multiple-choice and open-ended benchmarks. Confidence was elicited via explicit numeric scoring (0–100), and calibration was measured using Expected Calibration Error (ECE). Results reveal a stark inverse relationship: the lowest-performing model, Kimi K2 (23.3% accuracy), showed severe overconfidence (ECE=0.726), while the best-calibrated model, Claude Haiku 4.5 (75.4% accuracy), achieved near-optimal calibration (ECE=0.122). All models exhibited a weak correlation between confidence and correctness, with Gemini variants maintaining rigidly high confidence regardless of accuracy. These findings demonstrate that miscalibration patterns in poorly performing LLMs directly parallel the human Dunning-Kruger effect, raising significant safety concerns for high-stakes deployments.

## Method Summary
The study employed a systematic evaluation framework. Four LLMs were tested on four diverse benchmarks (MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, TriviaQA), with 1,500 stratified samples per dataset, totaling 24,000 trials. Models were prompted to answer each question and provide a numeric confidence rating between 0 and 100. Inference used extended thinking mode (token budget 8,192) and a deterministic temperature of 0.0. Calibration was quantified via Expected Calibration Error (ECE), computed by binning confidence scores into 10 equal-width intervals and measuring the weighted absolute difference between bin accuracy and mean confidence. Secondary metrics included Pearson and Spearman correlations between confidence and correctness, as well as overconfidence scores (mean confidence minus accuracy). Statistical significance was assessed using pairwise t-tests.

## Key Results
- **Inverse correlation**: Kimi K2 (23.3% accuracy) had ECE=0.726; Claude Haiku 4.5 (75.4% accuracy) had ECE=0.122.
- **Weak confidence–correctness linkage**: All models showed low correlation; Gemini variants maintained near-perfect mean confidence (~95–99%) irrespective of accuracy.
- **Systemic overconfidence**: Poorly performing LLMs display miscalibration patterns directly analogous to the human Dunning-Kruger effect, posing risks for high-stakes applications.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Evaluation Setup Drives Inverse Correlation
**Claim:** The inverse relationship between competence and overconfidence emerges directly from the evaluation setup.  
**Mechanism:** Models are evaluated with explicit numeric confidence elicitation. Miscalibration is measured by ECE, which captures the gap between confidence and accuracy across bins. Poorly performing models fail to modulate confidence downward on difficult questions, leading to consistently high confidence that diverges from low accuracy.  
**Evidence:** Kimi K2’s mean confidence is 95.7% with only 23.3% accuracy and ECE=0.726; Claude Haiku’s mean confidence varies appropriately, yielding low ECE ([section 4.1], Table 1).  
**Break condition:** Using non-numeric confidence prompts (e.g., high/medium/low) could weaken or alter the inverse correlation.

### Mechanism 2: Training Objectives Decouple Confidence from Correctness
**Claim:** Miscalibration stems from training objectives that do not penalize overconfident incorrect outputs, leading models to associate high confidence with helpfulness.  
**Mechanism:** During supervised fine-tuning or RLHF, models are optimized for traits like assertiveness without explicit calibration losses. Consequently, they learn to output high confidence as a default “helpful” behavior, regardless of actual correctness, especially in models with weaker foundational knowledge.  
**Evidence:** Section 5.2 hypothesizes that Kimi K2’s severe miscalibration arises from training dynamics that reward confidence irrespective of correctness. Citations to Leng et al. (2024) and Zhu et al. (2023) link training methodology to calibration.  
**Break condition:** If models are fine-tuned with calibration-aware losses (e.g., ECE minimization), the inverse correlation should diminish.

### Mechanism 3: Alignment and Confidence Variability Enable Calibration
**Claim:** Superior calibration (e.g., Claude Haiku’s) is enabled by high confidence variability and alignment procedures that encourage honest uncertainty expression.  
**Mechanism:** A well-calibrated model must modulate confidence based on question difficulty. Claude Haiku’s low ECE (0.122) and high confidence standard deviation (41.0) indicate it expresses lower confidence on harder items. This modulation is likely fostered by training that rewards accurate uncertainty reporting—e.g., via process-oriented RLHF or exposure to unanswerable questions.  
**Evidence:** Section 4.2 notes Claude’s high confidence variability; Section 5.2 attributes this to Anthropic’s training approach.  
**Break condition:** Removing extended thinking mode might degrade calibration, indicating an interaction between inference-time reasoning and training.

## Foundational Learning

**Expected Calibration Error (ECE)**  
*Why needed*: Primary metric for quantifying miscalibration; essential to interpret results.  
*Quick check*: If a model outputs 90% confidence on 100 samples and is correct in 72, what is the contribution to ECE from this bin (assuming equal bin width)?

**Confidence Elicitation Protocols**  
*Why needed*: The method of asking for confidence shapes what “confidence” means; this study’s findings depend on numeric 0–100 prompting.  
*Quick check*: How might the confidence–correctness correlation change if the prompt asked for “high/medium/low” instead of a 0–100 score?

**Reliability Diagrams**  
*Why needed*: Visual diagnostic tool; interpreting these diagrams reveals how miscalibration manifests (e.g., high-confidence bins with low accuracy).  
*Quick check*: In a reliability diagram, what does a point far below the diagonal indicate about the model’s confidence in that bin?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Benchmark Loader → Prompt Formatter → Model Inference Client → Metrics Calculator → Visualization Engine

**Critical path**  
Prompt design → API call → parse confidence → bin → compute ECE. Confidence parsing is most fragile.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Numeric vs. verbal confidence*: Numeric enables precise ECE but may force unnatural scaling; verbal is natural but mapping is ambiguous.  
- *Extended thinking vs. standard inference*: Extended mode may improve reasoning but increases cost/latency; results may not generalize.  
- *Single-turn vs. multi-turn*: Single-turn used; multi-turn may induce calibration drift.

**Failure signatures**  
- ECE > 0.5 with accuracy < 50% (severe Dunning-Kruger)  
- Confidence standard deviation < 15% across all questions (rigid overconfidence)  
- Pearson correlation |r| < 0.1 despite sufficient samples (decoupled confidence)

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Baseline replication**: Run exact protocol on Claude Haiku 4.5 with MMLU to reproduce ECE ~0.09; verify parsing and binning.  
2. **Prompt variation test**: On Kimi K2, compare numeric 0–100 prompt vs. verbal “high/medium/low” prompt; hypothesize ECE remains high but correlation drops further.  
3. **Dataset sensitivity check**: For Gemini Flash, compute per-dataset ECE; expect TriviaQA to show highest ECE, confirming domain-specific failures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1: Alternative Confidence Elicitation Methods**  
*Question:* How do alternative confidence elicitation methods (e.g., implicit signals like output entropy, natural language expressions) compare to explicit numeric prompting in assessing LLM calibration?  
*Basis in paper:* The study notes that confidence elicitation relied on explicit prompting, which may not capture implicit model uncertainty (Section 5.4).  
*Why unresolved:* Only one elicitation method was used; results may not generalize.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Parallel experiments using diverse elicitation techniques on same models/benchmarks, comparing calibration metrics.

**Open Question 2: Extended Thinking vs. Standard Inference**  
*Question:* Does extended thinking mode systematically improve calibration compared to standard inference, or do reasoning capabilities introduce new miscalibration patterns?  
*Basis in paper:* The study evaluated extended thinking mode exclusively; calibration patterns may differ under standard inference (Section 5.4).  
*Why unresolved:* No comparison with standard mode.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Replicate with standard inference (no extended token budget) and compare accuracy, confidence distributions, and ECE.

**Open Question 3: Calibration in Creative Tasks**  
*Question:* How does LLM calibration behave in creative or open-ended tasks, where correctness is multi-faceted and confidence assessment is less straightforward?  
*Basis in paper:* The study focused on factual/reasoning tasks; calibration in creative tasks may differ (Section 5.4).  
*Why unresolved:* Benchmarks were factual/reasoning; creative tasks involve subjectivity.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Design evaluations with creative tasks (e.g., story generation), define multi-dimensional correctness and confidence metrics, and check if the inverse competence-overconfidence relationship persists.

**Open Question 4: Calibration-Aware Training**  
*Question:* Can training techniques that explicitly optimize for calibration (e.g., calibration-aware loss functions, RLHF with uncertainty rewards) reduce overconfidence while preserving accuracy?  
*Basis in paper:* Future work should investigate calibration-aware training objectives; without explicit calibration objectives, bias can become severe (Section 5.3, Conclusion).  
*Why unresolved:* No interventions tested.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Fine-tune models with added calibration objectives (e.g., minimize ECE alongside task loss) and evaluate on the same benchmarks to quantify trade-offs.

## Limitations
- The numeric 0–100 confidence prompt may force unnatural scaling and fails to capture implicit uncertainty signals.
- Exact API parameters for extended thinking mode and model identifiers (as of February 2026) are unspecified, hindering reproducibility.
- The confidence parsing logic is unreported; models may output confidence in non-standard formats, risking systematic extraction errors.

## Confidence

**Confidence in claim clusters**  
- Empirical inverse correlation (accuracy vs. overconfidence): **High** (consistent across 24k trials).  
- Mechanism 1 (evaluation-setup driven miscalibration): **Medium** (plausible but prompt-dependent).  
- Mechanism 2 (training objectives decouple confidence): **Low** (inferred, no direct ablation).  
- Mechanism 3 (alignment enables calibration): **Medium** (correlational, not causal).

## Next Checks

1. **Prompt variation test**: On Kimi K2, replace numeric 0–100 prompt with a verbal “high/medium/low” prompt and map to numeric; expect ECE to remain high but Pearson correlation to drop further, indicating scaling artifact sensitivity.
2. **Calibration-aware fine-tuning**: Fine-tune a low-performing model (e.g., Kimi K2) with an ECE-minimization loss on a held-out set; the inverse accuracy-ECE pattern should attenuate or reverse.
3. **Extended-thinking ablation**: Re-evaluate Claude Haiku 4.5 with standard inference (no extended token budget); a significant ECE increase would impute reasoning depth as a contributing factor to calibration.
