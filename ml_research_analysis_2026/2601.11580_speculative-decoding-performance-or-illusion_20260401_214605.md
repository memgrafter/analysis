# Speculative Decoding: Performance or Illusion?

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2601.11580
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11580
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Speculative decoding (SD) improves throughput over baseline in production LLM inference, but speedup decreases with batch size; an oracle simulator shows large gaps between actual and theoretical maximum speedups, suggesting opportunities for adaptive proposer selection.

## Executive Summary
Speculative decoding (SD) promises faster LLM inference by generating draft tokens that are verified in parallel by the target model. However, prior studies used prototypes and unrealistically small batch sizes, raising questions about real-world performance. This paper systematically benchmarks multiple SD variants—n-gram, EAGLE/EAGLE-3, draft models, and multi-token prediction—on the production-grade vLLM engine across diverse models, datasets, and batch sizes. Key findings reveal that verification by the target model dominates execution time (42–95%), acceptance length varies significantly across token positions, requests, and datasets, and all SD variants improve throughput but their speedup declines as batch size grows due to increased verification overhead from rejected tokens. Moreover, an oracle simulator uncovers substantial gaps between observed and theoretical speedups, and combining complementary proposers yields up to 2.2× further speedup over the best single method. The work underscores the need for adaptive SD strategies in production deployments.

## Method Summary
The study conducts end-to-end throughput profiling on vLLM (v0.10.1.1) with continuous batching and CUDA graphs. It evaluates several large language models (Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama3-70B-Instruct, Qwen3-8B, GLM-4.5-Air-106B) on a variety of datasets (CNN/DailyMail, ShareGPT, InstructCoder, GSM8K, AIME22-24, GPQA-Main). Speculative decoding variants include n-gram (proposal lengths 3/5), EAGLE/EAGLE-3, draft-model (paired smaller model), and multi-token prediction (MTP). The authors measure throughput speedup relative to no-SD, instrument the system to break down time spent in drafting, verification, rejection sampling, and overhead, and record acceptance traces (number of accepted tokens per step). These traces feed an oracle simulator that estimates theoretical upper bounds by assuming perfect knowledge of acceptance length and the ability to dynamically select the best proposer per step. The methodology enables analysis of SD behavior under realistic production conditions, including varying batch sizes (1–128) and model scales.

## Key Results
- Verification by the target model dominates execution time, consuming 42% to 95% of total time depending on model and batch size.
- Token acceptance length varies widely across token positions, individual requests, and datasets, leading to unpredictable speedup and high variance in per-request performance.
- All SD variants improve throughput over baseline, but speedup declines as batch size increases, especially for larger models, because verification overhead from rejected tokens becomes more costly.
- An oracle simulator reveals substantial gaps between observed and theoretical maximum speedups; for example, on InstructCoder with n-gram, oracle speedup reaches 2.75× vs. 2.1× for fixed proposals at batch size 1, with the gap widening at larger batches.
- Combining complementary proposers (e.g., n-gram and EAGLE) in an oracle setup yields up to 2.2× additional speedup over the best single method, highlighting potential for adaptive proposer selection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Token acceptance length variability drives speedup
**Claim**: Token acceptance length varies significantly across token positions, individual requests, and datasets, directly determining SD's speedup.  
**Mechanism**: The draft proposer generates a sequence of candidate tokens. The target model verifies them in parallel, accepting a contiguous prefix that matches its distribution. The number of accepted tokens per step (α) fluctuates based on local context similarity and model behavior.  
**Core assumption**: Higher average acceptance length yields greater speedup, but variance causes unpredictable performance.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract] "acceptance length varies significantly across token positions, requests, and datasets."  
- [section 6] Detailed analysis of per-position, per-request, and dataset-level acceptance patterns using heatmaps and box plots (Figs. 5, 6, 7, 14; Table 4).  
- [section 7] Case study linking n-gram performance to prompt-output overlap (BLEU scores) in code editing.  
**Break condition**: When acceptance length drops to near 1 token across most positions/requests, SD offers minimal or negative speedup due to verification overhead dominating without sufficient token gains.

### Mechanism 2: Verification overhead dominates and grows with batch size
**Claim**: Verification by the target model dominates total execution time (42–95%), and its cost increases with batch size, reducing SD's relative speedup.  
**Mechanism**: SD introduces drafting, verification (parallel forward pass for k+1 positions), and rejection sampling. The verification step is the most expensive. As batch size grows, systems become compute-bound; wasted cycles on rejected proposals become more costly.  
**Core assumption**: Speedup is sensitive to the ratio of verification cost to drafting cost (c) and the acceptance rate (α). High verification overhead with low α erodes benefits.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract] "verification by the target model dominates the execution time (42–95%)"  
- [section 5.1] Time breakdown figures showing verification's growing share at larger batches and larger models.  
- [section 3.2] Observation that speedup declines as batch size increases, especially for larger models.  
- [corpus] Neighbors like "Nightjar: Dynamic Adaptive Speculative Decoding" and "Speculative Verification" discuss the same compute-bound degradation.  
**Break condition**: When batch size is large enough that the system is saturated on target model compute, and acceptance rate is modest, the extra verification steps for rejected tokens cause net slowdown versus standard decoding.

### Mechanism 3: Oracle gap and potential of complementary proposers
**Claim**: A substantial gap exists between observed SD speedup and a theoretical upper bound (oracle), primarily due to suboptimal proposer selection and fixed proposal lengths.  
**Mechanism**: An oracle proposer knows the exact acceptance length at each step and can perfectly choose the best proposer per position. Real proposers use fixed k and have non-complementary strengths, leading to wasted verification on mismatched proposals and missed opportunities for longer accepted spans.  
**Core assumption**: Adaptive, position-aware proposer selection and dynamic k could significantly narrow the gap between current and optimal performance.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract] "substantial gaps between observed and theoretical upper bounds" and "Combining complementary proposers... up to 2.2× further speedup."  
- [section 8] Oracle simulator design: fixes k to actual acceptance length vs. using a fixed k. Shows oracle speedup much higher, especially at larger batches.  
- [section 8.2] "Oracle Combine" experiment using perfect knowledge to pick the better of n-gram or EAGLE per position, yielding up to 2.2× extra speedup over best single method.  
- [corpus] Neighbor "A Theoretical Perspective for Speculative Decoding Algorithm" likely formalizes such bounds.  
**Break condition**: When proposers have highly correlated failure modes (no complementary strengths), adaptive selection provides minimal gain. The gap also remains if dynamic k adjustment overhead outweighs benefits.

## Foundational Learning

### Autoregressive Decoding & Parallel Evaluation
**Why needed here**: SD's core premise exploits the ability of transformers to evaluate a candidate token sequence in parallel (computing P(x_{n+1}...x_{n+k}|context)) versus generating tokens sequentially. Understanding this distinction is key to grasping why SD can theoretically preserve the model's distribution while increasing throughput.  
**Quick check question**: Why can a large language model evaluate multiple token probabilities in a single forward pass, but only generate one token at a time in standard decoding?

### KV Cache and PagedAttention
**Why needed here**: The verification stage re-uses the KV cache from the prompt/prefill. The paper's experiments run on vLLM, which uses PagedAttention for efficient memory management. Cache hits during verification are crucial for performance; failures cause recomputation.  
**Quick check question**: In speculative decoding, which parts of the KV cache are reused during the verification stage, and what would happen if a requested cache block was evicted?

### Token Acceptance Rate & Rejection Sampling
**Why needed here**: The acceptance rate (α) is the single most important metric for SD speedup. It's derived from the draft proposer's quality. Rejection sampling ensures the final output distribution matches the target model's. Low α means high verification overhead with little token gain.  
**Quick check question**: If a draft proposer has an acceptance rate of 0.4 and proposes 3 tokens, what is the expected number of *new* tokens produced per verification step (including the "bonus" token)?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
vLLM Scheduler -> Draft Proposer Module -> Verification Engine -> Rejection Sampler

### Critical path
1. Scheduler selects a request ready for decoding.  
2. Proposer generates k draft tokens (autoregressively for model-based methods, or via lookup for n-gram).  
3. Scheduler constructs a new sequence: prompt + previously_generated + draft_tokens.  
4. Verification Engine runs target model forward pass on this extended sequence in parallel, obtaining logits for all k+1 new positions.  
5. Rejection Sampler applies the chosen sampling rule (e.g., for greedy: accept draft x_i if argmax(target logits at i) == draft token). Stops at first rejection.  
6. Accepted tokens are appended to the request's output. If all k drafts were accepted, one additional "bonus" token from the target's distribution at position k+1 is generated.  
7. Return to step 1 for next request or next decoding step for same request.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Proposer Quality (α) vs. Overhead (c)**: A more accurate proposer (e.g., fine-tuned EAGLE vs. n-gram) increases α but also increases c (more compute for drafting). Draft-model-based methods have high α but also high c (running a separate model). n-gram has near-zero c but low/volatile α.
- **Fixed k vs. Dynamic k**: Fixed k is simple but leads to wasted verification if actual acceptance length is shorter. Dynamic k (like tree-based methods) can adapt but increases complexity in tree management and KV cache layout.
- **Memory vs. Speed**: Draft-model and EAGLE add static weight memory and per-token KV cache overhead. This can reduce the number of concurrent requests that fit in GPU memory, potentially offsetting throughput gains.

### Failure signatures
- **Speedup < 1.0**: SD is slower than baseline. Caused by very low acceptance rate (α ≈ 1) combined with high verification overhead dominance (large c), typical at high batch sizes on compute-bound systems.
- **High variance in per-request speedup**: Indicates proposer quality is highly workload/request-dependent (e.g., n-gram only works on repetitive outputs). Some requests get long accepted spans, others get none.
- **Reasoning workload slowdown with MTP**: If only the first MTP head is available (as with GLM-4.5-Air), acceptance rate plummets after the first drafted token because later tokens are predicted by a head not trained for that position.

### First 3 experiments
1. **End-to-End Throughput Profiling**: Run the provided script for Llama3.1-8B on vLLM with target model. Measure tokens/sec for baseline (no SD) vs. each SD variant (n-gram, EAGLE-3) across datasets (GSM8K, InstructCoder) and batch sizes (1, 16, 64, 128). Expect to replicate trends: speedup decreases with batch size, n-gram excels on InstructCoder.
2. **Time Breakdown Analysis**: Instrument vLLM to measure time spent in Drafting, Verification, Rejection Sampling, and Other. Use the same workload as Experiment 1. Expect verification to take 42-95% of time, increasing with model/batch size; drafting overhead for n-gram should be negligible (<2%).
3. **Acceptance Behavior Heatmap**: Instrument SD to log the number of accepted tokens at every generation position for a sample of requests (e.g., 200 requests). Plot a heatmap (rows=requests, columns=position, color=accepted tokens). Expect high variance within a request (some positions generate many tokens, others only one) and heavy tails for n-gram.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Dynamic context overlap for n-gram**: How does n-gram SD performance correlate with dynamic context overlap (including both prompt and generated tokens) across different token positions and workloads? The current analysis only measures BLEU between complete prompt and complete output; understanding dynamic overlap could explain n-gram's bursty acceptance.
2. **Adaptive proposer selection**: Can a lightweight, online predictor be developed to adaptively select the optimal proposer (or combination) at each decoding step based on real-time acceptance patterns, and what is its overhead? The paper shows oracle combination yields up to 2.2× extra speedup, but real-time prediction overhead is unknown.
3. **Systematic guidelines for SD variant selection**: What guidelines can be derived for selecting the most effective SD variant given a specific model architecture, workload, and deployment scenario (e.g., batch size, hardware)? The paper evaluates variants but does not synthesize a decision framework.
4. **Improving multi-token prediction (MTP)**: How can MTP be improved to maintain high acceptance rates over long reasoning sequences, given that current MTP heads suffer from quality degradation when reused autoregressively? MTP is attractive because it requires no extra model, but its effectiveness diminishes with longer drafts.
5. **Tree-style SD scalability**: Can tree-style speculative decoding be made robust to larger batch sizes by optimizing the tree structure to balance acceptance length and verification overhead, and what is the scalability limit? Tree-style offers potential for higher throughput at batch size 1 but becomes less efficient as batch size grows.

## Limitations
- The benchmark uses a limited set of models (up to 106B) and datasets; generalizability to other architectures (e.g., MoE, multimodal) or longer contexts remains uncertain.
- The study relies on vLLM, so results may differ with other inference engines.
- The oracle simulator assumes perfect proposer knowledge and zero overhead for switching, which is unrealistic; actual adaptive schemes may incur significant costs.
- Memory overhead of draft models was not deeply analyzed.
- Experiments use fixed proposal lengths, leaving dynamic k unexplored.

## Next Checks
1. Implement an adaptive proposer that dynamically selects between n-gram and EAGLE (or other) based on request features (e.g., prompt length, token history) to measure actual speedup versus the oracle combine prediction.
2. Design and evaluate a dynamic k selection policy that predicts the acceptance length from recent draft outcomes, reducing wasted verification on rejected tokens.
3. Extend benchmarking to extreme batch sizes (256–1024) and memory-constrained GPU settings to identify the precise break-even point where SD becomes detrimental, testing the verification overhead hypothesis under saturation.
