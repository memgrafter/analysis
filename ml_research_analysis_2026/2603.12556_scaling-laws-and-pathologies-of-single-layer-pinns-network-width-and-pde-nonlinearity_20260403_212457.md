# Scaling Laws and Pathologies of Single-Layer PINNs: Network Width and PDE Nonlinearity

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12556
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12556
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Increasing width in single-layer PINNs fails to reduce solution error as theory predicts; width scaling exponents are near-zero or negative, and nonlinearity induces non-separable scaling interactions, pointing to optimization—not approximation—as the core bottleneck.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the width of a single-hidden-layer Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) affects its ability to solve partial differential equations (PDEs). Contrary to theoretical approximation guarantees, systematic empirical sweeps show that increasing network width does not meaningfully reduce prediction error across a suite of canonical nonlinear PDEs. The failure is compounded by PDE nonlinearity: scaling laws become non-separable, meaning the effect of width depends critically on problem hardness. The authors attribute these pathologies to optimization difficulties, specifically spectral bias, which hinders the network's ability to fit high-frequency components required by the PDE residual loss. Findings suggest that for shallow PINNs, architectural capacity is not the limiting factor; the training dynamics themselves are.

## Method Summary
The study conducts large-scale, controlled experiments with single-layer networks (widths N ∈ {16, 32, ..., 1024}) solving four PDEs: linear Poisson and three nonlinear equations (KdV, Sine-Gordon, Allen-Cahn), each with a tunable hardness parameter κ. Networks use tanh or ReLU activations, are trained with Adam (lr=1e-3, 25k epochs) on a combined loss (PDE residual + boundary/initial conditions, all weights=1). Collocation points are sampled via Sobol (Poisson) or uniform random (nonlinear). For each (PDE, κ, N, activation) combination, 5 seeds are run, and the final mean relative L₂ error on a fixed test grid is recorded. Scaling exponents are extracted by fitting power laws (error ∝ N^−α κ^−γ) and testing for interaction terms in a multivariate linear model on log-log data: log(error) = β₀ + β_N log(N) + β_κ log(κ) + ν log(N)·log(κ).

## Key Results
- Width scaling exponent α is near-zero or negative (e.g., α ≈ −0.32 for Sine-Gordon with ReLU), contradicting theoretical predictions (α ≈ 0.5) and indicating a baseline optimization pathology.
- For ReLU networks on nonlinear PDEs, the interaction term ν is statistically significant (p<0.05), proving a non-separable relationship between width and hardness; increasing width can be harmful at high κ.
- Spectral bias is identified as the primary culprit: networks struggle to learn high-frequency solution components, a problem exacerbated by non-smooth activations (ReLU) and PDE nonlinearity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1:** Optimization failure in a complex, non-convex loss landscape prevents the network from finding the low-error solution that its capacity should theoretically allow. The gradient descent process gets stuck in poor local minima or plateaus.
- *Break condition:* An optimizer or architectural change successfully navigates the loss landscape to realize theoretical width scaling benefits.

**Mechanism 2:** Nonlinearity intensifies high-frequency components in the true solution. This interacts with the network's spectral bias, making the optimization landscape harder in a way that depends on both width *and* nonlinearity simultaneously. Error scaling is not a simple product of independent width and hardness exponents.
- *Break condition:* A method decouples width scaling from problem hardness, restoring a separable power law with α>0.

**Mechanism 3:** Spectral bias—the tendency of gradient-based networks to fit low-frequency functions first—is the fundamental optimizer-level cause. The PINN loss penalizes derivatives, requiring high-frequency learning, but standard optimizers prioritize low-frequency errors, leaving high-frequency residuals large. Non-smooth activations (ReLU) exacerbate this.
- *Break condition:* Training dynamics (e.g., frequency-aware loss weighting, Fourier features) can be altered to overcome the innate spectral bias.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** PDE Suite (κ) -> PINN (SLN, N) -> Adam Optimizer (lr=1e-3, 25k epochs) -> Loss (L_pde+L_bc+L_ic) -> Evaluation (Mean rel. L2 error) -> Analysis (Fit scaling models: univariate, separable, interaction)
- **Critical path:**
  1. Validate framework on linear Poisson; confirm baseline pathology (α≈0.06 tanh, α≈0.01 ReLU).
  2. Perform full systematic sweep: for each nonlinear PDE, loop over all κ, all N {16..1024}, both activations, 5 seeds.
  3. Statistical analysis: compute mean error over seeds; fit scaling models (univariate, separable, interaction); extract coefficients with CIs/p-values.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Adam vs. others:* Adam is representative but may contribute to pathology; L-BFGS could change scaling.
  - *Shallow vs. Deep:* Isolating width; depth might mitigate pathologies but introduces new optimization challenges.
  - *Loss weighting:* Fixed equal weights (w=1) is a choice; adaptive weighting could confound pure width-scaling measurement.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Baseline: α ≤ 0 (univariate fits per κ).
  - Compounding: Significant interaction ν (p<0.05) in interaction model, especially for ReLU; Adj. R² improves.
  - Activation-specific: For tanh, β_N often insignificant, indicating width irrelevance.
  - Allen-Cahn anomaly: For ReLU, β_κ negative; a red flag for PDE-specific dynamics.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate Poisson benchmark: Train SLNs (N=16,128,1024) with tanh/ReLU; verify α values.
  2. Nonlinear hardness sweep: For Sine-Gordon, run full κ×N×activation sweep; plot error vs. N for sample κ.
  3. Interaction model test: From Exp. 2 results, fit separable and interaction models; check ν significance and R² improvement for ReLU.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can architectural modifications (deeper networks, Fourier features, attention) recover a positive width scaling exponent (α > 0.3) and mitigate the baseline pathology?
2. For ReLU, what is the mechanistic origin of the significant non-separable interaction between width and hardness, and can optimizer-level interventions (adaptive loss weighting, second-order methods) eliminate it?
3. Why does Allen-Cahn exhibit a negative hardness exponent (γ < 0) for ReLU and near-zero width exponent for tanh, suggesting a different failure mode in reactive-parabolic settings?
4. Can a universal, PDE-agnostic "hardness" metric be defined that predicts the form of the scaling law (separable, interactive, regime shifts)?
5. Is the baseline pathology (α ≤ 0) an intrinsic limitation of first-order stochastic optimizers like Adam on PINN loss landscapes, or could deterministic/second-order methods recover positive scaling?

## Limitations
- Missing critical hyperparameters (collocation point counts, test grid resolution, initialization) that strongly affect PINN performance and could alter scaling exponents.
- Scope limited to single-layer networks, Adam optimizer, two activations, three nonlinear PDEs; generalizability to deeper architectures unknown.
- Spectral bias claim is inferred, not directly tested; alternative optimization issues (e.g., conditioning) may contribute.

## Confidence
- **Baseline pathology (α≈0/negative):** Medium. Evidence strong but depends on unreported hyperparameters.
- **Non-separable scaling (interaction ν):** Medium. Statistical significance clear, but sensitivity to point density or loss weighting unknown.
- **Spectral bias as root cause:** Low. Plausible but lacks direct manipulation experiment.
