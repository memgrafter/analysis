# Language on Demand, Knowledge at Core: Composing LLMs with Encoder-Decoder Translation Models for Extensible Multilinguality

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.17512
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17512
- Reference count: 36
- Primary result: XBridge dramatically improves multilingual LLM performance, especially for low-resource and unseen languages (e.g., MetaMath-7B FLORES X-En BLEU from 18.62 to 33.37; MGSM accuracy from 37.32% to 53.80%).

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) suffer from severe multilingual imbalance, performing poorly on low-resource languages despite encoding cross-lingual knowledge. This paper presents XBridge, a framework that composes a frozen LLM with a pretrained multilingual encoder-decoder NMT model (NLLB-200) using lightweight mapping layers and an optimal transport alignment objective. XBridge bridges the heterogeneous representation spaces without retraining the LLM core. Training proceeds in three stages: cross-model mapping, encoder-side adaptation, and decoder-side adaptation. Evaluations on FLORES-101, MGSM, and XL-Sum across multiple LLMs show substantial gains in multilingual performance, particularly for low-resource and unseen languages, while preserving high-resource strengths. The approach enables language-on-demand generation by leveraging the NMT decoder for output.

## Method Summary
XBridge connects a frozen multilingual encoder-decoder (NLLB-200) with a frozen LLM via two lightweight MLP mapping layers. The first (`Mappingenc`) projects encoder outputs into the LLM’s input space; the second (`Mappingdec`) projects penultimate LLM layer states into the decoder’s cross-attention key-value space. An optimal transport (OT) loss aligns representations between the re-encoded LLM outputs and the decoder-side projections, ensuring semantic consistency despite tokenization mismatches. Training is staged: Stage 1 jointly trains the mapping layers and decoder cross-attention with all losses on trilingual data to establish base alignment. Stage 2 adapts only `Mappingenc` on downstream task data using the LLM loss. Stage 3 refines `Mappingdec` and decoder cross-attention with decoder and OT losses. This decoupling prevents objective interference between understanding and generation.

## Key Results
- On MetaMath-7B, average FLORES X-En BLEU rises from 18.62 to 33.37; MGSM accuracy increases from 37.32% to 53.80%.
- XL-Sum Rouge-L improves across evaluated languages.
- XBridge generalizes to 41 untuned languages, nearing NLLB’s performance.
- Ablations confirm the decoder component, OT alignment, and staged training are essential; removing any degrades results significantly.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Lightweight Cross-Model Mapping
**Claim:** Two learnable MLP projections resolve the fundamental representation misalignment between the frozen LLM and NMT decoder by mapping heterogeneous hidden states into compatible spaces without altering core models.
**Mechanism:** `Mappingenc` (2-layer MLP) projects encoder outputs `Hx` into the LLM's input space. After LLM processing, `Mappingdec` (4-layer MLP) projects the penultimate LLM layer `Hz'` into the decoder's cross-attention key-value space.
**Core assumption:** Representation gaps between models are learnable via low-capacity adapters, preserving both the LLM's reasoning knowledge and the NMT's multilingual alignment.
**Evidence anchors:** Architecture details in Section 3.1; comparison to encoder-only methods (MindMerger) shows decoder-side mapping is novel and critical for generation.
**Break condition:** If mapping layers lack capacity for non-linear transforms between highly dissimilar spaces (e.g., distant language families), semantic drift occurs, degrading high-resource performance despite low-resource gains.

### Mechanism 2: Optimal Transport Alignment
**Claim:** The OT alignment objective enforces token-level semantic consistency at the LLM-decoder interface, accommodating heterogeneous tokenizations and sequence length mismatches.
**Mechanism:** The LLM output sequence `z` is re-encoded by the frozen multilingual encoder to obtain canonical representations `Hz`. OT minimizes transport cost between `Hz` and decoder-mapped `Hz''` under relaxed marginal constraints, allowing soft many-to-many alignment without assuming positional correspondence.
**Core assumption:** The multilingual encoder's frozen representations provide a stable semantic anchor that the decoder-side mapping must preserve, keeping LLM reasoning grounded during generation.
**Evidence anchors:** Formal OT problem in Section 3.2; ablations show OT improves En→X directions and untuned languages, especially where tokenization diverges.
**Break condition:** If token importance mass (based on embedding norm) poorly correlates with semantic salience, or if cosine cost fails for morphologically complex languages, OT may misalign, harming cross-lingual generalization.

### Mechanism 3: Three-Stage Training
**Claim:** The staged training strategy (cross-model mapping → encoder-side adaptation → decoder-side adaptation) stabilizes optimization by decoupling coarse alignment from task-specific adaptation, preventing conflicts between LLM and decoder objectives.
**Mechanism:** Stage 1 trains `Mappingenc`, `Mappingdec`, and decoder cross-attention with all losses on trilingual data. Stage 2 tunes only `Mappingenc` on task data using the LLM loss. Stage 3 tunes only `Mappingdec` and decoder cross-attention with decoder and OT losses.
**Core assumption:** Joint optimization of understanding (LLM loss) and generation (decoder loss) conflicts because they require different conditional distributions; staging isolates signals.
**Evidence anchors:** Ablation (Figure 4b) shows joint Stage 2&3 underperforms staged training.
**Break condition:** Skipping or shortening stages (e.g., adapting decoder too early) allows generation objectives to overwrite encoder-side learning, causing instability and degraded multilingual understanding.

## Foundational Learning
- **Heterogeneous representation spaces:** LLMs and NMT models are pretrained with different objectives (causal LM vs. seq2seq translation) and tokenizers, leading to incompatible hidden state distributions. *Why needed here:* Direct connection causes semantic loss; mapping layers explicitly bridge the gap. *Quick check:* What architectural component maps the LLM's penultimate layer output to the decoder's cross-attention inputs? (Answer: `Mappingdec`.)
- **Encoder-decoder closed semantic loop:** Standard NMT forms a closed loop where encoder and decoder share a semantic space. Inserting an LLM breaks this because the LLM transforms representations into its own space. *Why needed here:* XBridge must reconstruct a coherent loop across three modules for reliable generation. *Quick check:* Why is merely adding a multilingual encoder (as in MindMerger) insufficient for robust multilingual generation? (Answer: Encoder-only augmentation improves understanding but generation remains LLM-native, which is English-centric and cannot produce non-English outputs.)
- **Progressive alignment via staged fine-tuning:** Aligning three models with different pretraining objectives is a high-variance optimization problem. *Why needed here:* Staging prevents gradient interference: first establish coarse alignment, then specialize understanding, finally refine generation. *Quick check:* According to the ablation in Figure 4b, what happens if Stage 1 (cross-model mapping) is skipped? (Answer: Performance drops substantially, showing task-level fine-tuning cannot compensate for large representation gaps.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Enc (NLLB encoder) -> Mappingenc (2-layer MLP) -> LLM (frozen) -> extract penultimate layer Hz' -> Mappingdec (4-layer MLP) -> Dec (NLLB decoder, frozen except cross-attention) -> output y.
- **Critical path:** Multilingual input x → Enc → Hx → Mappingenc → Hx' (LLM input) → LLM (with English instruction) → z (English sequence) → Hz' (penultimate) → Mappingdec → Hz'' → Dec (with target language token ⟨y⟩) → multilingual output y. The OT loss runs between re-encoded Hz (from z via Enc) and Hz''.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Decoder addition vs. inference cost:* Adding the decoder enables multilingual output but doubles decoding steps (LLM + NMT decoder), making inference ~0.66x faster than Translate-Test but slower than LLM-only.
  - *OT complexity vs. generalization:* OT adds minimal parameters but requires computing transport between unequal-length sequences; the relaxed formulation trades exact optimality for speed, sufficient for regularization but may fail for highly divergent tokenizations.
  - *Staging vs. training time:* Three stages increase total steps but prevent objective interference; skipping stages or joint training reduces time but harms performance, as shown in ablations.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - *Low-resource performance plateaus:* Check `Mappingenc/dec` capacity; if FLORES BLEU on low-resource languages lags NLLB baseline, OT loss may not be converging or mapping layers too shallow.
  - *High-resource degradation:* If performance on high-resource languages drops relative to base LLM, staging may have overwritten English capabilities; verify Stage 2 uses sufficient English-centric data and λ weights.
  - *OT alignment not improving untuned languages:* Visualize `Hz` vs. `Hz''` with t-SNE; if clusters remain separate, OT may be ineffective due to poor cost function or insufficient Stage 1 data diversity.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Ablate decoder:** Remove `Mappingdec` and `Dec`. Expect: Strong X→En scores (encoder-side works) but near-zero En→X and poor MGSM multilingual reasoning. Confirms decoder necessity for generation.
  2. **Ablate OT:** Train without OT loss. Expect: Performance drop on untuned languages and En→X directions, especially for languages with large tokenization mismatch (e.g., Urdu). Confirms OT’s role in cross-lingual generalization.
  3. **Unseen language evaluation:** Evaluate on 41 untuned languages from Table 3. Expect: XBridge approaches NLLB performance; w/o OT gap widens. Validates that Stage 1 alignment is language-agnostic, not memorized.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How can the residual multilingual imbalance in XBridge be further reduced to achieve uniform performance across all languages? The current design freezes the NMT model and LLM, connecting them with lightweight mapping layers; yet performance varies across languages, especially between high- and low-resource. Harmonizing these two very different models remains an open engineering and research challenge.

### Open Question 2
Does the relaxed optimal transport alignment objective generalize to languages with extremely divergent tokenization characteristics (e.g., logographic, abjad, or highly polysynthetic languages), and how does it compare to alternative alignment methods? The paper shows OT improves results particularly for languages with tokenization length mismatch (e.g., Urdu), but evaluation covers only a limited set; the relaxed OT might break down when token correspondences are extremely non-monotonic.

### Open Question 3
Can XBridge be trained in an end-to-end manner without the three-stage curriculum, and if so, what techniques (e.g., gradient normalization, multi-task balancing) can prevent the observed performance degradation? The paper reports that jointly training stage 2 and stage 3 underperforms the staged approach, indicating a conflict between encoder-side and decoder-side objectives. Without understanding the cause, designing a unified training regime is difficult.

### Open Question 4
Is the XBridge framework applicable to other multilingual external models (e.g., speech encoders, multimodal encoders) to extend LLMs to non-textual input-output modalities? The architecture is described specifically for encoder-decoder NMT and uses the decoder for text generation. The interaction between modality gaps and the proposed alignment objectives is unknown; effectiveness of OT for aligning heterogeneous feature spaces beyond token embeddings remains open.

## Limitations
- Stage 1 trilingual data specifics (exact sampling, augmentation) are unclear, risking reproducibility.
- Training procedure ambiguity: whether English sequence *z* is teacher-forced with gold references or LLM-generated affects Hz extraction noise and alignment quality.
- Optimal transport implementation details (token matching heuristics, relaxed margins) are high-level and may diverge from description, affecting generalization.
- Missing critical hyperparameters (weight decay, dropout, gradient clipping) could cause training instability or divergence in replication.

## Confidence
- **Lightweight cross-model mapping bridges heterogeneous spaces**: Medium. Architecture and ablations support learnable low-capacity adapters, but untested for distant language families; break condition plausible.
- **OT alignment enforces semantic consistency**: Low. Novel in this context with weak neighbor evidence; relies on token importance heuristics that may fail for morphologically complex languages.
- **Three-stage training prevents objective interference**: High. Ablation (Figure 4b) shows joint training underperforms, empirically validating staging.
- **Generalization to untuned languages**: Medium. Results on 41 languages are strong, but depend on unclear Stage 1 data and OT details; replication needed.

## Next Checks
1. **Decoder ablation:** Train without `Mappingdec` and `Dec`; expect preserved X→En but collapse on En→X and MGSM, confirming decoder necessity for generation.
2. **OT effect on tokenization mismatch:** Ablate OT and evaluate on languages with high tokenizer divergence (e.g., Urdu, Thai); expect performance drop to isolate OT’s role in cross-lingual alignment.
3. **Untuned language robustness:** Replicate evaluation on all 41 untuned languages; with OT, results should approach NLLB baseline, without OT degrade significantly, validating Stage 1’s language-agnostic alignment.
