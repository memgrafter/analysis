# Cast-R1: Learning Tool-Augmented Sequential Decision Policies for Time Series Forecasting
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.13802
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13802
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Cast-R1 achieves state-of-the-art forecasting accuracy on multiple real-world datasets, significantly reducing MSE and MAE compared to strong baselines.

## Executive Summary
Traditional time series forecasting is often treated as a one‑shot prediction, lacking the ability to gather evidence, reason about changes, or revise forecasts. Cast-R1 re‑conceptualizes forecasting as a sequential decision‑making process. It introduces a memory‑based state management that accumulates contextual evidence across steps, and a tool‑augmented agentic workflow that leverages a modular toolkit for feature extraction, statistical diagnostics, and invocation of specialized forecasting models. The agent selects tools adaptively, reasons, and refines predictions through self‑reflection. Training combines supervised fine‑tuning for stable tool usage with multi‑turn reinforcement learning (GRPO) and curriculum learning. Evaluated on ETT, Wind, and electricity price datasets, Cast‑R1 consistently attains the lowest MSE and top‑2 MAE, outperforming baselines like PatchTST and TimesFM; ablations confirm the necessity of memory, the toolkit, and the two‑stage learning strategy.

## Method Summary
Cast‑R1 operates by framing forecasting as a sequence of actions—tool calls and refinements—within an LLM‑driven agent (Qwen3). The agent maintains a memory structure that stores tool outputs, predictions, and actions, enabling long‑horizon reasoning. A modular toolkit provides statistical feature extraction, data quality checks, and lightweight forecasting models (e.g., ARIMA, PatchTST). The workflow follows three stages: (1) Preparation: diagnostic tools analyze the input series; (2) Forecasting: a selected model generates an initial prediction; (3) Refinement: self‑reflection tools assess and correct the forecast. Training begins with supervised fine‑tuning on curated demonstration trajectories to establish reliable tool usage. This is followed by reinforcement learning with GRPO, optimizing a composite reward that combines prediction error, structural alignment (trend, seasonality, turning points), and format adherence. A curriculum scheduler organizes training samples by difficulty (based on teacher model error and permutation entropy) to progressively challenge the agent. The resulting policy adaptively chooses tools and iteratively improves forecasts, yielding state‑of‑the‑art results.

## Key Results
- On the ETTm1 dataset, Cast‑R1 achieves an MSE of 3.465, substantially lower than PatchTST’s 5.838.
- For the Wind dataset, Cast‑R1 attains MSE 1331, outperforming TimesFM’s 1445.
- Across multiple benchmarks (ETT hourly/15min, electricity prices), Cast‑R1 consistently ranks in the top two for MAE and achieves the lowest MSE among all baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Memory‑based state management enables accumulation of contextual evidence across decision steps, supporting long‑horizon reasoning in forecasting.
- **Mechanism**: A memory structure Mₖ stores heterogeneous information (tool outputs, predictions, actions) and updates incrementally. This decouples state representation from action selection, reducing redundancy and maintaining continuity under partial observability.
- **Core assumption**: Forecast quality improves when the system retains and summarizes relevant historical context rather than reprocessing raw data each step.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: “introduces a memory‑based state management mechanism that maintains decision‑relevant information across interaction steps, enabling the accumulation of contextual evidence to support long‑horizon reasoning.”
  - Section 3.2 details memory update rules and state construction from Mₖ.
  - Corpus: Neighbor papers focus on RNNs and diffusion models without explicit memory mechanisms for sequential decision‑making; evidence weak.
- **Break condition**: Memory saturation with irrelevant details or failure to compress information leads to state degradation and poor action selection.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Tool‑augmented agentic workflow improves forecasting by adaptively selecting specialized tools for feature extraction, model invocation, and self‑reflection.
- **Mechanism**: The agent interacts with a modular toolkit (data quality, statistics, forecasting models) in a structured three‑stage process (preparation, forecasting, refinement), using diagnostic outputs to guide model selection and prediction refinement.
- **Core assumption**: Forecasting can be decomposed into meaningful, interdependent decisions that external tools can execute more reliably than a monolithic model.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: “forecasting is carried out through a tool‑augmented agentic workflow, in which the agent autonomously interacts with a modular toolkit to extract statistical features, invoke lightweight forecasting models for decision support, perform reasoning‑based prediction, and iteratively refine forecasts through self‑reflection.”
  - Section 3.3 defines toolkit capabilities; Section 3.4 describes the workflow stages and state transitions.
  - Corpus: Neighbor “AnomaMind” uses tool‑augmented reasoning for anomaly detection, supporting generalizability of agentic approaches but not directly forecasting‑specific.
- **Break condition**: Toolkit misalignment with task requirements or poor agent policy leads to invalid tool calls, irrelevant diagnostics, or unrefined predictions.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Two‑stage learning (SFT + multi‑turn RL with curriculum) stably optimizes long‑horizon decision policies for forecasting.
- **Mechanism**: SFT initializes with stable tool‑usage behaviors via demonstration trajectories; RL (GRPO) optimizes episode‑level rewards with composite terms (accuracy, structure); curriculum groups samples by difficulty (prediction error, entropy) for progressive training.
- **Core assumption**: Sequential forecasting policies require imitation for basic competence and RL for long‑term optimization, with curriculum easing learning under complexity.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: “adopt a two‑stage learning strategy that combines supervised fine‑tuning with multi‑turn reinforcement learning, together with a curriculum learning scheme that progressively increases task difficulty to improve policy learning.”
  - Section 3.5 specifies SFT on curated trajectories, GRPO with group advantages, and curriculum bands based on teacher MSE and permutation entropy.
  - Corpus: Neighbors explore RL for time series but lack Cast‑R1’s specific SFT‑RL‑curriculum integration; indirect support.
- **Break condition**: Poor reward component weighting or abrupt curriculum jumps cause reward hacking, unstable training, or failure to generalize to hard tasks.

## Foundational Learning
### Sequential Decision‑Making
- **Why needed here**: Cast‑R1 redefines forecasting as a sequence of actions (tool calls, refinements) rather than a single mapping; engineers must design state transitions and action spaces.
- **Quick check**: How does framing forecasting as a PSDM (partially observable sequential decision‑making) problem change the loss function compared to standard point‑wise MSE minimization?

### Reinforcement Learning with Delayed Rewards
- **Why needed here**: The RL stage optimizes episode‑level forecasting quality, requiring credit assignment across multiple tool invocations and reasoning steps.
- **Quick check**: Why use group‑relative advantages (GRPO) instead of a critic‑based method like PPO for this multi‑turn agent?

### Tool‑Augmented Agent Design
- **Why needed here**: The agent must dynamically select and coordinate diverse tools; understanding tool interfaces, error handling, and state updates is critical.
- **Quick check**: What are the risks of allowing the agent to invoke tools in arbitrary order versus enforcing a staged workflow?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Learning Pipeline → Agent Workflow → (Memory Manager, Toolkit)

**Critical path**
1. Input initialization: Load historical series and context into initial state.
2. Turn 1 (Diagnostic): Agent calls feature extraction tools only; memory accumulates analysis history.
3. Turn 2 (Model Selection): Based on analysis history, agent invokes a forecasting model; memory stores predictions.
4. Turn 3 (Refinement): Agent uses analysis + predictions for self‑correction; outputs final forecast.
5. Learning phases: SFT on demonstration trajectories, then RL with GRPO on sampled episodes.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Memory depth vs. efficiency: Longer memory retains more context but increases prompt length and computational cost; truncation strategies balance this.
- Tool granularity: More tools increase flexibility but complicate policy learning; toolkit was curated to cover essential diagnostics and models without redundancy.
- Reward complexity: Composite rewards (accuracy, structure, length) improve forecast quality but require careful weighting to avoid conflicts; ablation studies guided design.
- Curriculum pacing: Difficulty bands based on teacher MSE and entropy organize samples from easy to hard; misestimation leads to either stagnation or instability.

**Failure signatures**
- Performance: Sudden MSE spikes on validation splits, especially on high‑entropy datasets (e.g., Wind, PJM).
- Behavior: Agent invokes tools out of order (e.g., predicting in turn 1), produces malformed outputs (missing tags), or repeats actions.
- Training: RL reward plateaus or decreases; KL divergence between policy and reference model grows; curriculum progression causes forgetting.
- Memory: Memory logs show irrelevant or duplicate entries; state summaries become noisy or collapse to defaults.

**First 3 experiments**
1. Memory integrity test: Run a fixed episode (e.g., ETTm1 sample) and log memory updates after each turn; verify that tool outputs are stored correctly and that summaries remain coherent.
2. Tool invocation validation: Use a held‑out set with known characteristics (e.g., high volatility) and check if agent selects appropriate models (Chronos‑2 for volatile, ARIMA for stable) as per workflow design.
3. Reward ablation impact: Train three lightweight models with (a) only prediction error reward, (b) full reward, (c) reward without structural terms; compare validation MSE to confirm each component’s contribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How does Cast‑R1's computational cost and memory usage scale with increasing time series length, dimensionality, and number of interaction steps, and can alternative architectures maintain performance while reducing reliance on large LLMs?**  
   – *Basis*: The framework uses an LLM backbone (Qwen3‑8B) and maintains a memory structure that stores analysis history and predictions within the LLM context; experiments limited to fixed look‑back windows.  
   – *Why unresolved*: Real‑world deployments often require low‑latency forecasts; without understanding scaling behavior, it’s unclear if the agentic approach is practical for large‑scale or high‑frequency scenarios.  
   – *What evidence would resolve*: Systematic experiments varying look‑back length, number of variables, and interaction steps, measuring inference time, memory footprint, and accuracy; comparisons with specialized encoders or compressed memory representations.

2. **To what extent does Cast‑R1 generalize to time series from domains significantly different from the training data (e.g., finance, healthcare) and to scenarios with extreme distribution shifts, without additional fine‑tuning?**  
   – *Basis*: Evaluation restricted to energy and transformer datasets; claims of domain‑agnosticism unvalidated.  
   – *Why unresolved*: Generalization is a key promise; verification essential before adoption in sensitive domains.  
   – *What evidence would resolve*: Zero‑shot transfer experiments on out‑of‑distribution datasets (e.g., stock prices, medical vitals, traffic flows); include controlled distribution‑shift benchmarks.

3. **Can the toolkit in Cast‑R1 be automatically expanded or learned to include new tools beyond the hand‑crafted set, and would such automation improve forecast accuracy or adaptability?**  
   – *Basis*: The toolkit is manually engineered and fixed; no tool discovery, composition, or adaptation considered.  
   – *Why unresolved*: Future tasks might require specialized operations not anticipated; an agent that can learn/create new tools would be more flexible.  
   – *What evidence would resolve*: Introduce a meta‑learning layer that parameterizes a space of tools (e.g., via code generation or primitive operations) and lets the policy select or instantiate them; evaluate on complex/novel datasets.

4. **To what extent does the multi‑turn reinforcement learning stage contribute to performance beyond what could be achieved with more sophisticated supervised fine‑tuning?**  
   – *Basis*: Paper employs SFT followed by RL; ablations show removing RL degrades performance, but alternative supervised methods not examined.  
   – *Why unresolved*: RL adds complexity and cost; if supervised methods alone could achieve similar results, the system would be simpler.  
   – *What evidence would resolve*: Controlled study contrasting SFT+RL against: (a) extensive SFT on diverse expert trajectories; (b) supervised learning with intermediate rewards; (c) imitation learning from human expert traces; compare accuracy, stability, sample efficiency.

## Limitations
- SFT trajectory construction details are unspecified, hindering faithful replication of the imitation learning stage.
- Reward component coefficients and teacher model identity for curriculum are not provided, making RL training fragile.
- Evaluation is limited to energy/weather datasets; cross‑domain generalization and robustness to distribution shifts remain untested.

## Confidence
- Memory‑based state management: **High**
- Tool‑augmented agentic workflow: **High**
- Two‑stage learning (SFT+RL): **Medium**
- Broad generalization: **Low**
