# Language Statistics and False Belief Reasoning: Evidence from 41 Open-Weight LMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16085
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16085
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** 34% of LMs exhibited sensitivity to belief states, with top accuracy at 74.5% versus human 82.7%.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluated 41 open-weight language models on a standardized false belief task to assess the generalizability and reproducibility of mental state reasoning research. Using log odds measurements and linear mixed-effects models, the authors measured model sensitivity to knowledge state manipulations and compared performance to human data. Results showed that larger model parameter counts predicted higher sensitivity and psychometric predictive power, but no model matched human accuracy. Both humans and LMs demonstrated a bias toward false beliefs when probes used non-factive verbs (e.g., “thinks”), and the magnitude of this human effect fell within the distribution of LM effect sizes, suggesting language statistics can account for this cue effect. However, the failure of any LM to fully explain the primary knowledge state effect observed in humans indicates that current distributional learning is insufficient for robust theory-of-mind reasoning.

## Method Summary
The study reused 192 written passages from Trott et al. (2023), each depicting a character’s belief about an object’s location (Start vs. End). Passages systematically varied by Knowledge State (True/False Belief), Knowledge Cue (Explicit “thinks” vs. Implicit “looks”), First Mention, and Recent Mention. For each of 41 open-weight models (from Gemma, Llama 3, OLMo 2, Pythia, and Qwen 2.5 families, with Base/Instruct variants), the model’s log probability for the Start and End location tokens was extracted, and the log odds (log P_start – log P_end) was computed. Sensitivity to Knowledge State was tested via likelihood ratio tests of linear mixed-effects models (log_odds ~ Knowledge State + Cue + … + (1+Knowledge State|Item)). Human trial responses from 613 participants were aggregated into per-item log odds for comparison. Psychometric predictive power (PPP) was assessed by correlating LM log odds with human log odds (R²) and via likelihood ratio tests in generalized linear mixed models predicting human categorical responses.

## Key Results
- **Sensitivity and accuracy:** 34% of LMs (14/41) showed statistically significant sensitivity to Knowledge State; the best model achieved 74.5% accuracy versus 82.7% for humans.
- **Scaling effect:** Larger parameter counts predicted higher sensitivity and PPP (R² up to 0.26) in linear regressions.
- **Cue effect alignment:** The human coefficient for the Knowledge Cue manipulation (negative bias for explicit “thinks”) fell at the 49th percentile of the LM coefficient distribution, indicating that distributional statistics can account for this bias in both groups.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1:** Larger model parameter counts are associated with increased sensitivity to knowledge state manipulations.  
*Mechanism:* Increased scale enhances capacity to capture distributional patterns in training data that correlate with mental state reasoning (e.g., verb-narrative-location co-occurrences).  
*Core assumption:* Within the studied families and ranges, performance on this task improves monotonically with parameter count.  
*Evidence anchors:* [abstract] "Larger LMs show increased sensitivity and also exhibit higher psychometric predictive power."; [section 4.3] Linear regression: Log Parameters β=0.08, SE=0.02, p<.001.  
*Break condition:* Non-monotonic or inverted-U relationship between scale and sensitivity emerges when testing broader architectures (e.g., non-transformer, different pretraining objectives).

**Mechanism 2:** The bias toward attributing false beliefs with non-factive verbs (e.g., “thinks”) is accounted for by distributional language statistics.  
*Mechanism:* LMs learn lexical associations where non-factive verbs often co-occur with propositions that are false or uncertain from the speaker’s perspective, driving a default “Start” (incorrect) location bias.  
*Core assumption:* The human effect size falls within the LM distribution because both are driven by the same corpus-based lexical statistics.  
*Evidence anchors:* [abstract] "the magnitude of the human knowledge cue effect falls squarely within the distribution of LM effect sizes"; [section 4.5] Human β_KC = –1.02, SE=0.27, p<.001; at 49th percentile of LM distribution.  
*Break condition:* Controlled training decouples “thinks” from false propositions; LM bias disappears while human bias remains.

**Mechanism 3:** Distributional language statistics are insufficient to fully explain the primary human sensitivity to knowledge state (false vs. true belief).  
*Mechanism:* The core false belief effect likely requires constructing a situation model that tracks character-specific knowledge states over time—processes (e.g., sustained representation, inhibition) not fully captured by next-token prediction on narrative text. LMs may rely on heuristic shortcuts.  
*Core assumption:* The gap between top LM accuracy (74.5%) and human accuracy (82.7%), and the failure of LM Log Odds to fully “explain away” the Knowledge State effect in human responses, reflects a limitation of pure distributional learning.  
*Evidence anchors:* [abstract] "no LM fully 'explain away' the effect in humans"; [section 4.2] Adding Knowledge State to models with LM Log Odds significantly improved fit (likelihood ratios 123.6–195.4); [section 4.4] Best PPP R² = 0.26.  
*Break condition:* A future LM trained only on vast text but with radically different architecture/objective (e.g., explicit knowledge state tracking) achieves human-level accuracy and fully accounts for the human Knowledge State effect.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Mixed-Effects Models**  
*Why needed:* The core analysis uses linear mixed-effects models with by-item random slopes to test if Log Odds vary systematically with Knowledge State while accounting for variability across the 192 stimulus items. This is crucial for generalizing from items to the underlying construct.  
*Quick check:* What is the purpose of including random slopes for Knowledge State by item in the model for RQ1?

**Concept: Log Odds as Behavioral Measure**  
*Why needed:* The primary dependent variable is the log ratio of the probability assigned to the Start location token versus the End location token. This continuous measure captures the model’s relative location bias for each passage.  
*Quick check:* If a model assigns a higher log probability to the End token than the Start token for a False Belief passage, what is the sign of its Log Odds, and is this considered accurate?

**Concept: Psychometric Predictive Power (PPP)**  
*Why needed:* PPP quantifies how well LM behavior correlates with or predicts individual human trial-level responses (aggregated Log Odds), moving beyond simple accuracy to assess behavioral alignment.  
*Quick check:* How was PPP approximated in the first approach (R²), and what did an R² of 0.26 for the best LM indicate?

**Concept: False Belief Task Structure**  
*Why needed:* Understanding the canonical structure (object transfer, character presence/absence, probe) is essential to interpret the Knowledge State manipulation and the Knowledge Cue distinction.  
*Quick check:* In a True Belief passage, is the target character present or absent during the object’s relocation?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Stimuli (192 passages) -> Model Layer (41 open-weight LMs) -> Inference Engine (HuggingFace transformers, tokenization, log prob extraction) -> Analysis Layer (R: lme4, ggplot2 for mixed-effects models, regressions, PPP)

### Critical Path
1. **Data Preparation:** Format passages identically; handle multi-token locations by summing log probs.
2. **Model Inference Loop:** For each model, run all 192 passages, capture log probs for Start/End tokens, compute Log Odds.
3. **Statistical Modeling (RQ1/RQ5):** For each LM, fit mixed model (Log Odds ~ Knowledge State + Cue + … + (1+Knowledge State|Item)); perform LRT against null without Knowledge State.
4. **Human Comparison (RQ2/RQ4):** Use public human data; for RQ2, fit GLMM predicting human response with LM Log Odds and Knowledge State; LRT tests if Knowledge State adds variance. For PPP, correlate LM Log Odds with human-by-item Log Odds.
5. **Scaling Analysis (RQ3/RQ4):** Regress performance (accuracy, PPP) on log(parameters), log(tokens), tune-status.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Open-weight vs. Closed-source:** Chose open-weight for reproducibility/verifiability, but excluded potentially higher-performing closed-source models (e.g., GPT-4, Claude).
- **Prompting strategy:** Uniform sentence-completion prompt for all models to control confounds, potentially underrepresenting instruction-tuned models.
- **Stimulus coverage:** 192 items from 12 scenarios offers control but may not generalize to all false belief narratives.
- **Metric choice:** Log Odds is sensitive but assumes token-level semantics align with location concepts; alternative metrics (e.g., prompted answer choice) could yield different patterns.

### Failure Signatures
- **Data Processing:** Incorrect token handling for multi-word locations (e.g., “coffee table”) corrupts Log Odds. *Diagnostic:* Manually inspect tokenizer output for random subset.
- **Model Loading:** Mismatched model revision or tokenizer causes inference errors. *Diagnostic:* Use pinned HuggingFace IDs.
- **Statistical Mis-specification:** Omitting by-item random slopes for Knowledge State inflates Type I error. *Diagnostic:* Check convergence warnings; if failed, try simpler random effects or use `bobyqa` optimizer.
- **Human Data Alignment:** Failure to correctly aggregate human responses to by-item Log Odds invalidates PPP. *Diagnostic:* Replicate aggregation formula from original study.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate Sensitivity Test (RQ1):** For a single model (e.g., OLMo 2 13B Instruct), fit mixed-effects model with and without Knowledge State. Confirm significant LRT (p<.05, Holm-corrected) and negative β for True Belief. Plot Log Odds by condition.
2. **Scale-Performance Correlation (RQ3):** For all 41 models, compute mean accuracy. Plot accuracy vs. log10(parameter count). Fit linear regression; confirm significant positive β for parameter count. Identify best-performing model family/variant.
3. **Knowledge Cue Hypothesis Test (RQ5):** For human data and all LMs, fit GLMM predicting categorical response (Start/End) with Knowledge Cue. Extract β_KC. Confirm human β_KC is negative and falls within the LM β_KC distribution (e.g., 49th percentile).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the false belief task validly measure theory of mind in LMs, or are LMs relying on heuristic shortcuts rather than genuine mental state reasoning?
2. Are the performance patterns observed across 41 open-weight LMs generalizable to the broader population of LMs, including closed-source and future architectures?
3. What accounts for humans’ greater sample efficiency in theory of mind acquisition compared to LMs, given that LMs are trained on orders of magnitude more linguistic data?
4. Why do both humans and LMs exhibit a bias toward attributing false beliefs when knowledge states are cued with non-factive verbs (e.g., “thinks”) compared to indirect cues?
5. Which specific architectural or training modifications can enhance LMs’ sensitivity to knowledge states and narrow the gap with human performance on false belief tasks?

## Limitations
- Model sample restricted to 41 open-weight transformer models from five families, excluding closed-source or unconventional architectures.
- Stimulus set of 192 items (12 scenarios) may not capture the full diversity of false belief narratives.
- Tokenization mismatches for multi-word location names could invalidate Log Odds extraction.

## Confidence
- **Scaling relationship** (larger parameters → higher sensitivity/PPP): **Medium** – correlational within studied families; non-monotonic trends may emerge with broader architectures.
- **Cue effect alignment** (human bias within LM distribution): **High** – human effect size at 49th percentile of LM distribution provides robust match.
- **Insufficiency of distributional statistics** (no LM fully explains knowledge state effect): **Medium** – strong empirical result for current models, but future architectures/training could close the gap.

## Next Checks
1. **Architecture diversity test:** Evaluate non-transformer models (e.g., state-space, RNNs) and models with varying pretraining objectives to test generality of scaling relationship and identify break points.
2. **Controlled corpus manipulation:** Train LMs on corpora where non-factive verbs (e.g., “thinks”) are decorrelated from false propositions; test if the cue effect vanishes while human bias persists.
3. **Extended task battery:** Replicate analysis across multiple Theory-of-Mind benchmarks (e.g., second-order belief, sarcasm) to assess whether the insufficiency is task-specific or generalizable.
