# Count Bridges enable Modeling and Deconvolving Transcriptomic Data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.04730  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04730  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Count Bridges introduces a stochastic bridge process on integers with Poisson birth‑death dynamics, yielding exact closed‑form conditionals and tractable training via a distributional scoring loss; it achieves state‑of‑the‑art performance on synthetic integer distribution matching and on deconvolution tasks in transcriptomics, including nucleotide‑level gene expression prediction and bulk/spatial RNA‑seq deconvolution.

## Executive Summary
Count Bridges tackles the problem of modeling integer‑count biological data (e.g., RNA‑seq) that are often aggregated across cells. The method defines a stochastic bridge process on integers based on Poisson birth‑death dynamics, which admits exact closed‑form transition probabilities (Binomial or Hypergeometric). This enables efficient training using a distributional scoring loss (energy score) and exact sampling without density estimation. An EM algorithm with a learned projection module extends the framework to deconvolution, enforcing that recovered single‑cell counts sum to observed aggregates. Empirical results show state‑of‑the‑art performance on synthetic benchmarks (Wasserstein‑2, MMD, EMD) and strong results on real biological tasks, including nucleotide‑level gene expression prediction from sequence and deconvolution of bulk and spatial transcriptomics, matching or surpassing specialized tools.

## Method Summary
The core of Count Bridges is a continuous‑time birth‑death Markov process on integers that forms a stochastic bridge between a simple prior (e.g., Poisson noise) and the data distribution. The forward transition probability from state \(x_s\) at time \(s\) to \(x_t\) at time \(t\) (\(s<t\)) is available in closed form as a Binomial or Hypergeometric distribution, thanks to the properties of the Poisson process. Training uses a distributional scoring loss (the energy score) that directly compares the model’s reverse transition distribution to the empirical distribution, avoiding explicit density estimation and allowing Monte Carlo estimation.

For deconvolution of aggregated counts, the authors propose an EM‑style algorithm. The E‑step uses the learned Count Bridge to sample plausible single‑cell counts given the aggregate. The M‑step updates a projection module that maps latent variables to count vectors while exactly satisfying the sum constraint (via a custom rounding procedure). Architecturally, the backbone is chosen based on the task: a multi‑layer perceptron (MLP) for synthetic data, an attention‑plus‑MLP for PBMC nucleotide‑resolution modeling, and a UViT (U‑Net Vision Transformer) for spatial transcriptomics.

## Key Results
- State‑of‑the‑art distribution matching on synthetic integer datasets (W2, MMD, EMD) compared to flow matching baselines.
- Nucleotide‑level gene expression prediction from DNA sequence: Count Bridges matches or fine‑tuned Enformer on MSE.
- Deconvolution of bulk RNA‑seq and spatial transcriptomics: lower JSD, RMSE, and higher Spearman correlation than specialized tools like CIBERSORTx and STDeconvolve.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Poisson birth‑death bridge yields exact conditional distributions because the underlying continuous‑time Markov chain has transition intensities that lead to Binomial/Hypergeometric probabilities. This eliminates the need for approximations in both forward and reverse directions and makes sampling and density evaluation tractable. The energy score loss is a proper scoring rule that can be estimated by Monte Carlo without requiring the model to compute densities explicitly, which is crucial for discrete data where likelihoods can be sparse or expensive. For deconvolution, the EM algorithm with projection iteratively refines single‑cell estimates while enforcing aggregate constraints; the learned projection module adapts to the data and ensures that the sums of inferred counts exactly match the observed aggregates. These components together provide a flexible and theoretically sound pipeline for integer‑valued count data.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Poisson Birth‑Death Process** – *Why needed*: Provides a discrete‑state continuous‑time Markov process with tractable transition probabilities; forms the basis of the stochastic bridge. *Quick check*: Simulate a simple birth‑death process and verify that the empirical distribution after a fixed time matches the closed‑form binomial/hypergeometric distribution.
2. **Stochastic Bridge** – *Why needed*: Allows constructing a process that bridges between two distributions (e.g., noise and data) while preserving discrete structure; essential for generative modeling of counts. *Quick check*: Given fixed endpoints \(x_0\) and \(x_1\), sample intermediate \(x_t\) and verify that the one‑step transition from \(t\) to \(s\) is consistent with the two‑step transition via an intermediate time.
3. **Distributional Scoring Loss (Energy Score)** – *Why needed*: Enables training without explicit density estimation by directly comparing distributions; suitable for integer‑valued data where densities may be sparse. *Quick check*: Compute the energy score between two simple distributions (e.g., Poisson with different means); a lower score indicates better alignment.
4. **EM Algorithm with Projection** – *Why needed*: Solves deconvolution under aggregate constraints by iteratively inferring latent variables and updating projection parameters; ensures that sums of inferred counts match observed aggregates. *Quick check*: For a small synthetic aggregate (e.g., total count 10 from 3 cells), run one EM step and verify that the projected single‑cell counts sum exactly to the aggregate.
5. **Attention and UViT Architectures** – *Why needed*: Capture complex dependencies in high‑dimensional biological data (e.g., nucleotide sequences or spatial spots) while handling variable‑length inputs. *Quick check*: Pass a dummy tensor through the attention+MLP or UViT and check output shape and absence of NaNs.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input Counts → Encoder (linear embedding) → Backbone (MLP / Attention+MLP / UViT) → Parameter Head (predicts rates or latent variables) → Poisson Bridge Sampler → Distribution Output.  
For deconvolution: Latent Variables → Projection Module → Aggregated Counts.

**Critical path**:  
- Training: Compute energy score loss by comparing the model’s reverse distribution (parameterized by the backbone) to the empirical distribution. The reverse distribution uses closed‑form Binomial/Hypergeometric conditionals that depend on the backbone’s output.  
- Sampling: Start from a prior (e.g., Poisson noise), then iteratively apply reverse transitions (using the backbone to compute necessary parameters) to obtain data samples.  
- Deconvolution: Given an aggregate, run EM: E‑step uses the bridge to sample single‑cell counts; M‑step updates the projection module parameters to minimize reconstruction error under sum constraints.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Closed‑form conditionals avoid approximations but require careful handling of the time‑rescaling function \(w(t)\) and exact rounding.  
- Backbone choice balances expressivity and cost: MLP is fast but may miss long‑range dependencies; attention+MLP and UViT add capacity for biological sequences and spatial data at the cost of memory.  
- The projection module adds an optimization layer that may slow convergence but guarantees exact aggregate matching.  
- The energy score loss is biased but has lower variance than likelihood‑based losses for discrete data.

**Failure signatures**:  
1. Bridge inconsistency: one‑step and two‑step transition distributions diverge.  
2. Aggregate mismatch: after projection, sum of recovered counts != target aggregate.  
3. Energy score collapse: loss near zero due to constant predictions.  
4. Gene alignment errors: mismatch between gene names causes poor deconvolution.  
5. Training instability: NaNs or exploding gradients.  
6. Synthetic benchmark performance significantly worse than baselines.

**First experiments**:  
1. **Unit test the binomial sampler**: Fix \(x_0=5\), \(x_1=10\), sample \(x_t\) at \(t=0.5\) using Algorithm 1; compare empirical histogram to analytical Binomial distribution (e.g., KL divergence < 0.01).  
2. **Projection sanity check**: Train the projection module on random latent vectors with known target aggregates; verify that projected outputs sum exactly to the target (within rounding) and that reconstruction loss decreases.  
3. **Minimal training run**: Train Count Bridge on a tiny 2‑Moons dataset (100 samples) for 10 epochs; ensure loss decreases and visualize samples to confirm the model learns the crescent shape.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What are the precise statistical conditions under which discrete‑native count models (e.g., Count Bridges) provide a measurable advantage over continuous diffusion models (e.g., DDIM) applied to rounded or transformed count data?  
- **Basis in paper**: The authors note as a limitation: “When counts are well‑approximated as continuous, Euclidean models may match or exceed performance.” This highlights an unresolved “when” rather than a “whether.”  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper presents empirical comparisons on a few tasks, but there is no systematic study of the count distributions, sparsity levels, sample sizes, or metrics where discrete‑native architectures yield statistically and practically significant gains. Without such a characterization, it is unclear whether the added complexity of count‑specific pipelines is justified.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Large‑scale benchmarking across a diverse suite of synthetic count distributions (Poisson, negative binomial, zero‑inflated, sparse vs. dense) with varying mean–variance relationships, combined with theoretical analysis of approximation errors in continuous relaxations. A decision tree or set of sufficient conditions (e.g., on support size, entropy, or order structure) would clarify the domain of advantage.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How does the identifiability of unit‑level count distributions from aggregated observations depend on group size and between‑group heterogeneity?  
- **Basis in paper**: The authors state that identifiability “inherently [is] lost as groups become large” and that between‑group heterogeneity is essential. Yet no explicit bounds are provided linking group size, heterogeneity, and estimation accuracy.  
- **Why unresolved**: Without theoretical guarantees, it is impossible to know how many cells per spot or how much variation in cell‑type proportions is needed for reliable deconvolution. This is a core practical question for spatial transcriptomics or bulk deconvolution applications.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Derive information‑theoretic limits (e.g., minimax rates) for recovering unit‑level distributions from sums under factorial cumulant models. Empirically, conduct a controlled study varying group size and Dirichlet concentration to map the phase transition between identifiable and non‑identifiable regimes. A result could be a formula for the effective “degrees of freedom” preserved in the aggregate.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What are the convergence properties and statistical guarantees (e.g., consistency, asymptotic normality) of the projection‑guided EM algorithm used for deconvolution, and does the first‑order projection step preserve these properties?  
- **Basis in paper**: The authors acknowledge: “The projection step we use is a first‑order surrogate and lacks serious theoretical support,” suggesting they recognize a gap in understanding the algorithm’s behavior.  
- **Why unresolved**: EM is widely used but its convergence is not guaranteed to reach the global optimum; the additional approximation error from projection may worsen the local landscape or introduce bias. Without analysis, it is uncertain whether the method will scale to high dimensions or complex aggregate maps.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Provide a theoretical analysis of the EM updates under the projection, perhaps showing that the projected lower bound remains informative and that the fixed point corresponds to a stationary point of the original problem. Complement with empirical studies on simple parametric families where the true conditional is known, measuring bias as a function of group size and projection accuracy.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Do dependencies between spatial units (e.g., cells in tissue) violate the conditional independence assumption in Count Bridges, and can the framework be extended to model spatial correlations without sacrificing tractability?  
- **Basis in paper**: The model in Sec. 4 treats unit‑level counts as i.i.d. given covariates. Real spatial transcriptomics data exhibit spatial autocorrelation—nearby cells often have correlated expression due to tissue organization. The paper does not address this assumption.  
- **Why unresolved**: If independence is strongly violated, the learned distribution may not capture true biological variation, and the EM deconvolution may produce biased unit profiles. Extending to dependent units would greatly increase applicability to spatial systems where spatial structure is a key signal.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Introduce spatial Markov or Gaussian process priors into the bridge dynamics for units, while preserving closed‑form conditionals if possible (or approximate via learned samplers). Then evaluate deconvolution accuracy on synthetic data with known spatial correlation structures and on real spatial transcriptomics datasets with spatial statistics (e.g., Moran’s I) indicating dependence.

## Limitations
- Official repository URL missing; reimplementation may diverge from the authors’ version.
- The time‑rescaling function \(w(t)\) is not explicitly defined (only monotonic with \(w(0)=0\), \(w(1)=1\)); crucial for Binomial/Hypergeometric conditionals.
- Data acquisition and preprocessing steps for PBMC (Onek1k) and MERFISH are not described; aggregate simulation parameters unknown.

## Confidence
- Theoretical foundation (closed‑form conditionals, tractable scoring loss): **High**.
- Synthetic benchmark performance (W2, MMD, EMD): **Medium**.
- Biological deconvolution and nucleotide prediction: **Low**.
