# Complementary Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.17621
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17621
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Complementary RL improves sample efficiency in LLM agents by co-evolving a policy actor and an experience extractor, achieving up to 10% relative improvement in single-task and 8.1% in multi-task settings with negligible rollout latency overhead.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning for large language model (LLM) agents often suffers from low sample efficiency due to sparse rewards and limited experience. Complementary RL addresses this by introducing a closed-loop co-evolutionary framework: a policy actor learns from sparse outcome rewards while simultaneously an experience extractor distills valuable experiences from the actor’s trajectories. The extractor’s optimization objective is derived from the actor’s success when using those experiences, ensuring alignment with the actor’s improving capabilities. An asynchronous infrastructure with a centralized manager coordinates experience consolidation and retrieval without blocking training. Evaluations on MiniHack, WebShop, ALFWorld, and SWE-Bench demonstrate consistent gains (10% relative improvement in single-task, 8.1% in multi-task mixtures) and negligible computational overhead. The approach opens a path toward more data-efficient RL for LLM agents.

## Method Summary
Complementary RL comprises two trainable components and an asynchronous management layer. The actor (πθ) interacts with environments to generate trajectories, trained via standard RL with sparse outcome rewards. The experience extractor (πϕ) processes collected trajectories to produce compact experience entries (state-action-outcome tuples) stored in a shared experience bank M. The extractor’s reward signal comes from evaluating the actor’s performance when leveraging the extracted experiences; thus, the extractor is incentivized to generate entries that are genuinely helpful to the current actor. A centralized manager handles experience writes, consolidations, and retrievals asynchronously, using locks and batching to avoid training stalls. The actor queries M during training to augment its batch with distilled experiences, creating a feedback loop where the actor’s progress continually reshapes the experience pool. Stabilization techniques such as Retrieval Diversification and Training-Count-Aware Advantage Reweighting are employed to maintain extractor stability under off-policy updates.

## Key Results
- Achieved a 10% relative improvement over experience-free baselines in single-task settings across MiniHack, WebShop, ALFWorld, and SWE-Bench.
- In multi-task mixtures, Complementary RL outperformed static experience baselines by up to 8.1% average reward.
- The asynchronous infrastructure adds negligible rollout latency overhead, preserving training throughput.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism is a closed-loop co-evolution between the actor and the experience extractor. As the actor improves, its trajectories become more informative; the extractor responds by producing higher-quality experiences that further boost the actor. This mutual reinforcement creates a virtuous cycle where the experience bank M automatically adapts to the actor’s evolving policy distribution. The extractor’s reward, computed from the actor’s success when using its outputs, directly aligns its objective with the actor’s learning progress. Moreover, the asynchronous manager ensures that experience consolidation and retrieval scale without blocking, maintaining high throughput. Stabilization tricks mitigate the inherent off-policy instability of training the extractor on past data, while the search-and-ask tool enriches the experience bank with targeted knowledge gaps identified by the actor. Together, these design choices yield a system that internalizes external experience efficiently and robustly.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Actor ↔ Environment  
Actor → Centralized Manager (writes raw trajectories)  
Experience Extractor → Centralized Manager (reads trajectories, writes extracted experiences)  
Centralized Manager → Experience Bank M (stores consolidated experiences)  
Actor → Experience Bank M (retrieves distilled experiences for training)  
Experience Bank M provides feedback to Experience Extractor via reward signals derived from Actor performance.

**Critical path**  
1. Actor collects a trajectory by interacting with an environment.  
2. Trajectory is sent to the manager and queued for processing.  
3. Extractor asynchronously pulls a trajectory, extracts key experience entries, and returns them to the manager.  
4. Manager updates the experience bank M with new entries and retires old ones.  
5. Actor samples a mix of raw and distilled experiences from M to form a training batch.  
6. Actor updates its policy using RL, guided by sparse outcome rewards and auxiliary losses if any.  
7. Periodically, the manager evaluates extractor performance by measuring how often actor-used experiences lead to success, generating the extractor’s reward.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Asynchronous vs synchronous updates*: Asynchrony avoids blocking but introduces non-stationarity; the manager mitigates this with batching and locks.  
- *Centralized manager*: Simplifies coordination but can be a scalability bottleneck; however, batching and caching keep overhead low.  
- *Extractor capacity*: Larger extractors can capture nuanced patterns but increase computation and risk overfitting; capacity must match task diversity.  
- *Off-policy extractor training*: Training on past trajectories risks divergence; stabilization tricks (diversification, count-aware reweighting) are heuristic and may not cover all edge cases.

**Failure signatures**  
- Extractor loss spikes or becomes NaN, indicating instability from off-policy updates.  
- Actor’s learning curve plateaus or degrades after initial gains, suggesting experience bank corruption.  
- Retrieval latency increases dramatically, pointing to manager overload or bank bloat.  
- Large performance gap between eval-with-experience and eval-without-experience, indicating poor internalization.

**First three experiments**  
1. Single-task baseline: Train the actor alone (no extractor) on MiniHack; then enable Complementary RL and compare sample efficiency and final performance.  
2. Co-evolution ablation: Disable extractor updates (fixed extractor) while keeping the experience bank; measure the contribution of extractor adaptation.  
3. Extractor size scaling: Compare 4B, 14B, and 30B extractors on a 3-task mixture to assess the scaling trend and identify diminishing returns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific mechanisms in the co-evolutionary loop cause the experience extractor's updates to become destabilized when trained off-policy with delayed rewards?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper identifies "off-policy" training and "data redundancy" as core instabilities for the extractor (§2.3, Appendix B). Stabilization tricks like Retrieval Diversification and Training-Count-Aware Advantage Reweighting are introduced heuristically without a theoretical analysis of *why* they work or what the fundamental limits of stable co-evolution are.
- Why unresolved: The paper provides empirical fixes but no formal model of the joint dynamics between the evolving actor policy distribution and the extractor's optimization objective. It is unclear if the proposed tricks are necessary/sufficient or if alternative, more principled co-evolution algorithms exist.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical analysis (e.g., in a simplified tabular or linear setting) deriving conditions for stable co-evolution. Ablations that systematically vary the policy lag between actor and extractor and measure gradient variance/divergence. Comparison with alternative co-evolution frameworks from multi-agent RL.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Under what conditions does the "search-and-ask" tool provide a net positive signal for the experience extractor versus introducing noisy or spurious correlations?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper claims search-and-ask "increases M utilization, enriches the training signal for πϕ" (§3.2.2). However, this assumes the actor's *context-aware query* accurately reflects a genuine knowledge gap that the retrieved experience can fill. The paper does not analyze the quality or relevance of these on-policy queries.
- Why unresolved: A poorly formulated query (e.g., from a confused actor) could lead πϕ to distill experience that reinforces the actor's misconceptions or produces overly specific, non-generalizable entries. This could pollute M over time, a risk not measured.
- What evidence would resolve it: An analysis of the semantic content and success rate of experience entries originating from search-and-ask versus standard trajectory distillation. Human evaluation or automated metrics to judge query relevance and distilled experience quality. Controlled experiments where the actor's ability to formulate useful queries is varied.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does the performance benefit of a larger experience extractor (e.g., 30B vs 4B) scale with the complexity and diversity of the task mixture, and is there a point of diminishing returns or negative transfer?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper shows a larger extractor yields a +5% average gain in the 3-task setting (§4.3, Figure 9a). However, this is tested on a specific mixture. The paper then shows improved scaling to 6 tasks (§4.3, Figure 9d) but does not re-test the extractor capacity effect in that harder setting.
- Why unresolved: A larger extractor may have greater capacity to extract nuanced, cross-task experience, but it may also be more prone to overfitting to spurious patterns in a diverse mixture or extracting experience that is too general to be useful. The optimal extractor capacity likely depends on the actor's capacity and task heterogeneity.
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study varying extractor size (e.g., 4B, 14B, 30B) across a gradient of task mixtures (1, 3, 6, 10+ tasks). Analysis of the experience bank's semantic diversity and cluster structure as a function of extractor size and task diversity. Measuring transfer performance on held-out, novel task types.

### Open Question 4
- Question: To what extent does co-evolution internalize external experience into the actor's inherent policy (vs. creating a persistent dependency on retrieval at test time)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper contrasts "eval w/exp." and "eval w/oexp." results in Table 1. The performance gap between these two settings for Complementary RL (e.g., +3% on WebShop) suggests not all benefits are internalized. The authors state co-evolution "internalizes useful experience into the actor itself" but the residual gap indicates a remaining dependency.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not measure *how much* of the improved policy is a result of changed weights (internalization) versus the actor having learned a more effective retrieval/use policy. The "Exp. Only" baseline (fixed extractor, fixed actor) performing poorly suggests the actor must update, but the mechanics of this update (does it learn to mimic the extractor's suggestions? Learn a meta-policy over when to trust retrieval?) are not studied.
- What evidence would resolve it: Probing experiments: 1) Train the actor to convergence with Complementary RL, then evaluate with a *random* experience retrieval system. A small performance drop would indicate partial internalization. 2) Analyze the actor's policy gradients or activation patterns when conditioned on good vs. bad/no experience to see if it learns to discount or hinge on specific retrieval cues. 3) Measure the divergence between the actor's policy with and without experience after co-evolution.

## Limitations
- The paper lacks details on baseline implementations, hyperparameters, statistical tests, and computational cost, making it hard to assess the rigor of comparisons.
- Generalizability is uncertain because evaluation is limited to four benchmarks; performance on other domains or real-world tasks remains unverified.
- Ablations are mentioned qualitatively but not quantified, obscuring the individual contribution of components such as subgroup advantage estimation and search-and-ask.
