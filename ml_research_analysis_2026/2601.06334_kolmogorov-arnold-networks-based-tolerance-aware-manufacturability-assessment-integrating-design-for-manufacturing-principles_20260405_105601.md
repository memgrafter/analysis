# Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks-Based Tolerance-Aware Manufacturability Assessment Integrating Design-for-Manufacturing Principles

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06334
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06334
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN) operating directly on parametric design features and tolerances achieved state-of-the-art manufacturability assessment (validation AUCs 0.9406–0.9919) while providing intrinsic interpretability, enabling iterative design improvement.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel framework for manufacturability assessment that bypasses traditional CAD preprocessing by using Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) to evaluate designs directly from their parametric descriptions, including dimensional tolerances. The method addresses key limitations of CAD-based approaches—such as high preprocessing costs, discretization-induced information loss, and poor model interpretability. Validated on a large synthetic dataset of 300,000 designs across drilling and milling scenarios, the KAN model outperformed 14 benchmark models. Its spline-based architecture offers inherent interpretability, allowing engineers to pinpoint critical parameters and their nonlinear effects via SHAP analysis. An industrial case study demonstrated the framework's practical utility, guiding parameter adjustments to transform a non-manufacturable design into a defect-free fabricated component.

## Method Summary
The proposed method represents each design as a fixed-length vector of continuous geometric parameters (e.g., hole diameter, pocket depth) and tolerance bounds (e.g., upper/lower limits). A Kolmogorov-Arnold Network processes this tabular input; its edges implement learnable univariate spline functions that compose to model the complex, nonlinear mapping from parameters to a binary manufacturability label. Tolerances are seamlessly integrated as additional input features. The model’s interpretability arises from the spline visualizations (showing each parameter’s transformation) and SHAP values computed over the functional network, which identify the most critical parameters and their directional influence. Training uses a synthetically generated dataset where labels are derived from established Design for Manufacturing (DFM) rules.

## Key Results
- Achieved top performance among 15 models on held-out test sets, with validation AUCs of 0.9919 (drilling), 0.9841 (milling), and 0.9406 (combined scenario).
- Demonstrated high interpretability: spline functions and SHAP analysis correctly identified parameters aligned with core DFM constraints (e.g., depth-to-diameter ratios, corner radii).
- Industrial case study confirmed practical utility: iterative parameter adjustments guided by SHAP values successfully modified a non-manufacturable milling feature into a fabricable, defect-free component.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** KANs bypass CAD preprocessing by operating directly on parametric design features, avoiding discretization-induced information loss.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of converting B-Rep CAD models to voxel grids, the method represents each design as a vector of continuous geometric and tolerance parameters. KANs learn nonlinear mappings from this tabular input to manufacturability labels using spline-based activation functions on network edges, preserving exact geometry semantics.
- **Core assumption:** Critical manufacturability constraints (e.g., depth-to-diameter ratios, wall thickness) are fully captured by a finite set of parametric descriptors aligned with DFM rules.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "evaluating manufacturability directly from parametric design features... without requiring computer-aided design (CAD) processing."
  - [section 3.1] Details limitations of voxelization: "information loss... unable to accurately capture fine geometric micro-features."
- **Break condition:** If a manufacturability constraint depends on global topology or surface curvature not reducible to local parameters (e.g., complex freeform accessibility), the parametric representation fails, requiring geometric deep learning.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Tolerances are seamlessly integrated as explicit input dimensions, enabling tolerance-aware assessment without modifying the core architecture.
- **Mechanism:** Upper/lower tolerance bounds (e.g., `H1_UT`, `H1_LT`) are appended as separate numerical features to the design parameter vector. The KAN learns how tolerance ranges shift manufacturability boundaries through its spline functions, capturing interactions like how a loose hole position tolerance may violate minimum wall thickness.
- **Core assumption:** Tolerance effects on manufacturability are monotonic or smoothly varying within the sampled ranges and can be modeled without stochastic tolerance analysis.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "seamlessly integrating dimensional tolerances without CAD processing."
  - [section 3.3] Tables 2–4 list tolerance parameters (e.g., `H1_UT`, `H1_LT`) as explicit inputs; dataset generation includes "dimensional tolerances... across all relevant geometric parameters."
- **Break condition:** If tolerances exhibit complex non-monotonic interactions (e.g., statistical stack-up effects) or require probabilistic rather than deterministic bounds, the current feature representation becomes insufficient.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Interpretability emerges intrinsically from KAN’s spline-based functional composition, enabling parameter-level diagnosis without post-hoc explainers.
- **Mechanism:** Each network edge implements a learned univariate spline `ϕ(x)`. Visualizing `ϕ` across its domain shows how a parameter (e.g., pocket depth) transforms toward the output. SHAP values computed on this functional structure quantify each parameter’s marginal contribution, while the final hidden layer’s two-neuron projection forms a latent space where manufacturability boundaries are smooth and physically interpretable.
- **Core assumption:** The Kolmogorov–Arnold superposition principle holds approximately for the manufacturability function: it can be decomposed into sums of univariate functions with manageable interactions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "high interpretability via spline functions and SHAP analysis, pinpointing critical parameters."
  - [section 4.2] Equation (2) shows edge-wise univariate functions; [section 4.5] describes spline visualizations and SHAP.
  - [section 5.4] Latent space projections show structured separation; Figure 8 shows diverse splines reflecting complexity.
- **Break condition:** If the true manufacturability mapping requires high-frequency oscillations or discontinuities that splines cannot capture efficiently, interpretability degrades as spline complexity explodes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Kolmogorov–Arnold Representation Theorem**
  - **Why needed here:** Underpins KAN architecture; justifies that a multivariate function (manufacturability) can be expressed as sums of univariate functions, enabling parameter-level interpretability.
  - **Quick check question:** *If a function can be represented as a superposition of univariate functions, what does that imply about the necessity of dense weight matrices in standard neural networks?*
- **Concept: Design for Manufacturing (DFM) Rules**
  - **Why needed here:** The synthetic dataset’s labels are generated from DFM constraints (e.g., corner radius ≥ tool radius, depth/diameter ratios). Understanding these rules is essential to interpret why parameters like `P3` (pocket radius) have strong SHAP values.
  - **Quick check question:** *Why would a pocket with zero corner radius be non-manufacturable in milling, and what parameter adjustment does the model suggest?*
- **Concept: SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)**
  - **Why needed here:** Quantifies each parameter’s contribution to a prediction, translating spline-based functional relationships into actionable design feedback (e.g., "increasing hole depth *decreases* manufacturability").
  - **Quick check question:** *In a SHAP summary plot, what does a long red bar for "hole depth" indicate about its effect on manufacturability?*

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input vector (design parameters + tolerances) -> KAN layers (edges with spline functions) -> Output layer (sigmoid neuron)
- **Critical path:** Raw CAD design → Parametric extraction → Min-Max scaling → KAN forward pass → Probability ≥ 0.5? → If no: run SHAP on input → Identify top negative-contribution parameters → Modify CAD → repeat.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Grid size (G):** Higher `G` increases spline resolution but risks overfitting; paper found `G=3` optimal.
  - **Spline order (k):** `k=3` (cubic) balances smoothness and expressiveness; higher `k` may oscillate.
  - **Latent dimensionality:** Fixed 2 neurons for visualization; more neurons may improve accuracy but reduce interpretability.
  - **Tolerance encoding:** Treating upper/lower tolerances as independent features assumes symmetry; asymmetric tolerances require careful feature engineering.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Spline instability:** Wild oscillations in `ϕ(x)` plots indicate overfitting (high `G`/`k` with insufficient data).
  - **SHAP inconsistency:** If SHAP values flip sign across similar samples, the model hasn’t learned stable monotonic relationships (violates DFM principles).
  - **Latent space entanglement:** If UMAP/t-SNE clusters overlap but KAN’s latent space is structured, it validates KAN’s functional learning; if *both* are entangled, the problem may be inherently ambiguous.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Learning curve:** Train KAN on increasing data sizes (1k→100k) to find stabilization point (~30k samples for single-feature scenarios). Confirms data efficiency vs. voxel CNNs.
  2. **Benchmark validation:** Compare AUC/F1 against 14 models (MLP, XGBoost, etc.) on held-out test set. Expect KAN top-ranked; tree ensembles next best.
  3. **Case study iteration:** Input a deliberately non-manufacturable design (e.g., sharp pocket corner). Use SHAP to identify `corner_radius` as top negative contributor; increase it; re-evaluate until `p̂ ≥ 0.5`. Fabricate final design to validate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Extension to other manufacturing processes:** How can the framework be extended to processes beyond drilling and milling (e.g., turning, additive manufacturing) which have distinct DFM constraints and geometric characteristics?
- **Multi-objective evaluation:** How can cost and time objectives be incorporated to enable multi-objective manufacturability evaluation beyond a binary feasibility label?
- **Hybrid representations:** Can combining parametric features with geometric data (e.g., voxels, point clouds) improve accuracy for complex geometries while maintaining interpretability?
- **Scalability to industrial datasets:** How well does the methodology scale to high-dimensional industrial-scale designs (hundreds of parameters), and can interpretability be effectively maintained?

## Limitations
- **Generalization to complex geometries:** The parametric representation may fail for manufacturability constraints dependent on global topology or surface curvature not reducible to local parameters.
- **Tolerance modeling realism:** Tolerances are encoded as deterministic bounds; the method may not capture complex non-monotonic interactions or statistical stack-up effects.
- **Synthetic dataset bias:** Labels are derived from simplified DFM rules; real-world manufacturability involves machine dynamics, tool wear, and material behavior not modeled.

## Confidence
- **Performance superiority (AUC >0.94):** High. Supported by large-scale benchmark against 14 models on held-out synthetic test sets.
- **Tolerance integration without CAD:** Medium. Novel feature-engineering approach is shown, but lacks validation against probabilistic tolerance analysis or real toleranced parts.
- **Intrinsic interpretability via splines/SHAP:** Medium. Aligns with KAN theory and shows structured latent space, but depends on the assumed decomposability of the manufacturability function, which is not theoretically guaranteed.

## Next Checks
1. **Real-world geometric complexity test:** Apply the trained KAN to a held-out set of actual CAD models with complex freeform features, using geometric deep learning as a reference. Failure mode: sharp drop in AUC indicates parametric representation insufficiency.
2. **Stochastic tolerance stress test:** Re-label the synthetic dataset using Monte Carlo tolerance stack-up simulations. Compare KAN’s deterministic tolerance inputs against a model trained on statistical tolerance descriptors. Failure mode: significant performance gap reveals limitation in handling tolerance variation.
3. **Cross-manufacturing process validation:** Deploy the method in at least two additional industrial settings (e.g., sheet metal bending, additive manufacturing). Collect engineer feedback on actionability of SHAP-derived insights. Failure mode: inconsistent parameter importance or low adoption indicates poor transferability.
