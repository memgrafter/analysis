# Online Experiential Learning for Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.16856
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16856
- **Reference count**: 11
- **Primary result**: OEL yields consistent pass rate gains over iterations, reduces response length to ~70% of initial by iteration three, and preserves out-of-distribution IF-Eval accuracy (avoiding catastrophic forgetting seen with off-policy distillation).

## Executive Summary
Large language models are typically trained offline using static datasets or simulated environments, missing opportunities to learn from real-world deployment experiences. This paper introduces Online Experiential Learning (OEL), a reward‑free framework that enables continuous model improvement directly from user interaction trajectories. OEL iteratively extracts structured experiential knowledge from collected trajectories and consolidates it into the model’s parameters via on‑policy context distillation, thereby avoiding the train‑inference mismatch that leads to forgetting. Evaluated on text‑based games (Frozen Lake, Sokoban) with Qwen3 models, OEL demonstrates consistent pass‑rate gains across iterations, reduces response length by ~30 %, and preserves general‑purpose capabilities measured by IF‑Eval. The approach offers a promising direction for online, experience‑driven learning without human annotation or environment simulation.

## Method Summary
OEL operates in two iterative stages without requiring reward signals or server‑side environment access. In the extraction stage, the current policy model (πθ) acts as an extractor to process collected user interaction trajectories via accumulative prompting, producing a condensed set of transferable experiential knowledge items (structured or unstructured). This knowledge is accumulated across trajectories. In the consolidation stage, the model undergoes on‑policy context distillation: partial rollout prefixes from new trajectories are paired with sampled experiential knowledge, and the model learns to mimic a frozen teacher (the pre‑consolidation model conditioned on the same knowledge) on its own generated responses using reverse KL divergence. After consolidation, the updated model is redeployed, and the loop repeats. The on‑policy nature prevents catastrophic forgetting and eliminates the need to provide lengthy knowledge at inference time.

## Key Results
- Consistent pass‑rate improvements over multiple iterations on both Frozen Lake and Sokoban.
- Response length reduced to approximately 70 % of the initial length by the third iteration.
- Preservation of out‑of‑distribution IF‑Eval accuracy (no catastrophic forgetting), with extracted knowledge outperforming raw trajectories and on‑policy consistency being essential.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Extracting structured "experiential knowledge" from raw interaction trajectories is more effective for learning than using raw trajectories directly.
- **Mechanism:** An extractor model (initially the deployed policy) processes collected trajectories in an accumulative chain. It conditions on previously extracted knowledge and the new trajectory to generate concise, transferable insights (e.g., rules, strategies). This filters noise and abstracts general principles from specific interactions.
- **Core assumption:** Raw trajectories contain actionable knowledge but are noisy and instance‑specific; an LLM can act as a "knowledge extractor" to synthesize transferable insights when conditioned on prior accumulations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "transferable experiential knowledge is extracted and accumulated from interaction trajectories"
  - [section 3.1]: Details the recursive accumulation process `e_i = [e_{i-1}; e'_i]`
  - [section 4.6.1]: Table 1 shows structured knowledge yields 18.2% in‑context pass rate vs. 10.9% for raw trajectories on Sokoban.
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; related papers like "Experiential Reflective Learning" suggest reflection improves agents but don’t test extraction vs. raw data.
- **Break condition:** If the extractor model is too weak to generalize or if `L_max` truncation discards critical knowledge, accumulation may degrade or plateau prematurely.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** On‑policy context distillation consolidates experiential knowledge into model parameters without causing catastrophic forgetting.
- **Mechanism:** The policy model (`π_θ`) generates single‑turn responses from partial rollout prefixes (`x`). A frozen teacher (`π_teacher`, same model but conditioned on knowledge `e`) provides token‑level targets. The student minimizes reverse KL divergence `D_KL(π_θ || π_teacher)` on its own generated tokens. This on‑policy approach aligns training and inference distributions.
- **Core assumption:** Training on the student’s own distribution (on‑policy) mitigates the train‑inference mismatch that causes forgetting in off‑policy distillation, where teacher‑generated data differs from student’s deployment distribution.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "consolidated into model parameters using on‑policy context distillation"
  - [section 3.2]: Equation (2) defines the loss; emphasizes "single‑turn rollouts" and "without requiring the knowledge context at inference time."
  - [section 4.4]: Figure 6 shows on‑policy consolidation preserves IF‑Eval OOD accuracy while off‑policy distillation degrades it.
  - [corpus]: Weak; on‑policy distillation literature (e.g., MiniLLM) supports the mechanism but not in this exact experiential loop.
- **Break condition:** If the student’s policy drifts too far from the teacher’s (e.g., early training with high temperature), reverse KL may fail to provide stable gradients, leading to divergence or collapse.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Iterating extraction and consolidation creates a virtuous cycle where each round’s improved model collects higher‑quality trajectories, yielding richer knowledge and compounding gains.
- **Mechanism:** After consolidation, the updated model is redeployed. Its improved policy produces trajectories with more successful outcomes and fewer wasted steps. The extractor then processes these higher‑quality trajectories to produce more effective experiential knowledge, which fuels the next consolidation round.
- **Core assumption:** Model improvement leads to measurably better interaction data (higher success rate, more efficient steps), and the extractor can identify and compress this improvement into transferable knowledge.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "The two stages are iterated to form an online learning loop, where the improved model collects higher‑quality trajectories that yield richer experiential knowledge for subsequent rounds."
  - [section 3.3]: Explicitly describes the iterative loop and redeployment.
  - [section 4.2]: Figure 4 shows pass rate increasing over rounds (e.g., Frozen Lake: 40% → 50% over 2 rounds).
  - [corpus]: Weak; "Self‑Evolving GPT" and "FLEX" propose similar loops but lack ablations on round‑by‑round trajectory quality.
- **Break condition:** If performance saturates (Figure 4 shows accumulation saturation) or if trajectory quality fails to improve, the loop stagnates. Could also degrade if extractor overfits to recent trajectories and loses generality.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: On‑Policy vs. Off‑Policy Distillation**
  - *Why needed here:* Core to OEL’s consolidation stage. Understanding the distinction explains why OEL avoids catastrophic forgetting.
  - *Quick check question:* If you train a student on responses generated by a teacher model (not the student’s own), what distribution mismatch occurs and what forgetting risk does it create?
- **Concept: Context Distillation**
  - *Why needed here:* OEL is a form of context distillation where in‑context knowledge (`e`) is compressed into model weights. This removes the need to provide lengthy knowledge at inference.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the difference between forward KL and reverse KL in context distillation, and why does OEL use reverse KL?
- **Concept: Catastrophic Forgetting in Online Learning**
  - *Why needed here:* A key risk OEL mitigates. Without on‑policy training, new knowledge could overwrite general capabilities (measured by IF‑Eval).
  - *Quick check question:* Why might updating a model on new task‑specific data reduce its performance on unrelated tasks, and how does on‑policy learning help?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - *User‑side:* Environment `E`, deployed policy `π_θ`, trajectory collector `T`/`T'` (multi‑turn interactions).
  - *Server‑side:* Knowledge extractor (`π_extract`), knowledge store `C` (set of `e_k`), prefix constructor `D` (from `T'`), consolidation trainer (on‑policy context distillation loss).
- **Critical path:**
  1. Deploy `π_θ` → collect `T` and `T'`.
  2. Extract `C` from `T` using accumulative prompting.
  3. Build `D` from `T'` (partial prefixes).
  4. Train `π_θ` on `(x, e)` pairs via on‑policy distillation (sample `y~π_θ(x)`).
  5. Redeploy updated `π_θ`; repeat.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Knowledge format:* Structured (`- EXPERIENCE ITEM:`) vs. unstructured. Structured aids parsing but may restrict model’s expressiveness.
  - *On‑policy consistency:* Using same model as extractor/teacher vs. stronger model. Self‑knowledge (Table 2) works better for small models; stronger teacher may provide better targets but risks distribution mismatch.
  - *Accumulation step `n` and `L_max`:* More trajectories/longer context yields more knowledge but risks truncation and context overload for small models.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Extraction produces repetitive or low‑specificity knowledge (e.g., generic advice).
  - Consolidation loss diverges or plateaus without pass rate gain.
  - IF‑Eval accuracy drops >2% between rounds (catastrophic forgetting).
  - Response length does not decrease (knowledge not internalized).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline extraction utility:** Compare in‑context performance with no knowledge, raw trajectories, and extracted knowledge (replicate Table 1). Validates extraction stage’s value.
  2. **On‑policy necessity:** Run consolidation with on‑policy (student‑generated) vs. off‑policy (teacher‑generated) rollouts, tracking in‑distribution pass rate and IF‑Eval OOD accuracy (replicate Figure 6). Confirms forgetting mitigation.
  3. **Iteration loop:** Execute 3 full OEL rounds on one model/task, plotting pass rate and normalized response length per round (replicate Figure 4 & 5). Confirms compounding improvements and efficiency gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does OEL’s performance and knowledge quality scale when applied to complex, open-ended real‑world tasks (e.g., multi‑turn dialogue, coding, or web navigation) beyond the deterministic, grid‑based game environments used in the paper?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper explicitly states evaluation is conducted on "two text‑based game environments, Frozen Lake and Sokoban" and notes these simulate scenarios where "the model receives minimal prior knowledge about a new environment." The framework’s design targets "real‑world deployment" and "open‑world learning from text feedback."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper’s empirical validation is confined to highly constrained, deterministic games with clear success/failure signals. It is an unstated but critical assumption that the mechanisms (knowledge extraction from trajectories, on‑policy distillation) will hold in environments with ambiguous feedback, longer‑horizon dependencies, or subjective outcomes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting multi‑iteration OEL experiments on established, challenging benchmarks for complex language tasks (e.g., agent‑based web shopping, multi‑step code generation, or long‑form creative writing). Key metrics would include not just task success rates but also the coherence, safety, and generalizability of the extracted experiential knowledge and the stability of the online learning loop over many iterations.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the optimal relationship and potential for co‑evolution between the knowledge extractor model (π_extract) and the policy model (π_θ) in the OEL loop, especially concerning model capacity and knowledge fidelity?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper sets π_extract = π_θ by default and states that for the smallest model (Qwen3‑1.7B), they omit including previous accumulated knowledge in the extraction context due to capacity limits. Table 2 shows that knowledge extracted from a model’s own trajectories ("Self") outperforms knowledge from a larger model, suggesting on‑policy consistency is key.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper establishes that self‑knowledge is beneficial but does not explore whether the extractor should always be the current policy model. It leaves open questions about scenarios where a specialized, larger, or differently‑principled extractor might produce higher‑quality knowledge that the policy model can still internalize, or how to handle the extractor’s own limitations (e.g., a small model failing to extract meaningful insights from its mediocre trajectories).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic ablation studies varying the extractor’s size, architecture, and prompt tuning relative to the fixed policy model. Measuring the quality of extracted knowledge (e.g., via human evaluation or proxy metrics of "transferability") and the subsequent learning gains of the policy model when consolidating knowledge from different extractors. Analysis of failure cases where a mismatched extractor (either too weak or too strong) harms the loop.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What are the theoretical limits and failure modes of the accumulative prompting mechanism for knowledge extraction, particularly regarding noise accumulation, context window saturation, and the degradation of knowledge quality over multiple extraction rounds?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The methodology describes an "accumulative" process where e_i = [e_{i-1}; e'_i]. Figure 4 shows "saturation" in the in‑context learning pass rate as experiential knowledge accumulates. The authors note that "as the experiential knowledge accumulates, the context window becomes increasingly occupied, limiting the model’s capacity to absorb and leverage additional knowledge through in‑context learning alone."
- **Why unresolved:** While the paper demonstrates that subsequent consolidation distills knowledge into weights (circumventing the context limit), the fundamental properties of the *extraction process* itself are not analyzed. It is unclear how much "noise" or redundant information is accumulated, how the quality of new knowledge e'_i degrades as the context e_{i-1} grows, and whether there is a point where the extractor becomes ineffective due to overwhelming irrelevant history.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Quantitative analysis of the extracted knowledge corpus across accumulation steps (e.g., measuring redundancy, specificity vs. generality, or consistency with known ground‑truth rules in the games). Ablating the use of full previous history versus a fixed‑size sliding window or a learned memory retrieval mechanism during extraction. Tracking the divergence between the teacher’s (knowledge‑augmented) performance and the student’s post‑consolidation performance as a function of accumulation depth.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent does OEL mitigate or exacerbate "distribution shift" as the policy model’s own data distribution evolves during the online loop, and how does this interact with the preservation of out‑of‑distribution (OOD) capabilities?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper shows OEL (on‑policy distillation) better preserves IF‑Eval accuracy (an OOD metric) compared to off‑policy distillation in Figure 6. It states this "confirms that the on‑policy consolidation in OEL effectively internalizes experiential knowledge without sacrificing general capabilities."
- **Why unresolved:** The demonstration is on a single OOD benchmark (IF‑Eval) and focused on avoiding catastrophic forgetting relative to an *initial* model. The paper does not investigate whether the *continuously shifting* policy distribution in OEL could lead to a gradual, subtle form of forgetting or misalignment on *new* OOD tasks not seen during the online loop. It also does not analyze if the type of experiential knowledge extracted (e.g., highly game‑specific heuristics) might encourage overfitting to the online environment’s distribution at the expense of broader skills.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Running OEL for many more iterations (beyond 2‑3 rounds) and evaluating on a wide battery of diverse, held‑out benchmarks (including other text games, general NLP tasks, and safety evaluations) after each round. Monitoring the trajectory of OOD performance to detect slow degradation not captured in short runs. Comparing the nature of the internalized knowledge (via probing or analysis of model representations) to see if it becomes overly specialized to the patterns in the user‑side environment.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can the OEL framework be extended or modified to learn from environments that provide only sparse, delayed, or highly ambiguous textual feedback, and what modifications to the knowledge extraction or consolidation stages would be necessary?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] A core premise is that OEL works with "textual environment feedback" and is "reward‑free." The environments used provide immediate, concrete, and interpretable state transitions (e.g., "you fell in a hole," "you pushed the box").
- **Why unresolved:** Many real‑world deployment scenarios involve feedback that is delayed (e.g., user satisfaction after a long conversation), ambiguous (e.g., "that was okay"), or contradictory. The current extraction prompt (Figure 10) assumes clear, multi‑turn interaction histories with discernible outcomes. It is an open question whether the "accumulative prompting" can distill useful, transferable knowledge from such noisy, sparse signals, or if the entire paradigm needs a different mechanism for credit assignment or insight condensation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Designing synthetic or real‑world environments where textual feedback is degraded (e.g., only "good"/"bad" at the end, or with paraphrased/noisy descriptions of state changes). Applying OEL as‑is and measuring failure. Then, proposing and testing modifications, such as: incorporating an internal "critic" model during extraction to hypothesize long‑term outcomes, using contrastive learning to distinguish successful/failing trajectory segments, or implementing a form of recursive summarization that explicitly tracks uncertainty.

## Limitations
- Exact TextArena environment configuration (grid layouts, object placements) is not fully specified, hindering precise replication of game dynamics.
- Full prompt templates for integrating accumulated knowledge with the initial environment state are incomplete, requiring inference about concatenation logic.
- Criteria for selecting partial rollout prefixes during consolidation (e.g., whether to use all turns or only successes) are unspecified.
