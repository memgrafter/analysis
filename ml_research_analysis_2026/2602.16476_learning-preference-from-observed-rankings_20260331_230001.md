# Learning Preference from Observed Rankings
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16476
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16476
- Reference count: 8
- Primary result: The IPW-corrected low-rank preference model improves out-of-sample recommendation Precision@K and Recall@K, especially for new items, and identifies high-value customer segments via composition lift.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles exposure bias in learning user preferences from incomplete rankings, where observed pairwise comparisons are selected because both items appear in a user’s consideration set. To model preferences, the authors propose a random utility framework that combines interpretable product attributes (region, grape style, price tier), item fixed effects, and a low-rank user-item factor structure. Pairwise choices follow a logistic model derived from Type‑I extreme value errors. Selection bias is addressed by inverse‑probability weighting (IPW): item‑level observability propensities are estimated via a logistic model and combined as a product to form pairwise weights. Preference parameters are estimated by maximizing a ridge‑regularized IPW log‑likelihood using stochastic gradient descent with inverse‑probability resampling, which embeds the correction into the sampling process for scalability. The method is applied to transaction data from an online wine retailer, demonstrating improved recommendation performance—particularly for previously unconsumed items—over a popularity baseline. The model also uncovers nuanced preference heterogeneity through composition‑lift segmentation, highlighting high‑value customer groups such as young affluent males interested in Bordeaux wines.

## Method Summary
The model learns individual preferences from sparse pairwise comparisons derived from purchase histories. Utility for user *i* and item *j* is decomposed as \(u_{ij}=x_j^\top\beta_i + \alpha_j + \lambda_i^\top f_j + \varepsilon_{ij}\), where \(x_j\) are observed product attributes, \(\beta_i\) are user‑specific coefficients, \(\alpha_j\) are item fixed effects, \(\lambda_i\) and \(f_j\) are low‑dimensional latent factors capturing unobserved heterogeneity, and \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) follows a Type‑I extreme value distribution. The probability that a pairwise comparison \((j,j')\) is observed depends on both items entering the user’s consideration set. Under the assumption that pair observability factorizes into independent item propensities, \(\pi_{jj'}=\pi_j\pi_{j'}\), each \(\pi_j\) is estimated by fitting a logistic model to aggregate counts of observed pairs. Given estimated propensities \(\hat{\pi}_{jj'}\), the parameters (including \(\beta_i,\alpha_j,\lambda_i,f_j\)) are estimated by maximizing the ridge‑regularized IPW log‑likelihood \(\sum_{(i,j,j')\in D} \frac{1}{\hat{\pi}_{jj'}} \log\sigma(u_{ij}-u_{ij'}) - \kappa\|\theta\|^2\). To scale to large datasets, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is used with inverse‑probability resampling: at each iteration a triple \((i,j,j')\) is sampled with probability proportional to \(\hat{\pi}_{jj'}^{-1}\), so the gradient of the unweighted log‑likelihood approximates the IPW‑weighted gradient. Hyperparameters such as latent factor dimension, ridge penalty, and learning rate are chosen via validation.

## Key Results
- The model significantly out‑performs a popularity‑based benchmark in Precision@K and Recall@K for item recommendation; the largest relative gains occur for previously unconsumed (new) items, indicating successful generalization.
- Composition‑lift segmentation reveals economically interpretable high‑value customer groups; for example, young male customers with high income exhibit a strong preference for Bordeaux wines.
- The IPW correction effectively mitigates exposure bias, as evidenced by improved predictive accuracy compared to an unweighted version of the model.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Attribute‑based extrapolation and information sharing
- **Claim:** Decomposing utility into interpretable product attributes and low‑rank latent factors enables generalization to unseen items and sharing of preference information across users and items.
- **Mechanism:** The utility specification \(u_{ij}=x_j^\top\beta_i+\alpha_j+\lambda_i^\top f_j+\varepsilon_{ij}\) separates systematic taste over observed attributes (\(x_j^\top\beta_i,\alpha_j\)) from residual similarity captured by user‑item factors (\(\lambda_i,f_j\)). When a user ranks only a subset of items, attributes allow inference about unseen items with similar features, while latent factors transfer information across users with similar patterns and across items co‑ranked by similar users.
- **Core assumption:** The low‑rank structure correctly captures the systematic, shared dimensions of preference heterogeneity.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states that modeling latent utility as the sum of interpretable attributes, item fixed effects, and low‑rank factors “enables both interpretability and information sharing.” Section 2.2 explains that latent factors capture systematic dependence beyond observed attributes and allow sharing information across individuals and items.
- **Break condition:** If the low‑rank assumption is violated (e.g., true heterogeneity is high‑rank) or factors overfit sparse data, the information‑sharing mechanism may propagate noise rather than signal, degrading inference for unobserved pairs.

### Mechanism 2: Inverse‑probability weighting for exposure bias correction
- **Claim:** Modeling pair observability as the product of item‑level propensities and reweighting observed comparisons by the inverse of that product corrects the bias induced by non‑random exposure.
- **Mechanism:** For each item *j*, a logistic propensity \(\pi_j=\sigma(x_j^\top\psi)\) estimates the marginal probability it enters a consideration set. The pair observability is assumed to factorize: \(\pi_{jj'}=\pi_j\pi_{j'}\). The IPW weight \(1/\hat{\pi}_{jj'}\) is applied to the log‑likelihood contribution of each observed comparison, upweighting pairs involving rarely seen items to recover unbiased preference parameters under selection on observables.
- **Core assumption:** Conditional on item‑side observables \(x_j\), the pair‑level observability probability factorizes into independent marginals; there is no unobserved user‑level selection bias that creates dependence between items beyond their marginals.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 2.3 explicitly present the factorization assumption and the IPW estimation procedure.
- **Break condition:** If the factorization fails (e.g., joint exposure depends on item interactions or user‑specific consideration sets) or if the propensity model is misspecified (e.g., \(x_j\) omits key visibility drivers), the IPW weights become inconsistent and preference estimates remain biased.

### Mechanism 3: SGD with inverse‑probability resampling for scalable optimization
- **Claim:** Inverse‑probability resampling within SGD enables efficient optimization of the IPW objective while maintaining unbiased gradient estimates.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of uniformly sampling observed comparisons, the algorithm draws a triple \((i,j,j')\) with probability proportional to \(\hat{\pi}_{jj'}^{-1}\) using alias tables. This embeds the inverse‑probability correction into the data stream, so the gradient computed on the unweighted log‑likelihood of the resampled triple is already an unbiased estimate of the IPW‑weighted gradient, with the constant weight absorbed into the learning rate.
- **Core assumption:** The SGD updates based on resampled batches converge to the optimum of the full IPW objective; the two‑stage sampling scheme (first pick an item pair according to resampling distribution, then uniformly pick a user with that pair) correctly reproduces the required distribution.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract describes the resampling scheme; Section 2.4 gives the algorithmic details.
- **Break condition:** If IPW weights have high variance (e.g., \(\hat{\pi}_j\) is very small for niche items), the resampling distribution becomes skewed, potentially causing high variance in gradient estimates or training instability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Random utility model with Type‑I extreme value errors**  
  *Why needed?* Converts discrete pairwise rankings into logistic choice probabilities, providing a likelihood‑based foundation for estimating latent preferences.  
  *Quick check:* Why does assuming i.i.d. Type‑I extreme value errors lead to a logistic form for pairwise comparison probabilities?

- **Inverse probability weighting (IPW) for selection bias**  
  *Why needed?* Corrects for the fact that observed comparisons are selected into the dataset based on item visibility/consideration, which would otherwise bias preference estimates toward popular items.  
  *Quick check:* What is the identifying assumption for IPW to recover unbiased preference parameters from selected comparisons?

- **Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with importance sampling**  
  *Why needed?* The number of pairwise comparisons is huge; SGD with inverse‑probability resampling makes optimization computationally feasible while accounting for IPW weights.  
  *Quick check:* How does inverse‑probability resampling avoid computing explicit IPW weights in every SGD step?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input (incomplete ranking matrix) → Utility model (attributes + fixed effects + low‑rank factors) → Propensity model (item‑level logistic regression) → IPW objective (ridge‑regularized weighted log‑likelihood) → Optimizer (SGD with inverse‑probability resampling)
- **Critical path:** Propensity estimation → Compute IPW weights → Inverse‑probability resampling for SGD → Preference parameter optimization
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Parsimony vs. accuracy in propensity model:* Using only item covariates keeps \(\psi\) low‑dimensional and avoids the curse of dimensionality but may omit user‑specific exposure drivers.
  - *Factorization of \(\pi_{jj'}\):* Assumes independence of item observability; relaxing this would require estimating pairwise propensities, which is infeasible for large numbers of items.
  - *Low‑rank factor dimension \(r\):* Smaller \(r\) improves regularization and speed but may underfit complex preference heterogeneity.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Propensity model poorly calibrated (predictions near 0/1) → extreme IPW weights → SGD instability.
  - SGD loss oscillating or diverging → learning rate too high or resampling distribution miscomputed.
  - Out‑of‑sample recommendation gains vanish for new items → latent factors not effectively transferring information from attributes to unseen products.
- **First experiments:**
  1. **Propensity calibration check:** Plot observed proportion of users seeing each item vs. predicted \(\hat{\pi}_j\); the relationship should be monotonic.
  2. **Resampling distribution sanity:** Sample many pairs via the proposed resampling and verify the empirical weight distribution matches the expected \(1/\hat{\pi}_{jj'}\).
  3. **Ablation on new‑item prediction:** Compare full model (attributes+factors+IPW) against attribute‑only+IPW and factor‑only+IPW on Precision@K for items not in the training set.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does the proposed IPW‑corrected estimator compare to doubly robust or machine‑learning‑based debiasing methods (e.g., targeted maximum likelihood estimation) in terms of bias, variance, and robustness to propensity model misspecification?
2. To what extent does the model's factorization assumption for pair observability (\(\pi_{jj'}=\pi_j\pi_{j'}\)) hold in real‑world digital marketplaces where exposure may depend on joint item characteristics (e.g., co‑occurrence in a recommendation carousel or search results page)?
3. Can the observed preference heterogeneity patterns (e.g., bimodal price‑tier coefficients, distinct regional preference correlations) be explained by latent consumer segments with discrete structural characteristics (e.g., “value‑seekers” vs. “collectors”), and does such a finite‑mixture extension improve predictive performance or managerial interpretability?
4. How sensitive are the segmentation results (e.g., the identification of “young affluent males for Bordeaux”) to the chosen aggregation level of items (region–grape–price tier) and the construction of rankings from purchase/non‑purchase?
5. Does the proposed SGD algorithm with inverse‑probability resampling provide meaningful computational advantages over standard SGD with uniform minibatching, particularly in very large‑scale settings with millions of users/items and extreme sparsity?

## Limitations
- Hyperparameters (factor dimension *r*, ridge penalty \(\kappa\), learning rate \(\eta\)) are unreported and must be chosen via validation, making exact replication challenging.
- The covariates used to estimate item‑level observability propensities are unspecified; omitting relevant visibility drivers could bias the IPW weights and thus the preference estimates.
- The factorization assumption for pair observability (\(\pi_{jj'}=\pi_j\pi_{j'}\)) is not empirically validated; user‑level selection dependence may violate the assumption and leave residual bias.

## Confidence
- Core IPW correction (addresses exposure bias): Medium
- Utility decomposition (attributes + low‑rank factors) for extrapolation and information sharing: Medium
- SGD scalability via inverse‑probability resampling: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Propensity calibration check:** After propensity estimation, bin items by predicted \(\hat{\pi}_j\) and plot observed pairwise observability rates against mean predicted \(\hat{\pi}_{jj'}\) per bin. Non‑monotonicity indicates misspecification.
2. **IPW weight distribution sanity:** Compute the distribution of weights \(w_{jj'} = 1/\hat{\pi}_{jj'}\). If extreme tails exist (e.g., 99th percentile >> median), consider clipping or stronger regularization to stabilize SGD.
3. **Ablation on new‑item prediction:** Train three models—(a) full (attributes+factors+IPW), (b) attributes‑only+IPW, (c) factors‑only+IPW—and compare Precision@K/Recall@K on held‑out new items. Disproportionate gains from (a) confirm the synergy of mechanisms.
