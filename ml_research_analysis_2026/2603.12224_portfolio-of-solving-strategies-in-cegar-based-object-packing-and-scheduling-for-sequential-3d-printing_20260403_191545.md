# Portfolio of Solving Strategies in CEGAR-based Object Packing and Scheduling for Sequential 3D Printing

## Quick Facts

- arXiv ID: 2603.12224  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12224  
- Reference count: 24  
- Primary result: Portfolio-CEGAR-SEQ reduces printing plates (e.g., 7→6 for 30 objects) with acceptable runtime increase on multi-core CPUs.  

## Executive Summary

This paper addresses the NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem of sequential 3D printing, where objects must be arranged on rectangular printing plates in a way that avoids collisions between the extruder and previously printed objects, while minimizing the number of plates used. The core contribution is an extension of the CEGAR-SEQ algorithm—a counterexample-guided abstraction refinement approach that uses an SMT solver to handle geometric constraints—by introducing a portfolio of object arrangement tactics (e.g., centering, corner placement) and ordering heuristics (e.g., sorting by height). The portfolio runs up to 20 composite strategies in parallel, leveraging modern multi-core CPUs. Empirical evaluation on a benchmark of 34 Prusa MK3S printer parts shows that the combined portfolio reduced the number of required plates from 7 to 6 when scheduling 30 objects, with only a modest runtime overhead (up to a factor of 2 for 20 strategies). The results demonstrate that high-level portfolio parallelism can effectively improve solution quality for complex packing problems.  

## Method Summary

The SEQ-PACK+S problem involves arranging a set of 3D objects (e.g., cuboids or STL models) on a sequence of identical rectangular plates, ensuring that during printing the extruder never collides with any already printed object. CEGAR-SEQ formulates this as a linear arithmetic SMT problem: constraints enforce that each object lies within the plate (PiP), that printed objects do not overlap (via point-cloud (PoP) and line-intersection (LnI) constraints), and that the extruder can travel safely between objects. The algorithm employs a CEGAR loop: it starts with an abstract formula containing only coarse PoP constraints, which are cheap to check. When the solver (Z3) finds a candidate placement that satisfies the abstract model but violates a finer-grained constraint (detected by checking LnI), the specific LnI constraint is added and solving restarts. This incremental refinement avoids the combinatorial explosion of encoding all LnI constraints upfront. To minimize the number of plates, CEGAR-SEQ performs a binary search on a scaling factor σ that uniformly enlarges the plate; the smallest σ for which a feasible schedule exists yields the minimal plate count.

The portfolio extension constructs a Cartesian product of five placement tactics (e.g., placing new objects at the plate center, or towards the minimum/maximum X/Y corners) and four ordering heuristics (e.g., sorting objects by height, by input order, etc.). This yields up to 20 composite strategies. The algorithm Portfolio-CEGAR-SEQ launches a separate CEGAR-SEQ instance for each composite strategy in parallel. After all instances terminate (within a shared timeout), the schedule with the fewest plates is selected. To guarantee termination on large batches, the method operates in a sub-optimal mode where each plate is limited to at most k objects (typically k=4), trading optimality for scalability.  

## Key Results

- Portfolio-CEGAR-SEQ consistently uses fewer printing plates than the original single-strategy CEGAR-SEQ.  
- On a benchmark of 34 Prusa MK3S printer parts, the combined portfolio reduced the plate count from 7 to 6 for 30 objects.  
- Runtime increased acceptably (up to a factor of ~2 with 20 parallel strategies), confirming the portfolio approach leverages multi-core CPUs effectively to find better solutions.  

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Parallel Strategy Portfolio

The portfolio’s parallel execution of multiple CEGAR-SEQ instances, each configured with a distinct combination of placement tactic and ordering heuristic, improves solution quality by exploring a broader search space within the same wall-clock time budget. Each instance independently attempts to pack the objects using its assigned strategy; the best solution across all instances is then selected. The underlying assumption is that no single strategy dominates across all problem instances—different object sets or shapes benefit from different spatial biases and processing orders. Evidence for this comes from the paper’s experiments showing that combining all 20 strategies (5 tactics × 4 orderings) yields lower plate counts than any single strategy or than portfolios that only vary one dimension (e.g., tactics-only). The benefit plateaus when all instances converge to similar solutions, indicating diminishing returns from further diversification.

### Mechanism 2: Orthogonal Dimensions of Tactic and Ordering

The portfolio separates two policy dimensions—placement tactic (where to put a new object on the plate) and object ordering (which object to consider next)—whose effects are largely orthogonal and can synergize. Tactic influences spatial cluster growth (e.g., corner placement may pack more tightly for certain shapes), while ordering determines the composition of groups placed early. Their interaction changes the feasibility landscape for subsequent objects. Experimental data (Figure 5 and 6 in the paper) show that portfolios combining both dimensions outperform those that only vary one dimension, particularly in terms of average objects per plate. This orthogonality suggests that diversifying along both axes is more effective than focusing on a single axis.

### Mechanism 3: CEGAR Efficiency Enables Viable Instances

The CEGAR-style refinement loop within each solver instance is essential for keeping individual strategy runs tractable. By starting with a simplified abstract model (only bounding-box non-overlap) and adding precise edge-intersection constraints only when counterexamples arise, the algorithm avoids the initial combinatorial explosion of full geometric constraints. This incremental approach allows each CEGAR-SEQ instance to solve reasonably hard subproblems within the timeout. The paper’s runtime comparisons (e.g., Figure 4) show that even the baseline “Center” strategy remains competitive, indicating that the CEGAR core is efficient on its own. The portfolio thus distributes the outer strategy selection across cores while relying on CEGAR to keep each instance’s solving time manageable.  

## Foundational Learning

### Combinatorial Optimization & NP-Hardness
- **Why needed:** The SEQ-PACK+S problem is NP-hard, which justifies the use of heuristic and portfolio approaches rather than attempting exact single-shot solutions that would not scale.  
- **Quick check:** Why is arranging 3D objects for sequential printing considered harder than standard simultaneous 3D printing? (Answer: Sequential printing introduces a temporal dimension; objects must avoid collisions with all previously printed objects, adding scheduling constraints beyond spatial packing.)

### Counterexample Guided Abstraction Refinement (CEGAR)
- **Why needed:** CEGAR is the core efficiency technique in CEGAR-SEQ; it manages geometric constraints by starting with an abstract, coarse model and incrementally refining it only when necessary, thus avoiding the cost of encoding all detailed constraints upfront.  
- **Quick check:** What is the trade-off in CEGAR between initial model simplification and the cost of iterative refinement? (Answer: Simplification speeds up the initial solve but may require many refinement iterations if the abstract model is too coarse, potentially increasing total runtime.)

### Portfolio Approaches in Search/Solving
- **Why needed:** The paper’s central innovation is applying a portfolio of complementary heuristics (tactics + orderings) in parallel, assuming that different heuristics excel on different subsets of problem instances. This high-level diversification leverages multi-core systems to improve solution quality without altering the underlying solver.  
- **Quick check:** How does a high-level portfolio (different algorithms/heuristics) differ from low-level parallel search (e.g., parallel depth-first search) in terms of diversification? (Answer: Portfolio diversifies at the algorithmic level, exploring qualitatively different search spaces; low-level parallel search typically replicates the same algorithm with different branching decisions or random seeds, offering less structural diversity.)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Strategy Composer → Parallel Orchestrator → (Solver Instance (CEGAR-SEQ) → SMT Backend) (multiple in parallel) → Solution Selector

### Critical path
The outer critical path is the parallel loop over all composite strategies (Algorithm 2, lines 7-12), which waits for all solver instances to finish. Within each instance, the critical path consists of the binary search on plate scaling factor σ (Algorithm 1, lines 12-21) and, for each σ, the inner Solve-CEGAR-SEQ-Bounded routine that iteratively refines constraints via the SMT solver (lines 29-47). The overall runtime is dominated by the slowest instance, as the portfolio must complete all instances before selecting the best schedule.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Parallelism vs. Overhead:** Adding more strategies increases the chance of finding a better solution but linearly increases CPU usage and wall-clock time if the number of parallel threads exceeds available cores or if solver instances become memory-bound. The paper shows a runtime increase of up to a factor of 2 for 20 strategies.  
- **Sub-optimal vs. Optimal:** The experiments use a sub-optimal mode that limits each plate to at most k objects (k=4) to guarantee termination on larger batches. This trades off the potential for fewer plates (which might be achievable with global optimization) for scalable performance.  
- **Tactic Complexity:** More aggressive placement tactics (e.g., corner-biased) may yield higher packing density for some object sets but could increase fragmentation for others. The “Center” tactic remains a necessary baseline to ensure robustness.

### Failure signatures
- **Timeout per instance:** An individual CEGAR-SEQ instance fails to solve its assigned batch within the time limit, returning UNSAT for that plate. The portfolio must then rely on solutions from other instances.  
- **Uniform solutions:** All parallel instances return the same number of plates, indicating insufficient strategy diversity for the given object set; expanding the portfolio may be necessary.  
- **SMT solver bottleneck:** Z3 becomes the choke point; if a particular geometric configuration is hard for the solver, all parallel instances that encounter similar constraints may suffer slowdowns, reducing the portfolio’s effectiveness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Incorporating object rotations:** The current model assumes fixed orientations. Adding rotations introduces discrete decision variables (or continuous angles) that may blow up the search space. How can rotations be incorporated efficiently into the linear arithmetic model without prohibitive overhead?  
2. **Heuristic warm-start:** The algorithm could benefit from initializing the SMT solver with a heuristically found solution. What heuristic methods (e.g., simple packing heuristics, machine learning) can consistently provide beneficial starting points to prune the search space and improve convergence?  
3. **Scalability to larger instances:** Experiments were limited to at most 64 objects (sub-optimal) or ~30 objects (optimal). How does the portfolio approach scale in terms of plate count reduction and wall-clock time for industrial-scale instances with hundreds of objects? The portfolio leverages parallelism but does not address the fundamental exponential complexity; it is unclear whether the quality improvements persist on larger batches.  

## Limitations

- **Reproduction parameters:** Undocumented Z3 version, solver seeds, and exact grouping logic (k=4) may cause result drift when attempting to replicate the experiments.  
- **Benchmark scope:** Evaluation was limited to 34 Prusa printer parts and simple cuboids; complex geometries and non-rectangular plates remain untested, limiting generalizability.  
- **Scalability bounds:** Runtime overhead was measured only up to 20 parallel strategies; behavior on many-core systems or under memory constraints is unknown, and the approach may become infeasible for very large object sets.
