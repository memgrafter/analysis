# Lexical and Statistical Analysis of Bangla Newspaper and Literature: A Corpus-Driven Study on Diversity, Readability, and NLP Adaptation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06041
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06041
- **Reference count:** 37
- **Primary result:** The literary corpus (Vācaspati) exhibits significantly higher lexical richness, lower redundancy, and higher entropy than the newspaper corpus (IndicCorp), and models pretrained with any fraction of literary data outperform newspaper-only models on downstream tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the lack of comparative analysis between Bangla literary and newspaper corpora by investigating their lexical diversity, structural complexity, and impact on NLP tasks. Using the literature-only Vācaspati (102M words) and newspaper-only IndicCorp (844M words), it computes a suite of metrics—including type-token ratio, hapax legomena ratio, Zipf’s Law adherence, entropy, redundancy, and readability indices—and evaluates n-gram perplexity and downstream model performance. Results show that Vācaspati is intrinsically richer and more information-dense, while IndicCorp is more repetitive and entity-heavy. Crucially, incorporating even a portion of the literary corpus into pretraining consistently improves results on poem classification, sentiment analysis, spelling correction, natural language inference, and real-word merge detection.

## Method Summary
The study leverages two genre-pure Bangla corpora: Vācaspati (102M tokens, literature) and IndicCorp (844M tokens, newspaper). After consistent preprocessing—tokenization, normalization, sentence splitting, and non-Bangla script filtering—it calculates lexical diversity metrics (TTR at varying sample sizes, HLR, bigram diversity), distributional properties (Zipf’s Law slope via log-log regression, Shannon entropy and redundancy for n-grams), and readability scores (Flesch Reading Ease, Coleman-Liau Index). N-gram language models (uni/bi/trigram) are trained on subsampled data to compute perplexity. Downstream evaluation uses Electra-small models pretrained on different mixtures of the corpora and fine-tuned on tasks: poem classification, sentiment analysis, spelling error detection, natural language inference, and a lexicon-based real-word merge detection test.

## Key Results
- **Lexical richness:** At ≥1M tokens, Vācaspati’s TTR exceeds IndicCorp’s; its HLR is ~4.8× higher and bigram diversity ~76% higher.
- **Distributional properties:** Vācaspati adheres more closely to Zipf’s Law (slope −1.10 vs. −1.36), exhibits higher entropy, lower redundancy, and yields higher n-gram perplexity, indicating greater information density.
- **Downstream improvement:** Any literary fraction in pretraining matches or outperforms newspaper-only models across all tasks. Vācaspati-derived lexicons achieve 91.89% accuracy in real-word merge detection versus 78.87% for IndicCorp.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Distinct Stylistic and Functional Pressures Shape Corpus Statistics
**Claim:** The lexical and structural differences between literary (Vācaspati) and newspaper (IndicCorp) corpora arise from their distinct purposes and conventions.  
**Mechanism:** Newspapers prioritize topical relevance, repetition for clarity, and frequent named entities to efficiently convey time-sensitive information. Literature emphasizes expressive variety, morphological richness, and syntactic complexity for aesthetic and narrative goals.  
**Core assumption:** Genre conventions systematically influence word choice, sentence structure, and vocabulary distribution.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- The abstract states the literary corpus shows higher lexical richness and structural variation, while the newspaper uses limited vocabulary.  
- Section 3.2.1 reports IndicCorp has ~1.21× more proper nouns, contributing to its raw type count despite lower overall diversity.  
- The corpora are genre-pure, minimizing internal contamination.

### Mechanism 2: Information Density and Unpredictability
**Claim:** Higher entropy and lower redundancy in Vācaspati indicate it is a more information-rich source for language modeling.  
**Mechanism:** The greater unpredictability of word sequences (higher entropy) and lower repetition rate (lower redundancy) in literary text make the modeling problem harder and more compressive; n-gram models thus exhibit higher perplexity.  
**Core assumption:** Entropy and redundancy computed over n-grams are valid proxies for information density and sequential unpredictability.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Tables 7, 8, 9 show Vācaspati has consistently higher entropy and significantly lower redundancy across unigram, bigram, trigram, even when controlling for sentence count.  
- Section 6: n-gram models trained on Vācaspati yield systematically higher perplexity than those on IndicCorp, a pattern that holds for English as well, suggesting a cross-linguistic genre effect.

### Mechanism 3: Exposure to the Full Linguistic Spectrum Improves Generalization
**Claim:** Including literary data in pretraining enriches the model’s exposure to morphological and syntactic variety, boosting downstream performance.  
**Mechanism:** Models trained only on newspaper data learn representations biased toward a restricted, repetitive, entity-heavy distribution. Injecting literary data exposes them to rarer word forms, complex constructions, and semantic nuance, improving generalization on tasks requiring diverse language understanding and morphological ambiguity handling.  
**Core assumption:** A performance bottleneck in downstream tasks is insufficient exposure to the full range of linguistic variation, which literary corpora provide.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Table 15: For all downstream tasks (spell check, sentiment, NLI, poem classification), models trained with any fraction of Vācaspati match or exceed the newspaper-only model.  
- Section 8.1: Vācaspati lexicons achieve 91.89% accuracy in real-word merge detection vs. 78.87% for IndicCorp, directly linking lexical richness to a morphological task.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Type-Token Ratio (TTR) & Heaps' Law**  
  *Why needed here:* TTR measures lexical diversity; Heaps' Law models vocabulary growth with corpus size. The paper’s core finding—that a smaller literary corpus can outperform a larger newspaper corpus in unique words—relies on TTR curves and Heaps' Law.  
  *Quick check:* Given two corpora, one with a steeper Heaps' Law curve (higher β), what does that imply about its vocabulary growth as you sample more text?

- **Concept: Hapax Legomena Ratio (HLR)**  
  *Why needed here:* HLR (words occurring exactly once) measures the “long tail” of vocabulary. A higher HLR in Vācaspati signals many rare words, a key aspect of lexical richness beyond unique word count.  
  *Quick check:* Would you expect a corpus with high HLR to have high or low redundancy? Relate this to the entropy/redundancy results.

- **Concept: Zipf’s Law**  
  *Why needed here:* Zipf’s Law describes the inverse rank-frequency relationship. The paper uses the slope of the log-log rank-frequency plot to quantify distributional regularity; a slope near −1 indicates a balanced vocabulary typical of literary texts.  
  *Quick check:* If a corpus has a Zipf slope of −1.2 versus another’s −1.5, which has a steeper drop for lower-ranked words? What does that imply about its vocabulary distribution?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Corpus Ingest & Normalization -> Lexical Statistics Engine -> Distributional Analysis Module -> (Parallel: n-gram Model Trainer & Readability Calculator) -> Downstream Task Suite  
- **Critical path:** Data Ingest → Lexical Statistics Engine → Distributional Analysis Module → (Parallel: n-gram Model Trainer & Readability Calculator) → Downstream Task Suite. This path validates the hypothesis that literary corpus properties explain downstream gains.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Corpus Size vs. Genre Purity:** Size mismatch (102M vs. 844M tokens) is mitigated via robust subsampling (fixed seeds, non-overlapping chunks).  
  - **Metric Selection for Bangla:** Standard formulas require Bangla-specific syllable segmentation rules.  
  - **Downstream Control:** Data mixing pipelines must be deterministic to create exact fractions of literary data.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Lexical analysis: TTR curves for Vācaspati falling below IndicCorp at all chunk sizes; HLR for Vācaspati not significantly higher.  
  - Zipf’s Law: Vācaspati slope (−1.10) being less ideal than IndicCorp’s (−1.36).  
  - Downstream: Pure IndicCorp models outperforming mixed models on all tasks, especially creative ones.  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate core lexical comparison: subsampled TTR and HLR (Table 3,4); verify Vācaspati TTR > IndicCorp at ≥1M tokens and HLR higher at all fractions.  
  2. Validate Zipf’s Law & entropy/redundancy: compute slopes, bigram entropy and redundancy (Table 7); confirm Vācaspati slope nearer −1.0, higher entropy, lower redundancy.  
  3. Downstream merge detection: build vocabularies from each corpus; run greedy bipartition; confirm Vācaspati lexicon > Merged > IndicCorp accuracy (Table 14).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the higher lexical richness, lower redundancy, and higher perplexity of literary corpora generalize to other Indic languages beyond Bangla? The paper proposes investigating this in future work.
2. What specific linguistic or stylistic features cause Bangla literary texts to exhibit higher entropy and lower redundancy? The paper reports the statistics but does not explain the root causes.
3. Why do simple n-gram models yield higher perplexity on Bangla texts than on English texts, and how does this relate to Bangla’s morphological and orthographic characteristics? The paper only speculates that English modeling techniques may not transfer.
4. What is the optimal mixture ratio of literary to newspaper data in pretraining Bangla language models to maximize downstream performance? The study only tested two ratios (1/3 and 1/2) and did not systematically vary the fraction.

## Limitations
- **Confound from named entities and corpus size:** IndicCorp’s higher raw type count may reflect more proper nouns and larger volume, complicating isolation of genre-driven diversity.
- **Tokenization ambiguity:** Exact Bangla tokenization rules (conjunct handling, normalization) are unspecified, risking metric divergence.
- **Downstream reproducibility:** Electra pretraining hyperparameters (batch size, steps, masking ratio) are unreported, limiting confidence in claimed gains.

## Confidence
- **Lexical/structural differences** (TTR, HLR, Zipf, entropy): **Medium-High**  
- **Genre-as-mechanism explanation**: **Medium**  
- **Downstream NLP improvement**: **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Proper noun masking ablation:** Mask all proper nouns in both corpora (via NER), then recompute TTR/HLR at matched sample sizes. If Vācaspati’s advantage persists, it strengthens the genre claim.
2. **Standardized perplexity replication:** Train Kneser-Ney smoothed 3-gram models on the same 4M-sentence subsample (fixed seed) using KenLM; report mean ± std over 10 CV folds; compare to Table 9.
3. **Balanced-volume pretraining test:** Pretrain Electra-small on mixtures where total tokens are equal (e.g., 100M IndicCorp + 100M Vācaspati vs. 200M IndicCorp-only). Evaluate on poem classification and NLI to confirm the effect is not from increased data volume.
