# Principled Confidence Estimation for Deep Computed Tomography
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.05812
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05812
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Sequential likelihood mixing provides finite-sample, anytime-valid confidence sequences for CT reconstruction; deep models (U-Nets, ensembles, diffusion) achieve orders-of-magnitude tighter bounds than classical methods while maintaining strict coverage.

## Executive Summary
Computed tomography (CT) reconstruction faces significant challenges in quantifying uncertainty, particularly under realistic Poisson noise and nonlinear forward operators. This paper introduces a principled framework based on sequential likelihood mixing to construct anytime-valid confidence sequences. The method is reconstruction-agnostic and can incorporate deep learning models (U-Nets, ensembles, diffusion). By leveraging Ville’s inequality, the derived confidence sets contain the true image with probability at least 1−δ at all times. Empirical results show deep models yield dramatically tighter confidence sets than classical approaches like FBP or MLE, with coverage strictly respecting δ=0.05 across datasets and intensities. The framework further enables hallucination detection and interpretable uncertainty visualizations.

## Method Summary
The core idea is to track the cumulative negative log-likelihood L_t(x) of a candidate image x against sequentially observed measurements Z₁,...,Zₜ. A data-dependent threshold βₜ is computed from a mixing distribution µₛ₋₁ that aggregates past predictions (point estimates, ensembles, or diffusion samples). The confidence set Cₜ = { x | Lₜ(x) ≤ βₜ + log(1/δ) } is guaranteed to contain the ground truth x∗ with probability ≥1−δ for all t, assuming realizability (Poisson likelihood with correct forward operator). The mixing distribution updates adaptively: for point predictions µₛ₋₁ = δ_{ˆxₛ₋₁}; for ensembles or diffusion, µₛ₋₁ = (1/K) Σₖ δ_{x̃ₖₛ₋₁}. This mixture structure yields tighter bounds because if any sample accurately reflects the data, its high likelihood caps the penalty. The framework supports both fixed and adaptive acquisition protocols. Practical tools include worst-case optimization for pixel-wise uncertainty and diffusion-based boundary sampling.

## Key Results
- Deep reconstructions (especially diffusion) produce confidence sets that are orders of magnitude tighter than classical methods: at total intensity 10⁹, diffusion achieves a sequential negative log-likelihood gap of ~4.4×10⁴ versus ~1.6×10⁶ for FBP.
- Mixing distributions (ensemble or diffusion) consistently outperform point prediction (mean aggregation), with β_mean > β_mix across all intensities and datasets.
- Empirical coverage strictly respects the user-specified δ=0.05, confirming the anytime-valid guarantee in practice.
- Confidence sets effectively flag hallucinations: samples outside Cₜ have significantly lower PSNR than those inside.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Sequential likelihood mixing for anytime-valid confidence sequences
- **Claim:** The martingale property of sequential likelihood ratios enables construction of confidence sequences that are valid at any stopping time.
- **Mechanism:** For candidate x, compute cumulative negative log-likelihood L_t(x) = Σₛ=1^t -log p_x(Z_s|α_s). Define threshold β_t = E_{µ_{t-1}}[L_t(x)] using mixing distribution µ_{t-1} from past predictions. The set C_t = {x | L_t(x) ≤ β_t + log(1/δ)} yields a non-negative martingale M_t(x) = Πₛ [∫ p_{x'}(Z_s) dµ_{s-1}(x′) / p_{x*}(Z_s)], and Ville’s inequality gives P(sup_t M_t(x*) ≥ 1/δ) ≤ δ, hence P(x* ∉ C_t for some t) ≤ δ.
- **Core assumption:** Realizability—true image x* and measurements follow the specified Poisson model with correct forward operator.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “constructs finite-sample, anytime-valid confidence sequences”; [section 3.1] definition and Proposition 3.2; [appendix] proof of martingale and Ville application.
- **Break condition:** Violation of realizability (e.g., forward model mismatch, non-Poisson noise) invalidates the guarantee.

### Mechanism 2: Mixing distributions tighten bounds via log-sum-exp robustness
- **Claim:** Using a mixture of predictions rather than a point estimate yields systematically tighter confidence sets.
- **Mechanism:** For a uniform mixture of K samples {x̃_k}, the β_t contribution becomes -log( (1/K) Σ_k p_{x̃_k}(Z_t|α_t) ). This log-sum-exp structure means that if any sample is highly likely under the data, it dominates the sum and prevents the large penalty that a smoothed mean (or a single biased point) would incur. The bound thus “caps” at the best sample’s likelihood.
- **Core assumption:** At least one sample in the mixture is statistically consistent with the true image across the sequence.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section D.1] explanation of log-sum-exp capping; [figure 6] empirical β_mean > β_mix across intensities; literature on Bayesian mixtures.
- **Break condition:** All samples share a common bias or misspecification, so none achieve high likelihood; then mixture offers no advantage.

### Mechanism 3: Confidence set geometry reflects reconstruction quality and detects hallucinations
- **Claim:** The threshold β_t tracks predictor performance; samples with unusually low likelihood relative to β_t are likely hallucinations.
- **Mechanism:** β_t grows with the predictor’s historical negative log-likelihood. If a new reconstruction x̃ is plausible, its cumulative L_t(x̃) should stay below β_t + log(1/δ). If it falls outside C_t, its likelihood is worse than what the predictor deems plausible, flagging it. This provides an operational test for hallucination.
- **Core assumption:** The predictor used to define µ_s is causally upstream (depends only on past data), preserving the martingale property.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.7] hallucination detection experiments; [figure 3] correlation between low β_tfinal and high PSNR; [section 4.5] exclusion of rotated images.
- **Break condition:** Correlation between PSNR and likelihood can break if predictor overfits or if forward model is misspecified; a blurry but structurally correct image might have moderate PSNR yet low likelihood, while high-frequency artifacts might have high PSNR but also low likelihood.

## Foundational Learning
### Sequential confidence sequences (Ville’s inequality, Robbins–Siegmund)
- **Why needed:** The anytime-valid guarantee hinges on constructing a non-negative martingale from likelihood ratios and applying Ville’s inequality to bound its supremum, ensuring coverage at all times without predetermining a stopping rule.
- **Quick check:** Under realizability, why is M_t(x) = Πₛ [ ∫ p_{x'}(Zₛ) dµ_{s-1}(x′) / p_{x*}(Zₛ) ] a martingale? How does P(sup_t M_t(x*) ≥ 1/δ) ≤ δ imply P(x* ∉ C_t ∃t) ≤ δ?

### Beer–Lambert law and Poisson log-linear forward model
- **Why needed:** This physical model defines the likelihood p_x(y|α, I₀). Accurate specification is essential; if the forward operator is incorrect, confidence sets become miscalibrated.
- **Quick check:** Write the Poisson log-likelihood for CT and explain its non-convexity in x. What role does the Radon transform R_α play?

### Mixing distributions and mixture likelihoods
- **Why needed:** The framework must handle uncertainty in reconstructions. A mixing distribution (e.g., ensemble, diffusion) allows the threshold to be derived from a distribution rather than a point, leveraging the mixture’s robustness.
- **Quick check:** Show that for a uniform mixture of K Diracs, β_t contribution is -log( (1/K) Σ_k p_{x̃_k}(y_s|α_s) ). Why is this ≤ the negative log-likelihood of the mean prediction? (Hint: Jensen + log-sum-exp.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Forward model module (Radon transform + Beer-Lambert) → Likelihood evaluator (Poisson log-likelihood) → Reconstruction engine (any method: FBP/MLE/U-Net/Diffusion) → Sequential mixing manager (maintains β_t by accumulating -log ∫ p_x(Z_s) dµ_{s-1}(x)) → Confidence set constructor (C_t = {x | L_t(x) ≤ β_t + log(1/δ)}) → Visualization & diagnostics (worst-case optimization, diffusion boundary sampling, hallucination flag).

**Critical path to prototype:**  
1. Implement L_t accumulator and β_t updater using a simple point predictor (e.g., FBP or early-stopped MLE).  
2. Validate coverage on simulated data: generate many (x*, Z_{1:T}) sequences; confirm P(x* ∈ C_t ∀t) ≥ 1−δ.  
3. Replace point predictor with trained U-Net or diffusion sampler; compute β_t and compare to baseline to show tighter sets.  
4. Add pixel-wise uncertainty visualization (worst-case optimization) and hallucination detection.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Point vs. mixing:* Point estimates are cheap but yield looser bounds; mixing (ensemble/diffusion) costs O(K) likelihood evaluations per step but can shrink bounds dramatically, especially at high intensity.  
- *Worst-case vs. boundary sampling:* Worst-case optimization explores the full feasible set, giving conservative pixel intervals but may produce unrealistic extremal images. Diffusion-guided boundary sampling yields plausible tight intervals but requires a pre-trained diffusion model and guidance tuning.  
- *Fixed-angle vs. adaptive:* The framework supports deterministic sparse views; adaptive acquisition (choosing α_t based on past data) is possible but requires careful filtration handling; Theorem 3.2 may still hold under adaptive policies that are measurable w.r.t. past data.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Coverage violation:* Empirical crossover rate exceeds δ. Check realizability (forward model mismatch, discretization), numerical stability of likelihood, or information leak (mixing distribution using future data).  
- *Loose bounds:* β_t much larger than expected from PSNR. Diagnose predictor bias, lack of diversity in mixture, or hyperparameter mismatch (λ, I₀).  
- *Optimization failure:* Worst-case optimization fails to converge or yields intervals missing ground truth. Check projection step and constraint tolerance.  
- *Diffusion sampling instability:* Guided samples escape C_t. Increase guidance weight γ or guidance steps; verify gradient computation of log-likelihood.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. *Coverage on toy 2D CT:* Use a small phantom (e.g., Shepp–Logan, r=32) with 10 sparse angles. Run FBP predictor across intensities and δ; plot empirical crossover rate vs δ to confirm ≤ δ.  
2. *Mixing strategy ablation:* For a fixed diffusion model, compute β_t_final with K=1 (point mean) vs K=16 (mixture). Verify β_mean > β_mix consistently across intensities/datasets; repeat with a U-Net ensemble.  
3. *Hallucination detection robustness:* Sample reconstructions from an unconditional diffusion (weak guidance). Use confidence set from strongly guided diffusion. Compare PSNR of samples inside vs. outside Cₜ; also test artificially rotated images to assess specificity. Expect significant PSNR drop for excluded samples.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Statistical power for hallucination detection:** Theorem 3.2 guarantees coverage but not the ability to reject false reconstructions. How often will hallucinations be flagged? Resolving this requires bounds on false acceptance probability or a method to adjust δ for desired power.
- **Robustness to model misspecification:** Real CT systems exhibit beam hardening, scatter, etc. The current forward model may be inaccurate, leading to miscalibrated confidence sets. Validation on realistic simulations or real data is needed.
- **Validity under adaptive acquisition:** The theory assumes a pre-specified sequence of angles. If α_t is chosen based on past data, the martingale property may break unless the adaptive policy respects the filtration. Formal guarantees for adaptive protocols remain open.
- **Reducing conservatism:** Ville’s inequality yields a universal bound that is often loose, especially at high doses. Can we use tighter sequential concentration inequalities or calibration to obtain narrower sets while still controlling Type I error?

## Limitations
- **Realizability assumption:** Coverage guarantee relies on correct Poisson noise and forward operator; violations (e.g., electronic noise, beam hardening) can invalidate guarantees.
- **Mixing diversity:** Tightness improvement requires at least one mixture sample to be statistically consistent; systematic shared biases among samples negate the benefit.
- **Computational cost:** Mixture likelihood evaluation scales linearly with number of samples K, which can be prohibitive for high-resolution or large ensembles.
- **Generalization to real CT:** All experiments use simulated data with idealized models; real-world performance remains unproven.
