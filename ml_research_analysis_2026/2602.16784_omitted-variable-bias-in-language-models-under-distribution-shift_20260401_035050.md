# Omitted Variable Bias in Language Models Under Distribution Shift

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16784
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16784
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: A distributionally robust optimization framework that bounds worst-case generalization under omitted variable bias, improving OOD performance and enabling quantification of omitted variable strength.

## Executive Summary
Language models often suffer from omitted variable bias (OVB) when deployed on target domains where unobserved covariates shift. This paper develops a worst-case distributionally robust optimization (DRO) framework that augments the doubly robust (DR) objective with a penalty term λσν, where λ = ρ CY CD are sensitivity parameters bounding the influence of omitted variables, and σ, ν are empirical estimates of nuisance quantities. The approach yields a bound on the target risk that is robust to such unobservable shifts. Experiments on four datasets—Math reasoning, Amazon reviews, EmoBank, and Hate Speech—show that the bound provides more accurate OOD performance estimates than standard DR, improves true OOD generalization (with larger gains when omitted variables are stronger, as on Amazon), and allows comparing omitted variable strength across models (e.g., GPT-4.1 shows lowest sensitivity on a logic-altered benchmark).

## Method Summary
The method builds on doubly robust estimation for domain adaptation. Given source data P and target unlabeled data Q, the authors define a short representation h(X) (e.g., lexicon features) and train two nuisance models on heldout source: bg, which predicts the outcome Y from h(X), and a density ratio w(x)=Q(x)/P(x) estimated by a binary classifier on pooled unlabeled source and target. For a candidate model f (linear in h(X)), they compute the DR loss L_DR = E_Q[b(f(X)) - f(X) bg(X)] - E_P[w(X) f(X) (Y - bg(X))], with b appropriate to the task (quadratic for regression, logistic loss for classification). Unknown: The exact forms of b for different tasks and the precise estimation of expectations (e.g., sample averages) are not specified. They then define σ = sqrt(E_P[(Y - bg)^2]) on heldout source and ν = sqrt(E_P[(w f)^2]) on source training. Assumption: These are empirical estimates, but the computation details (e.g., which data splits) are unclear. The worst-case objective adds a penalty: L_DR + λ σ ν, where λ = ρ CY CD are sensitivity parameters that bound the maximum bias due to omitted variables. Unknown: The parameters ρ, CY, CD and their calibration from data are missing from the main text. The overall objective is minimized over f using gradient-based optimization (L-BFGS). Unknown: Optimization hyperparameters (e.g., learning rates, convergence criteria) are omitted. For semi-synthetic Amazon data, the true λ is known because the data generating process is controlled; for real datasets (EmoBank, Hate Speech), λ is tuned by sweeping over plausible values, with a benchmark derived from comparing a long neural representation (MPNet) to the short lexicon. Unknown: The benchmark derivation procedure is vague, impeding cross-model comparisons.

## Key Results
- The worst-case bound yields OOD performance estimates that are more accurate than those from standard doubly robust methods, intersecting the true test risk when sensitivity parameters are chosen plausibly.
- It improves actual OOD generalization; performance gains grow with the strength of omitted variables, as demonstrated on the Amazon dataset where predictive features are systematically omitted.
- The framework enables quantifying omitted variable strength across different models; for example, GPT-4.1 exhibited the lowest sensitivity on the logic-altered MATH-P-Hard benchmark.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The worst-case DRO bound explicitly accounts for the maximum possible bias introduced by unobserved covariates, given user-specified bounds (ρ, CY, CD) on their influence. By penalizing the product σ ν—which captures the uncertainty in the nuisance models and the scale of the reweighted predictions—the optimization discourages solutions that are overly sensitive to distribution shift. This yields models that are more robust to the presence of omitted variables, as verified empirically. The sensitivity parameters provide a calibrated way to trade off optimism under the source distribution against worst-case shift, leading to improved generalization when the bounds are realistic.

## Foundational Learning
- Omitted Variable Bias (OVB): Why needed: The paper’s core problem is that unobserved covariates can cause biased model estimates under distribution shift. Quick check: If a model uses only a subset of true predictors, and those omitted predictors correlate with the outcome and shift between domains, its predictions will be biased.
- Doubly Robust (DR) Estimation: Why needed: DR provides an unbiased estimate of the target risk using both an outcome model and a covariate shift model; the paper uses it as a baseline that is not robust to OVB. Quick check: Can you derive DR for regression with an estimated outcome model and importance weights?
- Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO): Why needed: The paper formulates a worst-case risk bound within DRO to protect against ambiguity due to omitted variables. Quick check: Does a DRO objective minimize the maximum expected loss over an uncertainty set?
- Density Ratio Estimation: Why needed: The weight w(x)=Q(x)/P(x) corrects for covariate shift between source and target; essential for the DR term. Quick check: Is w(x) > 1 where target observations are more dense than source?
- Sensitivity Analysis: Why needed: The parameters ρ, CY, CD bound the influence of omitted variables; they control the conservativeness of the worst-case bound. Quick check: Are sensitivity parameters typically set based on domain knowledge or cross-validation?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Source training data (P_train) → train bg (MLP) on P_heldout; train w (classifier) on pooled P_unlabeled+Q_unlabeled → compute σ from P_heldout residuals; compute ν from w·f(X) on P_train → objective L_DR+λσν evaluated using Q_unlabeled (E_Q terms) and P_train (E_P terms) → optimizer (L-BFGS-B) updates linear model f(θ) → final model evaluated on target test (Q_test).

Critical path:
1. Preprocess: extract lexicon features (LIWC/SenteCon) from raw text.
2. Split source into train_f and heldout (70/30).
3. Train bg on heldout source.
4. Train w by binary classification on pooled unlabeled source (train_f+heldout) and target unlabeled; convert probabilities to density ratios.
5. Initialize linear model f (θ = 0).
6. For each optimization iteration: compute η = f(X_train_f), compute L_DR components using bg, w, Y; compute σ, ν; evaluate objective; update θ.
7. After convergence, test on target labeled test set.

Design tradeoffs:
- Representation choice (lexicon vs neural): Lexicons yield low-dimensional, interpretable features but may omit predictive signals; the paper’s bound specifically addresses missing predictive variables.
- λ selection: Larger λ increases robustness to OVB but may over-penalize and underfit; selection via sweeping or domain knowledge is critical.
- Nuisance model capacity: More flexible bg and w reduce bias in σ and ν but risk overfitting, especially with limited data; the paper uses an MLP with 100 hidden units as a default.
- Model class: Linear f is used for tractability; this limits expressiveness but keeps the optimization convex for regression.

Failure signatures:
- Density ratio w becomes extremely large (e.g., >1e5) or NaN, indicating source/target are not separable in the chosen representation; remedy: use more expressive w or adjust classifier regularization.
- bg yields poor predictive performance on heldout source (low R²/AUC), causing σ to be inflated; remedy: increase bg capacity or collect more heldout data.
- Optimization fails to converge or objective oscillates; try different initializations, use Adam optimizer, or add L2 regularization on θ.
- Sweeping λ produces no improvement or consistent degradation across all values, suggesting the representation may not capture the shift or the bound is too loose; verify that w and bg are reasonably accurate.
- In semi-synthetic Amazon, computed true λ does not match the reported monotonic trend; re-check the formulas for CY, CD, ρ from the true long/short models.

First three experiments:
1. Amazon semi-synthetic: For each random feature omission setting, compute true λ, train with worst-case objective, and verify that OOD MSE improves over DR as omitted variable strength increases.
2. EmoBank λ sweep: Train with a range of λ values, evaluate OOD MSE, and identify the λ that minimizes it; confirm it outperforms DR (λ=0) and the unadjusted baseline.
3. Hate Speech λ sweep: Repeat the same for binary classification, measuring OOD accuracy and binary cross-entropy, and observe similar benefits from tuning λ.

## Limitations
- The mathematical definitions of the sensitivity parameters (ρ, CY, CD) and the auxiliary terms (σ, ν) are not fully specified in the main text, making exact replication challenging.
- The procedure for estimating the benchmark λ on real datasets via MPNet is unclear; this impedes cross-model comparisons of omitted variable strength.
- Essential optimization details (learning rates, convergence criteria, validation splits for λ selection) are omitted, which could affect reproducibility and reported performance.

## Confidence
- Theoretical bound: Medium (the DRO formulation is standard, but core definitions are missing)
- Empirical OOD improvement: Medium (results are compelling but lack full hyperparameter details for replication)
- Omitted variable strength quantification: Low (benchmark λ estimation is vague and cross-model claim uncertain)
