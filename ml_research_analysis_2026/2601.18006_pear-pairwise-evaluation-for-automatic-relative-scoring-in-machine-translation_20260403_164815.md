# PEAR: Pairwise Evaluation for Automatic Relative Scoring in Machine Translation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.18006
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18006
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: PEAR reframes reference-free MT evaluation as a graded pairwise comparison task, achieving higher correlation than single-candidate QE baselines while using fewer parameters and even surpassing some reference-based metrics.

## Executive Summary
PEAR introduces a novel approach to reference-free machine translation evaluation by treating it as a graded pairwise comparison problem. Given a source sentence and two candidate translations, PEAR predicts a signed scalar representing the direction and magnitude of their quality difference. This contrasts with traditional quality estimation metrics that score translations in isolation, which are then compared by subtraction. PEAR employs a cross-encoder architecture with antisymmetry regularization to enforce consistency when candidate order is reversed. Evaluated on the WMT24 MQM benchmark, PEAR consistently outperforms matched single-candidate QE baselines and several reference-based metrics, while being more parameter-efficient. Its scores show lower correlation with other top metrics, suggesting a complementary evaluation signal. PEAR also proves efficient in Minimum Bayes Risk decoding by halving scoring cost with minimal performance loss.

## Method Summary
PEAR redefines reference-free MT evaluation as predicting the relative quality difference between two translations for a given source. Given a source `s` and candidates `mt_a`, `mt_b`, the model outputs `Δ̂_ab ≈ sh(s,mt_a) - sh(s,mt_b)`. Training examples are generated from human segment-level judgments (DA, DA+SQM, MQM) by pairing all candidate translations per source and computing the human score difference.

The model employs a cross‑encoder architecture: inputs are serialized as `[BOS] s [SEP] mt_a [SEP] mt_b [EOS]`; a pretrained multilingual encoder produces token embeddings, from which masked mean pooling yields span vectors `h_src`, `h_a`, `h_b`. For each candidate, a source‑aware representation `φ_k = [h_k; h_k ⊙ h_src; |h_k - h_src|]` is passed through a projection and feed‑forward network to obtain a utility `u_k`. The output logit is `z = u_a - u_b`, scaled by a positive factor `α`. The loss combines Huber regression on `(z/α) - Δ*` and an antisymmetry regularization term `L_flip = (Δ̂_ab + Δ̂_ba)^2` applied to the reversed-order pair. Training proceeds in two stages: pre‑training on DA/DA+SQM data and fine‑tuning on MQM (including optionally distilled GPT‑4.1‑mini MQM). Evaluation on the WMT24 MQM benchmark uses the `mt-metrics-eval` toolkit, reporting Soft Pairwise Accuracy (SPA) at system level and segment‑level pairwise accuracy with tie calibration (`acc*_eq`), averaged as Avg Corr. Inference can be performed in both orders and averaged for robustness, or in a reference‑anchored mode where one candidate is a fixed reference (human or MT output).

## Key Results
- PEAR consistently outperforms strictly matched single-candidate QE baselines trained on identical data and backbones, isolating the benefit of the pairwise formulation.
- Despite having far fewer parameters (560M) than larger QE models (10.5B+), PEAR achieves higher average correlation on WMT24 MQM and surpasses several reference-based metrics while remaining reference-free.
- PEAR's segment-level scores are less correlated with other top metrics, indicating a complementary evaluation signal; in Minimum Bayes Risk decoding, it serves as an efficient utility function, halving pairwise scoring cost with negligible performance loss due to its near-antisymmetric behavior.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Direct pairwise prediction yields more accurate comparative judgments**
- **Claim:** Directly predicting the quality difference from a joint encoding avoids error accumulation from separate absolute predictions and allows the model to focus on comparative features.
- **Mechanism:** PEAR jointly encodes the source and both candidates in a cross-encoder, directly outputting a relative score `Δ̂_ab = u_a - u_b`. This contrasts with subtracting two independent absolute scores, which can propagate errors.
- **Core assumption:** Human quality differences between two translations can be modeled as a single scalar target derived from subtracting human segment-level scores.
- **Evidence:** The paper shows PEAR consistently outperforms matched single-candidate QE baselines (abstract, section 3.1). Neighboring work (e.g., MT-Ranker) uses binary classification but cannot predict graded preferences; PEAR’s graded output shows measurable gains.
- **Break condition:** The advantage may diminish if the absolute scoring model is extremely large and trained on massive pairwise-distilled data; the paper only tests up to 3.5B parameters. Also, for extremely long inputs exceeding the encoder’s capacity, joint encoding might degrade.

**Mechanism 2: Antisymmetry regularization enforces consistency**
- **Claim:** Enforcing `f(s,a,b) = -f(s,b,a)` via regularization improves score reliability and transitivity.
- **Mechanism:** During training, for each `(s,a,b)` pair, the model also sees `(s,b,a)` and is regularized to predict opposite signs via `L_flip = (Δ̂_ab + Δ̂_ba)^2`. This encourages approximate antisymmetry.
- **Core assumption:** A well-behaved pairwise comparison function should satisfy antisymmetry, which also promotes additive transitivity across triples.
- **Evidence:** Appendix A shows with `λ_flip=0.1`, antisymmetry residual drops from 0.196 to 0.014 and transitivity residual from 0.561 to 0.189 (relative to mean absolute score). Section 5.1 notes that using both orders (averaging) yields nearly identical performance to single-order, indicating near-antisymmetry.
- **Break condition:** Setting `λ_flip` too high may overconstrain and harm regression performance; the paper found optimal around 0.1. If data contains many exact ties, antisymmetry holds trivially but transitivity may still be imperfect.

**Mechanism 3: Source-aware candidate representations capture nuanced differences**
- **Claim:** The cross-encoder architecture with source-aware candidate representations enables detection of errors relative to the source.
- **Mechanism:** For each candidate, the model constructs `φ_k = [h_k; h_k ⊙ h_src; |h_k - h_src|]`, explicitly tying the candidate representation to the source. The utility `u_k` derived from `φ_k` is then subtracted to yield the relative score.
- **Core assumption:** Accurate comparative evaluation requires understanding how each candidate deviates from the source, not just candidate-internal features.
- **Evidence:** Section 3.3 details the design. Section 5.4 reports that PEAR shows lower correlation with other top metrics (e.g., ~0.26 with MetricX-24-Hybrid-QE on Ja-Zh), suggesting a distinct signal possibly due to source-aware features.
- **Break condition:** If the source is extremely long or complex, compressed span representations may lose information. Also, if errors are purely lexical without source mismatches (e.g., style differences), source-aware features may not add value.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input -> Serialization ([BOS] s [SEP] mt_a [SEP] mt_b [EOS]) -> Encoder (pretrained multilingual model) -> Span pooling (masked mean over source, mt_a, mt_b) -> Pairwise head (φ_k = [h_k; h_k⊙h_src; |h_k - h_src|] -> Projection + FFN -> utility u_k) -> Scoring (z = u_a - u_b, scaled by α) -> Output Δ̂_ab.

**Critical path**:
- Forward: Input → Serialization → Encoder → Span pooling → Compute φ_a, φ_b → u_a, u_b → z = u_a - u_b → Δ̂_ab = (z/α).
- Backward: Compute Huber loss on Δ̂_ab vs. human Δ*; also compute reversed order Δ̂_ba and L_flip; total loss = Huber + λ_flip * L_flip; backpropagate to update encoder and pairwise head.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Cross‑encoder vs. bi‑encoder: Joint encoding enables interaction but requires O(N²) scoring for N systems; reference‑anchored mode reduces to O(N) but introduces anchor bias.
- Regularization strength λ_flip: balances antisymmetry vs. primary regression; paper used 0.1 after limited search.
- Model size: Smaller models (560M) already strong; scaling to 3.5B yields gains, but unknown beyond.
- Bidirectional inference: Averaging both orders improves robustness but doubles cost.

**Failure signatures**:
- Antisymmetry violation: high mean absolute `|Δ̂_ab + Δ̂_ba|` on dev set indicates model not respecting order inversion.
- Unexpected divergence: very low correlation with other metrics on a language pair might signal domain mismatch or data issues; verify with human evaluation.
- MBR shortcut failure: if antisymmetry is poor, using `u(h_i,h_j) = -u(h_j,h_i)` to halve utility matrix computation may degrade MBR performance.
- Length overflow: inputs exceeding encoder’s max length cause truncation; monitor source+candidate lengths.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Controlled baseline comparison**: Train PEAR and a single-candidate QE model with identical backbone/data. Compare Avg Corr on WMT24 MQM; expect PEAR higher.
2. **Antisymmetry ablation**: Train PEAR with `λ_flip=0` and `λ_flip=0.1`; compute average relative antisymmetry residual on dev set; expect `λ_flip=0.1` to yield much lower residual.
3. **Reference‑anchored robustness**: For a set of MT systems, compute PEAR_ref scores using (a) human reference, (b) an MT output as anchor (remove anchor system from set). Compare system rankings (by Avg Corr) across anchors; expect stable ranks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How does PEAR’s performance scale with model size beyond 3.5B parameters?** The authors only evaluated up to 3.5B; it is unclear whether the pairwise advantage persists, widens, or saturates with larger models.
- **What drives PEAR’s lower correlation with other top metrics, and how can this distinct signal be exploited?** The specific factors causing divergence are unknown, and potential benefits for metric ensembles or reinforcement‑learning rewards are unexplored.
- **Does adding targeted synthetic contrastive data (e.g., perturbed translations) improve PEAR’s ability to discriminate subtle quality differences?** The impact of synthetic contrastive pairs on graded pairwise training has not been studied.
- **Can PEAR be extended to predict fine‑grained error spans (MQM sequence tagging) while preserving its pairwise formulation?** Architectural modifications and data requirements for token‑level span prediction in a pairwise context are unexplored.

## Limitations
- MQM fine‑tuning covers only 10 language pairs, raising uncertainty about zero‑shot performance on other pairs.
- Antisymmetry regularization strength λ_flip was selected via limited search; its generality across settings is unknown.
- Key training details (pairing strategy, data splits, some hyperparameters) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- Cross‑encoder inference scales as O(N²); reference‑anchoring may introduce anchor bias not fully quantified.
- Distilled GPT‑4.1‑mini MQM data could embed teacher model biases.

## Confidence
- **Pairwise formulation superiority**: High – controlled match against single-candidate QE on WMT24 MQM.
- **Antisymmetry regularization**: Medium – residuals strongly reduced, but isolated effect on final metrics unclear.
- **Source‑aware complementarity**: Medium – lower correlation with other metrics suggests distinct signal.
- **Reference‑anchored robustness**: Medium – stable across few anchors; broader evaluation needed.
- **Parameter efficiency**: High – 560M outperforms larger models.
- **MBR efficiency via antisymmetry**: Medium – cost halved with negligible loss on one test set.

## Next Checks
1. **Ablate source‑aware terms**: Train PEAR without the `h_k⊙h_src` and without `|h_k - h_src|` components separately; measure changes in Avg Corr to quantify each term’s impact.
2. **Zero‑shot language test**: Evaluate PEAR on a held‑out language pair (e.g., En‑Is) using only pre‑training; compare to in‑pair fine‑tuned performance.
3. **Anchor bias quantification**: Systematically vary anchor quality (human, top‑MT, bottom‑MT) across multiple language pairs; compute Kendall τ stability to measure bias magnitude.
