# Can LLM generate interesting mathematical research problems?

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.18813
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18813
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** An LLM-based agent (DeepMath-generate) produced 665 novel, research-level mathematical problems in differential geometry, with many confirmed as new and valuable by human experts, though none reached the transformative scale of historic conjectures.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can generate valuable, unsolved mathematical research problems. The authors developed DeepMath-generate, an agent using GPT-5 models in a generator-evaluator loop with carefully engineered prompts emphasizing criteria like profound insight, interdisciplinary bridges, and extreme simplicity. Applied to 200 specific topics in differential geometry, it produced 665 problems. Human expert validation confirmed many problems are novel to experts and possess genuine research potential, demonstrating that LLMs can interpolate into the frontier of well-defined mathematical niches. However, the system did not produce problems of historic, field-defining magnitude, indicating a ceiling on capturing the deepest patterns of mathematical creativity.

## Method Summary
The core method is an iterative agentic pipeline. A "Generator" LLM creates an initial problem from a specific research topic. An "Evaluator" LLM, using identical criteria, critiques the problem for adherence to standards like being research-level, unknown, and embodying one of three "good problem" attributes (profound insight, bridge, simplicity). The Generator revises based on feedback in a loop until the Evaluator approves. The entire process is grounded in a curated list of 200 concrete, current research directions in differential geometry, ensuring outputs are anchored to active frontiers rather than generic mathematics.

## Key Results
*   Generated 665 mathematical problems from 200 differential geometry topics.
*   Human expert verification confirmed many problems are novel (unknown to experts) and possess unique research value.
*   The system did not generate problems comparable in transformative potential to major historic conjectures like the Poincaré Conjecture.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
*   **Iterative Generator-Evaluator Loop:** The cycle of creation and critique progressively filters and improves outputs, with the Evaluator enforcing explicit quality criteria. The production of 665 human-verified novel problems suggests the loop effectively screens out low-quality or known results.
*   **Prompt-Encoded Mathematical Aesthetics:** Defining a "good problem" via iconic examples (Riemann Hypothesis, Poincaré Conjecture) and requiring a justification steers the LLM toward replicating structural patterns of historically significant questions. Example outputs show adherence to the defined "bridge" and "simplicity" attributes.
*   **Topic-Specific Grounding:** Constraining generation to 200 specific, current research directions (e.g., "exotic spheres with nonnegative curvature") provides the necessary scaffolding for the LLM to synthesize plausible open questions at a niche frontier, avoiding trivial or already-solved exercises.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Criteria for Research-Level Mathematical Problems**
    *   **Why needed:** The entire agent's goal is to generate problems meeting subjective "good problem" standards (insight, bridge, simplicity). An engineer must understand these criteria to design prompts, evaluate outputs, and interpret results.
    *   **Quick check:** For the problem on moduli spaces of exotic spheres, which attribute (insight, bridge, simplicity) is most strongly exemplified by its connection between Riemannian geometry and differential topology?
*   **Concept: Generator-Evaluator (Critic) Architecture for LLM Agents**
    *   **Why needed:** This is the core operational pattern. Understanding its self-improvement potential and risks (e.g., shared model biases) is crucial for debugging and designing robust systems.
    *   **Quick check:** What is a key risk of using the same model family (GPT-5) for both generator and evaluator, and how might a "closed-loop" validation failure manifest?
*   **Concept: Differential Geometry Prerequisite Knowledge**
    *   **Why needed:** The domain is highly technical. An engineer cannot assess outputs, design topics, or interpret significance without understanding foundational objects (manifolds, curvature) and theorems (Soul Theorem).
    *   **Quick check:** In the context of the examples, what does it mean for a manifold to admit a "complete nonnegatively curved metric," and why is this condition significant for the Cheeger-Gromoll soul theorem?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Topic Input Module -> Generator LLM (GPT-5/5.3) -> Problem+Explanation -> Evaluator LLM (GPT-5/5.3) -> (Valid? Yes -> Final Output | No -> Critique) -> (back to Generator for revision) -> Loop until Terminate signal.
*   **Critical path:** A specific research topic triggers the Generator. The output is evaluated. If rejected, the critique is fed back for revision. The cycle repeats until the Evaluator approves the problem.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   Depth vs. Breadth: 5 problems per narrow topic ensures depth but may miss broad, cross-cutting conjectures.
    *   Automation vs. Ground Truth: The LLM Evaluator scales but risks "closed-loop" biases; human verification is the ultimate but post-hoc ground truth.
    *   Constraint vs. Creativity: Strict prompt rules filter noise but may suppress fruitful problems adjacent to known results.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    1.  Theorem Restatement: A direct rephrasing of a known result that the Evaluator misses.
    2.  Logical Invalidity: Problem contains contradictory or false conditions.
    3.  Triviality/Non-Research Level: Solves a standard exercise, not an open research question.
    4.  Vagueness: The "Why good" justification is superficial or unconvincing.
    5.  Loop Stagnation: The Generator cannot address the Evaluator's critiques after multiple iterations.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Sanity Check - Avoid Known Theorems:** Input "Poincaré Conjecture." Output must not generate the original conjecture; it should be a novel extension or a system rejection.
    2.  **Validity Pressure Test:** Input "Kähler-Einstein metrics on Fano manifolds." Check outputs for logically invalid condition combinations (e.g., incompatible curvature signs).
    3.  **Novelty & Bridge Probe:** Input "Harmonic maps to buildings." A high-quality output should explicitly bridge analysis (harmonic maps) and geometric group theory (buildings) in its statement or justification.

## Limitations
*   **Topic List Dependency:** Success is tightly coupled to the expert-curated 200-topic list. Performance in other domains or for emerging, uncodified frontiers is unknown.
*   **Evaluator Reliability & Closed-Loop Risk:** Using the same model family for generator and evaluator risks shared blind spots, potentially failing to detect subtle restatements or propagating biases.
*   **Generalizability & Transformative Insight:** Results are domain-specific (differential geometry). The agent did not produce problems of historic, field-defining magnitude, suggesting a ceiling on capturing the deepest patterns of creativity.

## Confidence
*   **Iterative Generator-Evaluator Loop Improves Quality:** Medium. Supported by human-verified output volume and novelty, but tempered by unverified evaluator logic and convergent bias risks.
*   **Prompt-Encoded Aesthetics Steer Toward "Good" Problems:** Medium. Example outputs show adherence to attributes like "bridge" and "simplicity," but the absence of transformative results indicates partial success.
*   **Topic-Grounded Generation Reaches Novel Frontiers:** Medium-High. Strongly supported by human expert confirmation of novelty in specific niches.

## Next Checks
1.  **Blind Expert Replication Study:** Commission independent domain experts to blindly rate a random 10% sample of the 665 problems for (a) clear novelty, (b) genuine research potential, and (c) attribution of the three core attributes.
2.  **Cross-Domain Transfer Test:** Replicate the exact pipeline on a different mathematical domain (e.g., combinatorial design theory) with a new expert-curated topic list. Primary metric: fraction of problems passing a similar human novelty/value check.
3.  **Evaluator Stress Test with Known Solved Problems:** Input a list of 20-30 famous recently solved problems from differential geometry. Measure if the agent ever generates a direct restatement and if the evaluator consistently flags them as "Existing Theorem" or "Known."
