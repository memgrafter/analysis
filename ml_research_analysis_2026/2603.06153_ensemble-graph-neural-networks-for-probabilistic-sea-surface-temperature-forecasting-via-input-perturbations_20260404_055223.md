# Ensemble Graph Neural Networks for Probabilistic Sea Surface Temperature Forecasting via Input Perturbations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.06153  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06153  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Spatially coherent Perlin noise perturbations yield well-calibrated probabilistic SST forecasts without additional training cost.

## Executive Summary
This study introduces SeaCast, a hierarchical Graph Neural Network (GNN) for sea surface temperature (SST) forecasting, and proposes a computationally efficient method to generate probabilistic ensembles. Instead of training multiple models, the authors perturb the initial ocean state with structured noise (Gaussian, Perlin, fractal Perlin) during inference. Deterministic forecast skill remains comparable to the non-perturbed model. For probabilistic performance, Perlin noise—due to its spatial coherence—produces lower Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) and better-calibrated spread-skill ratios at longer lead times (15 days) compared to unstructured Gaussian noise. Fractal Perlin underperforms, likely due to excessive multi-scale smoothing. The work demonstrates that carefully designed input perturbations can yield reliable uncertainty estimates at minimal computational overhead.

## Method Summary
SeaCast is a pre-trained deterministic hierarchical GNN that maps historical ocean states to future SST fields. To form ensembles, the authors inject noise into the initial input state at inference time, generating multiple perturbed initial conditions. Each perturbation produces a deterministic forecast via the same trained model, and the collection constitutes an ensemble. Three noise types are tested: isotropic Gaussian (unstructured), classic Perlin (coherent, scale-controlled), and fractal Perlin (multi-scale coherent). No model retraining is required. Ensemble statistics (e.g., mean, variance) are used to compute probabilistic metrics like CRPS and spread-skill relationships.

## Key Results
- **Deterministic skill preservation**: Perturbed-model RMSE is comparable to the non-perturbed baseline, indicating that noise injection does not degrade mean forecast accuracy.
- **Perlin noise superiority**: Spatially coherent Perlin perturbations yield lower CRPS and spread-skill ratios near 1 at 15-day leads, outperforming Gaussian noise which tends to under-disperse.
- **Fractal Perlin underperformance**: Multi-scale coherence reduces structural alignment with actual SST variability, leading to worse calibration, highlighting the importance of matching noise coherence to the target field's spatial scales.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
SST fields exhibit strong spatial correlations due to ocean dynamics (e.g., currents, eddies). Perlin noise generates spatially smooth, continuous perturbations that respect these inherent correlations, producing ensemble members that are physically plausible and diverse. This coherence allows the ensemble to capture realistic uncertainty branches (e.g., evolving features) without violating dynamical constraints, thus improving probabilistic calibration. In contrast, Gaussian noise creates uncorrelated pixel-level disruptions that are dynamically inconsistent, leading to ensembles that either collapse (under-dispersion) or include unrealistic states. Fractal Perlin’s multi-scale structure may over-smooth or misalign with dominant SST scales, reducing its effectiveness.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)**  
   - *Why needed*: SST data often resides on irregular grids (e.g., satellite track mappings); GNNs operate natively on graph-structured data without resampling.  
   - *Quick check*: Replace graph convolutions with 2D convolutions on a regridded product; expect loss of efficiency and skill due to resampling artifacts.

2. **Probabilistic Forecasting**  
   - *Why needed*: Decision-makers require calibrated uncertainty estimates (e.g., probability of marine heatwaves).  
   - *Quick check*: Compare deterministic (single forecast) vs. ensemble spread-error alignment on a holdout set using spread-skill diagrams.

3. **Input Perturbation Ensembles**  
   - *Why needed*: Traditional ensembles (e.g., perturbed physics, multi-model) are computationally expensive; single-model perturbation offers a cheap alternative.  
   - *Quick check*: Add Gaussian noise of increasing variance to inputs; plot ensemble spread vs. noise magnitude to confirm monotonic relationship.

4. **Spatial Coherence in Noise Models**  
   - *Why needed*: Geospatial fields like SST have non-white power spectra; coherent perturbations maintain realistic feature geometry.  
   - *Quick check*: Compute spatial autocorrelation (e.g., Moran’s I) of perturbed fields; compare to autocorrelation of true SST residuals.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Initial Ocean State -> Perturbation Module (Noise Generator) -> Hierarchical GNN (SeaCast) -> Deterministic Forecasts -> Ensemble Aggregation (mean, variance)

**Critical path**:  
Perturbation design (noise type, scale) → Single-model inference on perturbed input → Ensemble statistics → Probabilistic evaluation (CRPS, spread-skill).

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Coherence vs. diversity*: Higher spatial coherence (Perlin) improves physical realism but may reduce ensemble diversity if scale is too large.  
- *Computational cost*: Perturbation during inference is near-free; however, selecting optimal noise parameters requires offline validation.  
- *Deterministic fidelity*: Perturbations must not dominate the signal; amplitude must be small enough to preserve mean skill.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Spread-skill ratio deviates significantly from 1 (e.g., >1.2 or <0.8) indicating mis-calibrated uncertainty.  
- Fractal Perlin’s poor performance suggests mismatch between noise’s multi-scale structure and SST’s dominant spatial scales.  
- Visual artifacts in perturbed forecasts (e.g., fragmented eddies, unrealistic gradients) indicate dynamically inconsistent perturbations.

**First experiments**:  
1. **Noise amplitude sweep**: Systematically vary Perlin noise magnitude (e.g., 0.01–0.5°C) and measure CRPS/spread-skill to locate optimal regime.  
2. **Perturbation structure ablation**: Compare ensemble performance using Gaussian, Perlin, fractal Perlin, and no perturbation on both deterministic (RMSE) and probabilistic (CRPS) metrics.  
3. **Perturbation temporal persistence**: Test whether temporally coherent (e.g., red noise) vs. independent per-time-step perturbations affect calibration, given SST’s temporal autocorrelation.

## Limitations
- **Scope limitation**: Results are confined to SeaCast on a single SST dataset; generalizability to other GNN architectures, geophysical variables (e.g., ocean heat content), or regions is unverified.
- **Perturbation design**: Only three noise types were examined; optimal coherence scales, amplitude schedules, and hybrid schemes remain unexplored. Fractal Perlin’s failure may be implementation-specific.
- **Uncertainty sources**: perturbations solely represent initial-condition uncertainty; model error and forcing uncertainties are omitted, potentially underestimating total forecast uncertainty.
- **Evaluation horizon**: Metrics are reported up to 15 days; long-lead calibration (>30 days) and extreme-event reliability are unassessed.

## Confidence
- **Method feasibility (single-model ensembles work)**: High  
- **Perlin noise > Gaussian for probabilistic skill**: Medium  
- **Fractal Perlin inferiority**: Low  
- **Deterministic skill preservation (RMSE unchanged)**: High  

## Next Checks

3. **Benchmark against established probabilistic methods**: Compare against deep ensembles, MC dropout, and stochastic parameterization using multiple metrics (CRPS, Brier score, reliability diagrams) to contextualize the improvement’s magnitude.
