# Communication-Corruption Coupling and Verification in Cooperative Multi-Objective Bandits

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.11924
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11924
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Raw-sample sharing amplifies corruption by factor N; sufficient-statistic and recommendation-only sharing avoid amplification and achieve centralized-rate regret O(√(KNT)+Γ). Verification with budget ν > Θ(K/Δ_min²) restores learnability in high-corruption regimes, yielding Γ-independent team regret.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies cooperative multi-agent multi-objective bandits under adversarial corruptions with a global budget Γ and a verification budget ν. Agents aim to maximize a monotone Lipschitz scalarization of vector-valued rewards. The key insight is that the communication protocol determines the effective corruption level: broadcasting raw samples multiplies Γ by the number of agents N, while sharing sufficient statistics or recommendations avoids this amplification. Consequently, team regret scales as O(√(KNT) + Γ_eff), where Γ_eff ∈ [Γ, NΓ] depends on the protocol. A matching lower bound confirms the N-fold amplification under raw-sample sharing is unavoidable. Moreover, in the high-corruption regime (Γ = Θ(NT)), sublinear regret becomes possible once ν exceeds a threshold Θ(K L²/Δ_min²); certified recommendation sharing then makes the team regret independent of Γ. The analysis and experiments on vector Bernoulli rewards validate these findings.

## Method Summary
The algorithms extend UCB to the corrupted multi-agent setting. Each agent maintains empirical means of arm rewards. Three communication protocols are considered: (S1) raw-sample sharing, where agents broadcast their observed reward vectors; (S2) sufficient-statistic sharing, where per-arm cumulative sums/counts are synchronized; (S3) recommendation-only sharing, where agents broadcast only the selected arm index (and optionally a certificate when using verification). Confidence radii combine stochastic deviations (1/√sample count) with a bias term proportional to the effective corruption Γ_eff on that arm. For S3 with verification, agents compute certificates (lower/upper confidence bounds) solely from verified samples. Once certificates are sufficiently tight to identify the optimal arm, the team commits to it. Verification is allocated uniformly across arms (e.g., round-robin) to guarantee each arm obtains enough clean observations.

## Key Results
- Raw-sample sharing (S1) multiplies effective corruption by N; sufficient-statistic (S2) and recommendation-only (S3) sharing keep Γ_eff = Γ.
- Team regret for monotone Lipschitz scalarization is O(√(KNT) + Γ_eff). A matching lower bound confirms the unavoidable additive NΓ term under S1.
- With verification budget ν > Θ(K L²/Δ_min²), certified recommendation sharing (S3‑Cert) achieves Γ‑independent regret even when Γ = Θ(NT).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism revolves around sample multiplicity. Under raw-sample sharing (S1), each broadcast observation is appended by all N agents, yielding a sample multiplicity of N; thus a single corrupted sample contributes N times to the global error, making Γ_eff = NΓ. In contrast, sufficient-statistic (S2) and recommendation-only (S3) sharing ensure each sample is counted at most once, so Γ_eff = Γ (Lemma 1).

The UCB‑type regret bound then incorporates Γ_eff via a bias term in the confidence intervals. Lemma 2 shows that the estimation error of an arm’s mean equals a stochastic deviation plus a bias proportional to the effective corruption accumulated on that arm. Summing over all agent‑rounds yields the additive Γ_eff term in the team regret (Theorem 1). The matching lower bound (Theorem 2) proves that S1’s NΓ amplification is inherent.

In high‑corruption regimes (Γ = Θ(NT)), sublinear regret is impossible without clean data. However, a verification budget ν exceeding Θ(K L²/Δ_min²) enables certified recommendation sharing (S3‑Cert). Verified samples are immune to corruption; agents broadcast recommendations together with confidence intervals computed solely from these clean observations. When each arm has enough verified samples, the certificates become tight enough to certify the optimal arm with high probability. The team then commits, making subsequent regret zero. Pre‑commitment regret is bounded by O(ν Δ_max) plus coordination overhead, independent of Γ (Theorem 3). Experiments confirm a sharp phase transition once ν crosses the threshold.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Agents, each with scalarization module, confidence module, verification controller, and (for certified sharing) filtering module. A reliable broadcast channel connects all agents: after each round, agents send messages to the channel, which distributes them to all agents. Local observations feed the confidence module to compute indices for arm selection; verification status is set by the controller; certificates (if any) are aggregated by the filtering module to decide commitment.

**Critical path:** 1. Pull arms based on current indices. 2. Observe (possibly corrupted) reward; if verified, observe clean reward. 3. Update local statistics (including verified‑only if applicable). 4. Broadcast message according to protocol. 5. Receive others’ messages and update aggregated statistics. 6. Compute new indices for next round. 7. If using certification, check for certified winner; if found, commit. 8. Repeat until horizon or commitment.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Communication vs robustness: S1 sends full vectors but amplifies corruption (Γ_eff = NΓ); S2 sends summary statistics (moderate communication) but requires synchronization; S3 sends minimal indices (low comm) and avoids amplification but may incur coordination overhead in the clean case.  
- Verification allocation: The analysis assumes uniform round‑robin; adaptive allocation could potentially reduce the required ν.  
- Certification tightness: Larger ν yields tighter certificates, reducing pre‑commitment exploration; however, over‑verification might waste budget on already‑identified arms.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Amplified regret under S1 when Γ is large; Γ_eff = NΓ can lead to linear regret.  
- Inability to commit under S3‑Cert if ν is below the threshold; certificates remain too loose to bound the optimal arm reliably.  
- Overcommitment risk: if randomness causes premature certification of a suboptimal arm, regret may become linear; the analysis ensures this occurs only after sufficient verified samples with high probability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Limited communication topologies** – How does effective corruption amplification change under graphs, peer‑to‑peer, or delayed messages compared to all‑to‑all broadcast? The paper only studies complete broadcast protocols.  
2. **Adaptive verification allocation** – Can an adaptive strategy reduce the required total verification budget ν below Θ(K/Δ_min²) while still achieving Γ‑independent regret? The analysis uses uniform allocation.  
3. **Broader scalarization classes** – Do the guarantees extend to non‑monotone or non‑Lipschitz scalarizations (e.g., hypervolume, discontinuous utilities)? The current assumptions are essential for the optimism argument.

## Limitations
- The adversary’s exact corruption rule in the numerical experiments is unspecified; only the lower bound construction is described.  
- The clean‑case coordination overhead term (Reg_clean_comm) is abstract and not quantified, making it difficult to predict S3’s exact performance in practice.  
- Verification allocation is assumed uniform; adaptive schemes that might lower the needed ν are not explored.  
- No lower bound demonstrates that Γ_eff ≤ Γ is necessary for S2/S3; the absence of amplification for these protocols is not proven optimal.  
- Results are restricted to monotone Lipschitz scalarizations, excluding many natural multi‑objective criteria.

## Confidence

- Regret bound O(√(KNT) + Γ_eff): High

- Verification threshold (ν > Θ(KL²/Δ_min²) ⇒ Γ‑independent regret): High
