# Bielik Guard: Efficient Polish Language Safety Classifiers for LLM Content Moderation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07954
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07954
- Reference count: 27
- Primary result: Two compact RoBERTa-based classifiers (0.1B and 0.5B) fine-tuned on a community-annotated Polish safety dataset; the 0.1B variant achieves 77.65% precision and 0.63% false positive rate on real user prompts, substantially outperforming baselines.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the scarcity of effective safety classifiers for Polish language large language models. Introducing Bielik Guard, the authors develop two lightweight RoBERTa-based models (124M and 443M parameters) trained on a novel dataset of 6,885 Polish texts annotated by a community of volunteers across five safety categories. Evaluation on authentic user prompts reveals that the smaller 0.1B model (v1.1) delivers exceptional deployment metrics (77.65% precision, 0.63% FPR), far exceeding the performance of existing Polish and multilingual safety filters. Both models maintain high specificity (>0.96) and the larger 0.5B version demonstrates greater resilience to surface-level text perturbations. This work enables efficient, high-precision content moderation for Polish LLM applications.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a community-annotated dataset of 6,885 unique Polish texts from user prompts and social media, with each sample rated by multiple volunteers to capture nuanced safety categories (Hate/Aggression, Vulgarities, Sexual Content, Crime, Self-Harm). Rather than converting these annotations into hard binary labels, they preserve annotator disagreement as continuous percentage scores, which are scaled to [0,1] and used as soft targets during training. Two RoBERTa-based models are fine-tuned: MMLW-RoBERTa-base (124M parameters, 50K vocabulary) and PKOBP/polish-roberta-8k (443M parameters, 128K vocabulary). Each model receives a multi-label classification head consisting of a dropout layer (p=0.1) followed by a linear projection to five logits, with sigmoid activation. Training employs binary cross-entropy loss with the soft labels, the AdamW optimizer (learning rate 2e-5, 500 warmup steps, linear decay, weight decay 0.01), batch size 32, and three epochs. Two data splits are used: a 2:1 split (2,295/4,590) for analysis and a near-complete split (6,285/600) for production models. The v1.1 release includes a calibrated decision threshold for the Crime category to reduce false positives.

## Key Results
- The 0.5B model achieves strong discrimination on the test set with micro F1=0.791 and macro F1=0.785.
- The 0.1B v1.1 model attains 77.65% precision and 0.63% false positive rate on real user prompts, dramatically outperforming HerBERT-PL-Guard (31.55% precision, 4.70% FPR) and larger multilingual models.
- Both variants maintain specificity above 0.96; the 0.5B model exhibits superior robustness to diacritic removal, capitalization changes, and character swaps.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success of Bielik Guard stems from three synergistic factors: (1) **Data with annotator ambiguity preserved**: Training on soft labels derived from community percentages allows the model to learn graded risk signals instead of overconfident binary decisions, improving calibration on borderline cases. (2) **Linguistic and architectural alignment**: Using Polish-specific RoBERTa variants ensures the encoder is adept at Polish morphology and idioms, while the compact multi-label head keeps inference efficient. (3) **Precision-oriented thresholding**: Recognizing that false positives are costly in production, the authors tuned the decision threshold for the Crime category upward in v1.1, reducing spurious flags at the expense of recall; the 0.1B model’s overall high precision demonstrates the efficacy of this trade-off. Together, these choices yield a lightweight, deployable safety filter tailored to Polish.

## Foundational Learning
- **Soft-label training**: Why needed? Captures annotator disagreement and models uncertainty. Quick check: Verify loss function uses BCE between sigmoid outputs and float labels in [0,1] (not integers).
- **Multi-label classification**: Why needed? A single text may belong to multiple safety categories. Quick check: Confirm output layer has 5 independent logits with sigmoid activation.
- **Threshold calibration**: Why needed? Balances precision and recall for each category, especially in deployment with asymmetric costs. Quick check: Check that the CRIME category threshold is >0.5 in v1.1.
- **Community annotation**: Why needed? Generates large-scale, culturally relevant data for low-resource Polish. Quick check: Confirm dataset contains ~6,885 texts with percentage annotations from >1,500 volunteers.
- **Compact RoBERTa architecture**: Why needed? Enables low-latency inference on edge devices. Quick check: Verify base model has ~124M parameters and 50K vocabulary (0.1B) or ~443M parameters and 128K vocabulary (0.5B).
- **Adversarial robustness (perturbations)**: Why needed? Ensures model performance degrades gracefully under natural noise. Quick check: Evaluate on text with removed diacritics, shuffled case, etc.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Polish text -> Tokenizer (vocabulary-specific) -> RoBERTa encoder -> [CLS] representation -> Dropout (p=0.1) -> Linear layer (5 outputs) -> Sigmoid -> Thresholding (0.5 default, CRIME adjusted) -> Binary safety decision.

Critical path: The accuracy of the model hinges on (1) the quality of the Polish-pretrained RoBERTa weights, (2) the soft-label training objective, and (3) the threshold calibration step for the Crime category. Any deviation in these elements degrades precision or recall.

Design tradeoffs: The authors prioritize precision over recall to minimize false alarms in live systems, accepting some missed unsafe content. This is achieved by raising the Crime threshold and using an optimal operating point on the precision-recall curve. Model size is another tradeoff: the 0.1B model is chosen for its balance of efficiency and real-world performance, while the larger 0.5B offers better robustness and slightly higher F1 at greater computational cost.

Failure signatures: (1) Unexpectedly high false positive rate on Crime-related prompts indicates threshold misconfiguration; (2) Poor recall on Hate/Aggression suggests the soft-label signal or taxonomy may be insufficient; (3) Large performance drops on perturbed text (e.g., diacritic removal) would contradict the reported robustness, possibly due to tokenization mismatches.

1. Load the published v1.1 checkpoint and run a validation set to confirm the CRIME category threshold is >0.5 and yields the reported 0.63% FPR on a sample of real prompts.
2. Re-train a 0.1B model using hard binary labels (60% majority vote) and compare its precision/FPR to the soft-label version to quantify the benefit of the soft approach.
3. Apply the 15 text augmentation techniques described in the paper to a held-out set and measure the change in F1 and precision for both models to verify robustness claims.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**To what extent do differences in safety taxonomy definitions account for the observed precision disparities between Bielik Guard and multilingual models, independent of linguistic adaptation?**  
The authors explicitly identify cross-taxonomy comparison as a limitation, noting that each model is evaluated against its own definitions of "unsafe". A shared benchmark with a unified taxonomy is needed to isolate the effects of data quality and model architecture from policy scope differences.

### Open Question 2
**Does the soft-label training strategy lead to better model calibration and handling of ambiguous cases compared to traditional hard binary labels, particularly for culturally subjective categories like Hate?**  
While the paper advocates graded labels as modeling bounded rationality, no ablation compares soft vs. hard labeling. Evidence from an ablation study (same architecture, different label schemes) would clarify the contribution of soft labels to the observed precision gains.

### Open Question 3
**How robust are Bielik Guard classifiers to sophisticated adversarial attacks and prompt injection techniques beyond simple character-level perturbations?**  
The current evaluation uses basic augmentations; the authors acknowledge not testing against adversarial attacks. A red-teaming evaluation using attack methods like TextAttack or human-crafted jailbreaks would quantify vulnerability and guide further hardening.

## Limitations
- The exact decision threshold for the Crime category in v1.1 is not disclosed; only a qualitative calibration is described.
- Annotator demographics, expertise, and inter-annotator agreement metrics are unavailable, limiting assessment of label reliability.
- Cross-model comparisons may be confounded by mismatched safety taxonomies, making reported precision gaps potentially misleading.
