# IndicSafe: A Benchmark for Evaluating Multilingual LLM Safety in South Asia

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.17915
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17915
- **Reference count**: 11
- **Primary result**: First benchmark to evaluate LLM safety across 12 Indic languages; reveals severe inconsistencies with cross-language agreement at only 12.8%.

## Executive Summary
IndicSafe introduces the first comprehensive benchmark for evaluating large language model (LLM) safety across twelve Indic languages, addressing a critical gap in multilingual safety research. The benchmark comprises 6,000 culturally specific prompts covering sensitive topics such as caste, religion, gender, and politics, carefully translated by native speakers to preserve sociocultural nuance. Testing ten diverse LLMs—including both open- and closed-source models—reveals substantial safety inconsistencies: cross-language agreement on safety judgments is a mere 12.8%, and the variance in SAFE rates across languages exceeds 17%. Models tend to over-refuse benign queries in low-resource languages while under-flagging harmful content, highlighting the failure of current alignment methods to generalize beyond high-resource contexts. The study calls for culturally informed alignment strategies and provides metrics like the Language Consistency Index and Category Bias Score to quantify multilingual safety drift.

This work underscores that safety alignment is not language-agnostic and exposes the urgent need for inclusion of diverse linguistic and cultural perspectives in AI development. By systematically quantifying safety volatility, IndicSafe sets a foundation for future research in building truly safe multilingual systems.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses the newly created IndicSafe dataset, which contains 500 prompts per language for twelve Indic languages (Hindi, Bengali, Odia, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi, Nepali, Urdu). Each prompt is a human-translated version of an English seed that targets nine harm categories, including caste-based discrimination, religious hate, gender bias, political manipulation, health misinformation, and harmless controls. The authors run inference on ten LLMs (GPT-4o Mini, Claude Sonnet v4, Grok-3, LLaMA 4, LLaMA 3.3, LLaMA 3.1, Qwen1.5-7B-Chat, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, Cohere Command R, Cohere Command A) using standardized API settings (temperature=0.3, max_tokens=200, top_p=0.75). Model outputs are labeled SAFE, UNSAFE, REFUSAL, or AMBIGUOUS by an automated judge powered by GPT-4o, guided by a fixed system prompt. The resulting judgment matrix (60,000 predictions) is analyzed with novel metrics: Cross-Language Consistency Rate, Language Consistency Index (entropy), Category Bias Score, Prompts-Level Entropy, and Intra-Model SAFE% standard deviation. A human annotation subset (5%, 3,000 instances) by bilingual annotators yields Cohen’s κ values up to 0.67, validating the automated judge.

## Key Results
- Cross-language agreement among the ten models is only 12.8%, revealing minimal consistency in safety judgments across languages.
- SAFE rate variance across languages exceeds 17%, with models over-refusing benign queries in low-resource languages (e.g., Odia, Punjabi) and under-flagging harmful content in others.
- Metrics such as Language Consistency Index (LCI) and Category Bias Score show significant drift; e.g., LCI ranges from 0.51 (Grok-3) to 1.35 (Mistral), and Political/Religious categories trigger the highest combined rates of UNSAFE/REFUSAL.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Safety alignment fine-tuned on high-resource languages (like English) does not reliably generalize to low-resource languages, leading to inconsistent safety judgments.
- **Mechanism**: Alignment processes (e.g., RLHF) rely on datasets and reward models that reflect cultural and linguistic norms from high-resource contexts. When applied to languages with different scripts, syntax, and sociocultural reference points (e.g., caste, regional politics), the model's internal safety heuristics fail to map correctly, causing drift.
- **Core assumption**: Safety is not a language-agnostic property; it is embedded in linguistic and cultural context.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "safety alignment does not transfer evenly across languages."
  - [section 6.2] Cross-language agreement is 12.8%, indicating minimal consistency.
  - [section 3.1] Prompts are grounded in South Asian sociocultural dynamics (caste, religion) absent from English benchmarks.
- **Break condition**: If a model were aligned on a perfectly balanced, culturally specific multilingual safety dataset, cross-language agreement would approach 100%. The paper shows this is not the case.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Translation, even by native speakers, can introduce subtle shifts in harm framing and intent that affect safety perception.
- **Mechanism**: While the paper uses human translation to preserve semantic intent, certain culturally loaded terms (e.g., caste names, religious references) may carry different connotations or levels of offensiveness across languages. This leads to a mismatch between the intended harm signal and the model's interpretation.
- **Core assumption**: Direct translation of culturally grounded prompts does not guarantee equivalent "harm salience" across languages.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.2] Translation guidelines aimed for "cultural fidelity" but acknowledge potential for "subtle shifts" (limitations section).
  - [section 6.3] Over-refusal on benign prompts in some languages (e.g., Punjabi, Odia) suggests models perceive translated benign prompts as more harmful than their English originals.
- **Break condition**: If all translations achieved perfect pragmatic equivalence (impossible to verify absolutely), the prompt-level entropy would drop significantly across language variants.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: LLMs develop disparate safety "strategies" (e.g., high refusal vs. high generation) based on their training and tuning, and these strategies are inconsistently applied across languages.
- **Mechanism**: Models differ in their safety thresholds and refusal heuristics. When faced with a linguistically unfamiliar or low-resource language input, a model's existing strategy (e.g., "refuse when uncertain") becomes amplified, leading to over-censorship. Conversely, models with looser alignment may generate unsafe content because their safety classifiers are less active in low-resource scripting contexts.
- **Core assumption**: Model-specific safety tuning interacts with language resource level to produce distinct failure modes.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 6.1] Claude (27% refusal) vs. Qwen (49.5% unsafe) show divergent strategies.
  - [section 6.3] "Over-censorship bias" in Mixtral/Qwen on Tamil/Odia, while Mistral shows high unsafe generation.
  - [section 6.2] SAFE% variance across languages exceeds 17%, and LCI varies from 0.51 (Grok-3) to 1.35 (Mistral), showing model-specific drift patterns.
- **Break condition**: If all models employed a uniform, language-aware safety mechanism calibrated on Indic data, strategy divergence and high variance would diminish.

## Foundational Learning

- **Multilingual Safety Drift**
  - *Why needed here*: The core finding is that a model's safety judgment for the same semantic prompt changes dramatically across language translations. Understanding this drift is essential before any mitigation.
  - *Quick check*: If a prompt about caste superiority is "UNSAFE" in Hindi but "SAFE" in Odia for the same model, what does that imply about the model's internal safety representation?

- **Culturally Grounded Harm Taxonomy**
  - *Why needed here*: The benchmark's categories (caste, religion, politics) are specific to South Asia. Safety evaluation must use harm definitions relevant to the deployment context, not generic toxicity.
  - *Quick check*: Why might a prompt about "religious practices" be harmless in one cultural context but a "Religious Hate" trigger in another?

- **Evaluation Metrics for Volatility**
  - *Why needed here*: Standard accuracy fails to capture instability. Metrics like Cross-Language Agreement, Language Consistency Index (entropy), and Category Bias Score are introduced to quantify the observed failures.
  - *Quick check*: What does a high prompt-level entropy (e.g., >1.8) tell you about a model's safety behavior on that prompt?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Dataset Pipeline -> Inference Engine -> Automated Judge -> Metrics Module -> Validation Subsystem

### Critical Path
1. Build dataset (prompt authoring, translation, review).
2. Run model inference across all languages and models.
3. Generate judge labels for each output.
4. Compute core metrics (Cross-Language Agreement, LCI, etc.).
5. Validate with human annotation subset.
6. Analyze bias and drift.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Automated judge vs. human eval**: GPT-4o enables scale (60k judgments) but may miss nuanced cultural slights; human audit is essential for calibration.
- **Prompt fidelity**: Human translation is costly but necessary; machine translation would likely increase drift.
- **Model diversity**: Testing 10 models provides breadth but increases compute cost; prioritize families with known multilingual claims.

### Failure Signatures
- High AMBIGUOUS rate (>25%) for a specific language (e.g., Odia, Punjabi) → potential tokenization or training data gap.
- Refusal rate >40% on Harmless Control prompts in a language → over-alignment/under-exposure.
- Cross-language agreement <20% → fundamental misalignment in safety concept.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline Consistency Check**: Run one model (e.g., GPT-4o Mini) on the full 6k prompts. Compute Cross-Language Agreement and LCI. Expect ~12.8% agreement, LCI ~0.8 per paper.
2. **Category Deep Dive**: Isolate one harm category (e.g., "Gender & Caste Discrimination"). Compute average UNSAFE vs. REFUSAL rates across languages for all models. Look for patterns like over-refusal in low-resource languages.
3. **Judge Validation**: Sample 100 prompt-model-language triples. Have bilingual annotators label. Compare with GPT-4o judgments. If κ < 0.6, recalibrate judge prompt or add few-shot examples.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **To what extent do subtle translation nuances and cultural contextualization in Indic languages contribute to the observed 12.8% cross-language agreement rate and high ambiguity in low-resource languages like Odia and Punjabi?**  
   *Basis in paper*: The paper details a manual translation process with native speakers and review (Section 3.2, Appendix A.1-A.2), noting that "literal translations were avoided in favor of preserving intent" and that review replaced 28 prompts. It reports high ambiguity rates for specific languages (e.g., Qwen in Odia: 53.8%).  
   *Why unresolved*: The benchmark uses translated prompts, so the paper cannot disentangle whether safety drift stems from (a) inherent linguistic/cultural differences in prompt interpretation or (b) artifacts/inconsistencies introduced during translation, despite review. The limitation section explicitly cites "subtle shifts in tone or intent may persist across languages."  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: A controlled study using parallel, back-translated prompts with multiple independent translator variants per language, coupled with fine-grained error analysis of where model judgments diverge relative to human consensus on the original English intent.

2. **How reliable is an LLM-based automated judge (GPT-4o) for evaluating safety in culturally specific, low-resource languages, given its possible cultural blind spots and the reported 8.25% false negative rate?**  
   *Basis in paper*: The authors use GPT-4o as a judge and validate it with human annotation (Section 4.3, Appendix A.6), reporting Cohen's κ as high as 0.67 but noting variation. Their error analysis finds 8.25% false negatives (unsafe responses labeled SAFE).  
   *Why unresolved*: The judge itself is a high-resource, predominantly English-aligned model. The paper acknowledges this limitation: "our use of GPT-4o as an automatic safety judge... may still miss nuanced harms or cultural offenses." The judge's own cultural biases could systematically mislabel safety in Indic contexts.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: A multi-judge study comparing GPT-4o judgments against a large, diverse panel of native-speaking human experts across all 12 languages and harm categories, analyzing discrepancies by language resource level and harm type to quantify judge bias.

3. **What specific characteristics of pretraining data (e.g., script representation, domain distribution, geographic sourcing) for different LLM families explain the divergent safety strategies (e.g., Claude's high refusal vs. Qwen's high unsafe rate) and the non-uniform safety drift across Indic languages?**  
   *Basis in paper*: The paper shows dramatic model-level differences in safety behavior (Table 5) and language-level drift (Table 6). It hypothesizes that "safety alignment does not transfer evenly across languages" due to "uneven web/Wikipedia coverage, orthographic variation, and fragmented digital resources" for Indic languages (Section 3.2). However, model training data details are opaque.  
   *Why unresolved*: The study uses black-box or partially-specified models (Table 8). Without access to training corpora metadata (language proportions, regional sources, safety-tuned data), the root cause of *why* models generalize safety differently remains speculative. The discussion urges "language-aware alignment strategies" but does not identify which data gaps are most predictive.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Correlational analysis between model safety metrics (e.g., Language Consistency Index, category bias) and quantifiable proxies for training data diversity/size for each Indic language (e.g., measured via dedicated Indic vs. global web crawls, presence in safety tuning sets) across multiple model families.

4. **Why do certain harm categories (e.g., Political Manipulation, Religious Hate) consistently trigger higher rates of REFUSAL or UNSAFE labels across models, and is this driven by genuine higher risk perception, cultural unfamiliarity, or overcautious alignment defaults?**  
   *Basis in paper*: Figure 5 and Section 6.3 show that Political and Religious categories have the highest combined rates of UNSAFE/REFUSAL. The paper notes "over-cautious alignment or lack of domain trust" and that models "overflag politically sensitive topics."  
   *Why unresolved*: The paper cannot determine if high refusal/unsafe rates correctly reflect the heightened real-world risk of these categories in Indic contexts or if they represent a model bias (e.g., from English-centric safety data over-indexing on politics/religion). The motivation mentions "caste, religion, gender" as key factors, but category-specific calibration is unknown.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: A targeted study comparing model safety judgments on the *same* high-risk category prompts (e.g., political misinformation) across languages against a gold-standard, culturally-nuanced harm rubric co-created with regional experts. This would test if high refusal is proportional to actual harm.

## Limitations
- Dataset and exact judge prompt are unreleased; translation fidelity cannot be fully verified.
- GPT-4o judge may impose English-centric safety norms, potentially under-detecting culturally specific harms.
- The cross-linguistic nature of the benchmark makes it difficult to isolate whether drift arises from linguistic factors or cultural nuance differences.

## Confidence
- Safety drift across languages: High
- Culturally grounded harm taxonomy: High
- Judge cultural neutrality: Medium
- Over-refusal in low-resource languages: High
- Generalizability beyond South Asia: Low
