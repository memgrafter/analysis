# Representation Collapse in Machine Translation Through the Lens of Angular Dispersion

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17287
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17287
- **Reference count:** 12
- **Primary result:** Angular dispersion regularization effectively mitigates representation collapse in Transformer-based neural machine translation, leading to improved translation quality (BLEU/COMET) and robustness to quantization.

## Executive Summary
Representation collapse—where internal representations become degenerate—is a known issue in deep neural networks, particularly in Transformer-based machine translation. This paper investigates collapse in encoder-decoder models, observing that deeper layers and continuous-output variants are especially vulnerable. To counteract collapse, the authors introduce angular dispersion regularization (sliced dispersion), a computationally efficient objective that promotes uniform coverage of the hyperspherical embedding space. The regularization term is applied to decoder embeddings or encoder outputs during training and combined with the standard NMT loss. Experiments on WMT English–German and Romanian–English demonstrate that the regularizer reduces collapse as measured by higher Rényi entropy, increased spherical variance, and lower average cosine similarity. Translation quality improves in BLEU and COMET scores, with notable gains for rare tokens. In continuous-output NMT—prone to a trivial solution where all vectors collapse—the regularization prevents complete collapse and yields viable translation performance. Moreover, the benefits persist after post‑training quantization, indicating robustness.

## Method Summary
The proposed method adds a regularization term *R(Z)* to the training objective, where *Z* is a matrix of normalized representations (e.g., decoder input embeddings *E*, encoder output *F*). Sliced dispersion estimates the angular dispersion of the vectors by projecting them onto randomly sampled great circles on the hypersphere and measuring deviation from an equidistant distribution. This avoids the quadratic cost of pairwise comparisons. The total loss becomes *L = L<sub>NMT</sub> + γ·R(Z)* for standard NMT, or *L = L<sub>CoNMT</sub> + γ·R(Z)* for continuous-output NMT, with γ controlling regularization strength. During training, the regularization is computed per batch on subsampled representations to reduce overhead. The approach is evaluated on Transformer‑base and Transformer‑big models, using metrics such as average cosine similarity, Rényi entropy (of the eigenvalue spectrum), and spherical variance to quantify collapse. Translation quality is measured with BLEU and COMET, and rare token performance is analyzed separately.

## Key Results
- Angular dispersion regularization reduces representation collapse significantly: higher Rényi entropy and spherical variance, lower average cosine similarity in targeted components.
- Translation quality improves (BLEU and COMET gains), with particularly strong improvements on rare token translation.
- In continuous-output NMT, the regularizer prevents complete collapse (which yields BLEU=0 without regularization) and produces finite translation quality.
- The benefits are retained after post‑training quantization, showing robustness to reduced precision.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper provides three complementary explanations for the effectiveness of angular dispersion regularization:

1. **Direct mitigation of dimensional collapse**: The regularization term *R(Z)* explicitly promotes uniform angular distribution on the hypersphere. By minimizing the expected distance to an optimally dispersed configuration, it counteracts the tendency of token vectors to clump (high cosine similarity) or occupy a low-dimensional subspace (low eigenvalue entropy). Evidence: applying *R* reduces average cosine similarity and increases entropy/spherical variance (Section 4.4). However, excessive regularization weight γ can degrade task performance, indicating a trade‑off between dispersion and semantic fidelity.

2. **Buffering effect of model dimensionality**: Larger models (e.g., Transformer‑big) exhibit naturally lower collapse than smaller ones (Transformer‑base). The authors hypothesize that higher representation dimensions provide more capacity to distribute variance, making collapse less likely. This suggests that dimension acts as an intrinsic regularizer, though it is insufficient in extreme settings like continuous‑output training.

3. **Stabilization of continuous‑output training**: The CoNMT loss (1 – cos(*E*, *H*)) has a global minimum when all target embeddings *E* become identical. Adding *R(E)* introduces a competing force that maintains separation between token vectors on the hypersphere, preventing the trivial collapse solution and enabling the model to learn meaningful regressors.

## Foundational Learning
To interpret the paper’s findings, several concepts are essential:

- **Representation Collapse Metrics**: Measures such as average cosine similarity (lower is better), Rényi entropy of the eigenvalue spectrum (higher is better), and spherical variance (higher is better) quantify how degenerate internal representations are. Rényi entropy, computed from the eigenvalues of the representation covariance matrix, measures the effective dimensionality; low entropy indicates variance concentrated in few dimensions (collapse). Spherical variance, defined as the average squared Euclidean distance to the mean vector on the hypersphere, ranges from 0 (all vectors identical) to 1 (maximally spread). *Why needed*: The paper’s central claim relies on demonstrating that dispersion regularization improves these metrics. *Quick check*: For a normalized representation matrix *Z*, if all rows are identical, spherical variance is near 0; if rows are antipodal or uniformly distributed, spherical variance approaches 1.

- **Hyperspherical Geometry & Angular Dispersion**: Normalized representations lie on the unit sphere *S<sup>d‑1</sup>*. Angular dispersion quantifies how uniformly vectors cover this sphere. Sliced dispersion specifically estimates dispersion by projecting vectors onto randomly sampled great circles (the intersection of the sphere with a 2D plane through the origin) and measuring the deviation of the resulting 1D angles from a uniform distribution. Maximizing dispersion ensures the full ambient space is utilized, preventing dimensional collapse. *Why needed*: The sliced dispersion regularizer operates directly on this geometry, and its efficiency stems from measuring dispersion via these 1D projections rather than all pairwise angles, reducing computational complexity. *Quick check*: Why is optimizing uniformity on the sphere computationally more feasible than in Euclidean space? Because the sphere’s constant norm bounds pairwise distances, and sliced dispersion reduces complexity from *O(N²)* to *O(N)* by sampling a linear number of random 2D planes and aggregating 1D statistics.

- **Continuous‑Output NMT (CoNMT)**: A variant of NMT where the model predicts a continuous embedding vector for each target token instead of discrete logits over a vocabulary. The loss is typically a cosine similarity (e.g., 1 − cos(*pred*, *target*)) between predicted and target embeddings. *Why needed*: CoNMT is particularly prone to collapse because the loss has a trivial global optimum where all target token embeddings become identical (any prediction matching this common vector yields zero loss for all tokens). Standard NMT’s softmax loss, in contrast, includes a normalization over the entire vocabulary, which inherently penalizes similarity between different token embeddings and encourages separation. This paper shows that adding dispersion regularization to CoNMT prevents collapse and yields viable translation performance. *Quick check*: What is the fundamental optimization difference that makes CoNMT collapse‑prone? Standard NMT’s multinomial logistic loss has a built‑in repulsion via the denominator, while CoNMT’s cosine regression loss only measures alignment and lacks any term that forces different tokens apart; thus, the model can collapse to a single embedding for all tokens without penalty.

## Architecture Onboarding
The method integrates seamlessly into standard Transformer training:

**Component map**: The system consists of: (1) Core Transformer (encoder‑decoder) that outputs three key tensors: decoder hidden states *H* (from decoder stack), decoder input embeddings *E* (token embeddings plus positional), and encoder output *F*. (2) Regularization Module that computes the sliced dispersion objective *R(Z)* on a chosen normalized representation *Z* (subsampled from *E*, *H*, or *F*); it samples random orthogonal projection pairs to define great circles, projects vectors onto them, and measures deviation from equidistant angles. (3) Training Loop that adds *γ·R(Z)* to the primary loss (standard NMT loss *L<sub>NMT</sub>* or continuous‑output loss *L<sub>CoNMT</sub>*) and updates parameters per batch. (4) Evaluation that reports collapse metrics (average cosine similarity, Rényi entropy, spherical variance) and translation quality (BLEU, COMET).

**Critical path**:
1. Identify which representation exhibits collapse (empirically often deeper decoder layers, rare token embeddings, or encoder output in small models).
2. Select the regularization target *Z* (e.g., *R(E)* for rare tokens, *R(F)* for encoder).
3. Tune the regularization weight γ on development data; sensitivity differs by component (*R(F)* typically requires lower γ).
4. Integrate *R(Z)* into the loss, ensuring *Z* is normalized before dispersion calculation.
5. Monitor collapse metrics during training to avoid over‑dispersion (e.g., spherical variance too high can be harmful).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Computational cost: Sliced dispersion is *O(N)* vs. *O(N²)* for pairwise, but still significant; subsampling (e.g., only rare tokens for *R(E)*) reduces overhead.
- Component choice: Regularizing *E* aids rare tokens without harming decoder output diversity; regularizing *F* helps encoder but may decrease diversity in *H*.
- Regularization weight γ: Needs per‑component tuning; too low ineffective, too high damages semantic structure.
- Quantization: Benefits persist after post‑training quantization, but quantization‑aware training with dispersion is unexplored.

**Failure signatures**:
- Collapse metrics remain poor: average cosine similarity >0.5, spherical variance/entropy low after regularization.
- BLEU/COMET drop relative to baseline, especially on frequent tokens; rare‑token F1 unchanged.
- Training instability: loss diverges or becomes NaN with excessive γ (especially for *R(F)*).
- Quantized models (e.g., int8_float16) still underperform fp32 baseline despite regularization.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline collapse profiling** on Transformer‑base (En‑De) without regularization; compute metrics for *E*, *H*, *F* at steps 10k/20k/30k/40k/50k.
2. **Component‑specific regularization**: Apply *R(E)* to Transformer‑big; compare metrics and BLEU/COMET to baseline; expect reduced cosine similarity for *E* and BLEU gain, especially on rare tokens.
3. **Quantization robustness**: Post‑quantize baseline and +*R(E)* models to int8_float16; evaluate on test set; expect both degrade but +*R(E)* retains relative advantage.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Combining angular dispersion across multiple layers**: The paper only regularizes a single representation matrix at a time. Jointly regularizing several layers could yield further benefits, but risks cross‑layer interference. *What evidence would resolve*: Train models with dispersion applied simultaneously to several layers, vary the per‑layer weights, and measure both collapse metrics and translation quality to identify non‑interfering combinations.
2. **Low‑resource and multilingual settings**: The experiments are limited to high‑resource language pairs. It is unknown whether gains extend to low‑resource or multilingual NMT, where rare‑token representations are even more critical. *What evidence would resolve*: Apply the regularizer to low‑resource pairs (e.g., from FLORES) and multilingual models, compare rare‑token F1 and overall BLEU/COMET.
3. **Reduced‑precision training**: The paper only tests post‑training quantization and a basic fp16 comparison. Systematic study of representation collapse under different training precisions (fp16, bf16, int8) and whether dispersion regularization mitigates any introduced collapse is lacking. *What evidence would resolve*: Train models at various precisions with and without dispersion, monitor collapse metrics throughout training, and report final translation quality.

## Limitations
- The hypothesized buffering effect of model dimensionality is based on a comparison of two model sizes; no systematic ablation isolates its causal role.
- The claim that hyperspherical uniformity directly improves Euclidean space utilization lacks rigorous proof or ablation; other factors could drive the observed gains.
- Evaluation is limited to two European language pairs; generalization to typologically distant languages (e.g., English–Japanese) is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Dispersion regularization mitigates collapse:** High confidence.
- **Larger models naturally resist collapse:** Medium confidence.
- **Regularization prevents CoNMT trivial solution:** Low‑to‑Medium confidence.

## Next Checks
1. **Probe CoNMT loss landscape**: Visualize the loss surface of a small CoNMT model with and without *R(E)* around the trivial solution (all embeddings equal). Verify that *R(E)* raises the loss basin’s floor, making collapse non‑optimal.
2. **Dimensionality ablation**: Train a series of models identical except for hidden dimension (e.g., 256, 512, 1024) without regularization. Plot collapse metrics (cosine similarity, entropy, variance) against dimension to test the hypothesized positive correlation.
3. **Cross‑linguistic generalization**: Replicate the main experiment (+*R(E)* vs. baseline) on a typologically distant language pair (e.g., English–Chinese). If gains disappear, the universality of hyperspherical uniformity as a prior is questionable.
