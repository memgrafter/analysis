# FinRule-Bench: A Benchmark for Joint Reasoning over Financial Tables and Principles

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11339
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11339
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs achieve high accuracy on single-rule verification (e.g., GPT-4o: 73.6% few-shot) but performance degrades sharply for rule identification and joint diagnosis (exact match as low as 23.4%). Causal-counterfactual prompting improves localization for complex statements at high token cost. Errors are dominated by incomplete coverage and mislocalization, exposing systematic failures in exhaustive, record-level reasoning.

## Executive Summary
FinRule-Bench introduces a benchmark for evaluating large language models on rule-based financial auditing. The benchmark defines three progressively challenging tasks—rule verification, rule identification, and joint rule diagnosis—using real-world financial tables and accounting principles encoded as deterministic validators. Models perform well on isolated compliance checks but struggle with multi‑rule localization, revealing limitations in handling interacting constraints. A causal‑counterfactual prompting technique modestly improves diagnostic completeness for complex statements while drastically increasing token usage. The findings highlight the need for more robust reasoning mechanisms in financial applications.

## Method Summary
The dataset comprises clean, real-world financial statements (Balance Sheets, Cash Flow, Income Statements, Statements of Equity). Each accounting rule is implemented as a deterministic validator function that assesses compliance on a table. Controlled error injection applies minimal, reversible edits (e.g., adjusting a numeric value) to create single- or multi‑violation instances with unambiguous ground truth. The three tasks are: (i) verification (binary compliance check for a given rule), (ii) identification (select the violated rule from a candidate set), and (iii) joint diagnosis (detect all violated rules and map them to specific records). Models are evaluated under zero-shot, few-shot, and few-shot with causal‑counterfactual prompting—where exemplars include a causal explanation of the violation and a minimal counterfactual edit that would restore compliance. Metrics include accuracy for the first two tasks and for joint diagnosis: Step‑1 detection accuracy and Step‑2 exact match (full rule‑record mapping), together with token cost analysis.

## Key Results
- Single‑rule verification reaches up to 73.6% accuracy (GPT‑4o, few‑shot), showing LLMs can handle isolated compliance checks.
- Performance drops sharply for rule identification and joint diagnosis; the best model (GPT‑4o) attains only 34.2% exact match on joint diagnosis with causal‑counterfactual prompting, and as low as 23.4% without it.
- Errors are dominated by incomplete coverage (missing some violations) and mislocalization (assigning correct rule to wrong record), especially for conditional and multi‑record rules.
- Causal‑counterfactual prompting yields modest improvements in localization for complex statements (e.g., Income Statements) but increases token usage by a factor of 2–3, with inconsistent gains or even degradations for some models on simpler tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Progressive Task Formulation
**Claim:** FinRule-Bench introduces three auditing tasks that require progressively stronger reasoning capabilities, enabling evaluation of diagnostic completeness.  
**Mechanism:** The tasks (rule verification, rule identification, joint rule diagnosis) are designed to test rule compliance, discrimination, and multi‑violation localization, respectively. Each task builds on the previous by adding complexity: single‑rule binary check → selecting one violated rule from a set → detecting and localizing all violations across records.  
**Core assumption:** Reasoning abilities can be decomposed into these distinct capabilities; performance degradation across tasks indicates specific reasoning limitations (e.g., from verification to joint diagnosis).  
**Break condition:** If models show similar performance across all tasks, the progression fails to differentiate reasoning facets; or if tasks are too easy/hard, they may not reveal the intended granularity of failures.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: "FinRule-Bench defines three auditing tasks that require progressively stronger reasoning capabilities: (i) rule verification ... (ii) rule identification ... (iii) joint rule diagnosis ..."  
- [section 3.4]: Formal task definitions with precise input/output specifications.  
- [corpus]: Neighbors like "Automating Financial Statement Audits with Large Language Models" focus on end‑to‑end auditing but lack progressive diagnostic breakdown; FinRule-Bench explicitly isolates these capabilities.

### Mechanism 2: Causal‑Counterfactual Prompting
**Claim:** Causal‑counterfactual prompting improves diagnostic localization for complex statements by enforcing consistency between decisions, explanations, and counterfactual judgments.  
**Mechanism:** In few‑shot exemplars, the model is shown not only the correct decision but also a causal explanation of why a rule is violated and a minimal counterfactual edit (e.g., changing one numeric value) that would restore compliance. This scaffolds models to reason about causal dependencies and coverage, particularly for multi‑violation scenarios.  
**Core assumption:** Exposing models to explicit causal structure during in‑context learning enhances their ability to perform exhaustive, record‑level diagnosis and reduces incomplete coverage/mislocalization.  
**Break condition:** If the prompting leads to degraded accuracy or excessive token cost without meaningful gains (e.g., Table 2 shows inconsistent improvements for rule identification), or if models merely mimic surface patterns without internalizing causal logic.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: "We introduce a causal‑counterfactual reasoning protocol that enforces consistency between decisions, explanations, and counterfactual judgments."  
- [section 3.5]: Describes how exemplars vary across tasks, with detailed causal and counterfactual components for joint diagnosis.  
- [section 4.2.1]: Shows that causal‑counterfactual prompting yields largest relative gains in Step‑2 exact‑match for joint diagnosis, especially for complex statements like Income Statements.  
- [corpus]: No neighbor uses such structured prompting for financial rule reasoning; this is a novel diagnostic tool.

### Mechanism 3: Deterministic Validators & Controlled Error Injection
**Claim:** Using deterministic validators and controlled, rule‑aware error injection ensures reliable ground truth and isolates reasoning fidelity from data artifacts.  
**Mechanism:** Each accounting rule has an executable validator function that returns a binary truth value on a financial table. Clean, real‑world tables are perturbed with minimal edits that invert a specific validator (e.g., adjusting a number to break an arithmetic identity) while preserving structure. This creates instances with unambiguous, reproducible violations.  
**Core assumption:** Ground truth can be objectively defined programmatically, and minimal edits maintain realism while introducing predictable failures for evaluation.  
**Break condition:** If validators miss edge cases or disagree with human judgment, ground truth is compromised; if error injection unintentionally introduces multiple violations or distorts table semantics, it confounds the evaluation.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 3.2]: "Each rule r∈R is associated with a deterministic validator function v_r(T) ∈ {True,False}."  
- [section 3.3]: "Error injection is carried out ... with respect to two task formulation‑based regimes ... deterministic and reversible."  
- [section 3.6]: "Ground‑truth labels are generated exclusively by deterministic rule validators."  
- [section E.1]: Human verification (Cohen’s κ > 93%) validates the deterministic process.  
- [corpus]: Prior benchmarks (e.g., FinQA, TAT‑QA) often rely on synthetic corruption or QA pairs; FinRule‑Bench’s validator‑driven approach is more controlled.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Accounting Principles as Formal Rules
**Why needed:** FinRule-Bench encodes GAAP/IFRS‑style principles as deterministic validators. Understanding that rules are precise (e.g., “Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Total Equity”) is crucial to grasp what the model must verify.  
**Quick check question:** What does the validator for BS‑01 check, and how would a minimal edit break it?

### Concept: Progressive Diagnostic Tasks
**Why needed:** The three tasks form a difficulty gradient. Recognizing that verification is binary compliance, identification requires rule discrimination, and joint diagnosis demands exhaustive multi‑rule coverage explains why performance drops sharply.  
**Quick check question:** Which task is most affected by “incomplete coverage” errors and why?

### Concept: Causal‑Counterfactual Reasoning in Prompts
**Why needed:** This prompting strategy is central to the paper’s diagnostic protocol. It goes beyond standard few-shot by embedding cause (why violation occurred) and minimal repair (what change would fix it).  
**Quick check question:** How does a counterfactual exemplar differ from a standard Chain‑of‑Thought example in this benchmark?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
tables → rules → validators → error injection → prompts → LLM → output parser → evaluation → results

**Critical path:**  
1. Choose statement type (BS/CF/SE/SI) and task (verification/identification/diagnosis).  
2. Load a clean financial table and the applicable set of rules.  
3. Apply error injection (if evaluating a corrupted instance) to obtain a perturbed table and the ground‑truth violation set.  
4. Construct the prompt (with or without exemplars) and query the LLM.  
5. Parse the model’s response into a structured prediction (binary answer, rule ID, or list of rule‑record mappings).  
6. Run the deterministic validators on the perturbed table to generate ground‑truth labels.  
7. Compare predictions to ground truth using exact match or F1, record token usage and other diagnostics.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Token cost vs. accuracy:* Causal‑counterfactual prompts are verbose (up to 3× tokens) but yield only modest improvements for joint diagnosis on complex statements; they provide little benefit for verification and can even harm identification.  
- *Rule‑set size:* Identification accuracy declines as more rules are considered, with multi‑record rules being particularly challenging because they span multiple rows/sections.  
- *Error‑injection minimalism:* Single‑value perturbations preserve table realism but multi‑violation injection must carefully avoid unintended side effects that create additional violations, which could confuse the evaluation.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Incomplete coverage:* The model detects some but not all violated rules in a record (most common in joint diagnosis).  
- *Mislocalization:* The correct rule is identified but assigned to the wrong record or section.  
- *Rule discrimination errors:* In identification tasks, models confuse similar rules, especially non‑arithmetic ones.  
- *Token explosion:* Causal‑counterfactual prompting can massively increase token counts without proportional accuracy gains, particularly for stronger models.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Run GPT‑4o on all three tasks with zero‑shot and standard few‑shot prompts; reproduce the accuracy gaps reported in Table 2 (e.g., verification ~73.6% vs. joint diagnosis ~30%).  
2. **Complexity stratification:** For joint diagnosis, split test instances by rule category (arithmetic, structural, conditional, multi‑record) and compute per‑category F1; confirm that multi‑record rules yield the lowest scores.  
3. **Prompting trade‑off analysis:** Compare token count versus balanced accuracy for rule identification across statement types; verify that causal‑counterfactual prompting harms performance on simpler statements (e.g., Balance Sheets) while offering limited gains on complex ones.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can LLMs be systematically improved to achieve complete and accurate **joint rule diagnosis** (detecting and localizing all violations in multi‑violation financial statements), given the persistent gap between detection and exact‑match localization (e.g., Step‑2 EM as low as 14.1% for LLaMA on SE)?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper states: "performance degrades sharply for rule discrimination and multi‑violation diagnosis" and "Errors are dominated by incomplete coverage and mislocalization." Table 2 shows Step‑2 Exact Match maxes at 34.2% (GPT‑4o + CR). The conclusion reiterates: "Joint diagnosis in particular reveals a gap between detecting violations and achieving complete, record‑level localization."  
**Why unresolved:** Current prompting (including causal‑counterfactual) provides only modest gains for localization at substantial token cost. The fundamental reasoning failure—exhaustively composing constraints across records—is not addressed by existing methods.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Development and evaluation of novel architectures or training paradigms (e.g., neurosymbolic integration, constrained decoding) that explicitly optimize for multi‑rule coverage and precise attribution, validated on FinRule‑Bench’s joint diagnosis task with statistically significant improvements in Exact Match without prohibitive token cost.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Why does **causal‑counterfactual prompting** yield inconsistent performance effects—often helping smaller models (Gemini 2.0 Flash, LLaMA) but sometimes degrading performance for stronger reasoning models (GPT‑4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro)—especially on rule identification?  
**Basis in paper:** Section 4.2.1 states: "causal‑counterfactual prompting yields its clearest benefits for lightweight models... In contrast, reasoning‑oriented models... exhibit inconsistent gains, and in some cases degraded performance, particularly on rule identification." Tables 13‑16 show accuracy drops for GPT‑4o on CF/SE/SI with +CR vs. few‑shot.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper hypothesizes "explicit causal scaffolding can introduce redundancy or interfere with discriminative decision‑making in models that already perform extensive internal reasoning," but provides no mechanistic analysis or ablation to confirm this. The interaction between model scale, inherent reasoning process, and external causal structure is not understood.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments isolating the effect of causal explanation length, logical formality, and counterfactual specificity on models with known reasoning capacities (e.g., via internal activation analysis or probing), coupled with human evaluations of prompt clarity, to determine the optimal structure and quantity of causal scaffolding for different model classes.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** To what extent do the failure patterns on **conditional and multi‑record rules** (the most challenging categories) stem from a model's inability to perform true **compositional reasoning over interdependent constraints**, versus lacking domain‑specific knowledge about accounting rule applicability and hierarchy?  
**Basis in paper:** Rule complexity analysis (Tables 3‑5) shows conditional and multi‑record rules have the lowest accuracies. For joint diagnosis, multi‑record rules have the lowest Step‑1 accuracy (57.6%) among categories for GPT‑4o. The paper notes: "performance degrades systematically as tasks require reasoning over contextual applicability and cross‑record dependencies."  
**Why unresolved:** The benchmark uses human‑curated rules but does not disentangle pure logical composition from embedded accounting semantics. It is unclear if models fail because they cannot chain logical dependencies (e.g., "if condition X, then Y must hold") or because they misinterpret accounting‑specific conditions (e.g., what constitutes a "Non‑current asset" or a "mezzanine equity" item). The rules are presented in natural language, blending logic and domain knowledge.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Designing a controlled orthogonal variation: create parallel rule sets with identical logical structure but different domain contexts (e.g., accounting vs. generic inventory management) and measure performance drop. Alternatively, providing models with explicit, symbolic representations of rule conditions (beyond natural language descriptions) and testing if performance improves, isolating the logical composition challenge.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** How do models' **diagnostic reasoning** capabilities on FinRule‑Bench correlate with, or fail to correlate with, their general reasoning abilities as measured by other benchmarks (e.g., logical deduction, mathematical reasoning, or table QA)?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper positions FinRule‑Bench as evaluating "rule‑governed reasoning" and "diagnostic completeness," which are distinct from QA or numerical reasoning. However, it does not compare model rankings or failure modes to other benchmarks. The observed non‑linear degradation from verification to joint diagnosis suggests a specific skill gap. The causal‑counterfactual prompting's mixed effectiveness hints that general reasoning strengths may not transfer directly to exhaustive, constraint‑based auditing.  
**Why unresolved:** Without a correlational study, we cannot tell if a model that excels on logical puzzle datasets (e.g., LogicGame) would also excel at FinRule‑Bench's joint diagnosis, or if the financial domain specifics and record‑level localization requirement create a unique hurdle. This affects whether progress on FinRule‑Bench requires domain‑specific training or general reasoning breakthroughs.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Running a suite of state‑of‑the‑art LLMs on both FinRule‑Bench and a diverse set of established reasoning benchmarks (e.g., BBH, MATH, logical entailment datasets, table QA like TAT‑QA). Perform correlation and factor analysis to determine if FinRule‑Bench performance loads onto a "diagnostic/exhaustive reasoning" factor separate from "general knowledge" or "single‑step inference" factors.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** How robust are the conclusions about model failure modes (incomplete coverage, mislocalization) to the **assumption of perfectly clean, complete input tables**? Would the introduction of realistic noise—such as OCR errors, missing rows/columns, or minor formatting inconsistencies from real‑world document parsing—dramatically alter the error profile and relative effectiveness of prompting strategies?  
**Basis in paper:** The Limitations section states: "FinRule‑Bench evaluates... under the assumption that... financial tables are complete and internally coherent... It does not address... reasoning under noisy or missing data." The dataset construction uses "ground‑truth financial statements" extracted and validated to be clean (Section 3.1, 3.2). Error injection is "minimal" and "rule‑aware," preserving structure.  
**Why unresolved:** Real‑world financial data ingestion is messy. Models might fail differently—e.g., false positives from noise vs. false negatives from complex rules. The benchmark’s clean setting may overestimate model capability for practical deployment and may misattribute failures purely to reasoning rather than robustness. The performance‑cost trade‑offs of causal prompting under noise are also unknown.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Creating a "noisy FinRule‑Bench" variant by simulating realistic extraction errors (random cell corruption, row omission, label misalignment) at varying severity. Re‑running key experiments (especially joint diagnosis) to see if (1) overall accuracy drops more for certain error types, (2) the dominant failure mode shifts (e.g., mislocalization becomes even worse), and (3) the benefit of causal‑counterfactual prompting diminishes or changes, indicating its utility may be contingent on pristine input structure.

## Limitations
- **Uncertainty 1:** Exact few‑shot exemplar content for each task/statement type is not documented in the paper (artifact reliance), hindering exact replication of prompt conditions.  
- **Uncertainty 2:** Inference hyperparameters beyond temperature=0 (e.g., max_tokens, top_p, presence/frequency penalties) and specific model endpoints/versions are unreported, which could affect token counts and minor output variations.  
- **Uncertainty 3:** Deterministic output parsing logic (e.g., regex for extracting ‘YES/NO’, rule identifiers, handling list formats) is not described, risking inconsistent evaluation across implementations.

- **Causal‑Counterfactual Prompting:** Medium  
- **Deterministic Validators & Error Injection:** High
