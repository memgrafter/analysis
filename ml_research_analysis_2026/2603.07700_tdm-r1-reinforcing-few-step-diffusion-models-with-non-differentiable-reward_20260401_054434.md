# TDM-R1: Reinforcing Few-Step Diffusion Models with Non-Differentiable Reward

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07700  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07700  
- **Reference count:** 15  
- **Primary result:** With only 4 NFEs, TDM-R1 raises SD3.5-M GenEval from 61% to 92% and OCR accuracy from 55% to 95%, outperforming the 80‑step base model and GPT‑4o.

## Executive Summary
TDM-R1 introduces a reinforcement learning framework that enables few‑step diffusion models to optimize non‑differentiable rewards (e.g., OCR accuracy, GenEval, human preferences). It exploits deterministic sampling trajectories from Trajectory Distribution Matching (TDM) to assign accurate per‑step rewards, and decouples training into two phases: learning a differentiable surrogate reward via group preference optimization, and updating the generator with reward maximization and reverse KL regularization. On SD3.5‑M, the method achieves dramatic gains with just 4 NFEs, surpassing both the many‑step base model and GPT‑4o on in‑domain metrics while also improving out‑of‑domain qualities like aesthetic score. Ablations confirm the necessity of deterministic paths and a dynamic surrogate reference. The approach scales to other backbones (Z‑Image) and consistently outperforms direct RL and distilled RL baselines.

## Method Summary
TDM‑R1 operates in iterative rounds. For each condition *c* from the dataset, it generates a group of *G* images using deterministic TDM trajectories to ensure low‑variance intermediate states. The final images are evaluated with a non‑differentiable scalar reward *r(c, x₀)*. These rewards are normalized into advantages and used to split the group into positive and negative subsets. The surrogate reward network *p*φ, itself parameterized as a diffusion model, is updated via a group preference loss that encourages its transition distributions to align with the advantage‑weighted partitions. The generator *p*θ is then updated by maximizing the expected surrogate reward on noisy steps *xₜₖ* while regularizing with a reverse KL divergence towards a dynamic reference model (the EMA of *p*φ). This decoupled design avoids the need for differentiable true rewards and stabilizes training.

## Key Results
- On SD3.5‑M (4 NFEs): GenEval 92% (↑31%), OCR 95% (↑40%) vs the 4‑step base; surpasses the 80‑step base (63% GenEval, 59% OCR) and GPT‑4o (84% GenEval).  
- Out‑of‑domain metrics improve: aesthetic score 5.45, ImageReward 1.11.  
- On Z‑Image (4 NFEs): 77% GenEval, 79% OCR, beating the 100‑step variant (66% GenEval, 74% OCR).  
- Ablations verify that deterministic trajectories and the dynamic EMA reference are critical; TDM‑R1 outperforms direct RL and distilled RL baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
TDM‑R1’s success stems from three key insights. First, deterministic TDM trajectories yield nearly identical per‑step log‑probability changes across samples, allowing the final reward to be accurately distributed to intermediate denoising steps with low variance (Eq. 6). Second, the decoupled two‑phase training circumvents the non‑differentiability of real‑world metrics: a surrogate reward *p*φ is learned from relative preferences within a group, producing a smooth, differentiable objective for the generator. Third, reverse KL regularization to a dynamic EMA reference prevents the generator from collapsing or overfitting to the surrogate, maintaining sample diversity and quality. The combination yields a stable RL algorithm that can leverage arbitrary scalar feedback.

## Foundational Learning
- **Trajectory Distribution Matching (TDM)** – Provides deterministic, few‑step sampling paths. *Why needed:* Enables low‑variance reward attribution to intermediate states. *Quick check:* Are generation trajectories deterministic? (Yes)  
- **Non‑differentiable rewards** – Metrics like OCR or human preferences lack gradients. *Why needed:* The method must optimize such signals. *Quick check:* Can the reward be differentiated w.r.t. the image? (No)  
- **Group preference optimization** – Trains *p*φ on relative advantages within a sample group. *Why needed:* Converts non‑differentiable rewards into a differentiable surrogate. *Quick check:* Is the surrogate trained on absolute values or pairwise preferences? (Pairwise)  
- **Reverse KL regularization** – Constrains the generator to stay close to the base distribution. *Why needed:* Prevents quality degradation from excessive reward chasing. *Quick check:* Does the KL term use forward or reverse KL? (Reverse)  
- **Dynamic reference model (EMA)** – The KL reference is an exponential moving average of *p*φ. *Why needed:* Keeps the regularization target up‑to‑date and stabilizes training. *Quick check:* Is the reference static or an EMA? (EMA)  
- **Deterministic trajectories for credit assignment** – The theoretical derivation (Eq. 6) relies on a Dirac distribution over trajectories. *Why needed:* High variance from stochasticity would break per‑step reward estimates. *Quick check:* Does the training loop use stochastic sampling? (No)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Condition *c* → Generator (deterministic TDM) → Group of images {*x₀*} → Reward *r(c, x₀)* → Advantages → Update *p*φ (group preference loss) → Update generator (surrogate reward + reverse KL using reference) → Reference update (EMA of *p*φ).  

**Critical path:** Generate group → evaluate reward → update surrogate → update generator → update reference. These steps repeat each iteration.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Deterministic vs. stochastic trajectories: deterministic reduces variance but limits exploration.  
- Surrogate complexity: a full diffusion model for *p*φ is accurate but computationally costly (80 NFEs in experiments).  
- Reverse KL strength: too stronghinders improvement; too weak risks quality collapse.  
- Group size *G*: larger groups improve preference signal but increase memory/time.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Slow/unstable convergence when using stochastic trajectories.  
- Surrogate reward loss collapsing or plateauing (poor group partitioning or static reference).  
- Out‑of‑domain metrics (aesthetic, ImageReward) dropping while target metric rises (overfitting to proxy).  
- Generator stops improving after few iterations (KL too strong or learning rate mis‑set).

**Three first experiments:**  
1. Reproduce baseline: train TDM‑R1 on SD3.5‑M with reported hyperparameters (or reasonable guesses) and verify GenEval/OCR gains.  
2. Ablate deterministic trajectories: switch to stochastic TDM sampling and compare convergence and final performance.  
3. Test a new reward: replace GenEval/OCR with CLIP score as the non‑differentiable reward and check for similar improvements.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can TDM‑R1 be effectively adapted to few‑step generative models that do not employ deterministic sampling trajectories, such as consistency models or adversarial distillation methods?  
2. Does TDM‑R1 generalize to video generation, where the generation process involves both spatial and temporal dimensions and rewards may include motion or temporal consistency?  
3. What explains the observed cross‑task improvement where training on one verifiable metric (e.g., GenEval) enhances performance on another (e.g., OCR accuracy), and can this be systematically leveraged to improve broad instruction‑following capabilities?  
4. Can the surrogate reward model (a full diffusion model requiring many NFEs) be replaced by a more efficient architecture without sacrificing the quality of RL supervision?

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Exact values for learning rates, group size *G*, EMA decay, and β₍g₎ ratio are not disclosed; without them, achieving the reported numbers is uncertain.  
- **Deterministic trajectory dependency:** The method’s low‑variance reward assignment relies on deterministic TDM paths, which may not generalize to all few‑step models (e.g., consistency models).  
- **Surrogate reward overhead:** The reward model itself uses many NFEs (e.g., 80) during training, limiting scalability and practicality for large‑scale or online RL.
