# An Energy-Efficient Spiking Neural Network Architecture for Predictive Insulin Delivery

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27589
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27589
- **Reference count:** 17
- **Primary result:** 85.90% validation accuracy, 90.72% HIGH recall on standard test set; 9.2% recall on non‑obvious hypoglycemia; 79,267× energy efficiency improvement over Bi‑LSTM (1,551 fJ vs. 122.9 nJ per inference)

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces PDDS, an event‑driven ultra‑low‑power insulin dose calculator for wearable edge devices. Its core is a three‑layer Leaky Integrate‑and‑Fire Spiking Neural Network (SNN) that processes ten CGM‑derived features from a 50‑minute sliding window. Features are encoded into stochastic Poisson spike trains, enabling neuromorphic hardware inference at an estimated 1,551 fJ per inference—79,267× more efficient than a bidirectional LSTM (122.9 nJ). On the standard test set, PDDS achieves 85.90% accuracy and 90.72% recall for HIGH‑severity hypoglycemia. However, on a temporal benchmark of clinically critical non‑obvious hypoglycemia windows, recall drops to 9.2%, worse than the ADA rule’s 16.7%. The event‑driven architecture—activating computation only when glucose crosses a dynamic threshold—reduces average power by an estimated 88% relative to continuous polling, making continuous deployment feasible despite accuracy trade‑offs and current limitations in temporal pattern recognition.

## Method Summary
PDDS employs a three‑layer LIF SNN (10→128→64→3) with Poisson spike encoding: each normalized feature is converted into a 50‑timestep Bernoulli spike train with added Gaussian noise (σ=0.05). The network uses leaky integration decay rates (β) of 0.95, 0.90, and 0.80; classification is by spike‑count argmax. Training uses RMaxProp with surrogate gradients (fast_sigmoid, slope=25), class‑weighted cross‑entropy (HIGH class minority, 18.4%), cosine annealing learning rate, and gradient clipping. Input features are extracted from CGM windows (50 min, 5‑min resolution) and include recent glucose, statistics, slope, and time‑in‑range percentages. The full pipeline is event‑driven: an EmergencyDetector runs on every CGM sample to suppress doses during rapid descents; a ThresholdBell comparator wakes the SNN and dose calculator only on a rising glucose threshold crossing. The final dose is computed via a Bergman‑inspired sigmoid, shifted by predicted severity and capped at 5.0 U.

## Key Results
- Standard test set: 85.90% validation accuracy, 90.72% HIGH‑class recall.
- Temporal benchmark (non‑obvious hypoglycemia): 9.2% recall (ADA rule: 16.7%).
- Energy efficiency: 79,267× improvement over Bi‑LSTM (1,551 fJ vs. 122.9 nJ per inference).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Event‑Driven Pipeline Activation Reduces Average Power
**Claim:** The system’s primary energy savings come from activating the full computational pipeline only on glucose threshold crossings, not on every CGM sample.  
**Mechanism:** A comparator (ThresholdBell) monitors the CGM‑derived voltage. The pipeline (SNN inference + dose calculation) wakes only on a rising edge crossing a dynamically adjusted threshold. Continuous polling would trigger computation every 5 minutes; event‑driven triggering reduces activations by an estimated 88%.  
**Core assumption:** Glucose changes are relatively infrequent compared to the sampling rate, so most CGM readings do not require a full dose calculation.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: “Motivated by neuromorphic computing principles for ultra‑low‑power wearable edge devices… event‑driven architecture that activates the inference pathway only on threshold crossings”  
- [section I]: “continuous polling—every reading triggers the full computational pipeline… On a coin‑cell‑powered wearable device… unsustainable power budget. PDDS addresses this with an event‑driven architecture”  
- [corpus]: Neighbors focus on energy‑efficient SNNs but do not directly address event‑triggered *system* pipelines.  
**Break condition:** If glucose becomes highly volatile (many threshold crossings within short periods), activation frequency approaches continuous polling, eroding energy savings.

### Mechanism 2: Stochastic Poisson Encoding Trades Deterministic Accuracy for Neuromorphic Compatibility
**Claim:** The SNN’s lower accuracy (85.24% vs. LSTM’s 99.06%) is causally linked to the Poisson rate encoding of features into stochastic spike trains, not an inherent architectural failure of LIF networks.  
**Mechanism:** Continuous‑valued features are converted into Bernoulli spike trains with a firing probability equal to the normalized feature value. This introduces random noise per inference pass, preventing the network from learning a deterministic decision boundary that perfectly matches the ADA‑label‑derived test set.  
**Core assumption:** The information loss from rate coding is acceptable given the massive energy efficiency gain on neuromorphic hardware (1,551 fJ vs. 122.9 nJ).  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section IV‑A]: “This gap reflects the stochastic encoding trade‑off, not architectural failure.”  
- [section IX‑E]: “The SNN’s lower accuracy (85.24%) reflects the information loss introduced by stochastic Poisson encoding… Unlike the deterministic dense networks, the SNN’s performance variance across inference runs is non‑zero by design.”  
- [section IV‑B]: Describes the Poisson encoding process with added Gaussian noise.  
**Break condition:** If the required classification accuracy for a given clinical safety metric cannot be met even with optimal training (due to irreducible noise from encoding), the trade‑off becomes unacceptable.

### Mechanism 3: Insufficient Temporal Supervision Limits Learning of Non‑Obvious Hypoglycemia Patterns
**Claim:** The SNN’s poor recall (9.2%) on the “non‑obvious hypoglycemia” temporal benchmark is caused by the extreme sparsity of clinician‑annotated hypoglycemia events (0.8% of training data) and the use of window‑based features that discard raw temporal sequence.  
**Mechanism:** The model is trained on fixed‑length windows (50 min, 10 features) with labels derived primarily from instantaneous ADA thresholds. The rare, clinically critical *temporal* patterns (e.g., post‑hypoglycemic rebound) are underrepresented in the loss function. The SNN’s three‑layer architecture, while hierarchical in integration timescales (β=0.95/0.90/0.80), operates on a pre‑processed feature vector, not a raw, long‑sequence time series.  
**Core assumption:** A classifier based on windowed features and majority‑class‑weighted loss will not develop strong representations for rare, long‑range temporal dependencies without specific architectural or data augmentation changes.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section IX‑D]: “The 895 hypo_event windows represent only 0.8% of training data—too sparse for the class‑weighted loss to develop strong temporal representations beyond the dominant ADA threshold pattern.”  
- [section IX‑D]: “Both achieve <20% recall on these dangerous pre‑ and post‑hypoglycaemia patterns. The SNN actually performs slightly worse (9.2%) than the ADA rule (16.7%)”  
- [section IV‑A]: Contrasts the SNN’s ability to learn *some* temporal patterns (post‑rebound) against its failure on the curated temporal benchmark.  
**Break condition:** Re‑weighting or oversampling the hypo_event class, or switching to a recurrent/transformer architecture on raw CGM sequences, might improve this recall. The current mechanism (windowed features + sparse labels) is the limiting factor.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Spiking Neural Networks (LIF Model)**  
  *Why needed here:* The entire efficiency claim rests on mapping computation to spiking neurons. Understanding membrane potential decay (β), threshold crossing, and spike‑based activation is essential to grasp why inference is event‑based and energy‑frugal.  
  *Quick check question:* How does a LIF neuron’s leaky integration (V[t] = β·V[t‑1] + I[t]) differ from a standard ReLU activation in terms of temporal state and computational events?

- **Concept: Event‑Driven vs. Clock‑Driven Systems**  
  *Why needed here:* The paper’s core power argument is that the system sleeps until a glucose threshold is crossed. This is a fundamental shift from periodic sampling (clock‑driven) to condition‑triggered (event‑driven) computation.  
  *Quick check question:* What are the two components of the system that must be event‑triggered to realize the ~88% activation reduction: the *sensor read* and the *inference pipeline*?

- **Concept: Poisson Rate Coding**  
  *Why needed here:* This is the stated source of the accuracy‑efficiency trade‑off. It converts analog features into discrete, stochastic spike trains. Understanding this is key to “Why is the SNN less accurate on a static test set?”  
  *Quick check question:* If a normalized feature value is 0.7 and you generate a 30‑timestep Poisson spike train, what is the expected number of spikes and what is the source of variance between inference runs?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
- `VoltageBuffer`: Ring buffer (60 samples) storing CGM voltage. Source of historical data.  
- `EmergencyDetector`: Runs on *every* reading. Computes slope, applies +15 min lag projection, suppresses dose if projected slope ≤ –0.25 V/min.  
- `ThresholdBell`: Comparator with dynamic threshold (τ_new = V_cur + min(τ_base + α·|˙V|, Δ_max)). Generates the single “wake” event.  
- `PDDSSpikingNet`: 3‑layer LIF SNN (10→128→64→3). Takes 10 Gold features, encodes via Poisson, outputs spike counts per class.  
- `DoseCalculator`: Bergman‑inspired sigmoid. Uses SNN’s predicted severity to shift dose‑response curve. Caps at 5.0 U.

**Critical path (happy path):**  
`CGM reading` → `EmergencyDetector` (block if emergency) → `ThresholdBell` (trigger on rising edge) → `Extract 10 Gold features from VoltageBuffer` → `Poisson encode` → `SNN forward pass` → `Spike count argmax → Severity` → `DoseCalculator (severity‑shifted sigmoid)` → `Clip to [0, 5.0U]` → `Output dose`.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Accuracy vs. Energy: Poisson noise lowers accuracy but enables 79,000× energy reduction on neuromorphic hardware.  
- Determinism vs. Robustness: Stochastic spikes introduce run‑to‑run variance but may improve generalisation (like dropout).  
- Rule‑based safety vs. Learned adaptation: ADA rule provides 100% recall on its own labels but cannot learn temporal patterns; SNN learns but underperforms on temporal edges.  
- Model simplicity vs. Temporal power: 3‑layer LIF on windowed features is simple and efficient but fails on long‑range dependencies learned by Bi‑LSTM.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High‑level: HIGH‑class recall on *non‑obvious* hypoglycemia windows < 20%.  
- Mid‑level: SNN output variance between runs with identical input is non‑zero (by design, but must be bounded).  
- Low‑level: EmergencyDetector fails to project lag correctly (e.g., slope calculation error); ThresholdBell re‑triggers on same event (epsilon guard failure).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **EmergencyDetector unit test:** Feed a synthetic CGM stream with a rapid, deep descent starting at 80 mg/dL. Verify the projected value (V_cur + ˙V·15) crosses the emergency threshold *before* the actual CGM reading hits 70 mg/dL, and that dose is suppressed.  
2. **SNN stochasticity quantification:** Run the SNN on the same 100 validation windows 100 times. Measure the standard deviation of the predicted class distribution. It should be >0 but bounded (e.g., <0.15 in probability mass).  
3. **Temporal benchmark replication:** Isolate the 426 non‑obvious hypo windows from the training set. Compute SNN and ADA rule recall. Expect SNN ~9%, ADA ~17%. This confirms the primary limitation before any hardware integration.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Q1:** How can the SNN's temporal pattern recognition be enhanced to improve recall on non‑obvious hypoglycemia windows?  
  *Basis:* SNN achieves only 9.2% recall on the temporal benchmark, worse than ADA’s 16.7%.  
  *Why unresolved:* Current feature‑based Poisson encoding and shallow LIF architecture fail to learn critical temporal dynamics due to sparse hypo_event labels (0.8%) and stochastic noise.  
  *Evidence needed:* Implement and evaluate solutions (reweighted hypo_event training, recurrent SNNs on raw CGM, descent sub‑classifier) on a held‑out temporal benchmark with clinician annotations, measuring recall and F1‑score.

- **Q2:** Does the SNN's energy efficiency advantage persist and translate to clinically acceptable performance when deployed on neuromorphic hardware with live CGM streams?  
  *Basis:* Energy analysis is theoretical (1,551 fJ vs. 122.9 nJ per inference); PDDS is software‑only and not yet integrated with physical sensors or neuromorphic chips.  
  *Why unresolved:* Real‑world factors (sensor noise, communication latency, hardware spike handling, asynchronous processing) could degrade accuracy or alter energy savings.  
  *Evidence needed:* Port the SNN to neuromorphic edge accelerators (e.g., SynSense Xylo, BrainChip Akida), integrate with a physical CGM device in a controlled environment, and measure real energy consumption, latency, and classification metrics under realistic conditions.

- **Q3:** How can the circular evaluation problem be mitigated to provide a non‑biased assessment of the SNN's clinical utility?  
  *Basis:* Evaluating on ADA‑rule‑derived labels is circular because labels are assigned by the same thresholds the ADA rule uses, artificially inflating rule‑based performance.  
  *Why unresolved:* The current test set (5,724 windows) uses ADA 2023 thresholds, which may not align with clinical judgment for complex temporal patterns, obscuring the SNN’s true generalisation.  
  *Evidence needed:* Conduct a retrospective study using OhioT1DM patients excluded from training, with hypoglycemia severity labels determined by blinded clinician review of CGM trends (not ADA rules), and compare SNN, ADA rule, and dense baselines on this non‑circular test set, focusing on HIGH recall.

- **Q4:** Can the dose calculation be improved with patient‑specific pharmacokinetic modelling to enhance safety and efficacy beyond the current Bergman‑inspired formula?  
  *Basis:* The DoseCalculator uses a “Bergman‑inspired sigmoidal formula” described as a clinically‑motivated proxy; full ODE‑based pharmacokinetic modelling with patient‑specific parameter fitting is required for clinical deployment.  
  *Why unresolved:* Fixed‑parameter sigmoid with severity shifts may not capture individual variations in insulin sensitivity, glucose absorption, or insulin action kinetics, potentially leading to over‑ or under‑dosing.  
  *Evidence needed:* Integrate a personalised pharmacokinetic model (e.g., fitting parameters of a glucose‑insulin dynamics ODE system with patient data), validate it in silico against clinical endpoints (Time‑in‑Range, hypoglycemia events) across diverse simulated patients, and compare dosing accuracy and safety margins with the current formula in simulation‑based trials.

## Limitations
- Reproduction is blocked by missing class weights and RMaxProp hyperparameters.  
- Dangerously low recall (9.2%) on non‑obvious hypoglycemia, worse than the ADA rule (16.7%), raising serious safety concerns.  
- Uncharacterized performance under high glucose volatility; event‑driven activation frequency may approach continuous polling, eroding energy savings.
