# GanitLLM: Difficulty-Aware Bengali Mathematical Reasoning through Curriculum-GRPO

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06767  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06767  
- **Reference count:** 29  
- **Primary result:** GanitLLM-4B improves accuracy by +8 (Bn-MGSM) and +7 (Bn-MSVAMP) points over its Qwen3-4B base, raises Bengali reasoning token percentage from 14% to 88.71%, and reduces average solution length by 79.5% (943 → 193 words).

## Executive Summary
GanitLLM closes a critical gap in Bengali mathematical reasoning by large language models, which often resort to English reasoning or fail on multi-step problems due to reward sparsity in low-resource settings. The approach combines two innovations: (1) GANIT, a rigorously filtered and decontaminated Bengali math dataset with automatic difficulty tags derived from pass@k scores of a strong evaluator model; and (2) Curriculum-GRPO, a two-stage training pipeline that first uses supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to ground the model’s reasoning in Bengali, followed by reinforcement learning with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) that samples problems from easy to hard to mitigate cold-start. Evaluated on Bn-MGSM and Bn-MSVAMP, GanitLLM-4B outperforms its Qwen3-4B base by 8 and 7 accuracy points respectively, achieves 88.71% Bengali reasoning tokens, and shortens solutions by 79.5%. Ablations confirm that Curriculum-GRPO converges 3.8–5.6× faster than standard GRPO while preserving accuracy.

## Method Summary
GanitLLM trains a Qwen3-4B base in two sequential phases. **Phase 1 (SFT):** The model is fine‑tuned on the `GANIT-SFT` split, a collection of Bengali chain‑of‑thought solutions. The prompt explicitly requires reasoning in Bengali and a final answer within `<answer>` tags. Full fine‑tuning (50 epochs, lr=1e‑6) establishes a strong Bengali reasoning prior, shifting the model’s internal language from English‑dominant to Bengali‑dominant. **Phase 2 (Curriculum‑GRPO):** Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards is applied using `GANIT-RLVR`, a set of (problem, verifiable answer) pairs. The GRPO algorithm samples eight rollouts per problem and computes advantages within each rollout group. A composite reward combines format compliance (+1), answer correctness (+2 if answer is numerically correct and in Bengali, +1 if correct but not Bengali), and a Bengali reasoning bonus (+1 if ≥80% of non‑whitespace characters in the reasoning are Bengali script). To alleviate cold‑start, training data is ordered by difficulty (pass@k count from the evaluator) and a 60/40 primary‑bucket sampling ratio ensures early batches contain a higher proportion of easy problems while maintaining diversity. LoRA (rank 16, alpha 32) is used for efficient adaptation, and additional controls (overlength filter, token‑level loss) reduce verbosity.

## Key Results
- **Accuracy gains:** +8 points on Bn‑MGSM and +7 points on Bn‑MSVAMP over the Qwen3‑4B base.
- **Language shift:** Bengali reasoning token share rises from 14% (base) to 88.71% (final model).
- **Efficiency:** Average solution length plummets by 79.5% (943 → 193 words); Curriculum‑GRPO reaches optimal checkpoint in ~600 steps versus ~2,300 for standard GRPO (3.8–5.6× faster).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Difficulty‑Tagged Dataset Construction
A rigorously filtered Bengali math dataset (GANIT) mitigates reward sparsity and cold‑start by providing a curriculum signal. Source data from multiple Bengali math corpora are cleaned (deduplication, decontamination, human‑verified >95% accuracy). Difficulty is automatically assigned using pass@k (32 rollouts) with Qwen3‑32B as evaluator, retaining only problems solved at least once. This creates a spectrum from easy to Olympiad‑level. Evidence: The abstract describes GANIT as “rigorously filtered and decontaminated Bengali math dataset with automatic difficulty tags derived from the pass@k of a strong evaluator model.” Section 3.3 details the pass@k tagging procedure. The break condition: if Qwen3‑32B’s biases misalign difficulty with smaller policy models (e.g., Qwen3‑4B), the curriculum may be suboptimal.

### Supervised Fine‑Tuning for Language Grounding
A CoT‑SFT stage grounds the model’s reasoning in Bengali before RL, overriding the pre‑training bias toward English. Fine‑tuning on GANIT‑SFT (high‑quality Bengali CoT demonstrations) establishes a Bengali reasoning prior, as evidenced by a jump in Bengali character percentage from 14.79% to ~86–97% (Section 7.1 & Fig.4). SFT alone yields limited accuracy gains, but it is a prerequisite for later RL to preserve language fidelity. Break condition: if SFT data contains English traces or insufficient diversity, the model may learn a hybrid “Bengali answer, English reasoning” pattern despite the ≥99% Bengali character filter.

### Curriculum‑GRPO for Cold‑Start Mitigation
Ordering GRPO training samples by difficulty (soft curriculum) alleviates the cold‑start problem of zero‑reward rollout groups and accelerates convergence without sacrificing final accuracy. Unlike random shuffling, the curriculum orders problems from easy (high pass@k) to hard (low pass@k), using a 60/40 primary‑bucket split to maintain diversity. Early training steps therefore yield non‑zero advantages, stabilizing gradients. Evidence: Table 5 shows CGRPO reaching optimal checkpoint at step 600 vs. 2,300 for GRPO (3.8× speedup for 4B) with comparable final accuracy. The core assumption—that model capability increases monotonically—holds in experiments. Break condition: if improvement is non‑monotonic or overfitting on easy samples occurs (catastrophic forgetting), a steeper curriculum may harm performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR):** Needs understanding because GRPO is the core RL algorithm; it computes advantages within groups of rollouts and requires verifiable, often binary, rewards. A “zero‑reward group” (all rollouts incorrect) yields no learning signal, causing training to stall. Quick check: How does GRPO compute advantages within a group, and why does a zero‑reward group halt learning?
- **Pass@k as a Difficulty Proxy:** Needed to grasp how the dataset’s difficulty tags are generated. Pass@k measures the probability that at least one of k independent model samples solves the problem. It is used here as an automatic difficulty estimator. Limitations: The evaluator model (Qwen3‑32B) may be biased, and its pass@k might not correlate perfectly with the learning curve of a smaller policy model. Quick check: What are the pitfalls of using a single evaluator’s pass@k to tag difficulty for a different, smaller model?
- **Language Grounding vs. Task Performance:** Essential to understand the dual objectives: solving math problems correctly *and* reasoning in Bengali. High accuracy on a Bengali benchmark does not guarantee the model’s internal reasoning is in Bengali; it may translate English reasoning. Quick check: Why might a model achieve high accuracy while still reasoning primarily in English?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input: Bengali Math Problem → GANIT Dataset (provides SFT and RLVR splits) → SFT Stage (ground Bengali reasoning) → Curriculum‑GRPO Stage (LoRA‑tune with ordered RL data) → GanitLLM (final model).  
Reward components: R_format (format compliance), R_correctness (numerical correctness + Bengali answer bonus), R_bengali (≥80% Bengali characters in reasoning).

**Critical path:**  
1. Dataset curation (filtering, pass@k tagging) must finish before any training.  
2. SFT is mandatory before Curriculum‑GRPO; without it, GRPO fails to maintain Bengali fidelity.  
3. The difficulty ordering of RL data is the key lever that speeds up GRPO convergence.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Data quality vs. scale: GANIT prioritizes rigorous filtering (down to ~58K from ~1.5M) at the cost of volume.  
- Curriculum strictness vs. diversity: The 60/40 primary‑bucket split balances challenge progression with retention of easier patterns; steeper ratios risk catastrophic forgetting.  
- Language fidelity reward heuristic: Using ≥80% Bengali characters may penalize valid use of numerals, symbols, or code‑switching.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Cold‑start in GRPO: validation accuracy plateaus near random; monitor per‑batch reward distribution—if all zero, the curriculum is ineffective.  
- Language reversion: Bengali token percentage drops after GRPO; indicates R_bengali is not being optimized or SFT prior was weak.  
- Overfitting on easy curriculum: Accuracy on hard validation subsets stalls while easy subset nears 100%; suggests curriculum too narrow.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Sanity‑check dataset difficulty tags by running 32 rollouts with Qwen3‑32B on a stratified sample and verifying pass@k ranges match bucket labels.  
2. Validate SFT language grounding by inferring on 50 Bn‑MGSM problems with the SFT‑only model; expect high Bengali character % (>85%) but moderate accuracy.  
3. Test Curriculum‑GRPO signal by logging the first 100 training steps: compare % of rollout groups with ≥1 correct solution and average reward between random‑shuffle GRPO and Curriculum‑GRPO; curriculum should show higher and earlier non‑zero‑group rates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalizability to other languages/domains:** The curriculum method is only validated on Bengali math; it is unclear if pass@k‑based difficulty ordering transfers to other low‑resource languages or non‑math reasoning tasks.  
2. **Biases in automatic difficulty tagging:** Using pass@k from a single evaluator (Qwen3‑32B) may systematically mischaracterize certain problem types (e.g., linguistically complex but algebraically simple), leading to a skewed curriculum that favors evaluator‑specific strengths.  
3. **Heuristic Bengali reasoning reward:** The ≥80% character‑level Bengali threshold is a blunt proxy; it may penalize mathematically valid outputs that mix English symbols or numerals, while rewarding verbose Bengali filler. A more semantic measure is needed.  
4. **Optimality of the 60/40 sampling ratio:** This heuristic was chosen for the 4B model and a balanced difficulty distribution; its performance may degrade for other model scales or naturally skewed datasets.  
5. **Causal role of SFT grounding:** Why does SFT enable GRPO to improve accuracy without losing Bengali fidelity? It may be more than just an initialization; SFT could fundamentally reshape the model’s response distribution in a way that RL can then optimize.

## Limitations
- **Dataset availability:** GANIT is not publicly released; reproduction requires constructing it from source datasets and filtering code, which is non‑trivial.  
- **GRPO implementation specifics:** The exact details of the “overlength filter and token‑level loss” from Yu et al. (2025) are not described, leaving ambiguity in reproducing that part.  
- **Reward function precision:** The precise logic for format checking (e.g., `<answer>` regex) and numerical correctness verification (especially handling Bengali numerals) is unspecified, risking inconsistent reward computation.

## Confidence
- SFT for Bengali grounding: **High** (ablations show large language shift; risk of hybrid outputs if SFT data imperfect).  
- Curriculum‑GRPO efficiency: **High** (empirical speedup demonstrated; long‑term performance on hardest problems still needs scrutiny).  
- Difficulty tagging via pass@k: **Medium** (relies on a single evaluator; potential misalignment with smaller policy models).  
- Language fidelity reward heuristic: **Low** (character‑percentage threshold is ad‑hoc and may not correlate with true reasoning language).

## Next Checks

2. **Early GRPO signal audit:** Run the first 200 steps of Curriculum‑GRPO vs. random‑shuffle GRPO; confirm the curriculum yields a significantly higher rate of rollout groups with at least one correct solution and a steeper initial reward increase.  
3. **Bengali reward metric audit:** Manually inspect 50 outputs from the final checkpoint. Verify that ≥80% Bengali character count aligns with human‑judged “native Bengali reasoning” and identify any false positives (e.g., Bengali script in non‑reasoning tokens).
