# LBM: Hierarchical Large Auto-Bidding Model via Reasoning and Acting

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05134
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05134
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LBM with GQPO achieves 382 conversions and a score of 348 on the AuctionNet dense dataset, surpassing the Decision Transformer baseline (371 conversions, 334 score).

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles counterintuitive behavior and poor generalization in auto‑bidding for online advertising. The authors propose a hierarchical Large auto‑Bidding Model (LBM) that separates high‑level reasoning (LBM‑Think) from low‑level action generation (LBM‑Act). LBM‑Think produces a chain‑of‑thought (CoT) summarizing history and suggesting bidding adjustments, while LBM‑Act consumes the CoT and numerical state sequence to output precise bids. A dual embedding mechanism fuses language and numerical inputs, and Group relative‑Q Policy Optimization (GQPO) fine‑tunes LBM‑Think offline using relative‑Q values, avoiding risky online rollouts. Experiments on the AuctionNet benchmark show LBM significantly outperforms prior non‑LLM generative and RL baselines, with superior budget utilization, better CPA constraint adherence, and robust generalization across varying budget levels.

## Method Summary
The method comprises two training stages. Stage 1 trains LBM‑Act, a decision transformer‑style model conditioned on CoT reasoning. It uses a dual embedding: a pretrained token embedding for language tokens and a learned MLP (“decision embedding”) to project numerical features (return‑to‑go, states, actions) into the same embedding space. Transformer layers fuse the multimodal sequence, and the model is trained with MSE loss to reproduce dataset actions. Stage 2 fine‑tunes LBM‑Think via GQPO. A Q‑network is first trained offline with Implicit Q‑Learning (IQL). For each state, multiple CoTs are sampled from LBM‑Think; each CoT guides LBM‑Act to produce an action, and the Q‑network computes Q(s,a). The relative‑Q ΔQ = Q(s, ã) – Q(s, a_dataset) measures improvement. LBM‑Think is updated to maximize the weighted log‑likelihood of CoTs with high ΔQ (exp(βΔQ)), entirely offline.

## Key Results
- LBM(GQPO) attains 382 conversions and a score of 348 on the dense AuctionNet test set, exceeding Decision Transformer’s 371 conversions and 334 score.
- It achieves higher budget utilization and lower CPA ratio than baselines, adhering better to constraints.
- The model generalizes robustly to out‑of‑distribution budget levels (0.5×–1.5×) while maintaining strong performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Decoupling reasoning (LBM‑Think) and acting (LBM‑Act) into separate modules improves interpretability and performance by allowing each to specialize.
- **Mechanism**: LBM‑Think asynchronously generates a chain‑of‑thought that summarizes historical performance and reasons about high‑level adjustment directions. LBM‑Act then consumes the pre‑generated CoT alongside the current numerical state sequence to produce precise bidding actions. This enables using a larger LLM for reasoning and a smaller one for fast acting.
- **Core assumption**: The reasoning process (CoT) can be performed ahead of the decision timestep and remains valid when the action is generated, provided the environment changes slowly relative to the decision interval.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract and Section 3.1 describe the hierarchical structure; neighboring auto‑bidding work does not employ such decoupling.
- **Break condition**: If the CoT becomes stale due to rapid environment shifts or misinterprets constraints, the hierarchical design fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: A dual embedding mechanism fuses language (CoT) and numerical (state/action) inputs efficiently, avoiding token explosion while leveraging pretrained LLM knowledge.
- **Mechanism**: Separate embedding layers process the two modalities: a pretrained token embedding for language tokens, and a learned “decision embedding” (MLP) that projects each numerical item into an embedding of the same dimension as a token. These embeddings are concatenated and fed to transformer layers that fuse information via attention.
- **Core assumption**: Numerical data can be effectively compressed into dense embeddings that retain decision‑relevant information, and attention can meaningfully combine the two modalities.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions dual embedding; Section 3.2 details the design. Related work (e.g., LLM‑DT) uses extra embeddings but not explicit dual fusion.
- **Break condition**: If decision embeddings fail to capture critical patterns or cross‑modality attention is ineffective, the fusion degrades.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Group relative‑Q Policy Optimization (GQPO) fine‑tunes LBM‑Think offline, mitigating hallucinations by using relative‑Q values as advantages, without real‑world rollouts.
- **Mechanism**: For a given state, multiple CoTs are sampled from LBM‑Think. Each CoT is fed to the trained LBM‑Act to produce an action, and a Q‑network (trained via IQL) computes Q(s,a). The relative‑Q ΔQ = Q(s, ã) – Q(s, a_dataset) measures improvement. The CoT with the highest positive ΔQ is selected, and the policy is updated with a weighted likelihood exp(βΔQ). This avoids on‑policy rollout.
- **Core assumption**: The relative‑Q difference accurately reflects the quality of the CoT for decision‑making, and the Q‑network provides a stable baseline.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract and Section 3.3 describe GQPO and its equations. Unlike online methods (e.g., GiGPO), GQPO operates offline.
- **Break condition**: If the Q‑network is poorly estimated (e.g., due to distribution shift) or ΔQ is uncorrelated with true performance, GQPO may reinforce harmful CoTs.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Offline Reinforcement Learning (e.g., Implicit Q‑Learning) – needed because GQPO relies on a Q‑function trained offline to compute relative‑Q without online interaction. Quick check: How does IQL avoid querying out‑of‑distribution actions, and why is it suitable for auto‑bidding datasets?
- **Concept**: Decision Transformer (DT) – needed because LBM‑Act extends DT by adding language guidance; DT’s sequence modeling approach (return‑to‑go, states, actions) is the foundation for LBM‑Act’s numerical input handling. Quick check: In DT, how does conditioning on return‑to‑go influence the generated action, and what are its limitations in continuous control?
- **Concept**: Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) Prompting – needed because LBM‑Think’s output is a CoT that summarizes history and reasons about adjustments; CoT is a core technique for eliciting reasoning from LLMs. Quick check: What are the benefits and pitfalls of CoT in numerical decision‑making tasks like auto‑bidding?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
LBM‑Think (LLM) → CoT (text) → LBM‑Act (LLM + dual embedding) → action  
GQPO (uses Q‑network from IQL) → fine‑tunes LBM‑Think

**Critical path**:
1. Pre‑processing: Construct historical performance summary (text) and numerical sequence (return‑to‑go, states, actions) for each timestep.
2. LBM‑Think asynchronously (at t‑1) generates CoT c_t.
3. At t, LBM‑Act receives c_t and numerical sequence, fuses via dual embedding, outputs action a_t.
4. Execute a_t; collect new state and reward.
5. Training Stage 1: Train LBM‑Act with MSE loss to predict dataset actions (CoT fixed or from LBM‑Think).
6. Training Stage 2: Train Q‑network with IQL; then fine‑tune LBM‑Think with GQPO using relative‑Q.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Hierarchical vs monolithic: Decoupling adds engineering complexity but enables asynchronous reasoning and a smaller acting model; monolithic might be simpler but risks conflating reasoning and precision.
- LLM size: Larger Think model improves reasoning but increases latency; smaller Act model speeds up inference but may limit expressiveness.
- Offline vs online fine‑tuning: GQPO avoids risky online rollouts but depends on accurate Q‑values; online RL could adapt better but is unsafe for real bidding.
- Language vs pure numerical: Language enables incorporating prior knowledge but introduces hallucination risk; pure numerical (DT) avoids this but lacks interpretability and generalization.

**Failure signatures**:
- LBM‑Think generates CoTs that contradict historical data (e.g., wrong CPA calculation).
- LBM‑Act ignores CoT and behaves like a DT (attention scores show no language focus).
- End‑to‑end metrics: budget utilization far below 1, CPA ratio > 1 repeatedly, or conversions collapse on unseen budget levels.
- GQPO training loss diverges or CoT length shrinks to zero.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Dual embedding validation**: Train LBM‑Act on a small subset (e.g., 10 k trajectories) with and without CoT input. Compare action MSE against dataset actions; expect CoT‑conditioned model to converge faster/lower.
2. **GQPO sanity check**: With fixed LBM‑Act, sample CoTs from LBM‑Think before/after GQPO for 100 states. Compute ΔQ; after fine‑tuning, the proportion of CoTs with ΔQ > 0 should increase, and selected CoTs should show more consistent reasoning.
3. **End‑to‑end benchmark**: Run LBM(GQPO) on AuctionNet dense test set (5 k trajectories) and compare conversions and score against DT baseline; expect LBM to achieve higher conversions (e.g., 382 vs 371) and score (348 vs 334).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How can LLMs be safely fine‑tuned online for auto‑bidding to adapt to dynamic auction environments? The paper notes online exploration risks and suggests future work on safe RL with safety control.
- **Open Question 2**: To what extent can the hierarchical design be optimized for inference speed without sacrificing decision quality? Current latency (e.g., 2.5 s for 3B model) may not suit high‑frequency bidding; trade‑offs need quantification.
- **Open Question 3**: Is GQPO effective for mitigating hallucinations compared to online RL methods? GQPO’s sample efficiency and stability relative to online approaches like GRPO are unknown.
- **Open Question 4**: How well does LBM generalize to unseen auction scenarios with different constraint types or competitive dynamics beyond AuctionNet? The paper tests varying budgets but not other constraints or external datasets.

## Limitations
- Prompt details for LBM‑Think (history serialization, KPI calculation steps) are underspecified, hindering reproduction.
- GQPO’s weighting factor β is unreported; its value may critically affect stability.
- The fallback when no CoT yields ΔQ>0 during GQPO is unspecified.
- Offline IQL Q‑values may suffer from distribution shift, compromising ΔQ reliability.
- No code release or use of external datasets limits independent verification.

## Confidence
The three main claims receive **Medium** confidence due to reliance on a single benchmark and lack of external validation.
- Hierarchical decoupling: Medium
- Dual embedding: Medium
- GQPO fine‑tuning: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt ablation**: Test multiple LBM‑Think prompt variants on a validation set; evaluate CoT correctness, LBM‑Act action MSE, and end‑to‑end conversions to identify sensitivity.
2. **β sensitivity**: Sweep β in GQPO (e.g., 0.01–10); monitor ΔQ, CoT length, and validation performance to detect non‑monotonic effects or collapse.
3. **Ground‑truth advantage correlation**: Using a simulator or held‑out rollouts, compute true performance gains of sampled CoTs and correlate with ΔQ from the Q‑network; low correlation would indicate Q‑value inaccuracies.
