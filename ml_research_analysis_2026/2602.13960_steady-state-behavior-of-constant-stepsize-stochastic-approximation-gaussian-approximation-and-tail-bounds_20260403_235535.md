# Steady-State Behavior of Constant-Stepsize Stochastic Approximation: Gaussian Approximation and Tail Bounds

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.13960
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13960
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: For constant‑stepsize stochastic approximation, the scaled steady state is within \(O(\sqrt{\alpha}\log(1/\alpha))\) in Wasserstein distance to a Gaussian, with explicit constants; this yields non‑asymptotic tail bounds and extends to non‑strongly‑convex SGD with a Gibbs limit.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies constant‑stepsize stochastic approximation (SA) algorithms, whose steady‑state distributions are typically intractable. Building on prior weak convergence results, the authors derive the first explicit non‑asymptotic error bounds between the centered‑and‑scaled steady state and its Gaussian limit as the stepsize \(\alpha \to 0\). Under standard smoothness and noise moment conditions (finite third moment for i.i.d. noise, boundedness and Poisson‑solution regularity for Markovian noise), they prove a Wasserstein distance bound of order \(\sqrt{\alpha}\log(1/\alpha)\) with an explicit constant that depends on dimension, smoothness, noise moments, and spectral gap. This result covers i.i.d. and Markovian settings, and yields uniform tail bounds for projected iterates: for any unit direction \(\zeta\) and threshold \(a>0\), the difference in tail probabilities is at most \(C \alpha^{1/4} \log^{1/2}(1/\alpha)/a\). For SGD beyond strong convexity, the authors identify the correct scaling \(\alpha^{1/h}\) (where \(h\) is the order of the first non‑vanishing derivative at the optimum) and a non‑Gaussian Gibbs limiting law, together with a pre‑limit Wasserstein bound of order \(\alpha^{1/h}\). The analysis relies on steady‑state Stein’s method, generator comparison, and moment bounds, and includes numerical experiments that validate the Gibbs limit.

## Method Summary
The core analytical tool is a steady‑state version of Stein’s method tailored to the Ornstein‑Uhlenbeck (OU) process, which is the formal continuous‑time limit of constant‑stepsize SA. The authors construct a Stein equation for the stationary distribution of the SA iterates, and compare its generator with that of the OU process. By bounding the difference in generators and using a Poisson equation to handle Markovian noise, they obtain a moment bound on the solution to the Stein equation. This moment bound leads directly to a Wasserstein distance estimate between the scaled steady state and the Gaussian limit. For non‑strongly‑convex objectives, the scaling changes and the limit becomes a Gibbs distribution; the same Stein‑based approach yields a corresponding bound. The method yields explicit constants and works under relatively mild regularity assumptions.

## Key Results
- **Wasserstein bound**: For constant stepsize \(\alpha\), the scaled steady state \(Y\) satisfies \(d_W(Y, Z) \le U \sqrt{\alpha} \log(1/\alpha)\), where \(Z\) is Gaussian and \(U\) is explicit.
- **Tail bound**: For any unit vector \(\zeta\) and \(a>0\), \(|P(\langle Y, \zeta \rangle > a) - P(Z > a)| \le C \alpha^{1/4} \log^{1/2}(1/\alpha)/a\).
- **Non‑strongly‑convex SGD**: With optimal scaling \(\alpha^{1/h}\) (order \(h\) of first non‑zero derivative), the limit is a Gibbs distribution; a pre‑limit Wasserstein bound of order \(\alpha^{1/h}\) is proved.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success of the analysis rests on three pillars: (1) a steady‑state Stein characterization that links the stationary distribution of the SA algorithm to the OU generator; (2) a careful generator comparison that quantifies the discrepancy arising from the discrete‑time updates and the constant stepsize; and (3) robust moment bounds for the solution of the Stein equation, derived under the drift and noise regularity assumptions. For Markovian noise, the Poisson equation provides a representation that controls dependence, allowing the same argument to go through. The Wasserstein metric emerges naturally as the dual of Lipschitz functions, which are exactly the class of test functions that the Stein equation can handle. The \(\sqrt{\alpha} \log(1/\alpha)\) rate reflects the fact that the SA iterates approximate a diffusion process with error of order \(\alpha\) per step, and the logarithmic factor comes from summing over a geometric number of steps in the steady‑state coupling. The extension to higher‑order stationary points uses a scaling argument that centers the iterates at the optimum and exploits the structure of the objective’s Taylor expansion; the Gibbs limit arises as the invariant measure of a Langevin diffusion with a degenerate drift.

## Foundational Learning
- **Stochastic Approximation (SA)**: iterative methods for solving equations or optimizing objectives using noisy observations. *Why needed*: The paper’s subject is constant‑stepsize SA. *Quick check*: Can you write the SA update \(x_{n+1} = x_n - \alpha \widehat{\nabla f}(x_n)\)?
- **Constant Stepsize**: using a fixed learning rate \(\alpha\) rather than diminishing. *Why needed*: Leads to a non‑degenerate stationary distribution; the paper analyzes its steady state. *Quick check*: Does a constant stepsize guarantee convergence to a point? (No, it converges to a neighborhood.)
- **Steady‑State Distribution**: the limiting distribution of the Markov chain induced by SA with constant stepsize. *Why needed*: Central object of study. *Quick check*: For linear SA \(x_{n+1}=Ax_n + \xi_n\), the stationary distribution solves a Lyapunov equation.
- **Wasserstein Distance**: a metric between probability distributions based on optimal transport. *Why needed*: Quantifies approximation error; used in main theorems. *Quick check*: For Gaussians, \(d_W(\mathcal N(0,\sigma_1^2),\mathcal N(0,\sigma_2^2)) = |\sigma_1-\sigma_2|\) in 1D.
- **Stein’s Method**: a technique to bound distances to a target distribution by solving a Stein equation. *Why needed*: Core analytical tool. *Quick check*: For a standard normal \(Z\), the Stein equation is \(f' - x f = h - \mathbb E[h(Z)]\).
- **Poisson Equation**: relates a Markov generator to a function via integration; used to handle Markovian noise. *Why needed*: Allows representation of the solution to the Stein equation. *Quick check*: For an ergodic Markov chain with generator \(\mathcal A\), solve \(\mathcal A u = f - \pi f\).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:
Stein’s method (steady‑state formulation) → Generator comparison (SA generator vs OU generator) → Moment bound for Stein solution → Wasserstein bound; Markovian extension via Poisson equation.

**Critical Path**:
1. Define the scaled process and its continuous‑time limit (OU).
2. Write the Stein equation for the stationary distribution with test function \(f\).
3. Decompose the generator difference into drift and noise terms.
4. Use Lyapunov/Poisson bounds to control the solution \(f\) and its derivatives.
5. Combine to obtain \(d_W\) estimate.
6. For non‑strongly‑convex case, rescale and derive a Gibbs‑type Stein equation.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- Constant stepsize simplifies steady‑state analysis but introduces discretization error; the \(\log(1/\alpha)\) factor comes from handling the geometric waiting times.
- Assuming Lipschitz drift and bounded derivatives enables clean Stein bounds but may be restrictive for nonsmooth objectives; the paper extends to higher‑order cases at the cost of more delicate scaling.
- Focusing on Wasserstein‑1 gives a clean bound for Lipschitz functions but yields suboptimal tail dependence; improving to Wasserstein‑\(p\) would require controlling higher derivatives.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Violation of drift conditions (e.g., non‑Lipschitz or unbounded derivative) can break the moment bounds, causing the Stein equation to have no bounded solution.
- Heavy‑tailed noise with infinite third moment would invalidate the moment assumptions; the bound likely deteriorates.
- For Markovian noise, if the Poisson equation cannot be solved with a uniformly bounded solution, the extension fails.

**First Experiments**:
1. **Linear SA**: Simulate a one‑dimensional affine SA with Gaussian noise; compute empirical Wasserstein distance to the theoretical Gaussian limit for various \(\alpha\); confirm \(\sqrt{\alpha}\log(1/\alpha)\) rate.
2. **SGD on quadratic**: Run constant‑stepsize SGD on a strongly convex quadratic; compare empirical tail distribution (e.g., maximum entry) against Gaussian tail bound \(\alpha^{1/4}/a\).
3. **Quartic objective**: For \(f(x)=x^4\), run SGD with small constant \(\alpha\); estimate the stationary density and compare to the predicted Gibbs density \(\propto \exp(-2x^4/\alpha^2)\); check Wasserstein convergence as \(\alpha\) decreases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Can the two conjectures in Section 5 (Conjecture 5.1 on steady‑state moment bounds under general convex objectives and Conjecture 5.2 on uniform derivative bounds for the Stein solution of the associated Gibbs distribution) be proved?
- **Basis in paper**: Conjecture 5.1 and Conjecture 5.2 are stated in Section 5. The Future Work (Section 6) says a natural next step is “to complete the proof of conjectures in Section 5,” highlighting the need for regularity bounds for the Stein equation solution and moment bounds on the steady state.
- **Why unresolved**: Conjecture 5.1 requires establishing a Lyapunov‑type drift condition for the scaled iterates that yields uniform higher‑order moments, perhaps by adapting the semigroup representation of the Langevin diffusion used in Conjecture 5.2. Conjecture 5.2 asks for globally bounded derivatives (up to third order) of the solution to the Stein equation for a one‑dimensional Gibbs law; such regularity is not automatic and hinges on the tail behavior of the solution to a second‑order ODE, which is non‑trivial without additional structural assumptions.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: For Conjecture 5.1, establishing a Lyapunov‑type drift condition for the scaled iterates that yields uniform higher‑order moments, perhaps by adapting the semigroup representation of the Langevin diffusion used in Conjecture 5.2. For Conjecture 5.2, solving the Stein equation explicitly (when possible) or bounding the solution via probabilistic representations (e.g., integrals over the underlying Ornstein‑Uhlenbeck semigroup) to obtain sup‑norm bounds on the derivatives. A combined effort proving both would yield a rigorous Gaussian‑to‑Gibbs approximation theorem for non‑strongly‑convex SGD.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can the non‑uniform Berry‑Esseen tail bounds be sharpened so that their dependence on the deviation level \(a\) is less conservative, especially for small \(a\)?
- **Basis in paper**: Section 6 (Future Work) notes that “numerical experiments indicate that the tail bounds in main theorems may be overly conservative, especially for small values of \(a\),” and suggests refining them by bounding Wasserstein‑\(p\) distances for \(p > 1\).
- **Why unresolved**: The current tail bound (C.33) stems from converting a Wasserstein‑1 bound via a Markov‑type inequality, giving an \(\mathcal{O}(1/a)\) factor. This is optimal for the worst‑case Lipschitz test functions used in the dual formulation but is loose for the tail of a sub‑Gaussian‑like distribution; the decay for large deviations can be improved but a richer functional class is needed.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A refined analysis that uses higher‑order Lipschitz or smooth test functions in the Wasserstein dual, leading to a bound on Wasserstein‑\(p\) metrics for some \(p > 1\). Such bounds often translate into tail probabilities with faster decay (e.g., \(\exp(-c a^2)\)). Concretely, one would need to extend the Stein‑based generator comparison to handle higher‑order test functions and control the additional derivatives of the Stein solution, perhaps borrowing from recent developments on concentration via modified logarithmic Sobolev inequalities.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Does the polynomial dependence on the dimension \(d\) in the Wasserstein error bounds necessarily appear, or can it be reduced to a logarithmic dependence, thereby clarifying the curse of dimensionality in constant‑stepsize stochastic approximation?
- **Basis in paper**: Section 6 explicitly asks “whether the polynomial dependence on dimension \(d\) in all the constants can be improved to logarithmic dependence would clarify the curse of dimensionality,” pointing to a tightness analysis of the bounds.
- **Why unresolved**: The current proof uses a high‑dimensional Stein method which typically incurs factors like \(\sqrt{d}\) from Euclidean norms and operator norms; while these factors are explicit, they may be an artifact of the proof technique. It is unknown whether the true approximation error genuinely suffers a polynomial dimension blow‑up or if a more refined analysis (e.g., dimension‑free chaining or Gaussian mollification) can yield log‑dimensional factors.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Either (i) a new upper bound analysis that replaces the Euclidean norm‑based controls with more dimension‑robust tools—for example, using Gaussian interpolations or Stein’s method with bounded differences—leading to constants that grow only poly‑logarithmically in \(d\); or (ii) a lower‑bound construction (perhaps via a product‑form chain or Gaussian experiment) that shows any algorithm must incur at least a \(\sqrt{d}\) or \(d\) factor in the steady‑state error, thereby confirming that the polynomial dependence is inherent.

## Limitations
- Assumptions (Lipschitz drift, bounded derivatives, finite third moment for i.i.d. noise, Poisson‑solution regularity for Markovian) may be restrictive and hard to verify in practice.
- The Wasserstein bound contains a logarithmic factor \(\log(1/\alpha)\); its tightness and the explicit constant \(U\)’s dependence on dimension and spectral gap are unclear.
- The non‑asymptotic tail bound scales as \(\alpha^{1/4}\), which is likely suboptimal, and the Gibbs limiting law for non‑strongly‑convex SGD lacks extensive numerical validation beyond the examples presented.
