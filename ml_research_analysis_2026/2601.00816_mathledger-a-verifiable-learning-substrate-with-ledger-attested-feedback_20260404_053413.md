# MathLedger: A Verifiable Learning Substrate with Ledger-Attested Feedback

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.00816
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00816
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** MathLedger validates core infrastructure (per-cycle success measurement, fail-closed governance, dual-root attestation) but does not demonstrate learning capability or convergence.

## Executive Summary
MathLedger introduces a verifiable learning substrate where AI policy updates are driven solely by cryptographically attested verification outcomes (pass/fail/abstain) through Reflexive Formal Learning (RFL). The Phase I prototype confirmed that core mechanisms—attestation, governance, and measurement—function as intended in a controlled environment. A synthetic verifier fed a monotone ledger that records both reasoning and UI roots, ensuring tamper‑evident epistemic state. Governance predicates successfully capped claims under stress, and the Mirror Auditor achieved complete dual‑root coverage. However, no evidence of actual learning or convergence was produced; the system remains a working proof‑of‑concept for ledger‑attested feedback loops.

## Method Summary
The authors built a closed‑loop harness: a Derivation Engine produces proof attempts evaluated by a synthetic verifier. The verifier’s binary outcome (or abstention) steers policy updates via RFL without gradient information. Each epoch commits a reasoning root (proof artifacts) and a UI root (interface state) to a monotone ledger using a cryptographic dual‑attestation hash. Governance monitors per‑cycle success proxies (Δp) and inter‑arm variance, triggering fail‑closed caps (e.g., claim level L0) when thresholds are breached. Phase I experiments focused on infrastructure validation—ensuring metrics are measurable, governance predicates fire correctly, and the ledger remains fully attested—rather than on demonstrating learning efficacy.

## Key Results
- Phase I confirmed operational measurement of Δp and inter‑arm variance.
- Fail‑closed governance triggers (F5.2, F5.3) correctly capped claims under induced out‑of‑bounds conditions.
- The Mirror Auditor verified 100 % dual‑root attestation coverage for the audited snapshot.
- No learning capability or convergence was demonstrated.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Reflexive Formal Learning (RFL)
RFL updates a policy πₜ at each epoch using only the verifier outcome V(eₜ) ∈ {1,0,⊥}. The adjustment πₜ₊₁ = πₜ ⊕ ηₜ·Φ(V(eₜ),πₜ) increases the likelihood of policies that yield passes and decreases it for failures/abstentions. The core assumption is that verification outcomes are *Meaningful and Persistent*: they correlate with long‑term policy quality and are recorded immutably. Breakage occurs if outcomes become random or adversarial, causing the update rule to lose directional signal.

### Mechanism 2: Monotone Ledger with Dual Attestation
Each epoch commits a reasoning root rₜ (proof artifacts) and a UI root uₜ (interface state) to an append‑only ledger. The head hash Hₜ = Hash(EPOCH:‖rₜ‖uₜ) binds both roots cryptographically. Only verifier‑accepted proofs are admitted, guaranteeing monotonic knowledge growth (Kₜ ⊆ Kₜ₊₁). The assumption of *Cryptographic Binding and Monotonicity*—collision‑resistant hashing and append‑only storage—ensures any alteration changes Hₜ and is detectable. A hash collision or ledger fork would break tamper‑evidence.

### Mechanism 3: Fail‑Closed Governance with Negative Knowledge
Non‑admitted events (refutations, abstentions, inadmissible updates) are typed (REFUTED, ABSTAINED, etc.) and recorded in the evidence pack, bound to a frozen Governance Commitment Registry (GCR) hash. RFL consumes only V(eₜ); negative artifacts constrain but do not teach, preserving soundness while ensuring non‑silent governance. The *Separation of Concerns* assumption—negative evidence is auditable yet epistemically inert—is critical. Governance fails if negative artifacts contaminate learning or GCR binding fails.

## Foundational Learning
- **Formal Verification (Binary Judgment)**
  - *Why needed:* RFL requires crisp V(eₜ) outcomes; without a verifier that pass/fail/abstain, there is no learning signal.
  - *Quick check:* Can the configured verifier reproducibly classify any proof attempt as pass/fail/abstain under bounded resources?

- **Cryptographic Attestation (Immutable Binding)**
  - *Why needed:* Learning updates must be anchored to outcomes that cannot be repudiated or altered. The dual‑attestation hash Hₜ provides this binding.
  - *Quick check:* Is every Hₜ uniquely derivable from (rₜ, uₜ) and the prior ledger head, with any alteration detectable via hash mismatch?

- **Stochastic Approximation (Learning Dynamics)**
  - *Why needed:* Proposition 1 frames RFL as a stochastic approximation process, giving theoretical structure for convergence analysis.
  - *Quick check:* Does the update Φ produce bounded adjustments with zero‑mean noise conditioned on the current policy, and are step sizes ηₜ square‑summable but not summable?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Layer (UI Event → Curriculum Gate) → Reasoning Layer (Derivation Engine → Configured Verifier) → Attestation Layer (Dual Attestation → Monotone Ledger) → Governance Layer (Governance predicates F5.2/F5.3 → claim capping) → Learning Layer (RFL update rule consumes V(eₜ))

**Critical path:** Derivation Engine output → Verifier outcome → Dual Attestation hash Hₜ → RFL policy update.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- SHADOW mode (Phase I) vs. production gating: future deployments need fail‑closed blocking on verifier failure.  
- Synthetic proxy verifier vs. Lean kernel: proxy enables fast iteration; kernel provides soundness but higher latency.  
- Monotone ledger vs. mutability: immutability ensures auditability but prevents retraction of erroneous commitments.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High abstention rate (>95 %) with stable Δp indicates an overly cautious policy or overly strict verifier.  
- F5.2 (variance) trigger signals unstable learning dynamics.  
- Ledger hash mismatch in Mirror Auditor indicates attestation or storage corruption.  
- Missing artifact_kind tags in the evidence pack break fail‑closed replay verification.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **CAL-EXP-3:** Compare baseline (lr=0) vs. treatment (lr=0.1) to validate Δp computation and measure inter‑arm variance. Success criterion: consistent Δp time‑series and calculable variance.  
2. **Governance Stress Test:** Intentionally induce out‑of‑bounds conditions (e.g., inflated variance) to confirm F5.2/F5.3 fire and cap claims at L0. Success criterion: verdict JSON shows trigger activation and L0 claim level.  
3. **Dual‑Root Attestation Audit:** Use Mirror Auditor on a snapshot (e.g., 9bc8076) to verify 100 % block coverage and correct Hₜ derivation. Success criterion: auditor reports full attestation with no binding failures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can Reflexive Formal Learning (RFL) achieve convergence (stable, non‑oscillatory Δp) under realistic conditions where the variance gate is not persistently exceeded, or is non‑convergence an inherent property of the current design?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper states all Phase I runs failed the variance gate and explicitly "No convergence or convergence claims are made." The oscillatory Δp dynamics observed are noted as informative, not a failure, but the design's ability to dampen these oscillations remains unproven.
- **Why unresolved:** Phase I intentionally avoided testing for convergence; the experimental conditions (synthetic verifier, hermetic environment) and the RFL update rule's stability assumptions (e.g., contraction of the mean‑field drift `h`) were not validated. The system's fundamental dynamics under sustained feedback loops are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A subsequent experimental phase (e.g., Phase II or beyond) where RFL is run for sufficient epochs with varied learning rates and curricula, showing a sustained period where inter‑arm variance remains below the F5.2 threshold *and* Δp stabilizes without frequent F5.3 (windowed drift) triggers. This must be demonstrated across multiple seeds and PL slice configurations.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How robust is the fail‑closed governance to perturbations in auxiliary parameters *not* part of the frozen predicate set, and do governance verdicts (failure codes, claim level L) remain invariant under such perturbations?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 8.1 states: "Phase II of the calibration program addresses governance stability: specifically, whether the governance verdict... is invariant under perturbation of auxiliary parameters not part of the frozen predicate set." Phase II specification is frozen but not executed.
- **Why unresolved:** The claim that governance is "fail‑closed" is based on stress tests under specific out‑of‑bounds conditions (F5.2, F5.3). Its stability against subtle, non‑obvious parameter drifts (e.g., slight timing variations, resource fluctuations, or curriculum ordering effects) is a stated open item for Phase II.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Execution of the frozen Phase II specification, which involves systematic perturbation of defined "auxiliary parameters" while holding the core predicate set constant. The evidence would be a published analysis showing that the recorded governance verdict (from the evidence pack) is identical across all perturbation runs, or a precise characterization of the sensitivity boundaries.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does integrating a real, formal proof verifier (e.g., the planned Lean kernel) instead of the Phase I synthetic proxy verifier maintain the integrity of the RFL feedback loop and measurement substrate, or does the increased complexity and potential for verifier‑related abstentions break the observed dynamics?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 2.1.1 notes: "Phase I: synthetic proxy; Phase II+: Lean kernel." Section 6.2 positions MathLedger as infrastructure for proof generators. The synthetic verifier is acknowledged as a placeholder.
- **Why unresolved:** The synthetic proxy likely produces a cleaner, more predictable stream of pass/fail/abstain outcomes. A full theorem prover introduces its own resource bounds, timeout behaviors, and potential for verifier‑induced bottlenecks or different abstention patterns (e.g., due to proof search failure). The interaction between a complex verifier and the RFL update rule is uncharacterized.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A Phase II+ implementation where the Lean kernel replaces the synthetic verifier in the FO cycle harness. Key evidence would include: (a) comparative time‑series of Δp, abstention rate, and Hₜ dynamics against the Phase I baseline; (b) an audit of the evidence pack showing that dual‑attestation coverage (like the 100 % in 5.3) remains intact; (c) analysis of whether new abstention modes from the verifier correlate with meaningful policy updates or merely cause lock‑ups.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the ledger‑attested learning paradigm meaningfully scale to complex, multi‑step mathematical reasoning domains beyond the propositional logic (PL) slices used in Phase I, and does the abstention‑as‑signal incentive structure remain effective in such settings?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Conclusion states: "Whether this paradigm scales to complex reasoning remains an open question." Phase I used "PL slice analysis" as a controlled domain.
- **Why unresolved:** PL slices are a highly constrained, closed‑domain curriculum. Scaling to open‑ended problems (e.g., higher‑order logic, analysis, or hybrid discrete‑continuous domains) would likely drastically increase abstention rates, create longer proof artifacts, and stress the ledger's monotonicity and governance predicates. The utility of high abstention as an informative signal in such a regime is unproven.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Subsequent experiments applying the MathLedger substrate to a substantially more complex formal domain (e.g., a library of undergraduate‑level theorems in Lean). Evidence would include: sustained ledger growth without frequent governance cap triggers (F5.2/F5.3) despite increased problem difficulty, a non‑decreasing trend in the fraction of *verified* (pass) commitments over time, and a demonstrable correlation between policy updates (via RFL) and eventual reductions in abstention rates on held‑out complex problems.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Under an adversarial or Byzantine fault model where components (verifier, registry author, environment) may be maliciously compromised, what specific parts of the auditability and fail‑closed guarantees remain intact, and what new cryptographic or architectural assumptions are necessary?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The Threat Model in Section 5 explicitly states: "This architecture... does *not* protect against malicious verifier design, compromised registry authorship, or adversarial manipulation of the run environment itself. Phase I assumes an honest‑but‑fallible operator; Byzantine fault tolerance is out of scope."
- **Why unresolved:** The entire Phase I validation rests on ideal, non‑adversarial components. The system's resilience to active attacks—such as a verifier that strategically passes invalid proofs or a registry that sneakily updates governance rules—is completely unaddressed. The separation between "governance‑bound negative knowledge" and "learning authority" may have different failure modes under malice.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A formal security analysis extending the threat model, followed by targeted Phase I‑style experiments where specific components are substituted with malicious actors (e.g., a verifier that randomly fails valid proofs, a registry that silently forks). The evidence would be a specification of which invariants (e.g., monotonicity, binding, dual‑attestation coverage) still hold under which adversarial conditions, and which fail, supported by reproducible test runs showing the system's observable behavior (e.g., flaky evidence pack verification, governance predicate bypass).

## Limitations
- RFL’s core assumption—that verifier outcomes are meaningful and persistent—remains untested; no convergence evidence, and the synthetic proxy may not match future Lean kernel behavior.
- Governance predicates were validated only under induced metric thresholds; their robustness against adversarial or adaptive attacks is unexamined.
- All experiments were isolated, small‑scale, and synthetic; scalability, performance, and integration with external agents are unknown.
