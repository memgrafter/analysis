# SSR-GS: Separating Specular Reflection in Gaussian Splatting for Glossy Surface Reconstruction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05152  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05152  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Achieves state-of-the-art geometry on glossy scenes; normal MAE 1.52 on ShinySynthetic and Chamfer distance 0.60 with normal MAE 2.05 on GlossySynthetic.

## Executive Summary
SSR-GS extends 3D Gaussian splatting to reconstruct high‑fidelity geometry of glossy surfaces under complex illumination. It separates specular reflections into direct and indirect components: direct specular uses a roughness‑aware Mip‑Cubemap for efficient environment sampling, while indirect specular uses a neural predictor (IndiASG) with anisotropic spherical Gaussian lobes. Visual Geometry Priors (VGP) downweight photometric loss in specular‑dominated regions via a reflection score and incorporate VGGT‑derived depth and normal constraints to stabilize geometry. A two‑stage training regime first optimizes geometry with VGP and direct specular only, then adds indirect specular and visibility weighting from a ray‑traced mesh. Evaluations on ShinySynthetic and GlossySynthetic show superior surface normals and geometry compared to prior methods.

## Method Summary
SSR‑GS builds on 3D Gaussian splatting with physically based deferred rendering. Each Gaussian carries material parameters: Fresnel reflectance at normal incidence (F₀), roughness (r), and diffuse albedo (c_diff). The rendering separates a volumetric diffuse term from a specular term. The specular component is split:  
- **Direct specular** samples a prefiltered Mip‑Cubemap. The roughness maps to a mip level ℓ = r²·(L_max−1), and a trilinear query yields the incident radiance.  
- **Indirect specular** uses the IndiASG module, which employs 33 fixed anisotropic spherical Gaussian lobes in tangent space. A 3‑layer MLP FΘ (128 units) takes position p, reflected direction ω_r, roughness r, and a residual color C_res (encoded with positional and integral‑direction embeddings) to output per‑lobe amplitude, sharpness, and orientation.  
- Visual Geometry Priors (VGP) consist of: (1) a Reflection Score (RS) that downweights photometric loss where multi‑view color consistency is low; (2) depth and normal regularizers derived from VGGT, using a confidence‑weighted ℓ₂ depth loss (with scale‑shift alignment) and a combined ℓ₁ + angular normal loss.  

Training proceeds in two stages: Stage 1 (10 k iterations) activates VGP (RS‑weighted L1) and direct specular only (no indirect, no visibility). Stage 2 (20 k iterations) adds full specular, including indirect lighting and a visibility weight w_vis obtained via ray tracing a TSDF mesh extracted each iteration; VGP reweighting is removed.

## Key Results
- **ShinySynthetic:** Normal mean angular error (MAE) of 1.52, the best reported.  
- **GlossySynthetic:** State‑of‑the‑art Chamfer distance of 0.60 and normal MAE of 2.05.  
- Outperforms prior glossy reconstruction methods such as Ref‑Gaussian and MaterialRefGS.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
- **Separation of direct/indirect specular** allows accurate modeling of glossy reflections and multi‑bounce lighting without cross‑talk.  
- **Mip‑Cubemap** efficiently pre‑filters the environment according to roughness, reducing variance and aliasing in direct reflections.  
- **IndiASG** uses a compact set of anisotropic spherical Gaussian lobes guided by a neural predictor to capture bounce lighting with limited overfitting.  
- **Visual Geometry Priors** mitigate photometric ambiguity in specular regions: RS downweights unreliable pixels, while VGGT depth/normal constraints provide geometric supervision from a robust pretrained model.  
- **Two‑stage training** stabilizes geometry reconstruction first (direct + VGP), then refines appearance with indirect effects and visibility, avoiding early interference from noisy indirect lighting.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  

Input images → VGGT (depth, normal, confidence)  
Environment map → Mip‑Cubemap (prefiltered)  
Gaussian parameters (position, covariance, opacity) + material (F₀, roughness, c_diff)  
→ Direct specular: roughness → mip level ℓ → Mip‑Cubemap(ω_r)  
→ Indirect specular: (p, ω_r, r, C_res) + PE/IDE → MLP FΘ → (a_j, λ_j, µ_j) → sum over 33 IndiASG lobes  
→ Combine diffuse + direct + indirect → rendered image, depth, normal, alpha  
→ Reflection Score (RS) from multi‑view consistency (using VGGT depth)  
→ Losses: L₁ photometric (RS‑weighted) + VGGT depth (scale‑shift aligned) + normal (ℓ₁ + angular, weighted by W_VGGT)  
→ Two‑stage scheduler: Stage 1 (direct only, VGP active) → Stage 2 (full specular, visibility w_vis from ray‑traced mesh)

**Critical path per iteration:**  
1. Sort and rasterize Gaussians to produce per‑pixel buffers (opacity, depth, normal).  
2. For each pixel, compute direct specular via Mip‑Cubemap using roughness and reflection direction.  
3. Compute indirect specular via IndiASG using the same Gaussians’ outputs.  
4. Combine with diffuse term.  
5. Compute photometric loss (weighted by RS).  
6. Compute VGGT‑based depth and normal losses.  
7. Back‑propagate and update Gaussians, material, and MLP parameters.  
8. Periodically (every few hundred iterations) fuse depth into a TSDF mesh, ray‑trace to obtain visibility weight w_vis for Stage 2.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Mip‑Cubemap** reduces memory and sampling cost but with only 4 mip levels it may blur high‑frequency details at intermediate roughness.  
- **IndiASG** uses fixed lobes and an MLP predictor to balance expressiveness and overfitting; the fixed lobe set limits anisotropy resolution.  
- **RS downweighting** protects against specular outliers but may discard valid data if the reflection score is miscomputed.  
- **Two‑stage training** avoids early indirect interference but requires careful scheduling of stage transitions and VGP removal.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Geometry collapse in glossy areas → RS not down‑weighting or VGGT depth misalignment.  
- Noisy indirect component → MLP predictor under‑regularized or insufficient training of IndiASG.  
- Banding/artifacts in direct specular → incorrect mip level selection or flawed trilinear filtering.  
- Over‑smoothing from VGGT → confidence weighting too aggressive, overriding photometric signal.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Ablate VGP components:** Disable Reflection Score (RS) and VGGT losses separately in Stage 1; measure impact on normal MAE and Chamfer distance to validate each prior’s contribution.  
2. **Direct specular sanity check:** Place a single Gaussian with known roughness in a controlled environment; render direct specular only and compare against ground‑truth reflection to verify Mip‑Cubemap sampling and mip level mapping.  
3. **Indirect specular visualization:** Render scenes with indirect component isolated (masking direct); inspect for haze or artifacts and confirm that bounce lighting appears plausible and not overly smooth.

## Limitations
- **VGP and VGGT integration:** The conversion of VGGT’s 3D feature grid into per‑pixel depth, normal, and confidence maps is vague; scale alignment and confidence handling are likely sources of error or over‑regularization.  
- **Ray tracing for visibility:** TSDF fusion parameters (voxel size, truncation) and the exact computation of w_vis (binary vs. soft) are unspecified, affecting multi‑bounce lighting accuracy.  
- **Training hyperparameters:** Loss weights, learning rates for Gaussian, material, and MLP parameters, and the precise schedule for enabling indirect specular are not provided, making exact replication difficult.
