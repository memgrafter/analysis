# HiPER: Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning with Explicit Credit Assignment for Large Language Model Agents

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16165
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16165
- Reference count: 40
- Key outcome: HiPER achieves 97.4% success on ALFWorld and 83.3% on WebShop with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, outperforming prior methods by 6.6% and 8.3% respectively

## Executive Summary
HiPER addresses the challenge of training LLM agents for long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks where flat reinforcement learning policies struggle with credit assignment. It introduces a hierarchical decomposition that separates high-level planning from low-level execution through a structured Plan-Execute interface. The method combines this with Hierarchical Advantage Estimation (HAE), which provides aligned learning signals for both planner and executor components while reducing variance compared to standard advantage estimation.

## Method Summary
HiPER factorizes the policy into a high-level planner that proposes subgoals and a low-level executor that carries out actions. These components coordinate through a structured Plan-Execute interface where the planner sets subgoals and the executor works toward them. Hierarchical Advantage Estimation (HAE) delivers aligned learning signals by aggregating returns over subgoal segments for planning advantages and computing stepwise advantages within segments for execution, coupled through boundary bootstrapping. This yields unbiased gradients with reduced variance compared to flat GAE, improving performance on complex sequential tasks.

## Key Results
- Achieves 97.4% success rate on ALFWorld compared to prior methods
- Reaches 83.3% success rate on WebShop, improving over strongest prior method by 8.3%
- Demonstrates especially large gains on tasks requiring multiple sequential subtasks
- Uses Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the underlying LLM model

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
HiPER's hierarchical decomposition addresses the credit assignment problem inherent in flat RL for LLM agents by separating concerns: the planner handles long-term goal reasoning while the executor focuses on immediate action execution. This structure naturally aligns with how humans approach complex tasks through planning and execution phases. The HAE mechanism provides temporally consistent advantages by bootstrapping at subgoal boundaries, ensuring that credit flows appropriately through the hierarchy. The variance reduction comes from using more stable return estimates over longer subgoal segments rather than noisy single-step returns.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Planner -> Subgoal Proposal -> Executor -> Actions -> Environment -> Returns -> HAE Computation
**Critical Path**: Planner proposes subgoal → Executor executes until subgoal completion or failure → HAE computes advantages using boundary bootstrapping → Policy gradients update both components
**Design Tradeoffs**: Hierarchical decomposition adds complexity but enables better credit assignment; HAE reduces variance but requires careful subgoal boundary detection; joint training ensures coordination but may be sensitive to hyperparameters
**Failure Signatures**: Poor subgoal identification leads to ineffective decomposition; excessive variance in HAE signals boundary detection problems; suboptimal performance indicates planner-executor misalignment
**First Experiments**: 1) Run ablation removing HAE to isolate credit assignment benefits, 2) Test with random subgoals to measure importance of planner quality, 3) Vary subgoal frequency to find optimal decomposition granularity

## Limitations
- No formal proof that HAE is unbiased, only intuitive explanation of variance reduction
- Performance evaluation limited to two domains (ALFWorld and WebShop) with controlled environments
- No ablation study to isolate contributions of hierarchical decomposition versus HAE
- Method may be sensitive to hyperparameter tuning and scalability beyond tested domains

## Confidence
- Hierarchical decomposition design: High
- HAE variance reduction claim: Medium
- Empirical performance gains: Medium
- Scalability to larger models or more complex environments: Low
