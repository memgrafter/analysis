# Super Research: Answering Highly Complex Questions with Large Language Models through Super Deep and Super Wide Research

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00582  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00582  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art Gemini Deep Research attains only 28.62% overall score on the Super Research benchmark, with particularly low logical consistency (22.92) and utility (22.98).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Super Research, a framework for answering highly complex questions that require long-horizon planning, massive evidence gathering, and synthesis across heterogeneous sources. The authors construct a challenging benchmark of 300 expert-written tasks spanning ten domains, each demanding up to 100+ retrieval steps and exposure to 1,000+ web pages. The proposed method integrates three pillars: structured decomposition into a research plan, super-wide retrieval to gather diverse perspectives, and super-deep investigation via iterative queries to resolve uncertainties. The output is a verifiable report with fine-grained citations and intermediate artifacts. Evaluation uses a graph-anchored auditing protocol covering five dimensions (Coverage & Comprehension, Logical Consistency, Report Utility, Objectivity, Citation Health). Experiments on 12 representative systems reveal that even the best model, Gemini Deep Research, scores below 30%, exposing a significant capability gap. The benchmark serves as a ceiling-level stress test, and model performance strongly predicts general research competence.

## Method Summary
The Super Research method proceeds in three main stages. First, the input question is broken down by a structured decomposition module into a detailed research plan of sub-questions. Second, a super-wide retriever collects a broad set of candidate documents or perspectives, ensuring diverse coverage. Third, an investigator engine performs iterative querying to deepen understanding, resolve contradictions, and fill evidence gaps. The process produces intermediate artifacts (e.g., retrieved snippets, extracted claims) and culminates in a synthesizer that assembles a final report with explicit citations. The accompanying graph-anchored evaluation constructs a graph linking claims, supporting evidence, and sources to compute multi-dimensional quality scores, providing fine-grained diagnostics and incentivizing verifiable outputs.

## Key Results
- Gemini Deep Research achieves an overall score of 28.62%, with low logical consistency (22.92) and utility (22.98).
- Graph-based evaluation metrics show greater sensitivity and higher inter-evaluator consistency than LLM-as-judge baselines.
- All 12 evaluated systems score below 30%, underscoring the benchmark’s difficulty and the current limitations of LLMs in complex research tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The three pillars address fundamental challenges in complex question answering. Structured decomposition modularizes the problem, reducing error propagation and enabling parallel exploration of sub-questions. Super-wide retrieval ensures a comprehensive evidence base, mitigating source bias and increasing the likelihood of covering all required aspects. Super-deep investigation allows the system to iteratively refine its understanding, resolve uncertainties, and gather targeted evidence, thereby reducing hallucinations and improving factual accuracy. Together, these components produce a robust research pipeline that can be scaled and audited. The graph-anchored evaluation further aligns incentives toward verifiable, well-structured reports.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)  
  **Why needed:** To supplement model parameters with up-to-date external knowledge, essential for fact-intensive tasks.  
  **Quick check:** Compare closed-book vs. retrieval-augmented performance on a knowledge QA dataset.

- **Concept:** Iterative Refinement  
  **Why needed:** Complex queries often require multiple passes to resolve ambiguities and accumulate sufficient evidence.  
  **Quick check:** Measure output quality with a single pass versus multiple iterative questioning cycles.

- **Concept:** Structured Decomposition  
  **Why needed:** Breaking a complex problem into manageable sub-tasks reduces cognitive load and enables systematic exploration.  
  **Quick check:** Decompose a multi-faceted question into sub-questions and verify each can be answered independently.

- **Concept:** Fine-grained Citation  
  **Why needed:** Enables verification, increases trust, and holds the system accountable for factual claims.  
  **Quick check:** Randomly sample citations and check precision/recall against the original source material.

- **Concept:** Graph-based Evaluation  
  **Why needed:** Captures multi-dimensional quality (coverage, consistency, utility) and provides actionable diagnostics beyond a single aggregate score.  
  **Quick check:** Compute graph metrics (e.g., claim-evidence graph connectivity) and correlate with human evaluations.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
User Question -> Decomposer -> Planner -> Super-wide Retriever -> Investigator (Iterative Query Engine) -> Synthesizer -> Report Generator -> Citation Verifier -> Final Report

**Critical path:** The pipeline must pass through decomposition, retrieval, iterative investigation, and synthesis; any break in this sequence prevents the generation of a final report.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Super-wide retrieval improves coverage but introduces higher computational cost and noisier evidence.  
- Deep iterative investigation increases depth and accuracy at the expense of latency and potential diminishing returns.  
- Structured decomposition assumes tasks can be cleanly partitioned; some queries have tightly coupled sub-questions that resist splitting.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low logical consistency or utility scores from graph evaluation.  
- Missing, inaccurate, or unverifiable citations.  
- Incomplete coverage of the question’s required aspects.  
- Excessive iteration with no improvement (staleness).  
- Excessive resource consumption (time, compute).

**First experiments:**  
1. Run the full pipeline on a small subset (e.g., 10 benchmark tasks) to validate end-to-end functionality.  
2. Ablate the super-wide retrieval (e.g., limit to top‑k results) and measure impact on coverage and citation health.  
3. Vary the maximum number of iterative investigation steps to find the optimal quality‑cost trade‑off.

## Limitations
- Expert-written benchmark tasks may introduce selection bias and cover a limited range of domains.  
- Graph-anchored criteria (e.g., Logical Consistency, Utility) lack precise operational definitions, potentially leading to subjective scoring.  
- The 12 “representative” systems evaluated are unspecified; it is unclear whether they span a diverse set of architectures and capabilities.
