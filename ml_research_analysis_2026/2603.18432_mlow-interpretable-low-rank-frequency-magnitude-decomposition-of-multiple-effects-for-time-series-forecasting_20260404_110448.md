# MLOW: Interpretable Low-Rank Frequency Magnitude Decomposition of Multiple Effects for Time Series Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.18432
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18432
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: MLOW reduces forecasting error (MSE) by ~8–9% on Traffic and ETTh1 datasets when integrated with iTransformer, while adding minimal architectural changes.

## Executive Summary
Time series forecasting can benefit from interpretable decomposition of signals into constituent patterns such as trend and seasonality. MLOW introduces a novel frequency‑domain approach that learns a low‑rank representation of magnitude spectra via Hyperplane‑NMF, a method that enforces non‑negativity and orthogonality. The decomposition yields sample‑specific components that, together with a residual, are fed to a forecasting backbone. Evaluated on eight datasets, MLOW consistently improves state‑of‑the‑art models (iTransformer, PatchTST) with negligible overhead, and visualizations confirm meaningful separation of temporal patterns. The method also offers strong efficiency gains compared to standard NMF.

## Method Summary
MLOW processes each sliding window of length \(2K\) (e.g., 336). It computes the real FFT to obtain magnitude coefficients \(R_i\) and phases \(\phi_k\). A basis matrix \(B_i\) (size \(K \times T\)) is built from cosines using the phases and scaling factors \(a_k\) (2 for \(k=1..K-1\), 1 for \(k=K\)). The core of the method is a pre‑trained non‑negative matrix \(H\) (size \(V \times K\)) obtained by Hyperplane‑NMF on all training magnitude vectors. Hyperplane‑NMF initializes \(H\) from the top PCA right singular vectors (with negatives replaced by small positives) and runs 1000 multiplicative update iterations with a cosine‑similarity regularizer (\(\lambda=20\)). During inference, for a sample: \(W_i = R_i H^\top\) (low‑rank coefficients), \(P_i = H B_i\) (component basis), and \(Z_i = W_i \odot P_i\) (element‑wise product) yields \(V\) reconstructed components. The residual is \(X_{i,r} = X_i - \sum Z_i - X_{i,m}\), where \(X_{i,m}\) is the DC mean repeated \(T\) times. The backbone input is formed by concatenating \(Z_i^\top\) (\(T \times V\)) and \(X_{i,r}\) (\(T \times 1\)), producing a tensor of shape \(T \times (V+1)\). The backbone’s initial linear layer is modified to accept \((V+1) \cdot T\) features (iTransformer) or \((V+1) \cdot f\) (PatchTST). \(H\) remains frozen during backbone training.

## Key Results
- **Consistent error reduction**: On iTransformer, average MSE drops from 0.428 to 0.393 on Traffic and from 0.456 to 0.424 on ETTh1; similar gains observed on other backbones and datasets.
- **Efficiency**: Hyperplane‑NMF inference costs ~0.26 ms vs 121 ms for standard NMF, while maintaining performance.
- **Interpretability**: Visualizations reveal hierarchical separation of trend, seasonality, and noise, and robustness across different series.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MLOW leverages the frequency domain’s natural separation of magnitude (energy distribution) and phase (temporal alignment). Learning a low‑rank basis \(H\) from training magnitudes captures common spectral patterns; the per‑sample coefficients \(W_i\) then adapt the reconstruction to each series. Hyperplane‑NMF’s cosine‑similarity regularizer promotes diversity among basis vectors, avoiding redundancy and improving generalization. Because \(H\) is fixed after training, it can be applied to new data without re‑optimization, greatly speeding up inference. The use of an extended FFT window (\(2K\)) provides finer frequency resolution than using only the input length (\(T\)), mitigating spectral leakage. Phase‑aware construction of \(B_i\) ensures that the reconstructed components align correctly in time. Finally, feeding both the structured components \(Z_i\) and the residual \(X_{i,r}\) to the backbone allows the model to focus on modeling the residual while the decomposition supplies interpretable features.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)**  
   *Why needed*: Efficiently transforms a time window into its frequency spectrum, providing magnitudes and phases that are the raw material for decomposition.  
   *Quick check*: For a random vector of length 336, compute `np.fft.rfft` and verify the output length is 169 (K+1).

2. **Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF)**  
   *Why needed*: A classic technique for parts‑based learning; its non‑negativity constraint leads to additive, interpretable components. Hyperplane‑NMF extends NMF with a regularizer.  
   *Quick check*: Apply `sklearn.decomposition.NMF` to a non‑negative matrix and ensure both factors are non‑negative and reconstruction error is low.

3. **Low‑rank approximation**  
   *Why needed*: Reduces the dimensionality of the magnitude space, focusing on dominant patterns and discarding noise, which aids generalization and efficiency.  
   *Quick check*: Perform PCA on a magnitude matrix and examine the explained variance ratio; a few components should capture most variance.

4. **Hyperplane‑NMF**  
   *Why needed*: Introduces a cosine‑similarity regularizer that encourages orthogonality of basis vectors, improving diversity and preventing trivial solutions.  
   *Quick check*: After training \(H\), compute pairwise cosine similarities among its rows; values should be significantly below 1.

5. **Phase‑aware basis reconstruction**  
   *Why needed*: Magnitude alone cannot reconstruct the original time series; the basis \(B_i\) built from phases ensures correct temporal alignment of components.  
   *Quick check*: For a sample, reconstruct \(Z_i\) and compute correlation with the original segment; it should be high for the sum of \(Z_i\) plus residual.

6. **Residual computation**  
   *Why needed*: Captures information not explained by the low‑rank components (e.g., noise or irregularities) and provides an additional informative channel to the backbone.  
   *Quick check*: Compute residual \(X_{i,r}\) and verify its variance is smaller than that of the original and that it appears high‑frequency.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Raw window (2K) -> FFT -> (R, φ) -> Compute B (K×T) from φ -> Load pre‑trained H (V×K) -> W = R Hᵀ, P = H B -> Z = W ⊙ P -> Residual = X_i - Σ Z - X_i_m -> Concat(Zᵀ, Residual) -> Modified backbone linear layer -> Forecast

**Critical path**  
The inference pipeline must execute in order: FFT on the raw window, extraction of R and φ, construction of B, multiplication with H to obtain Z and residual, concatenation, and finally the backbone forward pass. The offline training of H on all training magnitudes is a prerequisite but does not affect the sequential runtime.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Frequency vs. time domain*: Frequency decomposition offers global patterns but may sacrifice local detail; phase‑aware basis mitigates this.  
- *Rank V*: Larger V improves reconstructive fidelity but increases backbone input size and risk of overfitting; V must be tuned.  
- *Hyperplane‑NMF vs. PCA/NMF*: Hyperplane‑NMF ensures non‑negativity and learns diverse bases without re‑optimizing per new sample, unlike NMF, while being more interpretable than PCA.  
- *Plug‑and‑play integration*: Minimal backbone modifications enable reuse of existing models but freeze the decomposition, preventing end‑to‑end co‑adaptation.  
- *Extended window (2K)*: Provides finer frequency resolution but demands more past context; may be infeasible for short series.

**Failure signatures**  
- *Spectral leakage*: Using FFT on a window shorter than 2K smears frequencies.  
- *Basis mis‑alignment*: B must correspond to the last T indices; using earlier indices shifts patterns.  
- *Negative H entries*: Should not occur after updates; tiny negatives indicate a bug.  
- *Diverging Hyperplane‑NMF loss*: Improper multiplicative update or gradient splitting.  
- *Shape mismatch*: Backbone input must equal (V+1)*T or (V+1)*f; check before training.  
- *Poor forecasting*: Double‑counting X_i_m in residual or forgetting to freeze H.

**First experiments**  
1. **Reconstruction sanity check**: Generate synthetic series with known frequency components (e.g., sine waves), decompose with MLOW, and verify that each \(Z_i\) corresponds to the intended frequency and that the residual is near‑zero.  
2. **Hyperplane‑NMF convergence**: Train H on a small magnitude set, plot reconstruction loss over iterations, and confirm monotonic decrease with all \(H \ge 0\).  
3. **End‑to‑end improvement**: Train iTransformer on a subset of ETTh1 with and without MLOW (same backbone hyperparameters, fixed seeds); expect validation MSE to improve within a few epochs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Automatic rank selection**: How can the optimal rank V be determined without manual tuning? The authors note V is not universally fixed, suggesting an adaptive mechanism based on explained variance or validation performance.  
- **Transfer to other tasks**: Can MLOW’s interpretable decomposition benefit anomaly detection or classification? The authors plan to explore this beyond forecasting.  
- **Comparison with classical decomposition**: How does MLOW stack up against methods like STL in interpretability and forecasting accuracy? The paper lacks this comparison.  
- **Effect of regularization λ**: The cosine‑similarity weight λ is set to 20 by default; its impact on component diversity and forecasting performance remains unstudied.

## Limitations
- The optimal rank V must be manually tuned for each dataset, hindering automation.  
- Implementation ambiguities (e.g., channel count \(V+1\) vs. \(V+2\), Nyquist factor \(a_K\), distribution for initializing \(H\)) can lead to inconsistent reproductions.  
- No empirical comparison with classical decomposition methods (e.g., STL) is provided, leaving a gap in understanding relative benefits.

## Confidence
- **Hyperplane‑NMF algorithm correctness**: High  
- **Forecasting improvement (MSE/MAE) when integrated with strong backbones**: Medium (due to manual tuning and configuration choices)  
- **Efficiency advantage over standard NMF**: High  
- **Interpretability of decomposed components**: Medium (supported by visualizations but no quantitative human study)  
- **Generalization to unseen data without re‑optimization**: High
