# On-Policy Supervised Fine-Tuning for Efficient Reasoning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13407
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13407
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** On-policy supervised fine-tuning (SFT) simplifies reinforcement learning for efficient reasoning by removing KL regularization and group-wise normalization, reducing chain-of-thought length by 70–80% while matching or improving accuracy across five math benchmarks, surpassing ten RL baselines, cutting GPU memory by 50%, speeding convergence by 70%, and achieving the best accuracy-efficiency trade-off.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the standard reinforcement learning (RL) approach for efficient reasoning in large language models. The authors show that when correctness and length are directly verifiable (as in mathematical problem‑solving), common RL components such as KL regularization and group‑wise reward normalization are unnecessary and even harmful. By simplifying the RL objective to a truncation‑based length penalty, the training process reduces to on‑policy supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) on self‑generated responses filtered for correctness and conciseness. Evaluated on DeepSeek‑R1 1.5B and 7B models across five math benchmarks, the proposed method achieves dramatic reductions in chain‑of‑thought length (70–80%) without sacrificing accuracy, outperforms ten RL baselines, and improves training efficiency (e.g., 50% less GPU memory, 70% faster convergence). The work reframes efficient reasoning as a data‑filtering problem rather than a complex RL optimization.

## Method Summary
On‑policy SFT proceeds as follows: For each training batch, the current policy generates multiple rollouts per question using temperature 1.0. Each rollout is evaluated for answer correctness (via automated verification) and token count. Only responses that are correct and whose length does not exceed a threshold τ (e.g., 3500 tokens) are retained. The standard supervised fine‑tuning loss—negative log‑likelihood of the retained responses—is applied, with token‑wise losses normalized by the maximum response length in the batch to prevent bias toward shorter sequences. No reference model, reward model, or group‑wise normalization is used. This simple loop iteratively improves the policy using its own high‑quality, concise outputs, effectively turning the RL problem into an on‑policy SFT problem.

## Key Results
- Chain‑of‑thought length reduced by 70–80% while maintaining or improving accuracy on GSM8K, MATH‑500, AMC23, AIME24, and AIME25.
- Surpasses ten RL‑based baselines and achieves the best trade‑off between accuracy and efficiency (Eff metric).
- Training memory footprint cut by 50% and convergence speed increased by 70% compared to standard RL methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Removal of KL Regularization
- **Claim:** KL regularization is unnecessary when correctness and length are directly verifiable; dropping it simplifies training and reduces memory/compute overhead.
- **Mechanism:** In RLHF, KL penalties prevent policy deviation from a reference to avoid reward overoptimization under distribution shift. Here, rewards are exact scalars (accuracy, length), so a reference model is not needed. Removing the βKL term eliminates the need to maintain a reference model and avoids constraining updates.
- **Core assumption:** Correctness and length are objective, directly verifiable, and free from learned reward models.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.1 state that KL loses its role under verifiable rewards. Empirical results show stable training and improved efficiency without KL.
- **Break condition:** If rewards become noisy, subjective, or learned (e.g., from a reward model), KL regularization may be needed to prevent distributional collapse.

### Mechanism 2: Removal of Group‑Wise Reward Normalization
- **Claim:** Group‑wise reward normalization (as in GRPO) is ill‑suited for multi‑reward settings, causing gradient ambiguity; a simple baseline stabilizes training.
- **Mechanism:** GRPO normalizes rewards within each rollout group to avoid value estimation. With multiple reward components (accuracy and length), normalization can make distinct reward vectors appear identical (e.g., (0,1) vs. (1,1)), leading to uninformative gradients. Using a mean reward baseline instead retains signal distinctions and reduces bias.
- **Core assumption:** Optimization involves heterogeneous reward signals that combine non‑linearly.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.1 argue that group‑wise normalization introduces optimization bias under multiple rewards. Ablations in Section 5 confirm stability without it.
- **Break condition:** If only a single scalar reward is used, group‑wise normalization might still be beneficial for variance reduction.

### Mechanism 3: Equivalence to On‑Policy SFT via Truncation Reward
- **Claim:** With a truncation‑based length penalty and no KL/group normalization, the RL gradient equals the SFT loss; thus training reduces to supervised fine‑tuning on on‑policy data filtered for correctness and conciseness.
- **Mechanism:** A binary reward of 1 is given only to responses that are both correct and shorter than τ; otherwise 0. The resulting policy gradient update, after algebraic manipulation (Eq. 9), matches the negative log‑likelihood gradient of SFT applied to the filtered responses. The on‑policy nature ensures data relevance.
- **Core assumption:** A fixed τ effectively separates desirable (correct and concise) from undesirable outputs, and the on‑policy distribution remains well‑behaved.
- **Evidence anchors:** Derivation in Section 3.2 and empirical success across multiple benchmarks (Section 5). Figures show that on‑policy SFT attains the same or better performance than RL variants while being far simpler.
- **Break condition:** If τ is set too low, valid correct responses are discarded; if too high, inefficient responses survive. Also, if the on‑policy data degenerates (e.g., mode collapse), SFT may overfit.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Policy network (LLM) -> Rollout generator -> (Correctness verifier & Length filter) -> SFT trainer -> Optimizer (AdamW)

**Critical path:** 
1. Sample a batch of training questions.  
2. Generate G rollouts per question using the current policy (temperature 1.0).  
3. For each rollout, compute correctness (automated answer check) and token count; keep only if correct and length ≤ τ.  
4. Compute the SFT loss as the average negative log‑likelihood per token across retained responses, normalizing by the maximum length in the batch.  
5. Update policy parameters with AdamW (e.g., lr 1e‑7).  
6. Repeat for the desired number of steps.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Simplicity vs. flexibility: Removing KL and normalization reduces memory/compute but may not handle noisy rewards.
- Truncation threshold τ trades accuracy for compression; setting it too low discards valid responses, too high yields inefficient outputs.
- On‑policy data ensures relevance but requires frequent generation, incurring generation cost.

**Failure signatures:**
- Training instability if rollout temperature deviates from 1.0 (violates on‑policy assumption). Monitor loss and length variance.
- Length not decreasing: τ may be too high or rollout count G insufficient.
- Sudden accuracy drop: τ too low or on‑policy data lacks diversity (mode collapse).
- Normalizing by average response length instead of batch max can cause collapse toward short outputs.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Main benchmark comparison:** Evaluate on‑policy SFT against 10 baselines on five math benchmarks; report accuracy, token length, compression, and Eff score.
2. **On‑policy vs. off‑policy ablation:** Compare using on‑policy filtered data versus a fixed off‑policy dataset; on‑policy yields better compression without accuracy loss.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Test rollout temperature (0.3–1.5), group size G (4–32), and τ (3k, 3.5k, 4k); key findings: temperature must be 1.0, more rollouts improve compression, τ controls trade‑off.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Which property of on‑policy data drives its superiority?** The paper compares on‑policy and off‑policy SFT but does not isolate whether the benefit comes from data freshness, behavioral policy matching, or filtering cadence. Targeted ablations that independently vary data age, policy divergence, and filtering frequency are needed.
2. **Do conclusions generalize to non‑verifiable rewards?** The analysis assumes directly verifiable correctness and length; it is unclear whether KL regularization and group‑wise normalization become necessary in domains with noisy, subjective, or learned rewards (e.g., creative writing). Evidence from such tasks would test the limits of the claims.
3. **Why does on‑policy SFT achieve strong Pass@N without negative samples?** Recent work emphasizes negative samples for exploration, yet on‑policy SFT uses only positive (correct, concise) responses. The role of negative samples in efficient reasoning—whether they help or hurt—remains unexplained. A systematic study varying negative sample proportion across model scales and difficulties could resolve this.

## Limitations
- **Dataset and verification details are underspecified.** The paper does not describe preprocessing of DeepScaleR or the exact answer extraction/comparison method, which are critical for faithful reproduction.
- **Generalization scope is narrow.** Experiments are limited to mathematical reasoning benchmarks (five tasks) and two model sizes (1.5B, 7B). Extrapolation to other domains or larger models is uncertain.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity.** The truncation threshold τ and rollout temperature are crucial; the paper notes necessary conditions (e.g., temperature 1.0) but lacks a thorough robustness analysis.
