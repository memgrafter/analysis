# Beyond Static Alignment: Hierarchical Policy Control for LLM Safety via Risk-Aware Chain-of-Thought

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.06650
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06650
*   **Reference Count:** 19
*   **Primary Result:** PACT achieves state-of-the-art safety (0.959) while improving helpfulness and enabling 31% relative gain in runtime policy controllability without compromising global safety.

## Executive Summary
PACT introduces a hierarchical framework to resolve the inherent trade-off between safety and helpfulness in Large Language Models caused by static, monolithic safety policies. It separates a non-overridable, training-embedded Global Policy for critical, catastrophic risks from a runtime-configurable User Policy for domain-specific, negotiable risks. Decisions are made explicit via a risk-aware Chain-of-Thought (CoTPath) that structures reasoning into a deterministic Classify→Act pathway with label-to-action mappings. On five safety benchmarks, PACT matches leading models in safety while providing more helpful guidance than blanket-rejecting systems. Crucially, it demonstrates fine-grained controllability over user-defined policies, allowing developers to adjust risk tolerance for specific contexts while maintaining an unbreachable safety baseline for globally defined harms.

## Method Summary
PACT is trained via a self-distillation pipeline on a base model (Qwen3-8b). For a diverse set of prompts, the model generates structured reasoning traces that first classify a query under a Global Risk taxonomy (IR labels). If a critical risk is detected, an early-exit occurs. Otherwise, the model classifies the query under a User Policy taxonomy (UA labels). For the final label, the model consults the active policy's Label2Action mapping to select an action mode (Comply, Guide, Reject) and generates a response congruent with that mode. This entire sequence—global detection, optional user detection, action selection, and response—forms the CoTPath. The model is fine-tuned on ~573k such CoTPath samples using standard supervised loss. At inference, a runtime interface supplies the active User Policy, enabling dynamic configuration of risk tolerance for non-critical categories while the Global Policy remains immutable.

## Key Results
*   Under Global Policy alone, PACT achieves a safety score of **0.959** and a CoSA-Score of **0.201**, matching state-of-the-art safety while offering more helpful guidance than minimal-rejection baselines.
*   For runtime controllability on novel, user-defined risk categories (PACT-Test), PACT attains a CoSA-Score of **0.557**, a **31%** relative improvement over baselines, while maintaining **99.4%** safety on globally triggered risks.
*   Ablations confirm that using GUIDE (not REJECT) as the global action improves helpfulness by 53.3% with only a 2.5% safety drop, and removing the explicit CoTPath structure causes a **58%** drop in controllability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hierarchical Policy Separation
- **Claim:** Hierarchical policy separation mitigates the safety-helpfulness trade-off by enforcing immutable safety boundaries for critical risks while allowing runtime configuration for domain-specific needs.
- **Mechanism:** A non-overridable Global Policy (PG) is embedded during training to immediately veto high-severity risks (e.g., child exploitation, terrorism). For non-critical queries, a User Policy (PU) is evaluated to determine label-specific actions (Comply, Guide, Reject), enabling domain-appropriate risk exposure.
- **Core assumption:** Critical risks require fixed, parameter-level enforcement to prevent catastrophic failures, while lower-stakes contexts benefit from adaptable, prompt-level control to improve utility.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "non-overridable global safety policy... user-defined policies... runtime-configurable"
  - [section 1]: "hierarchical policy separation between non-negotiable safety baselines... and flexible domain-specific policies"
- **Break condition:** If the early-exit mechanism fails (e.g., misclassification of a global risk), the system may erroneously evaluate via User Policy, potentially exposing critical risks.

### Mechanism 2: Explicit CoTPath with Label2Action Mapping
- **Claim:** Explicit CoTPath with Label2Action mapping enables fine-grained, transparent policy control, which is necessary for achieving both safety and permissive helpfulness under runtime configurations.
- **Mechanism:** The model's reasoning is structured into a deterministic Classify→Act pathway. The classification step identifies a risk label, which is then mapped via a policy-specified action (Comply/Guide/Reject). This separation ensures the declared action matches the generated response.
- **Core assumption:** Decomposing the decision into interpretable stages prevents policy ambiguity, allows per-label control, and exposes failure modes for targeted remediation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "decomposes safety decisions into structured Classify→Act paths... render the decision-making process transparent"
  - [section 3.2]: Details CoTPath construction and the Label2Action mapping requirement.
  - [section 4.4.2]: Ablation shows wo-CoTPath causes a 58% drop in controllability (PACT-Test/C).
- **Break condition:** Cascading errors: a misclassification in the first stage (label identification) propagates to an incorrect action.

### Mechanism 3: GUIDE Mode as Global Default
- **Claim:** Using GUIDE (not REJECT) as the default Global Policy action mode better balances safety and helpfulness by providing constructive redirection instead of blanket refusal.
- **Mechanism:** For global risks, the system generates Guide responses that firmly reject harmful intent but offer educational alternatives or harm-reduction resources, maintaining user engagement while upholding safety.
- **Core assumption:** Minimal refusals damage user experience without improving safety, whereas informative refusals can satisfy user informational needs within safe boundaries.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.2]: PACT (GUIDE) matches TR1S-8b/pos safety (0.959 vs. 0.967) but with better helpfulness than gpt-oss-120b (0.259 vs. 0.120).
  - [section 4.4.1]: Switching from REJECT-only (PACT-R) to GUIDE mode (PACT-R+G/G) improves CoSA by 43.6% (0.140→0.201) via a 53.3% helpfulness gain with only a 2.5% safety drop.
- **Break condition:** If GUIDE responses are poorly crafted, they might inadvertently provide actionable harmful information or be exploited.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Hierarchical Policy Precedence**
  - *Why needed here:* The system's core logic depends on a strict two-tier evaluation order (Global then User). Understanding this control flow is essential to predict behavior and debug failures.
  - *Quick check question:* What happens if a query is classified as both a Global risk (IR08) and a User Policy risk (UA10) with conflicting action mappings? (Answer: Global Policy's early-exit override precludes User Policy evaluation.)
- **Concept: Label-to-Action Mapping**
  - *Why needed here:* This is the bridge between risk classification and response generation. It makes policy control explicit and deterministic, moving beyond implicit model behavior.
  - *Quick check question:* If a User Policy maps label "UA2" to COMPLY, what response will the model generate for a query classified as UA2? (Answer: A comply response, per the trained response mode.)
- **Concept: Self-Distillation for Multi-Policy Data**
  - *Why needed here:* The training data must cover diverse policy combinations and their corresponding reasoning/response triads. This technique generates the structured data without external annotation.
  - *Quick check question:* Why are three response types (Comply, Guide, Reject) generated per risky query during distillation? (Answer: To enable flexible Label2Action mapping and ensure 100% consistency between declared action and response content.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** 
  Input Processor -> Global Risk Detector -> (if Global Risk: Action Router -> Response Generator) OR (if Global Safe: User Policy Engine -> Action Router -> Response Generator). The Policy Configurator sits outside, supplying the active User Policy to the User Policy Engine and Action Router.
- **Critical path:** Query → Global Detection → (if Global Risk: Action via PG → Response) OR (if Global Safe: User Detection → Action via PU → Response).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Latency vs. Transparency:* CoTPath increases token generation/processing time versus implicit reasoning. Production requires optimizations like early-exit caching.
  - *Coverage vs. Controllability:* A broader Global Policy reduces gray-zone ambiguity but restricts User Policy's domain of control.
  - *Simplicity vs. Robustness:* The sequential early-exit design is simple but vulnerable to misclassification in the first stage.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Label Misclassification:** A global risk (e.g., IR06) is labeled as a User Policy safe class (UA20), leading to incorrect User Policy evaluation.
  - **Policy Lookup Failure:** A detected User Policy label (e.g., UA4) is missing from the active policy's Label2Action map, causing fallback to Global Policy or default behavior.
  - **Action Execution Drift:** The generated response does not match the semantics of the selected action mode (e.g., a "Guide" response contains comply-like detailed instructions).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Global Policy Evaluation:** Run PACT with *only* Global Policy active on 5 safety benchmarks. Verify safety score ~0.959 and CoSA 0.201.
  2. **Runtime Controllability - Custom Policies:** Load a randomized User Policy mapping for a novel UA-label. Check that the model's response mode matches the configured mapping with >85% accuracy while maintaining global safety on embedded risks.
  3. **CoTPath Ablation:** Compare the full model (w-CoTPath) against an identical model trained without the explicit Classify→Act structure (wo-CoTPath). Measure the >50% drop in PACT-Test controllability metric.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How can the ambiguous taxonomic boundary between Global Policy (critical, non-overridable risks) and User Policy (domain-specific, configurable risks) be formally defined and operationalized to balance safety baseline security with adaptive flexibility?
- **Basis:** Limitations section states: "The division between Global Policy and User Policy inherently gives rise to ambiguous gray zones, and the approach to achieving their effective balance needs further investigation."
- **Question 2:** What targeted architectural or algorithmic optimizations can reduce the computational overhead of PACT's explicit CoTPath reasoning while preserving its interpretability and fine-grained controllability for high-throughput production deployment?
- **Basis:** Limitations section identifies: "Computational Overhead of CoTPath... production deployment requires targeted optimizations (e.g., CoT caching, early-exit for low-risk queries, speculative decoding)."
- **Question 3:** How can the robustness of PACT's structured CoTPath reasoning be systematically hardened against novel adversarial attacks that exploit its multi-stage design, such as label manipulation, policy confusion via multi-label conflicts, or reasoning chain injection?
- **Basis:** Limitations section identifies new attack surfaces: "label manipulation (adversarial tokens biasing classification), policy confusion (multi-label conflicts), and reasoning chain injection (hijacking action selection)."
- **Question 4:** What parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods can be used to expand the scope of the embedded Global Policy (PG) to cover new, emerging critical risk categories without compromising the stability of its non-overridable safety guarantees or requiring full retraining?
- **Basis:** Conclusion states future work aims to: "expand Global Policy coverage via parameter-efficient updates."
- **Question 5:** Can the sequential, early-exit architecture of PACT's CoTPath be formalized into a verifiable programmatic safety specification, enabling formal verification of its core "Global-first veto" guarantee against all possible input perturbations or policy configurations?
- **Basis:** The design emphasizes "sequential execution with early-exit" and "non-overridable" global policy as a "strong, non-configurable safety baseline," but this is an empirical claim, not a formally proven property.

## Limitations
*   Missing reproduction materials—verbatim prompt templates, full IR/UA taxonomies with Label2Action rules, and raw risky query datasets—preclude exact replication.
*   The framework’s reliance on a perfectly calibrated Global Policy creates a single‑point failure risk: misclassification at the early‑exit stage could cause safety breaches or loss of controllability.
*   GUIDE‑mode responses, while improving helpfulness, may leak harmful details if training data is insufficiently vetted; real‑world red‑team testing and distribution shifts remain underexplored.

## Confidence
*   **Hierarchical policy separation:** **Medium** – ablations indicate benefit, but dependence on an infallible global detector is a vulnerability not fully stress‑tested.
*   **Explicit CoTPath structure:** **High** – ablation shows a 58% controllability drop without it, strongly validating its necessity.
*   **GUIDE mode default:** **Medium** – helpfulness gains are clear, yet long‑term safety of generated GUIDE content requires further scrutiny.
