# Rethinking the Harmonic Loss via Non-Euclidean Distance Layers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10225  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10225  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Replacing Euclidean distance with non‑Euclidean metrics in harmonic loss improves accuracy, interpretability, and reduces carbon emissions across vision and language models.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores the effect of substituting the Euclidean distance in harmonic loss with a diverse set of non‑Euclidean metrics. Through systematic evaluation on vision (MLP, CNN, ResNet, PVT) and language (GPT, BERT, Qwen) models, the authors assess performance, interpretability, and sustainability. Cosine distance emerges as the best trade‑off for vision, boosting accuracy while cutting emissions. Bray‑Curtis and Mahalanobis enhance interpretability by concentrating PCA variance, albeit with different computational costs. For language models, cosine‑based harmonic losses stabilize gradients, strengthen representation structure, and lower emissions relative to cross‑entropy and Euclidean harmonic loss. The work demonstrates that the choice of distance materially influences model behavior, offering a flexible framework to balance these factors.

## Method Summary
The authors extend harmonic loss by replacing its Euclidean distance component with a spectrum of alternatives (cosine, Manhattan, Chebyshev, Minkowski, Hamming, Canberra, Bray‑Curtis, Mahalanobis). Only the final classification head—implemented as a `DistLayer`—varies; all backbones remain unchanged. Models are trained from scratch on multiple vision and language datasets. Evaluation spans three axes: performance (accuracy, F1, perplexity), interpretability (PCA explained variance, PCA@90%, effective rank), and sustainability (training time, energy, CO₂ emissions). Hyperparameters (harmonic exponent n=1, epsilon 1e‑4) are fixed to isolate the effect of geometry. Experiments reveal consistent patterns in how distance choice shapes optimization and representation.

## Key Results
- **Cosine distance** provides the best accuracy‑emissions trade‑off for vision, improving accuracy while lowering carbon output.  
- **Bray‑Curtis and Mahalanobis** enhance interpretability (higher PCA variance concentration, lower PCA@90%) at varying computational overheads.  
- For **language models**, cosine‑based harmonic losses improve gradient stability, strengthen representation structure, and reduce emissions versus cross‑entropy and Euclidean harmonic loss.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benefits stem from three interconnected mechanisms:

**Scale invariance and finite convergence.** For 1‑homogeneous distances (e.g., Euclidean, Manhattan), the harmonic link κ(r)=r⁻ω cancels scaling factors, preventing unbounded weight growth and guaranteeing a finite minimizer where training aims for zero distance to the correct prototype. This property is formally proven (Theorem A.2) and ensures stable optimization.

**Gradient stability via bounded distances.** Bounded distances like cosine constrain logits and gradients to a fixed range (e.g., [0,2]), preventing explosion—especially in late training when feature norms may grow. This yields smoother optimization, reduces overconfidence, and lowers energy consumption, as evidenced by improved gradient stability metrics and reduced emissions in language models.

**Representation geometry shaping.** The loss landscape defined by d(h,wₖ) biases the optimizer toward specific cluster structures. For instance, Bray‑Curtis emphasizes proportional differences, encouraging sparse manifolds; Mahalanobis creates ellipsoidal clusters aligned with principal axes. These geometric effects translate into higher PCA variance concentration, enhancing interpretability. Computational costs vary: Mahalanobis requires covariance computation, while Bray‑Curtis remains lightweight.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input → Backbone → h → DistLayer → logits → loss → backprop  
The `DistLayer` replaces the standard linear head: it computes pairwise distances d(h, wₖ) between the feature vector h and each class prototype wₖ, applies the harmonic link (or negative similarity), and normalizes with log_softmax.

**Critical path:** The `DistLayer` must implement each distance with vectorized GPU operations, ensuring numerical stability (epsilon, clamping) and, for Mahalanobis, optional covariance updates. Training loops and optimizers remain unchanged.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Accuracy vs. efficiency:* Cosine offers excellent balance; Mahalanobis may boost interpretability but adds O(d²) covariance overhead.
- *Interpretability vs. simplicity:* Bray‑Curtis and Chebyshev strongly concentrate variance but may require tuning; Euclidean is simple but less structuring.
- *Sustainability:* Lighter distances (cosine, Manhattan) reduce per‑step energy; heavier ones (Mahalanobis, high‑order Minkowski) can increase emissions unless they significantly speed up convergence.

**Failure signatures:**
- Numerical instability: Division‑by‑zero in normalized metrics (Canberra, Bray‑Curtis) if inputs become exactly zero; mitigated by epsilon.
- Covariance ill‑conditioning: Mahalanobis may diverge with singular/noisy covariance; requires regularization and careful initialization.
- Metric mismatch: Hamming without proper binary relaxation yields non‑differentiable gradients; Gumbel‑Softmax approximations can suffer high variance.
- Emission blow‑up: On transformer backbones, Mahalanobis and p>2 Minkowski can noticeably increase per‑step FLOPs; monitor per‑step timings.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Benchmark accuracy & gradient stability:** Train across backbone‑dataset pairs (e.g., ResNet‑50 on CIFAR‑10) comparing Euclidean, cosine, Bray‑Curtis, Mahalanobis, and cross‑entropy. Track validation accuracy and Gradient Stability metric.
2. **Interpretability probe:** Train on CIFAR‑100 with a CNN for Euclidean, cosine, Bray‑Curtis, Mahalanobis. Compute PCA on penultimate features; compare PC2 explained variance and PCA@90% values.
3. **Sustainability measurement:** Run short fixed‑budget training (10k steps) on GPT‑2‑small with each distance. Log per‑1k‑step CO₂ using CodeCarbon, compute delta vs. cross‑entropy, and correlate with convergence speed (steps to reach 90% final accuracy).

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility details missing:** Exact random seeds, full software stack (PyTorch version, CodeCarbon config), and GPU power models are unspecified, hindering precise replication, especially for emissions.
- **Mahalanobis implementation ambiguity:** Variants (Cholesky, Diagonal, Standard) are mentioned but covariance update rules (online EMA vs. batch), regularization strength, and handling of ill‑conditioning are not detailed.
- **Theoretical boundaries:** Finite convergence guarantees apply only to 1‑homogeneous distances; the success of non‑homogeneous metrics like cosine lacks full theoretical backing, and the limits of this advantage are unclear.
- **Environmental measurement scope:** Emissions are tracked only during training; inference and data preprocessing are excluded, potentially understating total carbon footprint.
