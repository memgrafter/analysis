# Differences in Typological Alignment in Language Models' Treatment of Differential Argument Marking

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17653
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17653
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Language models exhibit a robust bias toward *natural markedness* (85% accuracy) but no significant *object preference* in differential argument marking (DAM) systems, suggesting discourse-level pressures shape typology beyond local predictability.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether language models (LMs) acquire human-like typological biases in differential argument marking (DAM)—a system where morphological marking depends on argument semantics. Using a synthetic corpus paradigm, the authors trained GPT-2-small models on 18 corpora implementing distinct DAM rules. Models reliably mastered natural markedness systems (where marking targets semantically atypical arguments) with 85% average accuracy, significantly outperforming inverse systems (68%). However, they showed no robust preference for object-targeting over subject-targeting rules (79% vs. 74% accuracy). The findings indicate a dissociation: natural markedness emerges from distributional learnability under next-token prediction, while argument preference likely depends on discourse-level pressures absent in standard LM training.

## Method Summary
The study employed a synthetic corpus paradigm built from English OpenSubtitles. Sentences were parsed to extract SVO frames, and semantic properties (animacy, definiteness, pronominality) of arguments were automatically annotated using fine-tuned BERT classifiers. For each of 18 DAM rule configurations—varying by semantic trigger, dependency complexity (local/global), markedness direction (natural/inverse), and argument target (subject/object)—a special marker token was injected at the right edge of the relevant NP when the licensing condition held. Separate GPT-2-small models were trained from scratch on each corpus (15k steps, AdamW, lr=3e-4). Rule mastery was evaluated via minimal pairs: for 1,000 held-out sentences per rule, a grammatical version (with marker as per rule) was contrasted with an ungrammatical version (marker removed or added incorrectly), and the model's length-normalized mean negative log-likelihood was compared.

## Key Results
- Models show a robust natural markedness advantage (85% avg. accuracy) over inverse systems (68%).
- No significant object preference: subject-targeting (74%) and object-targeting (79%) rules yield comparable mastery.
- Marker placement is learned near-ceiling (>99% accuracy) across all conditions, dissociated from rule mastery.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Natural Markness Preference via Surprisal Reduction
Language models prefer natural markedness systems because the next-token prediction objective optimizes for reducing local surprisal. In natural systems, overt marking targets semantically atypical arguments (e.g., definite objects), which generate higher baseline surprisal. Inserting a predictable marker token at a fixed position (e.g., NP edge) immediately reduces prediction error for upcoming tokens, creating a local learning advantage. The autoregressive objective thus preferentially reinforces rules that align marker insertion with surprisal reduction. Evidence: consistent accuracy advantage for natural rules across all triggers and dependency types; negative correlation between perturbed ratio (frequency of marked configs) and accuracy. Break condition: if corpus distribution eliminated the surprisal advantage (e.g., balanced marked/unmarked frequencies), the natural preference should diminish.

### Mechanism 2: Absence of Object Preference due to Missing Discourse Pressure
Human languages show an object preference in DAM because differential marking often signals discourse deviation (e.g., an object becoming topical). This requires tracking discourse-level prominence across sentences—a pressure not captured by standard next-token training over isolated sentences with limited context. Autoregressive LMs lack explicit mechanisms to maintain and update discourse-level representations; their objective does not optimize for coherence or information structure. Consequently, the functional pressure that favors object marking in humans does not manifest as a learning bias. Evidence: no significant mastery difference between subject-targeting and object-targeting rules. Break condition: training with an explicit discourse modeling objective (e.g., anaphora prediction) or much longer context might induce an object-targeting advantage.

### Mechanism 3: Reliable Marker Placement via Local Surface Patterns
Models reliably learn the syntactic placement of DAM markers (e.g., at NP right edge) because this is a deterministic, local surface-syntactic pattern. The marker token consistently appears in a fixed linear position relative to the NP, providing strong local distributional cues. The model's locality bias favors acquiring such positional regularities even when the semantic licensing condition is not fully mastered. Evidence: placement accuracy >0.99 uniformly, contrasting with variable rule mastery. Break condition: if placement were non-local (e.g., clitics on verbs) or conditioned on complex syntax beyond the immediate NP, accuracy would drop.

## Foundational Learning
- **Differential Argument Marking (DAM):** A morphological system where case/flagging marking depends on semantic properties of the argument (e.g., animacy, definiteness). *Why needed:* It is the target phenomenon; its typological dimensions (markedness direction, target, triggers) define the experimental conditions. *Quick check:* What distinguishes "natural" from "inverse" markedness direction?
- **Synthetic Corpus Paradigm:** Creating controlled, counterfactual languages by injecting grammatical rules into a natural corpus. *Why needed:* Isolates specific grammatical parameters to test LM biases without natural language confounds. *Quick check:* Why not just analyze LMs on existing natural languages?
- **Rule Mastery via Minimal Pairs:** Evaluating whether a model distinguishes grammatical from ungrammatical forms that differ only in the application of the target rule. *Why needed:* Directly tests acquisition of the DAM licensing condition, not just distributional fit. *Quick check:* How does this evaluation differ from measuring perplexity?
- **Autoregressive Language Modeling:** Next-token prediction objective (causal LM). *Why needed:* The training objective is posited as the source of the natural markedness bias. *Quick check:* How does next-token prediction differ from masked language modeling in terms of inductive biases?
- **Semantic Annotation (Automated):** Using BERT classifiers to label NP properties (animacy, definiteness, pronominality). *Why needed:* DAM rules depend on these semantic features; accurate annotation is critical for correct corpus perturbation. *Quick check:* What are risks of using automated rather than human semantic annotation?
- **Markedness Direction:** In DAM, whether the marker appears on the more *expected* (inverse) or *unexpected* (natural) argument semantically. *Why needed:* The core contrast testing the main hypothesis. *Quick check:* Give an example of natural vs. inverse marking for definite objects.

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map: Tokenizer (with special `A`, `P` tokens) -> Embedding Layer (token + positional) -> Transformer Stack (12-layer, 12-head decoder-only) -> Output Head (linear over vocab) -> Training Loop (AdamW, cosine LR) -> Evaluation Module (sentence NLL comparison)
- Critical path: Base Corpus (OpenSubtitles) -> Parse sentences -> Extract SVO frames with semantic annotations -> Inject DAM rule (determine marker insertion) -> Tokenize -> Train GPT-2 from scratch -> For each checkpoint: generate minimal pairs -> compute sentence NLL -> determine rule mastery accuracy.
- Design tradeoffs: Small model (GPT-2-small) limits capacity but enables extensive hyperparameter matching across 18 conditions; English OpenSubtitles provides natural SVO distribution but lacks rich case-marking environments; binary semantic triggers simplify space but miss gradient systems; minimal-pair evaluation tests rule mastery but not gradient acceptance.
- Failure signatures: Low rule mastery (<60%) on inverse DAM conditions; no significant accuracy difference between subject/object targeting; high marker placement accuracy (>0.98) even when rule mastery is low; BLiMP scores within normal range for all rules.
- First 3 experiments: 1) Baseline sanity check: train on unperturbed corpus, verify perplexity and BLiMP scores match known GPT-2-small performance. 2) Natural vs. Inverse contrast: train on `L-P-Def` (natural) and `L-P-Def-inv` (inverse); plot learning curves. 3) Marker placement sanity check: for a low-mastery rule (e.g., `G-Def-inv`), run the placement test; expect near-ceiling accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Do the DAM learning patterns (natural markedness advantage, lack of object preference) generalize to larger language models and multiple random seeds?
- **Open Question 2:** How would DAM biases differ in languages with freer word order (e.g., SOV) where case marking is more essential?
- **Open Question 3:** What is the relative contribution of input frequency versus structural learnability to DAM rule mastery?
- **Open Question 4:** Can incorporating discourse-level pressures (e.g., interactive training) induce object preference in DAM?

## Limitations
- Synthetic English corpora and small model may not generalize to natural languages or larger architectures.
- Causal claims about mechanisms (e.g., surprisal reduction) are inferred from correlations, not direct manipulation.
- Missing implementation details (BERT prompts, global minimal-pair logic, parser settings) hinder exact replication.

## Confidence
- **Natural markedness preference (85% vs 68%):** High. Consistent across all 18 conditions.
- **No object preference (74% vs 79%):** Medium. Non-significant; may be underpowered.
- **Marker placement accuracy (>0.99):** High. Near-ceiling uniform.
- **Surprisal-driven naturalness (Mechanism 1):** Medium. Supporting correlation exists but causal proof lacking.
- **Discourse-absence for object bias (Mechanism 2):** Low-Medium. Plausible but untested.
- **Local placement learning (Mechanism 3):** High. Break-condition holds independently.

## Next Checks
1. **Frequency balancing:** Construct corpora where surprisal reduction for natural/inverse rules is equalized (e.g., balanced marked argument frequencies). If natural advantage vanishes, Mechanism 1 gains causal support.
2. **Discourse enhancement:** Train models with longer context windows (2048 tokens) or an additional anaphora prediction loss. If object-targeting mastery then improves relative to subject-targeting, Mechanism 2 gains evidence.
3. **Annotation audit:** Perform human labeling on a substantial sample (e.g., 500 NPs per semantic property) to estimate annotation error rates and propagate uncertainty to rule mastery scores.
