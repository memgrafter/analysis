# Polyglots or Multitudes? Multilingual LLM Answers to Value-laden Multiple-Choice Questions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05932  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05932  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Larger, instruction‑tuned multilingual LLMs exhibit higher cross‑lingual consistency (up to 59.27% PPA), but consistency varies dramatically by question and topic, with some issues (e.g., gender equality) showing near‑universal agreement while others (e.g., work ethic) reveal robust language‑specific splits.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether multilingual large language models (LLMs) render stable, “polyglot” judgments across languages on value‑laden multiple‑choice questions or whether their answers reflect “multitudes” of language‑specific value orientations. To test this, the authors construct the Multilingual European Value Survey (MEVS), a corpus of human‑translated survey questions from the European Values Study spanning eight languages. They evaluate over 30 open‑weight multilingual LLMs under an exhaustive grid of prompt variations (answer order, symbol type, tail character) using multiple‑choice prompting (MCP). Metrics include Proportion of Plurality Agreement (PPA) and Shannon entropy, along with mutual information analysis to isolate the influence of language. Results show that larger, instruction‑tuned models achieve the highest consistency, yet even these models exhibit question‑dependent divergences. For the most consistent models, language emerges as the strongest predictor of response variation, suggesting that alignment fine‑-tuning may unevenly shape value representations across topics. The study thus reveals a nuanced picture: LLMs can approximate polyglot value stability on certain issues, but remain “multitudes” on others.

## Method Summary
The authors use the Multilingual European Value Survey (MEVS), which comprises 142 survey questions from the European Values Study, professionally translated into eight languages (Czech, English, French, German, Norwegian, Portuguese, Russian, Spanish). They focus on 24 items with five‑point Likert scales. Over 30 multilingual LLMs (ranging from 0.5B to 72B parameters, including base and instruction‑tuned variants) are evaluated. For each question, a prompt generator creates every combination of: 120 answer orders, 3 tail characters (none, space, newline), 2 symbol types (letters A–E or numbers 1–5), and 8 languages, yielding 5,760 variants. The model is queried via multiple‑choice prompting: the question stem, statement, and options are concatenated, and the first‑token probability after “Answer:” is used to select an option. This yields a distribution of answers across variants. Consistency is measured by PPA (the share of the most frequent answer) and Shannon entropy (both on the 5‑point scale and aggregated to 3‑point Agree/Neutral/Disagree). Models with >40% PPA on a development set (10 questions) are run on the full 24 questions. Mutual information between responses and each prompt variable (language, order, symbol, tail) is computed to determine which factor most influences the model’s output.

## Key Results
- Larger, instruction‑tuned models (e.g., Llama‑3.1‑70B‑Instruct, Qwen2.5‑72B‑Instruct) achieve the highest consistency, with PPA up to 59.27% and entropy as low as 0.84 on the 3‑point scale.  
- Consistency is highly question‑dependent: some items (e.g., gender discrimination, environmental taxation) generate near‑universal agreement across languages, while others (e.g., work duty, family obligations) produce robust language‑specific plurality splits.  
- For the most consistent models, mutual information analysis reveals that *language* is the strongest predictor of responses, indicating selective language‑induced value divergence despite overall stability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Larger, instruction‑tuned models show higher consistency across prompt variations because increased model capacity and alignment training (e.g., RLHF, SFT) enable more robust abstraction of semantic content from superficial linguistic surface forms. The underlying assumption is that consistency reflects a deeper, more abstract value representation. Evidence: the top‑ranking models by PPA are all instruction‑tuned and exceed 7B parameters. If smaller models were extensively fine‑tuned on similar value‑laden MCQs, they might match this consistency; conversely, questions requiring highly culture‑specific knowledge could still produce splits even in large models.

### Mechanism 2
Cross‑lingual consistency varies by topic because alignment objectives (e.g., harmlessness) are applied unevenly across value domains. Topics heavily curated in safety datasets (e.g., gender equality, environmental protection) become “hard‑coded” and thus consistent across languages, while less emphasized topics (e.g., work ethic, family duty) remain sensitive to language‑specific training data distributions. Evidence: some questions show near‑total agreement within and across models, whereas others reveal language‑specific plurality patterns. If alignment datasets were balanced across all domains and languages, or if models were trained on truly universal value data, the dichotomy might diminish.

### Mechanism 3
Robustness to superficial prompt variations (order, symbol, tail character) indicates that a model encodes the value statement at an abstract level rather than relying on token‑level heuristics. Brittleness across variants suggests context‑dependent shortcuts. Evidence: the paper reports that robustness varies dramatically by question, and entropy heatmaps show that even the most consistent models have high entropy on some items. The exhaustive prompt enumeration (5,760 variants per question) provides a fine‑grained measure. If variations included semantic paraphrases, robustness might drop for all questions, revealing the surface‑level nature of current tests; also, open‑ended generation instead of MCP could yield different patterns.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
MEVS Dataset → Prompt Generator → Inference Engine (with multilingual LLMs) → Metrics Module  

**Critical path:**  
1. Select 5‑point Likert MCQs from MEVS (N=24); define dev set (10 questions) and test set (14 questions).  
2. For each model, run inference on the dev‑set prompts (full or sampled variants).  
3. Compute consistency metrics (PPA, entropy) to identify the most consistent models (top 7 with dev PPA >40%).  
4. For these models, execute inference on all 24 questions with the full 5,760 variants per question.  
5. Compute mutual information I(R;X|q,m) for each prompt variable X (language, order, symbol, tail).  
6. Analyze and visualize results (entropy heatmaps, MI bar plots, plurality answer counts).  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- MCP vs. Cloze Prompting: MCP is efficient but order‑sensitive; CP is more robust but requires multiple forward passes. The study chooses MCP for scalability.  
- Prompt variation coverage: Exhaustive enumeration (120 orders) ensures thoroughness but is computationally heavy; sampling could be used when resources are limited.  
- Answer extraction: Using first‑token probabilities is deterministic and fast but may not match open‑ended generation; the study acknowledges this limitation.  
- Corpus size: Only 24 questions are evaluated due to compute constraints; a larger set would improve generalizability but increase cost.  

**Failure signatures:**  
- Model PPA near random baseline (20%) indicates inability to handle value‑laden MCQs.  
- High entropy across many questions suggests answer instability; such models should be excluded from further analysis.  
- If language MI is not the highest among prompt variables for a supposedly consistent model, it may indicate a bug in MI calculation or misalignment of language labels.  
- Inconsistency in reproducibility: re‑running a small subset yields different PPA; could stem from random seeds or tokenization issues.  

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate the consistency ranking: Run the 10 development questions on all models, compute average PPA and 3‑point entropy, verify that instruction‑tuned models >7B rank highest (as in Table 1).  
2. Validate language MI: For the top 7 models, compute I(R;L) for each question; check that average I(R;L) exceeds MI for order, symbol, and tail (as in Table 2).  
3. Reproduce question‑level dichotomy: Choose a consensual question (e.g., Q41) and a language‑split question (e.g., Q17); generate language plurality answer count plots (like Figure 4) to confirm the pattern.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1  
- **Question:** Do multilingual LLMs exhibit consistent responses across languages for value‑laden MCQs, or do they show language‑specific variations (i.e., polyglot vs. multitude behavior)?  
- **Basis in paper:** The abstract and introduction frame this as the core research question: “Are multilingual LLMs consistent in their responses across languages… or do they answer… depending on the language of the question?”  
- **Why unresolved:** Results show mixed patterns—some questions yield high cross‑lingual consistency while others show robust language‑specific splits, even for the same model. No unified behavior emerges across all questions or models.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic testing across the full MEVS corpus (142 questions) with extensive prompt variations, measuring consistency metrics per question and per language cluster to characterize which topics or linguistic features trigger polyglot vs. multitude responses.

### Open Question 2  
- **Question:** How does preference fine‑tuning (e.g., RLHF, constitutional AI) differentially influence LLM responses to specific value‑laden topics (e.g., work ethic vs. gender equality)?  
- **Basis in paper:** The abstract notes that “preference fine‑tuning may differentially affect models’ handling of specific value‑laden topics,” and the discussion speculates that alignment objectives like harmlessness may unevenly shape responses across value domains.  
- **Why unresolved:** The study compares fine‑tuned vs. base models but does not isolate fine‑tuning data or objectives per topic; observed topic‑dependent patterns are correlational, not causal.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled ablation studies where models are fine‑tuned on topic‑specific preference datasets (e.g., gender equality statements vs. work ethic statements) in multiple languages, then evaluated on MEVS to measure changes in cross‑lingual consistency and language mutual information per topic.

### Open Question 3  
- **Question:** To what extent do different answer extraction strategies (MCP, CP, open‑ended generation) affect the measurement of cross‑lingual consistency in value‑laden MCQ responses?  
- **Basis in paper:** The limitations explicitly state: “We only extract answers using MCP… A systematic comparison of the impact of the answer extraction strategy could also be undertaken.”  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper relies solely on MCP log‑probabilities; other methods (e.g., cloze prompting, free‑text generation with answer parsing) may mitigate known MCP brittleness and yield different consistency or language‑sensitivity patterns.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Administer the same MEVS questions to the most consistent models using MCP, CP, and open‑ended generation (with robust answer extraction from text), then compare consistency metrics (PPA, entropy) and language mutual information across methods to identify stable vs. method‑dependent cross‑lingual effects.

## Limitations
- MCP order‑sensitivity may bias consistency metrics.
- Only 24 European‑value questions limit generalizability to global or broader value domains.
- Prompt template details are missing; replication relies on assumptions about formatting and tokenization.

- Consistency is question‑dependent (some language splits): **Medium**  
- Robustness to prompt variations indicates stable values: **Medium**
