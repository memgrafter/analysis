# SAFARI: A Community-Engaged Approach and Dataset of Stereotype Resources in the Sub-Saharan African Context

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22404  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22404  
- **Reference count:** 28  
- **Primary result:** A dataset of 3,534 English and 3,206 multilingual stereotypes across 15 languages from Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa; evaluation of six frontier models reveals significant stereotype endorsement, often higher in English than local languages.

## Executive Summary
The SAFARI paper addresses the severe underrepresentation of sub‑Saharan African perspectives in stereotype resources used to assess generative AI safety, a gap that hinders equitable model development. To fill this gap, the authors employ a community‑engaged methodology involving telephonic surveys moderated in native languages, leveraging local partnerships to capture culturally grounded stereotypes across diverse ethnic and linguistic groups. The resulting dataset enables evaluation of frontier models (Gemini, GPT‑4/5, Claude) in both English and regional languages, uncovering significant stereotype endorsement—frequently higher in English—that prior resources would have missed.

## Method Summary
The research design centers on collaboration with local community‑based organizations (Mantaray, RAIN Africa) to recruit 410 participants via quota sampling, ensuring representation across 56 ethnic groups. Data collection uses telephonic interviews moderated in participants’ native languages (15 languages total), allowing oral expression aligned with regional orality traditions. Because many moderators lacked written proficiency in local scripts, a two‑step localization process was used: stereotypes were first recorded in English summaries (except Swahili, recorded directly), then professionally translated back into the native language. The final dataset is annotated with identity terms, attributes, stereotype axes, offensiveness (1–5), and prevalence (1–4), and released in parallel English and multilingual tabs.

## Key Results
- Dataset scale: 3,534 English and 3,206 multilingual stereotype entries covering 15 languages across Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa.
- Frontier models (Gemini, GPT‑4/5, Claude) exhibit significant stereotype endorsement in both English and local languages when tested via NLI‑based prompts.
- Endorsement rates are often higher in English than in local languages, revealing bias patterns that targeted regional evaluations would not detect.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Telephonic surveys moderated in native languages capture orally‑expressed cultural knowledge more authentically than written surveys, addressing the region’s linguistic diversity and oral traditions.  
**Mechanism:** Fluent interviewers conduct real‑time spoken interviews, letting participants express stereotypes in their natural oral style. This bypasses barriers like limited keyboard support for local scripts and low written proficiency.  
**Core assumption:** Oral expression in native languages preserves semantic and emotional nuance (e.g., offensiveness) more faithfully than written English transcription or direct translation.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract emphasizes “socioculturally‑situated, community‑engaged methods… sensitive to the region’s complex linguistic diversity and traditional orality”; Section 2.1 explains the dual linguistic strategy; corpus evidence for oral nuance preservation is weak (acknowledged in Limitations).

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Partnerships with local, community‑based organizations ensure culturally grounded participant recruitment, question localization, and data quality control.  
**Mechanism:** Local partners drive sampling strategy (quota‑based on ethnic groups), provide moderators fluent in relevant languages and cultural contexts, and manage logistics. This embeds local expertise, moving beyond Western‑centric protocols.  
**Core assumption:** Local organizations possess the social trust, cultural competence, and on‑ground networks necessary to access diverse demographics and interpret responses appropriately.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract references “community‑engaged approach”; Section 2.2 details how partnerships were vital for local perspectives on participant selection, localization, and quality; demographic breakdowns (Appendix D) show representation across 56 ethnic groups enabled by partner networks.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** A two‑step localization process (oral native‑language interview → English recording → professional translation back to native language) balances broad data standardization with multilingual coverage, despite potential nuance loss.  
**Mechanism:** Moderators, often lacking written proficiency in native languages, record responses in English. Professional linguists then translate these standardized English entries back into the participant’s native language, creating a parallel bilingual dataset.  
**Core assumption:** Professional linguists can sufficiently recover the original spoken intent and culturally‑specific meaning from the English summary, making the trade‑off acceptable for wide language coverage.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 2.4 describes the adoption of this approach due to moderator writing constraints and mitigation of nuance loss via native‑language moderation and professional linguists; corpus evidence for translation fidelity is missing (acknowledged in Limitations).

## Foundational Learning
- **Sociocultural Specificity of Stereotypes**  
  *Why needed:* Stereotypes are deeply tied to local histories, ethnic relations, and social structures. A stereotype from Nigeria about “Hausa” people differs in roots and impacts from a US racial stereotype. Assuming universality leads to flawed safety evaluations.

- **Orality vs. Literacy in Low‑Resource Language NLP**  
  *Why needed:* Many sub‑Saharan African languages have vibrant oral traditions but limited written resources and digital keyboard support. Standard NLP data collection (written forms, web‑crawls) systematically excludes these language communities.  
  *Quick check:* Why might a written survey in Twi or Zulu miss stereotypes that a spoken interview would capture, beyond the technical issue of typing?

## Architecture Onboarding
Local Partner Network -> Participant Recruitment (Quota Sampling) -> Telephonic Interview (Native Language Moderation) -> English Transcription/Summary -> Professional Linguistic Translation -> Bilingual Dataset Annotation (Identity Term, Attribute, Axis, Offensiveness, Prevalence) -> Quality & Diversity Reporting -> Public Dataset Release.

**Critical path:** The sequence from Local Partner Network to Bilingual Dataset Annotation is critical. Failures in partner collaboration, moderator fluency, or translation quality propagate downstream, corrupting the entire dataset.

**Design tradeoffs:**
1. Reach vs. Nuance: Telephonic surveys maximize reach across diverse geographies but rely on moderators’ summary skills and subsequent translation, risking nuance loss vs. in‑person, direct transcription.
2. Standardization vs. Authenticity: Recording in English first creates a standardized, analyzable corpus but may filter out culturally‑unique expressions without direct English equivalents.
3. Breadth vs. Depth: Quota sampling ensures broad ethnic/linguistic coverage (15 languages, 56 ethnic groups) but limits sample size per group, preventing deep statistical analysis for any single stereotype.

**Failure signatures:**
- Demographic skew: Participant distribution significantly diverges from national ethnic profiles, indicating recruitment failure.
- Translation anomalies: English summaries or back‑translations contain anachronistic, overly generic, or Western‑framed language.
- Moderator drift: Consistency checks show high variance in how different moderators elicited or phrased stereotypes for the same identity group.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Re‑run the NLI‑based stereotype endorsement test on the latest available model version for at least two languages (e.g., Swahili, Hausa) to verify if bias patterns persist.
2. For a subset of 50 multilingual entries, compare the participant’s original spoken description (or moderator’s initial English summary) against the final native‑language text; code for semantic and emotional (offensiveness) distortion.
3. Systematically compare SAFARI’s identity terms against academic/social science literature on stereotypes for each country; quantify novel vs. overlapping terms to argue for the dataset’s unique contribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why do frontier models exhibit higher stereotype endorsement in English compared to local languages?**  
   Basis: Explicitly shown in evaluation results (Table 3) and hypothesized due to poor model support for regional languages. Unresolved because the hypothesis is not tested; evidence needed: controlled experiments isolating language support effects or analysis of training data composition.

2. **How can future stereotype data collection minimize nuance loss during cross‑lingual translation?**  
   Basis: Limitations identify the multi‑step translation as risking nuance loss; unresolved because no method to measure or mitigate this is implemented. Evidence needed: direct oral recording with orthographic support or quantified nuance loss via back‑translation studies with native speaker ratings.

3. **How can community‑engaged methodologies reach populations with limited phone connectivity?**  
   Basis: Limitations note telephonic interviews may exclude remote areas; unresolved because no solution is evaluated. Evidence needed: comparative studies of phone vs. in‑person surveys in remote regions or hybrid models with offline tools.

4. **What scalable strategies are needed to cover all sub‑Saharan African languages and ethnicities?**  
   Basis: Authors state the resource is not exhaustive and future work must expand; unresolved because no scaling plan is outlined. Evidence needed: a roadmap for expanding the methodology across all African nations, including partnership models and funding strategies.

5. **How do SAFARI stereotypes align with traditional regional stereotype literature?**  
   Basis: Discussion recommends cross‑referencing with established literature; unresolved because no comparative analysis is presented. Evidence needed: systematic review of regional academic/ethnographic literature to compare identity axes, stereotype content, and offensiveness perceptions.

## Limitations
- Oral nuance loss: The two‑step localization (oral → English → native) may dilute semantic and emotional nuance, especially offensiveness; no fidelity metrics are provided.
- Sampling bias: Telephonic surveys risk excluding remote populations without phone connectivity, potentially skewing ethnic representation.
- Replication gaps: Exact survey scripts, rating scales, and compensation details are undisclosed, hindering precise reproduction.

## Confidence
- Community‑engaged telephonic approach captures authentic stereotypes: **Medium** (methodologically sound but untested for oral nuance preservation).
- Local partnerships enabled diverse recruitment: **High** (demographic breakdown across 56 ethnic groups supports this).
- Frontier models exhibit high stereotype endorsement (often higher in English): **Medium** (findings depend on dataset quality; require independent validation).
- Two‑step localization balances standardization and coverage: **Low** (known nuance loss; no evidence of translation fidelity).
