# Using the SEKF to Transfer NN Models of Dynamical Systems with Limited Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.02439
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02439
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Transfer learning with the Subset Extended Kalman Filter (SEKF) enables neural network models of dynamical systems to adapt to new target systems using minimal data, achieving target accuracy comparable to source models with as little as 1% of original training data.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the Subset Extended Kalman Filter (SEKF) for transfer learning in neural network models of dynamical systems. The method treats the parameters of a pre-trained source model as a Bayesian prior and updates them sequentially using target system data. Experiments on damped spring and temperature control systems show that with as little as 1% of the original training data, finetuning via SEKF achieves target accuracy comparable to the source model. The adapted parameters maintain high cosine similarity (>99%) with source weights, indicating small distributed perturbations across all layers. Compared to retraining from scratch, finetuning reduces overfitting, and while the choice of optimizer affects convergence speed, final generalization is similar.

## Method Summary
The approach begins by training a neural network (e.g., MLP or Neural ODE) on abundant source data for a dynamical system. For transfer to a target system with limited data, the source model parameters initialize a Bayesian posterior as a Gaussian prior $N(\pi_S, P_0)$. The SEKF then performs sequential Bayesian updates: at each step, a subset of parameters (size $m \ll n_\pi$) is selected for update. The SEKF predicts the parameter state and covariance, then corrects using the observation (target data) and the Jacobian of the network with respect to the selected parameters. Hyperparameters (process noise $Q$, measurement noise $R$, initial covariance $P_0$, subset size $m$) are tuned via validation. The method is compared against standard gradient-based finetuning (Adam, L-BFGS) and retraining from scratch.

## Key Results
- Finetuning with SEKF achieves target accuracy comparable to source models using as little as 1% of original training data.
- Adapted parameters maintain >99% cosine similarity with source weights, indicating small distributed perturbations across all layers.
- Finetuning reduces overfitting (smaller train-test gaps) versus retraining from scratch; optimizer choice affects speed but not final generalization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Bayesian prior regularization
- **Claim**: Treating source parameters as a Bayesian prior via SEKF enables effective transfer with minimal data by biasing adaptation toward well-generalized regions.
- **Mechanism**: SEKF initializes the target parameter posterior as Gaussian $N(\pi_S, P_0)$. Sequential updates balance prior certainty (via $P_k$) against new evidence (Kalman gain), implicitly regularizing to prevent overfitting.
- **Evidence**: 
  - [abstract] "treats source model parameters as a Bayesian prior and updates them via sequential Bayesian inference"
  - [section 3.3] Discusses process noise $Q$ and measurement noise $R$ controlling prior flexibility and observation reliability.
- **Break condition**: If source and target are functionally dissimilar, the prior misguides adaptation.

### Mechanism 2: Small distributed perturbations
- **Claim**: Effective transfer requires only small, distributed parameter perturbations across all layers, not selective layer freezing.
- **Mechanism**: SEKF (and other optimizers) achieve high cosine similarity (>0.99) between source and adapted parameters. Updates are distributed holistically but remain in a small neighborhood, suggesting dynamical system models encode dynamics in a distributed manner.
- **Evidence**:
  - [section 4.1] "finetuned models maintain high cosine similarity with source parameters, typically exceeding 0.99" and "weight changes distribute across all layers"
  - [figure 4 & 5] Empirical distributions.
- **Break condition**: If target dynamics require qualitatively new features, small perturbations may be insufficient.

### Mechanism 3: SEKF for overfitting mitigation and online adaptation
- **Claim**: SEKF reduces overfitting and enables online adaptation by modeling parameter and observation noise explicitly.
- **Mechanism**: SEKF updates only a subset of parameters per step (computational efficiency). Process noise $Q$ encodes permissible deviation; measurement noise $R$ weights observation trust. This probabilistic machinery provides implicit regularization and supports sequential updates without batch retraining.
- **Evidence**:
  - [abstract] "Finetuning reduces overfitting (smaller train-test gaps) versus retraining"
  - [section 3.3] "The process noise $Q$ controls regularization strength..."
  - [section 4.2.4] "optimizer choice does not significantly affect the train-test gap… SEKF… does not provide measurable generalization advantages over simpler gradient-based methods in the experimental settings studied." (SEKF's advantage is computational continuity for online learning, not final generalization.)
- **Break condition**: If target data is noiseless or abundant, explicit noise modeling provides negligible benefit; subset selection may omit crucial parameters if heuristic poor.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bayesian Inference for Parameter Estimation**
  - *Why needed*: SEKF frames transfer as sequential posterior estimation $p(\pi_T | D_T, \pi_S)$. Understanding priors, likelihoods, and recursive Bayes' rule is essential.
  - *Quick check*: "How does a Gaussian prior $N(\pi_S, P_0)$ influence the posterior when $P_0$ is small (high confidence) vs. large (low confidence)?"
- **Extended Kalman Filtering for Nonlinear Systems**
  - *Why needed*: SEKF approximates EKF for high-dimensional neural network parameters. Recognizing linearization via Jacobians $H_k = \partial\phi/\partial\pi$ and covariance propagation $P_k$ is key.
  - *Quick check*: "Why is the EKF 'Extended'? What role does the Jacobian play in updating neural network weights?"
- **Transfer Learning Paradigms in Dynamical Systems vs. Static Domains**
  - *Why needed*: Computer vision heuristics (freeze early layers) do not transfer. Dynamical systems require holistic, small-magnitude adjustments.
  - *Quick check*: "Why might early layers of a dynamical system model need adaptation even if they capture 'generic' dynamics?"
- **Neural Network System Identification**
  - *Why needed*: The paper assumes neural surrogates $\phi$ approximate $dx/dt = f(x,u,p)$. Grasping how discrete-time models $x_{k+1} = \phi(x_k, u_k, \pi)$ map to continuous dynamics is necessary.
  - *Quick check*: "What are the implications of using a multi-step prediction horizon $n_{out}$ during training for system stability?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  Source-trained NN $\phi(x,u; \pi_S)$ -> SEKF Engine (with subset selector $I_k$ and hyperparameters $Q,R,P_0,m$) -> Target-adapted NN $\phi(x,u; \pi_T)$

- **Critical path**:
  1. Train source model on large $D_S$ to convergence.
  2. Initialize target: set $\pi_0 = \pi_S$, $P_0 = \sigma_0^2 I$. Choose $Q$, $R$, $m$ via validation.
  3. Stream target data: for each mini-batch from $D_T^{train}$, run SEKF prediction-correction on selected subset $I_k$.
  4. Validate on held-out $D_T^{val}$ to detect overfitting (train-test gap).
  5. Terminate when validation loss plateaus or max epochs reached.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Subset size $m$**: Smaller $m$ → faster per-step, but may miss influential parameters. Larger $m$ → more accurate but $O(m^3)$ inversion cost.
  - **Process noise $Q$**: Small $Q$ → strong prior (less adaptation, lower overfitting risk). Large $Q$ → flexible adaptation (may overfit if $D_T$ tiny).
  - **Measurement noise $R$**: Larger $R$ → downweights observations, stronger regularization. Must reflect sensor/process noise level.
  - **Optimizer choice**: Adam/L-BFGS converge faster but lack online capability; SEKF enables sequential updates but slower.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - **High train-test gap** (> source gap): Overfitting. Likely $Q$ too large or $R$ too small; reduce adaptation flexibility.
  - **Low cosine similarity** ($\cos(\pi_S, \pi_T) < 0.95$): Under-constrained adaptation or dissimilar systems. Check functional similarity assumption; consider larger $Q$ or more data.
  - **Slow SEKF convergence**: Subset $m$ too large, or ill-conditioned $P_k$. Try smaller $m$ or regularize $P_k$ with diagonal loading.
  - **Adam/L-BFGS divergence**: Learning rate too high for small $D_T$; reduce LR or increase early stopping patience.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline comparison**: Finetune with Adam (subset = all params) vs. retrain from scratch on smallest $D_T$ (e.g., 10 samples). Expect finetune to achieve lower test loss and smaller train-test gap.
  2. **Subset sensitivity**: Run SEKF with $m$ = {10%, 30%, 50%, 100%} of parameters. Measure impact on convergence speed and final cosine similarity. Expect diminishing returns beyond ~30% if $Q$ well-tuned.
  3. **Noise covariance sweep**: For SEKF, vary $\log_{10}(Q)$ in $[-6, -1]$ and $\log_{10}(R)$ in $[-3, 1]$ on a held-out validation set. Identify region with minimal train-test gap. Anticipate optimal $Q$ small (e.g., $1e-4$) and $R$ matching observation noise level.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How does transfer learning using SEKF perform when the assumptions of functional similarity and overlapping operating domains are violated, such as with qualitatively different dynamics or non-overlapping operating conditions?
  - *Basis*: The authors state in the limitations: "Transfer learning performance when these assumptions are violated... remains to be characterized."
  - *Why unresolved*: The experimental evaluation was restricted to systems that meet these assumptions; scenarios with dissimilar dynamics or domain shift were not examined.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Systematic experiments with source-target pairs that violate functional similarity (e.g., different governing equations) or have disjoint state/input ranges, along with metrics like normalized MSE and parameter cosine similarity.

- **Open Question 2**: What is the scalability of the SEKF transfer learning method to larger neural network architectures and more complex dynamical systems?
  - *Basis*: The authors note: "the neural network architectures employed are relatively small; scaling behavior to larger models and more complex systems warrants investigation."
  - *Why unresolved*: All experiments used small MLPs; the computational complexity of SEKF (e.g., $O(m^2)$ for subset updates) and sparsity patterns may not scale well.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Apply SEKF to deeper/wider networks and complex systems (e.g., PDE-based simulations), measuring performance, convergence time, and memory usage.

- **Open Question 3**: Can adaptive hyperparameter tuning be developed for SEKF to automatically regulate the process noise covariance $Q$ and initial covariance $P_0$ based on source-target similarity, ensuring robust transfer under uncertainty?
  - *Basis*: The authors propose: "Future work should also explore adaptive methods that automatically determine the appropriate degree of parameter regularization based on the similarity between source and target systems."
  - *Why unresolved*: Current implementation relies on manual grid search or fixed values; no mechanism adjusts regularization to observed similarity.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Design algorithms that modify $Q$ and $P_0$ during transfer using online similarity estimates (e.g., Wasserstein distance), then validate on a range of similarity levels.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter opacity**: Exact ASHA-selected values (learning rates, $Q$, $P_0$, subset size $m$) for each experimental condition are unreported, hindering exact replication.
- **SEKF implementation details**: Subset selection criterion (e.g., highest gradient magnitude vs. uncertainty), Jacobian computation method, and numerical stability tricks are not fully specified.
- **Assumption validation**: The core assumption of “functional similarity” between source and target systems is not systematically tested; break conditions remain theoretical.

## Confidence
- **Bayesian prior regularization**: Medium (theoretical mechanism sound and partially evidenced by overfitting reduction, but critical “functional similarity” assumption untested)
- **Small distributed perturbations**: High (strong empirical support via >99% cosine similarity and layer-wise update patterns across two distinct systems)
- **SEKF overfitting mitigation**: Medium (train-test gap reduction shown, but paper itself notes SEKF’s final generalization comparable to Adam; its primary advantage is online continuity, not superior regularization)

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter recovery audit**: Re-run the ASHA search for a subset of conditions (e.g., damped spring, 10 samples) and report the full distribution of selected hyperparameters, not just ranges, to characterize search stability.
2. **Subset selection ablation**: For SEKF, compare alternative $m$-selection strategies (random vs. gradient magnitude vs. covariance diagonal magnitude) on convergence speed and final cosine similarity to isolate the impact of the chosen heuristic.
3. **Functional dissimilarity stress test**: Introduce a target system with deliberately mismatched dynamics (e.g., adding a nonlinearity absent in the source) and measure degradation in normalized MSE and cosine similarity to quantify the boundary of the “similar system” assumption.
