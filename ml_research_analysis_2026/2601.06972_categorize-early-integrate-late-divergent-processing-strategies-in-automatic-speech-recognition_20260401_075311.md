# Categorize Early, Integrate Late: Divergent Processing Strategies in Automatic Speech Recognition

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.06972
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06972
- Reference count: 19
- Primary result: Transformer and Conformer ASR encoders, despite similar performance, follow distinct processing hierarchies: Conformers frontload categorical features (phoneme, gender) early (∼21% depth), while Transformers integrate phoneme, accent, and duration late (∼49–57% depth), as shown by linear probing across 24 models and seven corpora.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether Transformer and Conformer ASR encoders employ different internal processing strategies despite comparable performance. Using a novel probing framework called Architectural Fingerprinting, the authors apply linear probes to 24 pre-trained models (39M–3.3B parameters) across seven speech corpora (>50k utterances) to measure when acoustic, demographic, phonetic, and temporal features become linearly accessible. Results reveal two divergent hierarchies: Conformers implement a "Categorize Early" strategy, with phoneme categories peaking at 21% depth (29% earlier than Transformers, p<0.01) and gender at 16% depth; Transformers use an "Integrate Late" approach, co-locating phoneme, accent, and duration in deeper layers (49–57%). These fingerprints are consistent across scale and training data, and a classifier can distinguish architectures from peak positions alone (AUC=0.88), suggesting practical heuristics for model selection (e.g., Conformers for low-latency streaming).

## Method Summary
The study employs linear probing to track when different features become linearly separable in ASR encoder representations. Pre-trained Transformer and Conformer models are evaluated on multiple corpora; for each model, hidden states from every layer are extracted and aligned with frame-level labels for acoustic, demographic, phonetic, and temporal features. Linear probes (logistic regression for classification, linear regression for continuous targets) are trained independently per feature per layer on disjoint speaker splits where possible. Probe performance curves are generated, and the normalized depth of maximum accuracy (peak position) is computed for each feature. These peak positions are then aggregated across models and corpora to compare representational timing between architectures, and a logistic classifier uses the set of peak positions to predict model family.

## Key Results
- Conformers show a "Categorize Early" strategy: phoneme categories peak at 21% depth and gender at 16% depth, significantly earlier than Transformers (p<0.01).
- Transformers use an "Integrate Late" approach, with phoneme, accent, and duration co-locating in deeper layers (49–57% depth).
- Architectural fingerprints are consistent across model scale and training data; a classifier on peak positions alone achieves AUC=0.88, indicating distinct processing hierarchies.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Convolutional Inductive Bias Enables Early Categorization in Conformers**  
Conformers incorporate depthwise separable convolutions that provide a strong inductive bias for local pattern matching. These convolution sublayers transform raw acoustic patterns into categorical cues (e.g., formant transitions for phonemes, spectral shape for gender) within a few layers, making such features linearly separable early in the network. This is evidenced by the significantly earlier peak positions for phoneme and gender in Conformers (mean π=0.21 and 0.16) compared to Transformers (p<0.01), and the consistency of this pattern across all Conformer models and seven corpora.

**Mechanism 2: Global Attention Requires Depth for Integration in Transformers**  
Transformer encoders rely solely on self-attention, which lacks explicit locality. Without convolutional priors, the model must gradually build sensitivity to local spectral-temporal patterns through successive layers of global mixing. This leads to a more gradual increase in linear accessibility, with high-level features like phoneme, accent, and duration emerging only after sufficient depth. The late co-location of these features at 49–57% depth in Transformers supports this mechanism, and the pattern holds across all Transformer variants in the study.

**Mechanism 3: Training Objectives Modulate Representational Timing**  
While architecture establishes a default representational profile, the training objective can shift feature emergence timing. Whisper models, trained with multitask objectives (transcription, translation, language ID), delay gender and accent processing compared to SSL Transformers. This "Whisper Effect" suggests that auxiliary tasks encourage retention of speaker-specific information deeper in the network, postponing purely phonemic representations. The effect is large and significant (e.g., gender peaks at 0.338 vs. 0.149, p<0.001) across Whisper scales.

## Foundational Learning
- **Probing for linear accessibility**: Linear probes assess when features become linearly separable in representations, indicating how the architecture transforms information into a form accessible to downstream classifiers. This methodology is central to the paper's evidence but requires understanding to interpret probe accuracy curves and their implications. - **Architectural distinction: Conformer vs. Transformer**: The analysis contrasts these two encoder families, fundamental to interpreting reported differences. Conformer blocks include depthwise convolutions alongside attention, while Transformer blocks use only self-attention, affecting local vs. global processing. Quick check: "What structural difference between a Conformer block and a Transformer block could affect how local information is processed?"
- **Peak position as a metric for representational timing**: The normalized layer where probe performance maximizes (peak position, π_f) quantifies when a feature becomes linearly accessible, underpinning the "categorize early" vs. "integrate late" characterization. Quick check: "How is the 'peak position' of a feature computed from probing curves, and what does a smaller value imply?"

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**: Pre-trained ASR encoders (Transformer/Conformer) → Layer-wise hidden state extraction → Frame-level feature labeling (acoustic, demographic, phonetic, temporal using forced alignment and Praat) → Linear probe training per layer (logistic/linear regression) → Performance curve computation → Peak position extraction → Architectural comparison (mean π_f differences) and classification (logistic regression on π_f vectors).

**Critical Path**: (1) Select model suite (24 models, mix of architectures/scales). (2) Extract layerwise hidden states on >50k utterances across seven corpora. (3) Align utterances to frame-level labels via Montreal Forced Aligner; extract acoustic features with Praat. (4) For each feature and layer, train linear probe on 80/10/10 train/val/test splits (speaker-disjoint where possible) using Adam (lr=1e-3), early stopping. (5) Compute peak position π_f = argmax_l score(l)/L. (6) Aggregate π_f across models; test differences between Transformer/Conformer groups (t-tests, regression controlling for size). (7) Train logistic regressor on [π_acoustic, π_gender, π_accent, π_phoneme, π_duration] to predict architecture, with leave-one-out cross-validation.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- *Linear vs. nonlinear probes*: Linear probes measure accessibility to simple downstream heads but may miss information present in nonlinear geometries; chosen for interpretability and comparability, though may underestimate early-layer capabilities.
- *Depth indexing*: Standardizing depth by counting only encoder blocks (excluding convolutional stems) focuses on architectural differences but could misalign indices for models with strong frontends (e.g., Whisper).
- *Feature taxonomy*: Covers acoustic→phonetic hierarchy but omits lexical/semantic processing, maintaining ASR focus at the cost of broader speech understanding.
- *Corpus diversity*: Multiple corpora improve generalizability but introduce dataset-specific noise; careful aggregation needed to avoid spurious patterns.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Probe accuracy near chance (<5% for phoneme) across all layers → feature not encoded or labels misaligned.
- High variance in peak positions across corpora for the same model → pattern not robust, possibly due to data leakage.
- Architecture classifier AUC ≈ 0.5 → no consistent fingerprint, indicating methodological issues.
- One architecture family shows uniformly distributed peaks → likely flaw in probing setup or feature definitions.
- Outlier model with extreme peak (e.g., phoneme at >0.8) → investigate training regime or data differences.

**First 3 Experiments**:
1. Reproduce probing curves for Whisper-large (Transformer) vs. Canary-1B (Conformer) on L2-ARCTIC; train linear phoneme probes per layer and plot accuracy vs. normalized depth. Expect Conformer curve to rise earlier.
2. Dataset ablation: For a single large corpus (e.g., Common Voice), compute mean phoneme peak positions for all models and compare to the aggregated mean across all corpora. The "Categorize Early" vs. "Integrate Late" pattern should persist.
3. Classifier sanity check: Using the provided peak position vectors for all 24 models, train a logistic regression with leave-one-out cross-validation. Replicate reported AUC ≈ 0.88 to confirm that fingerprints reliably distinguish architectures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Do early layers of Transformer ASR encoders encode phoneme categories in a nonlinearly separable geometry? Linear probes may miss such information.
2. In Conformer blocks, does phoneme category linear accessibility increase specifically after the convolution sublayer compared to the attention sublayer?
3. Do hybrid architectures (e.g., E-Branchformer, Squeezeformer) replicate the Conformer's early phoneme accessibility profile, supporting a convolution-driven account?
4. How does the interaction between architectural processing strategy and speaker nativeness affect robustness to non-native accents?
5. What distinct error patterns (e.g., phoneme confusions) characterize early vs. late processing layers, and do they align with different representational mechanisms?

## Limitations
- Linear probes may not detect information that is present but not linearly separable, potentially underrepresenting early-layer capabilities in Transformers.
- Depth standardization excludes convolutional stems, which could misalign depth indices especially for models like Whisper with strong frontends.
- Train/test splits for corpora without speaker metadata are unspecified, risking data leakage that particularly affects early-layer probe reliability.

## Confidence
- Conformers “Categorize Early” vs. Transformers “Integrate Late”: **High** confidence (robust across 24 models, 7 corpora, p<0.01; architecture classifier AUC=0.88).
- Training objective modulates timing (Whisper effect): **Medium** confidence (large effect within Transformer family but limited to Whisper; requires controlled studies).
- Fingerprint generalizability across scale/data: **Medium** confidence (regression controls for model size; pattern holds across corpora but Conformer sample size small).

## Next Checks
1. **Local bias intervention**: Modify Transformer encoders to include depthwise separable convolutions and Conformer blocks to remove convolutions. Apply Architectural Fingerprinting to see if phoneme/gender peak positions shift earlier/later, directly testing the inductive bias mechanism.
2. **Nonlinear probe comparison**: Train shallow MLP probes on the same representations and compare peak positions to linear probe results. Systematic earlier peaks with MLPs would indicate that linear probes underestimate early information, challenging the "early" claim for Conformers.
3. **Cross-corpora generalization test**: Train the architecture classifier on peak vectors from five corpora and evaluate on the two held-out corpora. Replicating AUC >0.85 would confirm that fingerprints are corpus-invariant and not artifacts of specific datasets.
