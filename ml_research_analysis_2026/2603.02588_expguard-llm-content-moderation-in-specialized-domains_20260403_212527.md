# ExpGuard: LLM Content Moderation in Specialized Domains

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02588
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02588
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** ExpGuard surpasses WildGuard by up to 8.9% (prompt) and 15.3% (response) F1 on domain-specific benchmarks while maintaining competitive performance on public safety benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Large language model (LLM) guardrails frequently fail to detect harmful content in specialized domains like finance, medicine, and law because they lack understanding of technical jargon and context-specific risks. This paper introduces ExpGuard, a dedicated safety model trained on ExpGuardMix—a large, curated dataset of 58,928 domain-specific prompts with expert-annotated test sets. The dataset is constructed through a pipeline that mines domain terminology, generates realistic harmful/benign prompts, and labels them via an ensemble of LLMs with human verification on a subset. ExpGuard outperforms state-of-the-art generalist guardrails on a domain-specific benchmark by significant margins and retains strong performance across eight standard public safety benchmarks. The code, data, and model are open-sourced.

## Method Summary
ExpGuard’s development centers on creating a high-quality, domain-focused safety dataset (ExpGuardMix). The process begins with mining 2,646 domain-specific terms from Wikipedia category hierarchies, filtered via Wikidata and LLMs. These terms guide GPT-4o to generate prompts that embed jargon within harmful or benign scenarios. For each prompt, two response types are generated: a compliant answer (Mistral-7B) and a refusal (Gemma-3-27B). Labeling uses an ensemble of three proprietary LLMs (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Qwen2.5-Max) with chain-of-thought reasoning; only samples with majority-vote agreement are kept. The final mix combines these domain-specific samples with in-the-wild chats and established human-written safety datasets. A Qwen2.5-7B model is then fine-tuned in a multi-task setup to classify harm in both user prompts and model responses. Evaluation uses a new expert-annotated test set (ExpGuardTest) for domains and eight public benchmarks for general competence.

## Key Results
- On the domain-specific ExpGuardTest benchmark, ExpGuard achieves up to 8.9% higher prompt-classification F1 and 15.3% higher response-classification F1 compared to WildGuard.
- ExpGuard maintains competitive performance across eight public safety benchmarks (e.g., ToxicChat, BeaverTails), demonstrating it does not sacrifice general safety for domain specialization.
- The full ExpGuardMix dataset (58,928 samples), training code, and model checkpoints are publicly released to support reproducible research.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Domain-Specific Terminology Mining and Targeted Data Generation
A four-stage pipeline recursively crawls Wikipedia categories, filters entities with Wikidata, uses GPT-4o to exclude non-sensitive terms, and applies human review to extract a controlled vocabulary of 2,646 finance/medical/legal terms. This vocabulary then directs GPT-4o to generate realistic, jargon-rich harmful and benign prompts grounded in Wikipedia abstracts. The core assumption is that general guardrails lack the lexical grounding to classify such queries; providing training data that systematically pairs specialized terms with harm labels closes this gap. Evidence includes the detailed terminology pipeline in Section 3.1.1 and the resulting term counts (989 finance, 1,012 medical, 645 legal). A limitation is reliance on Wikipedia, which may miss emerging or niche jargon.

### Mechanism 2: Ensemble LLM Labeling with Human-in-the-Loop Test Verification
For each prompt/response pair, three diverse proprietary LLMs produce chain-of-thought rationales and 14-category harm labels; a sample is retained only if at least two models agree on the exact category. For the test set, financial experts audit a subset, reporting high Cohen’s κ (0.89–0.98), which is used to justify trust in the medical/legal labels generated solely by LLM consensus. The assumption is that multi-model agreement reduces systematic bias and that expert validation in one domain generalizes if inter-rater reliability is high. Evidence is the labeling protocol in Section 3.1.3 and expert verification in Section 3.2. A break condition is if all LLMs share a blind spot on a domain-specific concept (e.g., misinterpreting financial “haircut”), consensus errors could propagate, especially for medical/legal without human checks.

### Mechanism 3: Multi-Task Training on a Heterogeneous Mixture
ExpGuard is trained on a mixture of domain-specific data (19,907 samples), in-the-wild chats (8,859), and human-written safety datasets (27,887). The multi-task setup predicts harm for prompts alone and for prompt–response pairs. The mixture balances specialization (domain jargon) and generalization (real-world interactions). Evidence includes ablations showing removal of domain-specific data reduces domain performance by ~8% (prompt F1), while removing general data harms public benchmark scores (Section 4.3). The training trade-off is that proportions must be tuned; too much domain data risks overfitting, too little forfeits domain gains.

## Foundational Learning
- **Specialized domain risk in LLMs**: Generic guardrails fail with technical jargon because they lack context for domain-specific harms (e.g., a finance query about “pump and dump” schemes). *Quick check*: Give an example of a query safe in general conversation but harmful in finance, and explain why.
- **Dataset curation via LLM-assisted pipelines**: Using LLMs to generate and label data risks model bias and incoherence. *Quick check*: What are two ways an LLM-based labeling pipeline could mislabel domain content, and how does ExpGuard’s design address them?
- **Multi-task safety classification**: Separate classifiers for prompts vs. responses catch different harms (e.g., a malicious user prompt vs. a toxic model reply). *Quick check*: Why might a guardrail need both? What harm could each catch that the other misses?
- **Ensemble majority voting for label quality**: Consensus among multiple LLMs aims to reduce individual model biases. *Quick check*: If three LLMs disagree on a category, what are plausible reasons? Does discarding such samples bias the dataset?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Wikipedia crawl -> Wikidata filter -> GPT-4o jargon filter -> Human review -> Term list -> GPT-4o prompt generation -> Mistral/Gemma response generation -> Three-LLM ensemble labeling -> Majority vote filter -> Deduplication -> Multi-task training (Qwen2.5-7B) -> Evaluation (ExpGuardTest + public benchmarks)

**Critical path**:  
1. Mine domain terms via recursive Wikipedia crawling, Wikidata, and LLM/human filtering.  
2. Generate harmful/benign prompts per term using GPT-4o with templates.  
3. Generate compliant and refusal responses for selected prompts using Mistral-7B and Gemma-3-27B.  
4. Label all samples with three LLMs using chain-of-thought; keep only those with category consensus.  
5. For test set, obtain expert verification (finance) or rely on LLM consensus (medical/legal).  
6. Fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B on the mixture in a multi-task setup.  
7. Evaluate on ExpGuardTest and eight public benchmarks; compare against baselines.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Scalability vs. label quality*: Expert verification limited to finance due to cost; medical/legal rely on unchecked LLM consensus.  
- *Diversity vs. coherence*: Multiple instruction templates increase surface diversity but may produce unnatural prompts.  
- *Specialization vs. generalization*: 19.9K domain samples boost domain performance but 36.7K general samples are needed for public benchmark competence; imbalance degrades one or the other.  
- *Open-source vs. proprietary models*: Using mixed LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude, etc.) introduces licensing and cost barriers for replication.

**Failure signatures**:  
- *Term mining incompleteness*: Missing a class of jargon (e.g., new financial instruments) leaves blind spots. Detect by sampling unseen domain documents for coverage.  
- *LLM labeling bias*: Systematic misclassification of a harm category (e.g., all “fraud” as “criminal planning”) skews distribution. Audit per-category confusion matrices.  
- *Expert verification gap*: Medical/legal test labels lack human checks; if LLM-expert agreement is lower than finance (κ=0.89/0.98), performance may be inflated.  
- *Training instability*: Multi-task loss weighting not detailed; poor balance could cause one task to dominate. Monitor per-task loss curves.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Replicate baseline: Train ExpGuard on ExpGuardTrain split and evaluate on ExpGuardTest and WildGuardTest to confirm reported F1 gaps.  
2. Ablate labeling strategy: Retrain using a single LLM instead of ensemble voting; expect degradation on ExpGuardTest if consensus quality matters.  
3. Domain transfer test: Apply terminology mining to a new domain (e.g., cybersecurity), fine-tune ExpGuard, and evaluate on a held-out cybersecurity test set to assess adaptability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization to other domains**: The paper validates only finance, medical, and legal. It is unclear how well the pipeline transfers to fields like aerospace or nuclear physics, which have distinct jargon and risk structures. The authors note this in Limitations and suggest the pipeline is "readily extended," but empirical validation is lacking.
- **Coverage of real-world query diversity**: The synthesized prompts may not fully capture the unpredictability of live user queries (e.g., novel phrasing, slang, multi-intent requests). The paper acknowledges this gap but does not quantify it via comparison to actual deployment logs.
- **False positives on benign technical inquiries**: The focus is reducing false negatives (missing harm). Over-refusal on legitimate but complex domain-specific questions (e.g., a lawyer’s nuanced query) could severely hurt usability. The dataset’s benign prompt construction may not optimize for permitting sophisticated legitimate discourse.
- **Long-term efficacy against evolving threats**: Harmful content and adversarial tactics evolve rapidly. ExpGuardMix is a static snapshot; the paper admits it "requires periodic updates" but does not study decay rates or operational update costs for live systems.

## Limitations
- Terminology mining depends on Wikipedia/Wikidata and may miss emerging, niche, or non-Wikipedia jargon.
- Expert verification is only performed for the finance test subset; medical/legal labels rely on unchecked LLM consensus, introducing potential hidden bias.
- Data generation and labeling specifics (e.g., GPT-4o prompts, temperature) are undisclosed, hindering exact replication of ExpGuardMix.
- Training hyperparameters (e.g., multi-task loss weighting, early stopping criteria) are incompletely reported, possibly affecting the balance between prompt and response tasks.

## Confidence
- **Domain-specific data construction**: Medium – pipeline is detailed and ablations show benefit, but coverage gaps (Wikipedia dependency) remain.
- **Ensemble labeling quality**: Medium – high inter-LLM agreement and finance expert κ validate; medical/legal lack human verification.
- **Multi-task mixture design**: High – clear ablations demonstrate trade-offs; strong results across 10 benchmarks support efficacy.
- **Superiority over WildGuard**: Medium – gains are substantial on domain test but based on a new, unreleased benchmark and baseline nuances.

## Next Checks
1. **Replication baseline**: Train ExpGuard using the released ExpGuardTrain split and evaluate on ExpGuardTest and WildGuardTest to independently confirm the reported F1 improvements.
2. **Error analysis on finance subset**: Audit the expert-verified finance samples where the three-LLM ensemble discarded data (disagreements) to uncover potential systematic labeling blind spots in the training set.
3. **New domain transfer**: Apply the full terminology mining and data generation pipeline to a distinct domain (e.g., cybersecurity), fine-tune ExpGuard, and evaluate on a held-out test set to test generalizability beyond the three studied domains.
