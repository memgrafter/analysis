# Sufficient Conditions for Stability of Minimum-Norm Interpolating Deep ReLU Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13910
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13910
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Deep ReLU networks obtained via minimum‑norm interpolation are stable when they contain a stable sub‑network followed by a layer with low stable rank (≈ 1); a counterexample shows the low‑rank condition is necessary.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies algorithmic stability of deep ReLU networks trained under minimum‑norm interpolation, a model for overparameterized gradient training with weight decay. The authors prove that stability is guaranteed if the network contains a stable sub‑network and immediately after it has a layer whose weight matrix has stable rank close to 1. They also provide a counterexample on a 3‑layer architecture to show the low‑rank assumption is necessary. The analysis uses singular value decomposition, margin bounds derived from the minimum‑norm property, and perturbation lemmas. Experiments on MNIST and Fashion‑MNIST with 8‑layer fully‑connected networks confirm that trained models exhibit both stable sub‑networks and decreasing stable rank as the sample size grows, supporting the theory.

## Method Summary
To validate the theory, the authors train 8‑layer fully‑connected ReLU networks on binarized MNIST and Fashion‑MNIST using Adam with weight decay, ensuring zero training error (minimum‑norm interpolation). For varying training set sizes, they train multiple models on disjoint subsets and evaluate stability of sub‑network outputs on a shared test set. They compute stable rank for each layer and measure how stability propagates through the network. The key steps are: (1) verify interpolation, (2) identify a layer where the preceding sub‑network is highly stable and the layer itself has low stable rank, and (3) demonstrate that artificially increasing the stable rank of that layer destroys full‑network stability, thereby illustrating the necessity condition.

## Key Results
- **Theorem 5 (sufficiency):** Under B‑admissibility, existence of a β‑uniformly ϵ‑stable sub‑network up to layer k, and a layer k with stable rank ≤ 1 + aϵ, the full network is β‑uniformly ϵ′‑stable with ϵ′ = (1+8 B^{2L‑k+1})ϵ + lower‑order terms, provided the sample size is large enough.
- **Theorem 6 (necessity):** A 3‑layer counterexample shows that without the low‑rank assumption, even with a stable sub‑network, the full network may be unstable.
- **Empirical confirmation:** Experiments reveal that (i) trained models indeed contain stable sub‑networks, (ii) stable ranks of layers decrease with more data, and (iii) the stability of the full network correlates with the presence of a low‑rank layer following a stable sub‑network.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper explains stability through three interconnected mechanisms:

1. **Low‑rank layer as a stabilizer:** If layer k has a weight matrix with stable rank close to 1 (i.e., its Frobenius norm is dominated by its top singular value), then after a stable sub‑network, this layer effectively projects the sub‑network’s output onto a scaled version. Deeper layers then multiply this signal by a fixed constant, making the final output insensitive to perturbations in the training set.

2. **Minimum‑norm interpolation forces a margin:** Because the solution minimizes the total ℓ₂ norm, the weights cannot concentrate in later layers if the sub‑network output has a tiny margin. This forces a non‑trivial margin (|vₖᵀ fₖ(xᵢ)| ≥ γ) at the sub‑network level, where vₖ is the top right singular vector of Wₖ. The margin scales inversely with powers of the admissibility constant B.

3. **Norm equi‑distribution:** At any global minimum‑norm interpolant, the Frobenius norms of all layers are equal (a consequence of KKT conditions). This property allows lower bounding individual layer contributions and ensures that the candidate solution in the analysis indeed attains the minimum norm.

If any of these mechanisms fails—e.g., the low‑rank assumption is violated, the margin vanishes due to high B, or the solution is not globally minimum‑norm—the stability guarantee may break down, as illustrated by the counterexample in Theorem 6.

## Foundational Learning
- **Algorithmic Stability (β‑uniform ϵ‑stability):** A predictor is stable if changing one training point changes its output by at most ϵ with probability ≥ 1‑β. *Why needed:* Stability is the core property under study and directly relates to generalization bounds. *Quick check:* Does the predictor’s prediction vary little when a single training example is altered?
- **Minimum‑Norm Interpolation:** Among all networks that fit the training set, the algorithm returns one minimizing the sum of squared Frobenius norms of weight matrices. *Why needed:* This models the implicit bias of gradient‑based training with weight decay. *Quick check:* Is the chosen solution the one with smallest parameter norm among all interpolants?
- **Stable Rank S(W) = ‖W‖_F² / ‖W‖₂²:** Measures how close a matrix is to rank‑1; low stable rank means the Frobenius norm is dominated by the largest singular value. *Why needed:* The key sufficient condition requires a layer with S(Wₖ) ≤ 1 + aϵ. *Quick check:* For the critical layer, is the top singular value almost as large as the entire Frobenius norm?
- **ReLU Homogeneity:** For any non‑negative scalar α, ReLU(αx)=α ReLU(x). *Why needed:* It enables scaling arguments when relating sub‑network outputs to final predictions. *Quick check:* Does scaling the input by α ≥ 0 scale the network’s output by α?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input → Hidden layers 1..k‑1 → Layer k → Hidden layers k+1..L → Output

**Critical path:** Stability hinges on a cascade: a stable sub‑network (layers 1..k‑1) produces an output that is then processed by a low‑rank layer k and subsequently by deeper layers (k+1..L). The low‑rank layer ensures that the mapping from sub‑network output to final prediction is essentially a fixed scaling, preserving stability.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Depth vs. B‑factor:* A deeper stable sub‑network (larger k) reduces the B^{2L‑k+1} term in the bound, but increasing total depth L amplifies that factor unless a is very small.  
- *Low‑rank vs. expressivity:* Very low stable rank (a≈0) yields tighter bounds but may limit the network’s representational capacity; a small aϵ allows slight rank while maintaining stability.  
- *Width:* The analysis assumes uniform hidden width; wider networks may naturally exhibit lower stable ranks, but the admissibility constant B depends on teacher complexity, not directly on width.

**Failure signatures:**  
- No layer with stable rank near 1 after a stable sub‑network.  
- The sub‑network itself is unstable.  
- The B‑admissibility constant is large, shrinking the margin and blowing up the B‑factor.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Verify that the training procedure indeed yields minimum‑norm interpolants (e.g., by comparing total norm to a convex reformulation or random search).  
2. For each depth k, measure the stability of sub‑network outputs and the stable rank of layer k; confirm that some k exhibits both high sub‑network stability and S(Wₖ) ≈ 1, and that full‑network stability correlates with this pattern.  
3. Artificially increase the stable rank of the layer following a stable sub‑network while keeping the sub‑network unchanged; observe that full‑network stability deteriorates, illustrating Theorem 6.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does every minimum‑norm interpolating deep ReLU network contain a stable sub‑network?  
- Can the stability guarantees be extended from gradient flow to practical optimizers like SGD?  
- Is the B^{2L‑k+1} factor in the stability bound tight, or can it be improved to B^L for deep stable sub‑networks?  
- Do the key assumptions (B‑admissibility and low‑rank bias) hold for larger, overparameterized networks on complex real‑world datasets?

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis relies on strong assumptions (B‑admissibility, existence of a stable sub‑network, global minimum‑norm solution) that may not hold universally.  
- The bound contains multiplicative factors such as B^{2L‑k+1} that can become large, limiting applicability to shallow or low‑B regimes.  
- Empirical validation is restricted to 8‑layer fully‑connected networks on MNIST/Fashion‑MNIST; extension to deeper, convolutional, or real‑world architectures remains unverified.

## Confidence
- Theoretical sufficiency (Theorem 5): **High**  
- Theoretical necessity (Theorem 6): **High**  
- Empirical validation: **Medium**  
- Mechanistic explanation (low‑rank preservation, margin forcing, norm equi‑distribution): **Medium**

3. **Test necessity of low‑rank assumption:** Artificially increase the stable rank of the layer after a stable sub‑network (e.g., by adding an orthogonal low‑norm component) and quantify the consequent drop in full‑network stability.
