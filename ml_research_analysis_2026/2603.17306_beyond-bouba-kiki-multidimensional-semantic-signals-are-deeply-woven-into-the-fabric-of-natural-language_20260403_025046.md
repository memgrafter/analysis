# Beyond bouba/kiki: Multidimensional semantic signals are deeply woven into the fabric of natural language

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17306  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17306  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** 96% of English letter pairs show significant semantic differentiation across nine dimensions (mean |Cohen’s d| = 0.59); these patterns are predictable from articulatory features (cross‑validated R² up to .98) and generalize across five languages.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the Saussurean principle of arbitrariness in the relationship between sound and meaning by demonstrating that individual phonemes carry structured, multidimensional semantic signals. Through a comprehensive analysis of all 220 pairwise letter contrasts in English, the authors used three large language models (LLMs) to rate 4,427 pseudoword pairs on nine semantic dimensions (e.g., size, brightness, shape). The results reveal pervasive and consistent phoneme–meaning associations: 96% of contrasts produced significant semantic differentiation, with a mean absolute Cohen’s d of 0.59. The semantic profiles are low‑dimensional (87.6% variance captured by two principal components) and can be accurately predicted from articulatory‑phonetic features (cross‑validated R² up to .98), indicating that manner of articulation primarily drives weight/size/brightness while place of articulation drives shape/texture. Human behavioral validation (written and auditory) and preliminary cross‑linguistic tests across five languages confirm the generalizability of these effects, suggesting that sound symbolism is a fundamental, structured property of natural language.

## Method Summary
The study employed a minimal‑pair design covering all 220 pairwise letter contrasts in the English alphabet. For each contrast, the authors generated 20 pseudoword pairs (4,427 total) by varying one or two occurrences of the target letter while keeping the surrounding letters constant and ensuring phonotactic legality and non‑word status. Three state‑of‑the‑art LLMs (GPT‑4o, Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Flash) rated each pseudoword on nine 0‑10 scales (size, shape, brightness, texture, speed, temperature, pleasantness, weight, elevation). For every letter contrast and semantic dimension, the mean rating difference between the two members of the pair was standardized as Cohen’s d; significance was assessed via permutation tests with false discovery rate correction. To test the hypothesis that articulatory properties underlie these associations, the authors encoded each letter’s canonical IPA segment using 24 binary features from the PanPhon database, computed signed feature differences for each contrast, and used ridge regression with nested cross‑validation to predict the semantic d‑scores. Human validation involved written forced‑choice tasks (English speakers) and auditory recognition tasks across five languages (English, Spanish, Japanese, Mandarin, Arabic). The semantic dimensionality was further explored via principal component analysis.

## Key Results
- Pervasive phoneme–meaning associations: 96% of the 220 letter contrasts yielded significant semantic differentiation (FDR‑corrected q<.05), with mean absolute Cohen’s d = 0.59, and cross‑model agreement was high (average pairwise r > 0.8). Two principal components captured 87.6% of the variance across the nine dimensions.
- Articulatory‑phonetic features predict semantic dimensions with high accuracy: cross‑validated R² reached .84 for consonants and up to .98 for vowels; manner of articulation strongly influences weight, size, and brightness, while place of articulation influences shape and texture.
- Human behavioral validation confirms the patterns: in a written forced‑choice task, participants identified the “correct” pseudoword (as defined by LLM consensus) with 80.8% accuracy (above chance). Auditory testing across five languages showed above‑chance performance (73.0–84.2%), supporting cross‑linguistic generalizability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Distributional statistics encode phoneme‑meaning associations
- **Claim:** Distributional statistics in language corpora encode pervasive, multidimensional phoneme‑meaning associations recoverable by models from orthographic input alone.
- **Mechanism:** Large language models (LLMs), trained on vast text corpora, implicitly learn statistical regularities where specific letter sequences co‑occur with semantically related contexts. When presented with pseudowords, these models generate ratings consistent with sound‑symbolic mappings because the training data contains subtle lexical distributions that reflect historically grounded iconicity (e.g., words with /i/ more frequently appear in contexts associated with smallness/brightness).
- **Core assumption:** Lexical statistics preserve articulatorily grounded sound‑meaning correspondences despite conventionalization.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes that LLMs recover these patterns using only text input; Study 1 shows three independent LLMs produce consistent Cohen’s d profiles across 220 contrasts, with split‑half reliability confirming robustness; corpus analysis reveals that neighboring work focuses on vision‑and‑language models, providing weaker direct text‑only support.
- **Break condition:** If LLMs show low cross‑model agreement (r < 0.5) or effects vanish when controlling for phonotactic probability/word frequency, the claim would be falsified.

### Mechanism 2: Articulatory‑phonetic features systematically predict semantic dimensions
- **Claim:** Articulatory‑phonetic features (manner, place) systematically predict the semantic dimensions associated with phoneme contrasts.
- **Mechanism:** The proprioceptive and kinesthetic experience of producing speech sounds creates cross‑modal mappings. For example, sonorant consonants (e.g., /l/, /m/) involve more open vocal tract configurations, which sensorimotor experience links to largeness/roundness; high front vowels (e.g., /i/) involve tongue advancement and lip spreading, linked to smallness/brightness. Regression models using articulatory feature differences can predict LLM‑derived semantic effect sizes with high cross‑validated R².
- **Core assumption:** The mapping is grounded in embodied production experience, not perception alone.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract reports that articulatory‑phonetic features predicted the semantic dimensions with high accuracy (cross‑validated R² up to .98), with manner dominating weight/size/brightness and place driving shape/texture; Study 4 uses ridge regression with PanPhon features to achieve R² = .56–.84 for consonants and shows that 14/16 classical hypothesis tests are directionally consistent.
- **Break condition:** If articulatory feature models lose predictive power (R² near zero) when tested on novel semantic dimensions or after partialling out acoustic correlates (e.g., formant frequencies), the claim of articulatory grounding would be undermined.

### Mechanism 3: Core phoneme‑meaning associations are cross‑linguistically generalizable
- **Claim:** Core phoneme‑meaning associations are cross‑linguistically generalizable, with universal tendencies modulated by native‑language phonological experience.
- **Mechanism:** Evolutionarily ancient cross‑modal correspondences (e.g., high pitch ↔ small size) provide a universal bias. These biases interact with language‑specific phonological inventories during acquisition, leading to shared core mappings (e.g., /a/ ↔ largeness) but variation in strength for specific contrasts (e.g., /r/’s roughness effect).
- **Core assumption:** A biologically rooted cross‑modal correspondence system exists independently of any particular language.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract mentions preliminary cross‑linguistic evidence from five typologically diverse languages with above‑chance performance (73.0–84.2%); Study 3 shows all five language groups (English, Spanish, Japanese, Mandarin, Arabic) perform significantly above chance, but pair‑level correlations between languages are low (mean r = .22), indicating modulation.
- **Break condition:** If no language group performs above chance in a fully powered study, or if pair‑level correlations are uniformly high across unrelated languages, the universal core with modulation model would be rejected.

## Foundational Learning

**Sound symbolism (non‑arbitrary form‑meaning mappings)**  
- *Why needed here:* The paper’s central challenge to Saussurean arbitrariness depends on establishing that individual phonemes carry consistent semantic signals across multiple dimensions.  
- *Quick check question:* What is the classic bouba/kiki effect, and why is it considered evidence against complete arbitrariness?

**Articulatory phonetics (manner/place/voicing features)**  
- *Why needed here:* Study 4’s predictive models rely on binary articulatory features (e.g., [+sonorant], [+high]) to explain semantic dimensions; understanding these features is essential to grasp the proposed grounding mechanism.  
- *Quick check question:* How does the articulatory feature [+continuant] differ between /s/ and /p/, and what semantic dimensions might this contrast predict?

**Large language models as distributional semantic simulators**  
- *Why needed here:* The study uses LLMs as tools to detect latent semantic structure in pseudowords; one must understand that LLMs reflect co‑occurrence statistics, not grounded perception.  
- *Quick check question:* Why is it surprising that text‑only LLMs recover sound‑symbolic patterns, and what does this imply about language corpora?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Nonword generator → LLM rating client → Articulatory feature engine → Statistical analysis pipeline → Human study platform

### Critical path
Nonword generation → LLM rating aggregation → articulatory prediction model → human pilot validation → full‑scale cross‑linguistic design.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Scale vs. ecological validity:** The study covers all 220 letter contrasts with 20 pairs each, but uses artificial pseudowords rather than real words.
- **Text vs. audio:** LLM analysis uses orthographic input, while human validation includes auditory stimuli to test modality independence.
- **English‑centric vs. cross‑ling:** Initial focus on English letter‑phonemes; cross‑ling pilots require careful phoneme mapping and are underpowered.

### Failure signatures
- Low cross‑model agreement in LLM ratings (r < 0.7) suggests an unstable signal.
- Articulatory regression cross‑validated R² < 0.3 indicates weak grounding.
- Human accuracy ≤ 55% in any dimension or language suggests absence of detectable sound symbolism.

### First 3 experiments
1. Replicate LLM patterns with open‑source models (e.g., Llama‑3.1, Mistral) to rule out proprietary artifacts; test if smaller models show gradient alignment with effect size.
2. Test alternative articulatory feature systems (e.g., SPE vs. PanPhon) and include acoustic correlates (F1/F2) in ridge regressions; use nested CV to isolate proprioceptive contributions.
3. Conduct full‑scale cross‑linguistic study with N ≥ 100 per language, using within‑language phoneme contrasts (not just English letters) to test universality directly.

## Limitations
- **IPA mapping ambiguity:** The canonical mapping from letters to IPA segments (e.g., for C, G, Y) is unspecified, potentially affecting articulatory feature assignments.
- **LLM protocol opacity:** Exact prompts, temperature settings, and response parsing are undocumented, risking variability in ratings.
- **Cross‑linguistic generalizability:** The pilot study is underpowered and uses English letter contrasts, limiting claims about universality.
