# Revisiting (Sub)Optimality of Best-of-N for Inference-Time Alignment

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05739
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05739
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Best-of-N (BoN) is optimal for win-rate under mild conditions; EM-regularized BoN eliminates reward hacking while maintaining optimality; prior chi²-regularized BoN can be arbitrarily worse.

## Executive Summary
This paper re-examines Best-of-N (BoN) inference-time alignment. Contrary to prior claims that BoN is suboptimal and prone to reward hacking under expected true reward, the authors argue that the appropriate metric is win-rate—the pairwise comparison objective used to train reward models. They prove that, with minimal assumptions (low pairwise win-rate error and small EM-divergence between comparator and reference policies), properly tuned BoN is both statistically and computationally optimal for win-rate. To address reward hacking, they introduce EM-regularized BoN, which selects uniformly from the top quantile of scored responses; this variant provably avoids reward hacking while preserving optimality. In contrast, chi²-regularized BoN from prior work can be arbitrarily suboptimal under the win-rate objective. The study emphasizes that the choice of objective fundamentally affects the optimality analysis of inference-time alignment algorithms.

## Method Summary
The authors analyze BoN sampling: draw N responses from a reference policy π_ref and select the highest-scoring under a learned reward model br. They decompose win-rate regret into three terms: (i) error in br on the comparator’s capped distribution, (ii) gap between the comparator’s optimal capped policy and BoN’s selection, and (iii) error under the BoN-selected distribution. By bounding these using pairwise error ε_pw and EM-divergence EM(π*||π_ref), they show BoN achieves near-optimal win-rate when N scales appropriately. To mitigate reward hacking (win-rate declining with larger N), they propose EM-regularized BoN: approximate the optimal EM-regularized policy (top 1/M quantile under br) by taking the top ⌈N/M⌉ samples and choosing uniformly. This caps the density ratio at M, making regret monotonic in N and eliminating hacking. They also prove that chi²-regularized BoN, which uses a chi² penalty, can over-concentrate on high-score, low-probability spikes and thus perform arbitrarily worse.

## Key Results
- Properly tuned BoN is statistically and computationally optimal for win-rate under assumptions of small pairwise error and EM-divergence.
- EM-regularized BoN provably eliminates reward hacking while maintaining the same optimality guarantees as optimally tuned BoN.
- Chi²-regularized BoN can be arbitrarily suboptimal compared to EM-regularized BoN under the win-rate objective, as demonstrated through a constructed hard instance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Best-of-N (BoN) is statistically and computationally optimal for win-rate (a pairwise comparison metric) when the reward model's pairwise error is small and the reference policy's EM-divergence to the comparator is controlled—explaining its empirical prevalence despite prior theoretical claims of suboptimality.
- **Mechanism:**  
  1. Decompose win-rate regret into three terms: (i) mismatch between true reward and learned reward under the comparator's capped distribution, (ii) gap between the comparator's capped policy and BoN's selection under the learned reward, (iii) mismatch between learned and true reward under the BoN-selected distribution.  
  2. Control term (i) using the fact that win-rate is 1-Lipschitz in total variation and that the TV distance to any M-capped policy is lower-bounded by EM-divergence.  
  3. Control term (ii) by noting that, conditioned on a fixed batch of samples, BoN picks the highest-scoring response under the learned reward—dominating any other selection rule (including the comparator's capped policy) within that batch.  
  4. Control term (iii) by bounding the density ratio of the BoN policy (≤N) and using change-of-measure to relate pairwise error under BoN's distribution to that under the reference.
- **Core assumption:**  
  - The reward model `br` has small pairwise win-rate error `ε_pw` on samples from the reference model `π_ref`.  
  - The comparator policy `π*` has small EM-divergence to `π_ref` (i.e., `EM(π*||π_ref)` is small).  
  - `π_ref` is non-atomic (for lower bounds and some approximations).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "properly tuned BoN is both statistically and computationally optimal in achieving high win-rate"  
  - [section 2.2] Definition of pairwise error `ε_pw` and argument against squared error  
  - [section 3] Theorem 3 upper bound and Theorem 4 lower bound for BoN  
  - [corpus] Neighbors (InfAlign, TreeBoN) also focus on win-rate and inference-time alignment, corroborating the practical relevance of this objective.
- **Break condition:**  
  - If `π_ref` is atomic (has point masses) and `π*` assigns mass to points with extremely low `π_ref` probability, the EM-divergence blowup requires N to grow exponentially, breaking sample-efficiency.  
  - If `ε_pw` is large (reward model disagrees with `r*` on typical outputs), the N-scaling in the first term dominates and BoN's regret scales linearly with N, offsetting gains from small EM-divergence.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The EM-regularized BoN (selecting uniformly from the top `⌊N/M⌋` scored responses) provably eliminates reward hacking—its win-rate regret is monotone in N—while maintaining the same statistical optimality as optimally tuned BoN.
- **Mechanism:**  
  1. Introduce the variational problem: maximize `E_π[br] - R_max * EM(π||π_ref)` over policies `π`.  
  2. Show via Lagrange multipliers that the optimal policy is a threshold policy: take the top `1/M` quantile of `br` scores under `π_ref` and sample uniformly from it.  
  3. Approximate this policy via sampling N candidates and picking uniformly among the top `⌈N/M⌉` (empirical quantile policy).  
  4. Bound regret: term (i) (reward model error under comparator's capped policy) becomes `M·ε_pw` (decoupled from N); term (ii) (gap to empirical quantile) decays as `O(1/N)` due to explicit order-statistic calculation; term (iii) (reward model error under selected policy) remains `M·ε_pw` because the density ratio is capped at M, not N.
- **Core assumption:**  
  - The reference model `π_ref` is rich enough that the `1/M` quantile exists and can be approximated by `N` samples when `N ≥ M`.  
  - The reward model `br` is bounded in `[0, R_max]`.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 4] Lemma 1 derivation of threshold policy, Theorem 5 upper bound  
  - [section D.2] Proof using order statistics for `R_br(π_M)` and `R_br(bπ_M)`  
  - [corpus] Soft Best-of-n (Neighbor #8) also aims to mitigate reward hacking, but lacks the same optimality guarantee under win-rate.
- **Break condition:**  
  - If `N < M`, the top-quantile approximation fails (the empirical quantile may be too crude), and the `1/N` error term in Theorem 5 may not vanish.  
  - If `π_ref` has atoms or discrete mass at the threshold, the quantile definition becomes ambiguous; uniform tie-breaking is required but may still incur small fluctuations.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The chi-squared-regularized BoN (from prior work) is arbitrarily suboptimal under win-rate regret compared to EM-regularized BoN, because it reweights by `(br(y)-λ)^+` / β, which can over-concentrate on high-score but low-true-reward spikes in the reference distribution.
- **Mechanism:**  
  1. Construct a three-outcome hard instance: reference mass splits into a low-reference-probability "spike" `P` (mass ε) with highest `br` score but true reward 0, a "good" outcome `C` (mass α-ε) with near-top `br` score and true reward 1, and a "bad" outcome `B` (mass 1-α) with low scores.  
  2. Show that EM-regularized BoN (top-α policy) necessarily splits mass between `P` and `C` in proportion to their reference masses, incurring regret proportional to `(1 - α/2)·ε/α`.  
  3. Show that chi-squared-regularized BoN's density ratio is proportional to `(br - λ)^+`, so even a tiny `br` advantage of `P` over `C` (controlled by δ) causes significant over-concentration on `P`, adding an extra `O(εδ/β)` regret term that cannot be tuned away uniformly over β.
- **Core assumption:**  
  - The reward model `br` can have small pairwise error `ε_pw` (≈2ε) while still giving `P` a slight edge over `C` (score gap δ).  
  - The comparator policy `π*` is δ_C (pure C), and the top-quantile mass α can be made arbitrarily small (α = k√ε) to amplify the spike's impact.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 4] Proposition 2 formal statement and proof sketch  
  - [section D.3] Detailed calculation of `Reg(π_χ_β)` in three regimes and comparison to `Reg(π_M)`  
  - [corpus] "χ^2-regularized BoN" is explicitly mentioned as a prior approach that can be arbitrarily worse.
- **Break condition:**  
  - If the reward model's scores are perfectly tied between `P` and `C` (δ=0), both regularized methods perform similarly; the separation vanishes.  
  - If `ε` (spike mass) is not sufficiently small relative to α, the `(1-α/2)·ε/α` term dominates and the additive gap from χ² may be less pronounced.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: f-divergences (e.g., χ², KL, TV) and their operational meanings**  
  Why needed here: The paper uses EM-divergence (a special f-divergence) to quantify the difficulty of approximating `π*` using samples from `π_ref`. It contrasts this with χ²-divergence (used in prior work) and shows EM is better aligned with win-rate regret. Understanding f-divergences is key to seeing why the "right" distributional discrepancy matters.  
  *Quick check:* If `EM(π*||π_ref)=0`, what does that imply about the density ratio `dπ*/dπ_ref` almost surely? (Answer: It is bounded above by M.)

- **Concept: Approximate rejection sampling & coverage**  
  Why needed here: The analysis of BoN and EM-regularized BoN relies on viewing selection rules as approximate rejection samplers. Block & Polyanskiy's result linking EM-divergence to the minimal N needed to achieve small TV error is repeatedly used. This provides the computational lower bound intuition.  
  *Quick check:* In approximate rejection sampling with truncation level M, what happens to the policy's TV distance to the target if `EM(π||π_ref) = ε` and N >> M? (Answer: TV ≤ ε + exp(-N/M).)

- **Concept: Bradley-Terry model and pairwise preference learning**  
  Why needed here: The paper's core metric (pairwise win-rate error `ε_pw`) directly mirrors how reward models are trained on comparison data. The scale-invariance argument against squared error relies on the ordinal nature of preferences.  
  *Quick check:* Why is squared error not scale-invariant for reward models used in BoN? (Answer: Because BoN only uses the *ranking* of responses under `br`; multiplying `br` by a constant does not change the selected output but does change squared error.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  1. **Reference model `π_ref`**: Provides candidate responses `y_i`. Must be easy to sample from. In practice, this is the base LLM.  
  2. **Reward model `br`**: Scores each candidate. Requires low pairwise error `ε_pw` on `π_ref`-typical outputs. In practice, a Bradley-Terry model trained on human comparisons.  
  3. **BoN selector**: Given N samples from `π_ref`, picks `argmax_i br(y_i)`. For EM-regularized: pick uniformly among top `⌈N/M⌉`.  
  4. **Comparator policy `π*`**: Defines the win-rate benchmark. Could be `π_ref` itself (self-comparison) or a stronger model (e.g., GPT-4 in AlpacaEval).  
  5. **Divergence estimators**: Theoretical analysis uses `EM(π*||π_ref)` and pairwise error `ε_pw`. In practice, these must be estimated or bounded via offline validation.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Estimate `ε_pw` on a held-out set of `π_ref` samples (compare `br` rankings to an oracle).  
  2. Estimate or bound `EM(π*||π_ref)` (e.g., via importance sampling if `π*` is known, or via coverage metrics).  
  3. If using EM-regularized BoN: choose `M` to balance `M·ε_pw` and `EM(π*||π_ref)` (theoretical optimum: `M ≈ sqrt(EM/ε_pw)` up to logs).  
  4. If using vanilla BoN: choose `N` to balance `N·ε_pw` and `EM(π*||π_ref)` (theoretical optimum: `N ≈ sqrt(EM/ε_pw)` up to logs).  
  5. Monitor for reward hacking: if win-rate decreases with larger N, switch to EM-regularized.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **BoN vs. EM-regularized BoN**: Vanilla BoN is simpler and often used; EM-regularized adds a quantile threshold but guarantees monotonicity in N.  
  - **Estimating `ε_pw`**: Requires pairwise comparisons of `br` vs. oracle on `π_ref` data—expensive if oracle is human. Could use a high-quality LM as proxy.  
  - **Estimating `EM`**: Requires knowing `π*` or having a comparator `q` that is close to `π_ref` (density ratio bound L small). If `q` is far, `L` blows up the bound in Theorem 6.  
  - **Choosing M/N**: Theoretical rates are worst-case; in practice, cross-validation on a validation set with pairwise win-rate against the comparator is more reliable.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  1. **Regret not improving with N**: Indicates `N·ε_pw` term dominates—reward model error is too high. Action: improve `br` or use EM-regularized BoN with appropriate M.  
  2. **Win-rate decreases with larger N**: Classic reward hacking. Action: switch to EM-regularized (top-quantile) selection.  
  3. **High variance in win-rate estimates**: Could indicate `π*` is far from `π_ref` (large `EM`). Action: improve reference model or use a more similar comparator.  
  4. **Theoretical bound loose**: The `log(1/ε_pw)` fudge factor and the L multiplier in Theorem 6 can be large; empirical performance may be better than theory suggests.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Pairwise error estimation**: Sample 1000 responses from `π_ref`, have an oracle (human or strong LM) rank pairs. Compute `ε_pw` for your `br`. If `ε_pw > 0.1`, reward model likely too noisy for BoN without regularization.  
  2. **Coverage/EM estimation**: If comparator `π*` is known (e.g., a fine-tuned model), estimate the maximum density ratio `max_y (dπ*/dπ_ref)(y)`. If this is huge, `EM(π*||π_ref)` is large and BoN may need impractically large N.  
  3. **Win-rate vs. N sweep**: Run BoN with increasing N on a fixed set of prompts, compare win-rate against the comparator. Look for peak then decay (reward hacking) vs. monotonic increase. If decay occurs, implement EM-regularized BoN with `M` set to the N where decay starts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Is there a unifying paradigm that captures both expected-reward and win-rate objectives, clarifying when BoN is optimal versus when more sophisticated methods are necessary?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors explicitly ask in Section 7: "First, we may ask if there is a unifying paradigm that captures both expected-reward and win-rate objectives as special cases, allowing us to better understand under what conditions (optimally tuned) BoN is optimal and when more sophisticated algorithms are necessary."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper shows BoN is optimal for win-rate but suboptimal for expected reward; a unified view would reconcile these discrepancies and guide the choice of objective.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derivation of a general objective (perhaps an f-divergence-reward trade-off) that recovers both settings, with proofs that BoN is optimal within the unified framework under appropriate constraints.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the theoretical guarantees for BoN and EM-regularized BoN be extended to adaptive or interactive inference-time schemes, where the reference model may be queried sequentially based on previous samples?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 7 states: "while we have focused on the sample-and-evaluate framework ... it would be interesting to understand if similar statistical and computational guarantees can be achieved in more general frameworks that allow for adaptive sampling or other forms of interaction with the reference model."
- **Why unresolved:** Adaptive sampling could potentially reduce sample complexity or improve robustness, but the analysis used for non-adaptive parallel sampling relies on i.i.d. draws and may not apply directly.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Lower and upper bounds for the adaptive setting showing comparable win-rate regret, perhaps via concentration inequalities for stochastic processes or reductions to best-arm identification.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can we go beyond the information-theoretic EM-regularization to leverage learned representations and avoid being overly conservative in practice?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors mention in the discussion: "our proposed variant of BoN eliminates reward-hacking in theory, it may still be too conservative in practice due to its reliance on information-theoretic guarantees; moving beyond such guarantees and leveraging the rich structure that learned representations provide ... remains an interesting question."
- **Why unresolved:** EM-regularization only caps likelihood ratios, ignoring possible latent structure that could distinguish "good" high-score regions from "bad" ones; better regularization could improve empirical performance while maintaining monotonicity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** New algorithms that incorporate representation-based metrics (e.g., feature diversity, clustering) and empirical validation on standard benchmarks showing improved win-rate without reward hacking.

## Limitations
- Assumptions may not hold: non-atomic reference distribution, small pairwise error ε_pw on reference samples, and small EM-divergence EM(π*||π_ref) are often violated in practice (discrete outputs, reward model errors under distribution shift, strong comparators with large EM).
- Theoretical tuning guidance (N ∝ √(EM/ε_pw)) is worst-case and may be overly conservative; empirical performance can be better.
- The hardness result for chi²-regularized BoN relies on a constructed instance; its practical impact remains uncertain. Win-rate itself is an imperfect proxy for true alignment.

## Confidence
- Mathematical correctness of theorems: High
- Practical optimality claims and superiority of EM-regularization: Medium
- How often key assumptions hold in real-world LLM alignment: Low
