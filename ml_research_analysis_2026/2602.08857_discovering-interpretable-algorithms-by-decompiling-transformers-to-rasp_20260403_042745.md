# Discovering Interpretable Algorithms by Decompiling Transformers to RASP
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08857
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08857
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Length‑generalizing Transformers decompose into short, interpretable RASP‑like programs (≥0.9 match accuracy), while non‑generalizing models resist simplification.

## Executive Summary
This paper asks whether Transformers that generalize to longer sequences internally compute simple, interpretable algorithms as suggested by RASP theory. The authors introduce a decompilation pipeline: first, a trained GPT‑2 style Transformer (with linearized layer norms) is reparameterized into an equivalent Decompiled RASP (D‑RASP) program that mirrors every computation step. Second, causally irrelevant components are pruned and the remaining operations replaced with library primitives via causal interventions, yielding a minimal subprogram. Tested on small Transformers trained on algorithmic tasks (Most Frequent, Unique Copy, Sort, Count) and formal languages (Dyck), models that length‑generalize almost always produce very short (often three‑line), human‑readable programs with match accuracy ≥0.9. In contrast, models that fail to length‑generalize retain large, dense programs and resist simplification. The recovered programs exhibit known motifs such as induction heads and histogram majority, providing the strongest evidence to date that length‑generalization correlates with the implementation of simple RASP‑like algorithms.

## Method Summary
The method begins by assuming the Linear Layer Norm Assumption (LLNA): layer‑norm operations can be approximated by linear transformations with negligible impact on behavior. Under this assumption, the trained Transformer is faithfully reparameterized into a D‑RASP program whose primitives (select, aggregate, element‑wise ops) match the model’s operations and expose intermediate states. Next, a multi‑stage causal pruning procedure removes edges and components that are not necessary to reproduce the model’s outputs. Using trainable masks and optimal ablation, the algorithm searches a Pareto frontier of programs trading off sparsity and match accuracy (threshold ≥0.9). Finally, the remaining operations—attention selectors, projection matrices, and MLP outputs—are matched against a library of known primitives (e.g., “k==q−1” for previous‑token attention). Successful matches are replaced by symbolic primitives; failures are inspected manually. The output is a minimal, interpretable subprogram that exactly replicates the original model on a held‑out set of inputs.

## Key Results
- Length‑generalizing Transformers consistently decompile to very short programs (≤5 lines) with match accuracy ≥0.9 across tasks like Most Frequent, Unique Copy, Sort, Count, and several Dyck languages.
- Models that do not length‑generalize resist pruning; they retain dense graphs and fail to achieve high match accuracy with few components, yielding large, uninterpretable programs.
- The recovered programs reveal recognizable algorithmic motifs: induction heads for copy tasks, histogram‑based majority for frequency tasks, and threshold‑based MLPs for bounded‑recursive Dyck patterns.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Decompilation via Re‑parameterization and Simplification
Length‑generalizing Transformers are hypothesized to compute simple, RASP‑like algorithms. The pipeline first translates the model into an exact D‑RASP equivalent (under LLNA), exposing all intermediate computations. Then it prunes causally irrelevant components and substitutes operations with symbolic primitives, extracting a minimal sufficient subprogram. Evidence: Sections 3.1–3.2 describe the reparameterization and simplification steps; empirical success across multiple tasks supports the claim. Breakdown occurs if LLNA fails or if the model lacks sparse, program‑like structure.

### Mechanism 2: Causal Pruning for Program Sparsification
Many length‑generalizing models exhibit sparse computational graphs, where numerous components are redundant. The pruning stage employs a multi‑stage (component, path, QK) mask‑learning procedure with optimal ablation to remove non‑essential edges while preserving behavior (match accuracy ≥0.9). Evidence: Abstract and Section 3.2 outline the approach; Appendix F provides algorithmic details. Pruning fails when high accuracy requires keeping nearly all edges—a signature of entangled, non‑programmatic computation.

### Mechanism 3: Primitive Matching for Interpretability
The pruned subprogram often consists of operations that correspond to known building blocks (e.g., attention pattern “k==q−1”, histogram aggregation). The method systematically tests whether each component matches a primitive from a library by measuring match accuracy. Success indicates the model uses an interpretable motif. Evidence: Section 3.2.2 and figures showing matched primitives; failures occur for novel or library‑missing functions, prompting manual analysis.

## Foundational Learning
**RASP Languages:** The Restricted Access Sequence Processing (RASP) paradigm defines a programming language whose primitives (select, aggregate, element‑wise ops) mirror Transformer computation. **Causal Interventions / Circuit Discovery:** These techniques establish which model components causally influence outputs by ablating parts and measuring changes. They are essential for pruning to identify a sufficient subprogram without mistaking correlation for causation. *Quick check:* What’s the difference between correlation and causation in circuit discovery, and how do causal interventions establish directionality?

**Length Generalization:** The ability to handle sequences longer than those seen during training. The paper links this capability to the presence of simple, robust programs. *Quick check:* Why might a model fail to length‑generalize even if it implements a correct short program? **Element‑wise Operation Decomposition:** MLPs are decomposed into single‑input functions to enable matching with primitives. This simplifies high‑dimensional, opaque operations into interpretable pieces. *Quick check:* How does splitting an MLP into multiple single‑input operations help with interpretability? **Attention Pattern Primitives:** Predefined templates for attention selectors (e.g., “k==q−1”, positional queries) correspond to common algorithmic patterns. Recognizing these allows automatic labeling of heads like induction heads. *Quick check:* Give an example of an attention pattern primitive and describe what algorithmic pattern it implements.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Transformer Engine -> D‑RASP Translator -> Causal Pruner -> Primitive Matcher -> Visualization Suite and Evaluation Tracker

**Critical path**  
1. Load trained Transformer; apply LLNA linearization (learn γ′ per layer norm) to produce an approximately equivalent linear model.  
2. Perform stage‑1 pruning (component‑level) with mask learning; keep Pareto points achieving match accuracy ≥0.9.  
3. Perform stage‑2 and stage‑3 pruning (path‑ and QK‑level) and optionally split MLPs into single‑input functions.  
4. For each remaining MLP: if single‑input, search the primitive library; on success, propagate linear transformations downstream; on failure, collect activation statistics for manual inspection.  
5. For select/project matrices, attempt primitive replacement or regularize toward sparsity/integer values.  
6. Output final D‑RASP program and visualizations (attention selectors, variable activations, etc.).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Completeness vs. Interpretability:* Full D‑RASP translation is exponentially large; pruning sacrifices completeness for a sparse, interpretable subprogram.  
- *Freshness vs. Stability:* Primitive matching prefers linear no‑op operations to keep programs simple, accepting slight accuracy loss; the 0.9 threshold balances faithfulness and simplicity.  
- *Generality vs. Primitive Coverage:* The library covers common patterns but may miss model‑specific innovations; unmatched parts require manual analysis.  
- *Layer Norm Handling:* LLNA enables a linear translation; if violated, retaining non‑linear layer norms yields denser, less interpretable programs.

**Failure signatures**  
1. LLNA violation – linearizing layer norms drops match accuracy below 0.9.  
2. Sparse pruning impossible – Pareto frontier requires keeping many (>50%) edges to maintain high accuracy.  
3. No primitive match – remaining operations cannot be explained by library primitives.  
4. Stage cascading failure – early stage fails to reach match threshold, blocking later stages.  
5. Attention overfitting – pruned program matches original only on training‑length inputs, not on longer test sequences.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Binary Majority:** 1‑layer, 1‑head model; expected decompilation to a 3‑line program (aggregate frequencies, identity projection). Validates basic pipeline.  
2. **Unique Copy:** 2‑layer, 1‑head model; should recover induction head pattern (previous‑token aggregation → token matching → output). Tests attention pattern matching and special‑token handling.  
3. **Dyck‑4:** 1‑layer, 2‑head model; requires histogram aggregation plus threshold MLP for bounded‑recursive pattern. Tests multi‑dimensional aggregation and per‑position thresholds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Does this scale to large language models?** The study uses small, single‑task Transformers. Whether multi‑task LLMs contain similarly decomposable sub‑algorithms is an open question.  
- **Is LLNA necessary?** Correlation between LLNA and decompilability is strong but not absolute; the causal role of the assumption remains unclear.  
- **Are all length‑generalizing models decompilable?** While correlation is high, it is unknown if there exist tasks where a Transformer length‑generalizes but the decompilation fails to produce a short program.  
- **Is the primitive library complete?** Some operations (e.g., the Sort MLP) do not match any existing primitive, suggesting the library may need extension for certain algorithms.  
- **Does the pruning procedure guarantee minimality?** The heuristic search for sparse subprograms may miss alternative equivalent circuits of smaller size; minimality is not formally proven.

## Limitations
- The Linear Layer Norm Assumption (LLNA) fails for some length‑generalizing models, preventing exact D‑RASP equivalence.
- The handcrafted primitive library may not capture all algorithmic motifs; unmatched components require manual interpretation.
- Experiments are limited to small Transformers (≤4 layers, ≤256 hidden dim) on synthetic tasks; scalability to large language models is unknown.
- The ≥0.9 match accuracy threshold might accept approximate programs that diverge on rare inputs.
- Decomposing MLPs into single‑input functions can lose cross‑feature interactions, potentially obscuring true computation.

## Confidence
- **Length‑generalization → simple RASP program:** Medium (consistent across 5 tasks and 17 languages but limited to small synthetic models).  
- **Decompilation pipeline yields faithful minimal programs:** Medium (LLNA exceptions, heuristic pruning, threshold sensitivity).  
- **Primitive matching recovers known motifs:** High (induction heads and histogram majority reliably matched).

## Next Checks
1. **Scale study:** Apply the pipeline to larger Transformers (≥12 layers, hidden dim ≥768) on algorithmic tasks (e.g., sorting, Dyck‑n with n=12) to test whether short programs still emerge and correlate with length‑generalization.  
2. **LLNA robustness test:** Replace layer norms with identity, linearized, or retain non‑linear forms across a suite of models; measure impact on match accuracy and program sparsity to quantify the assumption’s necessity and potential extensions.  
3. **Automatic primitive discovery:** Cluster unexplained MLP/attention matrices from many decompiled models; propose new primitives and re‑run matching to increase coverage, checking whether new primitives correspond to recognizable algorithmic building blocks.
