# Prompt replay: speeding up grpo with on-policy reuse of high-signal prompts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21177  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21177  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Prompt Replay reduces wasted rollouts and accelerates early learning in GRPO while maintaining on‑policy updates, but may plateau under aggressive reuse.

## Executive Summary
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is effective for reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, but suffers from high rollout cost and many prompts that yield zero‑variance (all correct or all incorrect) responses, leading to wasted computation. Prompt Replay introduces an overhead‑free online data selection method that reuses only prompts (not trajectories), thus preserving on‑policy optimization. It dynamically stores medium‑difficulty prompts based on pass rate and mixes them with fresh samples per batch, applying cooldown and reuse limits to balance speed and overfitting. Evaluations on Llama‑3.2‑3B and Qwen3‑8B with Dolci and Polaris math datasets show reductions in zero‑variance prompts, increased mean absolute advantage, and faster initial accuracy gains across six benchmarks. However, under aggressive hyperparameters the method plateaus and converges to baseline performance. The approach is most beneficial when rollouts are the primary bottleneck and tasks are difficult. Additionally, spurious reward effects observed on Qwen2.5‑Math models caution against using that family as a sole testbed for GRPO research.

## Method Summary
Prompt Replay augments the standard GRPO training loop with a replay buffer. After each GRPO step, the pass rate (fraction of correct responses) is computed for every prompt from the G rollouts used in that step. Prompts with pass rates within a predefined medium‑difficulty interval (e.g., [0.25,0.75]) become eligible for insertion into the buffer, which stores only the prompt string, its estimated pass rate, its last used step, and a usage count.

During training, each minibatch is constructed by mixing fresh dataset prompts with prompts sampled from the buffer. Buffer prompts are ranked by their absolute distance to a 0.5 pass rate (i.e., higher learning signal) and the top‑ε fraction is selected. When a buffer prompt is used, new responses are generated using the current old policy, ensuring that updates remain on‑policy. After the rollouts, buffer entries are updated: usage counters increment, cooldown periods are enforced, and prompts exceeding a maximum reuse count are evicted. This design eliminates the overhead of re‑generating old trajectories while preserving a distribution of medium‑difficulty, high‑signal prompts.

## Key Results
- Reduces the fraction of zero‑variance prompts and increases mean absolute advantage per batch.
- Achieves faster initial accuracy improvements on six math benchmarks compared to baseline GRPO.
- Under aggressive reuse (e.g., high buffer mixing fraction), performance plateaus and eventually matches baseline, highlighting a trade‑off between efficiency and overfitting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Prompt Replay reduces wasted rollouts and maintains on‑policy training by reusing only prompts (not trajectories), generating fresh responses each reuse.
- **Mechanism:** After each GRPO step, prompts with intermediate pass rates \(p \in [p_{\min}, p_{\max}]\) are stored in a buffer. Future batches mix fresh dataset prompts with buffer prompts (eligible after cooldown \(C\) and below max reuse \(R\)). For any sampled prompt (buffer or fresh), new rollouts are drawn from the current old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), preserving on‑policy updates.
- **Core assumption:** The pass rate estimated from current rollouts reliably indicates medium difficulty, and mixing buffer with fresh data prevents distribution collapse.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “an overhead‑free online data selection method … reuses prompts only (not trajectories)”; Section 4.1 describes buffer insertion and mixing.
- **Break condition:** Buffer depletes if most prompts become too easy/hard or hit max reuse without re‑qualifying; too short cooldown may cause overfitting.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Prioritizing prompts with pass rate closest to 0.5 maximizes per‑sample gradient signal, accelerating early learning.
- **Mechanism:** Within the replay buffer, eligible prompts are ranked by \(|p_\theta(x)-0.5|\) ascending; the top‑\(N\) are sampled preferentially. This enriches batches with prompts that maximize reward variance \(p(1-p)\), which bounds the expected policy improvement per step.
- **Core assumption:** Gradient signal (advantage magnitude) is the primary bottleneck; maximizing it per batch speeds convergence without harming stability due to the fresh‑prompt mixture.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “prioritize prompts closer to a pass rate of 0.5 … to maximize the advantage”; Section 4.2 derives gradient variance \(\propto p(1-p)\) and shows selecting smallest \(|p-0.5|\) maximizes signal.
- **Break condition:** As training advances, many medium‑difficulty prompts drift to \(p \approx 0\) or \(1\) and drop from buffer, reducing the pool of high‑signal prompts; deterministic top‑\(N\) may overfit to a small set.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** On‑policy preservation is achieved by generating new rollouts for reused prompts, avoiding off‑policy bias from replaying old trajectories.
- **Mechanism:** The replay buffer stores only prompt strings; when a buffer prompt is selected, \(G\) responses are generated using the current old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), exactly as for fresh prompts. This ensures responses are on‑policy with respect to the policy being updated.
- **Core assumption:** Storing and replaying old responses would introduce stale data that misrepresents the current policy’s behavior; generating fresh responses each time maintains the on‑policy guarantee.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “reuses prompts only (not trajectories)”; Section 4.1: “Prompt Replay is on‑policy: buffer entries store only prompts … and for each reused prompt we generate new completions using the current policy.”
- **Break condition:** If the old policy lags significantly behind the current policy (e.g., after many updates), the on‑policy assumption weakens, though this is inherent to GRPO’s snapshot scheme.

## Architecture Onboarding

#### Component Map
Replay Buffer -> Prompt Sampler -> GRPO Trainer -> Pass Rate Estimator -> Replay Buffer

#### Critical Path
1. Construct batch via sampler (mix fresh + buffer).
2. For each prompt, generate G rollouts from current old policy.
3. Compute pass rates; insert/update buffer for prompts with \(p \in [p_{\min}, p_{\max}]\).
4. For reused buffer prompts, increment usage, update \(last\_used\_step\); remove if usage \(\ge R\).
5. Perform GRPO update using batch (group-relative advantages).
6. Buffer fluctuates as prompts age out and fresh medium-difficulty prompts enter.

#### Design Tradeoffs
- **Prompt‑only storage:** Saves memory and ensures on‑policy responses, but discards response‑level information that could enable finer prioritization.
- **Deterministic top‑N sampling:** Guarantees high‑signal prompts each batch, but risks overfitting; stochastic priority sampling could mitigate.
- **Fixed hyperparameters:** Simple but sensitive; the paper found even \(C=2\) caused no overfitting on Qwen2.5‑Math due to spurious rewards, but may on other models; tuning likely needed per model/dataset.
- **Mixing fraction \(\epsilon\):** Higher \(\epsilon\) speeds early learning but may cause catastrophic forgetting if fresh prompts become too scarce.

#### Failure Signatures
- Buffer size consistently zero.
- Early plateau while baseline continues to improve.
- Entropy drops rapidly, indicating policy becoming deterministic.
- Zero‑variance prompt fraction remains high.
- Spurious accuracy spikes on Qwen2.5‑Math (indicating data leakage or reward inflation).

#### First 3 Experiments
1. **Unit test:** Mock prompts with fixed pass rates (0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9); verify buffer insertion only for \(p \in [0.25,0.75]\); simulate cooldown and reuse limits.
2. **Integration test:** Train a tiny model (1.5B) on 100 Dolci prompts for 10 steps; compare baseline GRPO vs Prompt Replay (\(\epsilon=0.5, C=5, R=3\)); measure rollouts wasted and early accuracy.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Ablate one parameter (e.g., \(\epsilon \in \{0.3,0.5,0.75,1.0\}\)) while fixing others; plot accuracy over time and buffer size to observe trade‑offs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Optimal hyperparameter configurations** – What are the optimal values for replay fraction \(\epsilon\), cooldown \(C\), max reuse \(R\), and pass‑rate bounds \(p_{\min}/p_{\max}\) across model‑dataset pairs? The authors only did limited sensitivity on cooldown; an extensive sweep is needed to balance reuse and overfitting.

2. **Spurious reward effects in Qwen2.5‑Math** – Why does this model family show performance gains even with random rewards? This phenomenon invalidates ablations on that testbed; the root cause is unknown and requires comparative analyses under random‑reward baselines and probing of internal representations.

3. **Scaling and integration with other techniques** – How does Prompt Replay behave under large‑scale training runs and in combination with other sample‑efficiency methods (e.g., offline difficulty filtering, trajectory replay)? The paper’s small compute budget leaves these interactions unexplored.

4. **Statistical variance of GRPO outcomes** – What is the variance across random seeds for GRPO variants, and does Prompt Replay affect it? The work used single seeds, so repeat experiments (≥5 seeds) are needed to assess robustness and significance of reported gains.

## Limitations
- Exact GRPO hyperparameters (e.g., \(\eta\) cap, clip \(\epsilon\), KL penalty) are not reported, hindering faithful replication.
- Prompt templates and token‑limit handling per model are unspecified, potentially altering pass‑rate distributions and buffer dynamics.
- Spurious reward effects in Qwen2.5‑Math models undermine the validity of some ablation results and limit generalizability.

## Confidence
- Reduces zero‑variance prompts & speeds early learning: **High** (empirically consistent).
- Converges to baseline when aggressive: **Medium** (observed, limited sweeps).
- Efficient mainly when rollouts bottleneck & tasks hard: **Low** (inferred).
- On‑policy via prompt‑only reuse: **High** (design clear).
- Pass‑rate 0.5 prioritization maximizes signal: **Medium** (theory + partial evidence).
- Break conditions (depletion, overfitting): **Low** (speculative).
- Spurious reward effects on Qwen2.5‑Math: **High** (empirically observed).
