# Failure of contextual invariance in gender inference with large language models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23485  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23485  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Minimal contextual priming induces large, systematic shifts in gender pronoun generation, undermining contextual invariance and altering the influence of cultural stereotypes.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether large language models (LLMs) maintain consistent outputs when presented with semantically equivalent formulations—a property known as contextual invariance. Using a pronoun selection task derived from WinoPron Schemas, the authors introduce a brief priming sentence containing a gendered pronoun that refers to a different entity. Across five instruction‑tuned LLMs (GPT, Phi‑4, Gemma, Llama 3.1, Qwen), the priming context causes substantial and systematic changes in the probabilities of selecting male versus female pronouns. The strength of cultural gender stereotypes, which strongly predict pronoun choices in decontextualized settings, is significantly reduced or disappears when context is added; instead, the gender of the priming pronoun becomes the dominant predictor. Moreover, a Contextuality‑by‑Default analysis reveals that a considerable fraction (19–52%) of template pairs exhibit irreducible context dependence that cannot be explained by simple repetition or marginal effects. These findings challenge the reliability of decontextualized evaluations for bias assessment and raise concerns about deploying LLMs in high‑stakes scenarios where contextual stability is expected.

## Method Summary
The authors adapted the WinoPron Schemas into a controlled pronoun selection task. Each template describes a situation requiring the model to choose between “he” and “she” to refer to a target entity. The experimental manipulation adds a minimal priming sentence placed before the template; this priming sentence contains a gendered pronoun (“he” or “she”) that refers to a different, unrelated entity and is theoretically uninformative for the coreference decision. Five state‑of‑the‑art instruction‑tuned LLMs were evaluated. The models’ output distributions for the target pronoun were compared between primed and unprimed conditions. Systematic shifts were measured via probability differences, mutual information between priming gender and output, and a novel Contextuality‑by‑Default metric that quantifies irreducible context dependence across template pairs.

## Key Results
- Adding a minimal priming context produces large, consistent shifts in pronoun generation probabilities across all tested models.
- Correlations between pronoun choices and cultural gender stereotypes, evident in decontextualized settings, weaken or vanish when priming is present; the priming pronoun’s gender becomes the most informative predictor.
- The Contextuality‑by‑Default analysis shows irreducible context dependence in 19–52% of template pairs, indicating that the effect is not reducible to simple repetition or marginal probability artifacts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The priming pronoun’s gender acts as a strong contextual cue that is rapidly integrated through the model’s self‑attention mechanisms. Because LLMs are trained to predict the next token based on the entire preceding sequence, even a single gendered pronoun can transiently activate gender‑related features in the representation space. This activation biases the attention pattern and consequently the output distribution for the subsequent pronoun slot, overriding the more stable stereotype‑based priors that dominate in decontextualized settings. The irreducibility revealed by Contextuality‑by‑Default suggests that the effect is not merely surface‑level repetition but reflects deep, non‑linear interactions within the transformer layers.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Tokens -> Token Embedding -> Positional Encoding -> Transformer Blocks (Self‑Attention + Feed‑Forward) -> Layer Normalization -> Output Logits -> Softmax

**Critical path:**  
The priming sentence and the main template are concatenated into a single input sequence. During forward propagation, self‑attention allows every token to attend to all others, propagating gender information from the priming pronoun throughout the representation. When the model reaches the target pronoun position, the hidden state is influenced by this propagated signal, thereby biasing the output distribution.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Context window size:* Larger windows increase the potential for priming effects but also computational cost.  
- *Model capacity:* Bigger models may integrate context more subtly, sometimes reducing or amplifying priming.  
- *Training objective:* Next‑token prediction inherently emphasizes local context, which can make models sensitive to nearby cues like priming pronouns.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. Pronoun gender flips to match priming gender even when stereotypical gender strongly contradicts.  
2. High mutual information between priming gender and target output across diverse templates.  
3. Inconsistent predictions for semantically equivalent templates differing only in the presence or gender of the prime.

**First experiments:**  
1. **Baseline measurement:** Collect pronoun selection probabilities for unprimed templates to establish stereotype strength.  
2. **Priming manipulation:** Introduce a priming sentence with either male or female pronoun; compare probabilities against baseline.  
3. **Irreducibility test:** Apply the Contextuality‑by‑Default metric to quantify the fraction of template pairs where context dependence cannot be explained by marginal or repetition effects.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly identify any open questions. However, the findings suggest several avenues for future research, including extending the paradigm to base models and other architectures, incorporating non-binary and cross-linguistic gender categories, and theoretically characterizing the neural mechanisms underlying irreducible context dependence.

## Limitations
- The evaluation is limited to five instruction‑tuned LLMs; results may not generalize to base models, other architectures, or different scales.  
- The priming contexts are minimal and artificial; ecological validity in natural, complex discourse remains uncertain.  
- The focus on binary gender excludes non‑binary and culturally specific gender categories, limiting inclusivity and universality.

## Confidence
Confidence assessments reflect the robustness and generalizability of the findings:
- **Context induces large systematic shifts**: **Medium–High** – Strong consistency across five diverse models and templates.
- **Stereotype correlations weaken; priming dominates**: **Medium** – Effect is clear but may vary with template design and model training data.
- **Irreducible context dependence (19–52%)**: **Medium** – Novel metric with variability; requires validation on broader datasets.
- **Decontextualized evaluations are unreliable**: **Low** – Inferential conclusion based on comparative data; direct causal evidence across all tasks is limited.

## Next Checks
1. **Broad model & task replication:** Test the same paradigm across a wider range of LLMs (including base models and diverse architectures) and additional gender inference tasks (e.g., coreference resolution, stereotype benchmarks).
2. **Non‑binary and cross‑linguistic extension:** Incorporate non‑binary pronouns and stimuli in multiple languages to assess the universality and inclusivity of the context effects.
3. **Human–LLM comparative study:** Run the identical experiment with human participants to quantify relative context sensitivity; a substantial divergence would indicate atypical LLM behavior.
