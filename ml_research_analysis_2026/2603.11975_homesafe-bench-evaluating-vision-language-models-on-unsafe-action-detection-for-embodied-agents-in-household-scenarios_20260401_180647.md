# HomeSafe-Bench: Evaluating Vision-Language Models on Unsafe Action Detection for Embodied Agents in Household Scenarios

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11975  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11975  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** HD-Guard achieves 86.5% hazard detection rate and 49.3% effective warning precision, operates 2× faster than Qwen3-Omni, and improves weighted safety score by 38% over standalone FastBrain.

## Executive Summary
Evaluating vision‑language models (VLMs) for dynamic unsafe‑action detection in household embodied agents remains an open challenge. Existing benchmarks are static or conflate planning with perception. This paper introduces **HomeSafe‑Bench**, a video benchmark with 438 diverse, physically plausible household scenarios, each annotated with fine‑grained temporal keyframes and multi‑dimensional hazard metadata. To address the latency‑accuracy trade‑off, the authors propose **HD‑Guard**, a hierarchical dual‑brain safety monitor. A lightweight FastBrain (MiniCPM‑o‑4.5) runs at high frequency (up to 5 FPS) to screen for hazards; ambiguous cases trigger an asynchronous SlowBrain (Qwen3‑VL‑30B) for deep multimodal reasoning. On HomeSafe‑Bench, HD‑Guard achieves state‑of‑the‑art detection rates while significantly reducing reasoning deficits and visual omissions, demonstrating a practical path toward real‑time safety monitoring in embodied agents.

## Method Summary
**HomeSafe‑Bench** construction combines physical simulation (BEHAVIOR‑1K) for accurate dynamics with advanced video generation (Veo‑3.1) for visual realism. LLM‑driven scenario design, human verification, and rigorous multi‑annotation (temporal phases, hazard categories, severity, reasoning difficulty) ensure quality. **HD‑Guard** uses two pre‑trained VLMs without fine‑tuning: FastBrain classifies each frame (green/yellow/red) at up to 5 FPS; SlowBrain receives a short temporal window when FastBrain flags “yellow”, applies chain‑of‑thought reasoning, and returns a binary hazard verdict. A controller dynamically adjusts FastBrain’s sampling rate, enforces hierarchical priority (FastBrain “red” overrides SlowBrain), and issues final stop commands. The system balances inference efficiency with detection accuracy, targeting real‑time robot deployment.

## Key Results
- Hazard Detection Rate (HDR): **86.5%**  
- Effective Warning Precision (EWP): **49.3%**  
- Reasoning deficits reduced to **0%** on hardest (D3) tasks; visual omissions near zero.  
- Latency **2× faster** than Qwen3‑Omni; Weighted Safety Score **+38%** over standalone FastBrain.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hierarchical Dual‑Brain Architecture
**Claim:** A hierarchical dual‑brain architecture (HD‑Guard) achieves a superior trade‑off between real‑time latency and safety detection accuracy for household embodied agents.  
**Mechanism:** FastBrain (lightweight, high‑frequency) performs coarse classification on every frame; only uncertain frames (“yellow”) trigger the heavyweight SlowBrain for deep reasoning. A priority rule lets FastBrain’s “red” alerts override the pending SlowBrain verdict, guaranteeing immediate response.  
**Core assumption:** Most frames in a typical household are nominally safe; only a small fraction require expensive analysis.  
**Evidence:** Abstract states the design “effectively balancing inference efficiency with detection accuracy”; Section 4 formalizes the decision function; Section 5.4 shows 2× speed vs. Qwen‑Omni and +38% WSS vs. FastBrain alone.  
**Break condition:** If hazardous events become frequent enough that most frames demand SlowBrain analysis, latency converges to the slow model’s inference time, erasing efficiency gains. Also, a high false‑negative rate on “red” events would defeat the override safeguard.

### Mechanism 2: Hybrid Benchmark Generation
**Claim:** Combining physical simulation (BEHAVIOR‑1K) with advanced video generation (Veo‑3.1) yields a diverse, physically plausible, and visually realistic benchmark (HomeSafe‑Bench) for evaluating embodied safety.  
**Mechanism:** LLM‑driven hazard cause collection creates textual scenario seeds. The simulator generates videos with ground‑truth dynamics; Veo‑3.1 produces high‑fidelity visual variants. Human verification removes physically implausible outputs. Multi‑dimensional annotations (temporal keyframes, hazard classes, severity, reasoning difficulty) are added.  
**Core assumption:** Simulation ensures accurate cause‑effect relationships; video generation provides realistic visual stress‑tests; together they cover the long‑tail of household risks.  
**Evidence:** Section 3.2 describes the hybrid pipeline; Section 3.1 uses NEISS data for long‑tail coverage; Section 3.4 reports high inter‑annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ, Lin’s CCC).  
**Break condition:** If generated videos consistently violate the simulator’s physics, or the simulator’s action space is too narrow for complex manipulations, the benchmark’s real‑world validity collapses.

### Mechanism 3: Fine‑Grained Temporal Scoring
**Claim:** Temporal annotations with a dynamic scoring function (Optimal/Sub‑optimal/Irreversible windows) produce a more meaningful evaluation than simple frame‑level accuracy.  
**Mechanism:** Each video contains five key timestamps: Intent Onset, Point‑of‑No‑Return (PNR), Intervention Deadline (PNR‑200 ms), Impact, Action End. Predictions receive phase‑dependent scores (e.g., 100 for Optimal window, penalized for later detections), encouraging early intervention.  
**Core assumption:** The utility of a warning declines steeply as it approaches impact; early warnings are vastly more valuable.  
**Evidence:** Section 3.3 defines the scoring; Figure 3 visualizes the timeline; Section 5.1.2 introduces Weighted Safety Score (WSS).  
**Break condition:** If the defined phases (e.g., 200 ms margin) do not match a real robot’s reaction‑stopping constant, a high WSS does not guarantee accident prevention in deployment.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map** (plain text):  
  FastBrain (MiniCPM‑o‑4.5) → Controller; SlowBrain (Qwen3‑VL‑30B) → Controller; Controller → Agent; Controller also adjusts FastBrain sampling rate.

- **Critical path** (per‑frame inference loop):  
  1. FastBrain processes current frame `vt` at sampling rate `γt`.  
  2. If FastBrain outputs “red”, Controller immediately issues halt (settled).  
  3. If “yellow”, Controller raises `γt+1` to 5 FPS and asynchronously queues the latest window `Wt` for SlowBrain.  
  4. While SlowBrain computes, FastBrain continues; any subsequent “red” triggers an override halt.  
  5. If SlowBrain returns “DANGER” before override, Controller halts.  
  6. If SlowBrain returns “SAFE”, Controller returns to low‑rate (`γt+1` = 1 FPS) monitoring.

- **Design tradeoffs**
  - *Sampling frequency:* Ablation shows 5 FPS optimal; lower misses transient hazards, higher adds noise/compute without WSS gain.
  - *SlowBrain context window:* Limited to ~2 seconds for efficiency; this causes failures where hazard cause lies earlier (Case II).
  - *Model selection:* FastBrain requires high FPS and reasonable accuracy (MiniCPM‑o‑4.5); SlowBrain prioritizes reasoning strength (Qwen3‑VL‑30B).
  - *Override policy:* FastBrain override prevents late warnings but increases false positives if sensitivity is too high.

- **Failure signatures**
  - Persistent “yellow” without “red”: FastBrain too conservative, flooding SlowBrain.
  - “Red” after “impact”: FastBrain’s perception bottleneck (visual omission) or insufficient sampling.
  - SlowBrain returns “SAFE” for clear hazard: reasoning deficit, especially on D3 or complex physics.
  - High false‑alarm (“premature”) warnings: FastBrain over‑reacts to proximity without trajectory understanding.

- **First experiments**
  1. **Benchmark validation:** Run proposed VLM (or HD‑Guard variant) on full HomeSafe‑Bench test set; compute HDR, EWP, PDA, WSS; compare against Table 1 baselines.
  2. **Sampling frequency ablation:** Fix component models; vary `γ_high` (1, 2, 5, 10 FPS); plot WSS vs. Avg. Latency to identify optimal FPS.
  3. **Fine‑grained error analysis:** Using annotations (`reasoning_difficulty`, `danger_type`), stratify predictions by D1/D2/D3 and C1‑C4; categorize failures into Format, Over‑reaction, Lag, Visual Omission, Reasoning Deficit (per Appendix C) to diagnose dominant failure modes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**1. How can SlowBrain incorporate longer temporal context without exceeding latency budget?**  
- *Basis:* Section 5.5 notes current restriction to last two frames for real‑time efficiency; Case 2 failure linked to this.  
- *Why unresolved:* Longer context likely improves causal reasoning but naively increases inference time, possibly breaking latency constraints.  
- *What evidence would resolve:* Systematic experiments varying context window size (e.g., 2, 5, 10 frames) measuring both WSS and end‑to‑end latency; perhaps efficient attention or keyframe selection.

**2. What causes vision‑language models to over‑react (premature warnings), and how can it be mitigated?**  
- *Basis:* Section 5.2 states “Top‑performing models often suffer from severe ‘over‑reaction’”; HD‑Guard’s 25.1% rate is better but not eliminated.  
- *Why unresolved:* The paper does not analyze why over‑reaction occurs (training bias, prompt misinterpretation, inherent uncertainty).  
- *What evidence would resolve:* Ablations on prompt phrasing, analysis of model confidence vs. ground truth, probing training data for spurious correlations, or architectural changes (temperature scaling, ensembling).

**3. Can a theoretical model link sampling frequency, hazard dynamics, and detection performance to predict optimal FPS for different hazard categories?**  
- *Basis:* Ablation (Section 5.6) shows non‑monotonic relationship: 5 FPS optimal, 10 FPS worse; speculation “excessively dense context frames introduce redundant visual information” lacks formal analysis.  
- *Why unresolved:* Without a model, setting sampling rates remains empirical and may not generalize to faster hazards.  
- *What evidence would resolve:* Collect hazard velocity distributions from HomeSafe‑Bench; fit performance curves; develop adaptive sampling that adjusts FPS based on estimated time‑to‑impact.

**4. To what extent do system‑level engineering latencies (data transfer, hardware inference) limit real‑world safety, and can algorithmic improvements compensate?**  
- *Basis:* Case III shows correct hazard detection at t=2.33 s but physical stop at t=3.89 s (1.56 s engineering latency) missed intervention deadline (impact at t=2.60 s). Paper notes safety compromised if total latency exceeds time‑to‑impact.  
- *Why unresolved:* Current evaluations assume negligible engineering latency; real deployments may have unpredictable delays.  
- *What evidence would resolve:* Measure end‑to‑end latency on actual robotic hardware; characterize latency distribution; test whether faster models or optimized pipelines can reduce latency below critical thresholds for different hazard speeds.

**5. Does HD‑Guard generalize to non‑household environments (e.g., factories, outdoors)?**  
- *Basis:* Paper focuses on six household areas; HD‑Guard’s design (e.g., FastBrain’s “caution” rules) is tailored to household contexts (“near hazardous zones (stove, microwave, sink)”). Generalization untested.  
- *Why unresolved:* Household hazards have specific object affordances and layouts; industrial/outdoor risks involve machinery, vehicles, or natural elements requiring different reasoning.  
- *What evidence would resolve:* Construct a new benchmark for another domain; port HD‑Guard with minimal adaptation; evaluate transfer performance and identify domain‑specific adjustments.

## Limitations
- Output format and hardware inference details are missing, risking inconsistent reproduction.
- Dataset split not predefined; performance metrics may vary with random splits.
- Video generation pipeline (Veo‑3.1) details are opaque, limiting full authenticity assessment.
- The architecture uses fixed pre‑trained backbones without fine‑tuning, potentially capping performance on household‑specific hazards.
