# Online Bayesian Imbalanced Learning with Bregman-Calibrated Deep Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08128
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08128
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** OBIL achieves superior F1-scores under severe class-prior shifts (e.g., Mammography at 4× shift: OBIL F1=0.68 vs. BBSE 0.61) while remaining competitive when train/test distributions match, provided models are well-calibrated (ECE<0.05).

## Executive Summary
Class imbalance causes standard classifiers to neglect minority classes, and existing solutions fail when deployment class priors shift, necessitating costly retraining. This paper introduces Online Bayesian Imbalanced Learning (OBIL), a method that trains deep networks with Bregman-divergence losses to produce well-calibrated posterior probability estimates. From these, prior-invariant likelihood ratios are derived. An online algorithm then uses unlabeled test data to adjust the decision threshold, tracking changing priors without retraining. Theoretically, OBIL achieves O(√T log T) regret against an oracle with perfect prior knowledge. Empirically, on benchmarks with extreme imbalance (up to 578.9:1) and simulated prior shifts, OBIL demonstrates robust and superior performance, particularly under severe distribution shifts, while maintaining competitiveness when train and test distributions align.

## Method Summary
OBIL operates in two phases. Offline, it trains an ensemble of deep networks using a Bregman loss (e.g., squared error) on balanced subsets obtained via undersampling. This ensures each network's outputs are calibrated posterior estimates. The ensemble fuses these estimates to obtain a robust likelihood ratio estimator, q̂L(x). Online, given an unlabeled test stream, OBIL maintains an exponential moving average (EMA) of two signals: the likelihood-ratio-based class probability and the frequency of confident predictions (q̂L>1). This EMA estimate, ̂P₁(t), tracks the shifting deployment prior. The Bayes-optimal threshold Q(t) is then computed as Q = Q_C · (1−̂P₁(t))/̂P₁(t), where Q_C is the known cost ratio. A new example x is classified as class 1 if q̂L(x) > Q(t). The algorithm includes stability clamps to prevent erratic updates from noisy pseudo-labels.

## Key Results
- OBIL achieves O(√T log T) regret against an omniscient oracle under smooth prior drift and bounded likelihood error.
- On the Mammography dataset with a 4× prior shift from training, OBIL achieves an F1-score of 0.68, outperforming BBSE (0.61) and other baselines.
- Under extreme imbalance ratios (up to 578.9:1) and various simulated prior shifts, OBIL consistently delivers superior or competitive F1-scores, while methods like BBSE and LA degrade significantly.
- The approach requires well-calibrated models (ECE<0.05) and tolerates moderate covariate shift, but performance declines under severe feature distribution changes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Bregman Losses Yield Calibrated Posteriors
- **Claim:** Training deep networks with Bregman-divergence losses produces calibrated posterior probability estimates.
- **Mechanism:** For a Bregman loss C(t,o) with generator g, the condition ∂C/∂o = -g(o)(t-o) holds. At optimality, this forces the network output f_θ*(x) to equal E[t|x] = 2P(y=1|x)-1. The posterior is then recovered as (f_θ(x)+1)/2.
- **Core Assumption:** The network architecture has sufficient capacity to represent the true posterior mapping (∃θ*: f_θ*(x)=2P(y=1|x)-1 ∀x), and training converges to this optimum.
- **Evidence Anchors:**
  - [Abstract] "deep networks trained with such losses produce posterior probability estimates."
  - [Section IV.B] Theorem 7 and Corollary 8 formally establish the Bregman calibration property independent of architecture under a capacity condition.
  - [Corpus] Neighboring work on imbalanced learning emphasizes loss design but does not provide the Bregman calibration link; this paper supplies the theoretical connection.
- **Break Condition:** Insufficient network capacity or suboptimal training leads to calibration error (ECE>0.05), which amplifies likelihood ratio errors per Theorem 13.

### Mechanism 2: Likelihood Ratios are Prior-Invariant
- **Claim:** Likelihood ratios derived from calibrated posteriors are invariant to changes in class priors and misclassification costs.
- **Mechanism:** By Bayes' theorem, the estimated likelihood ratio q̃L(x) = ̃Q_P · ̃P(y=1|x)/(1-̃P(y=1|x)) under any associated problem (with prior ̃Q_P). The prior factor cancels, leaving the true likelihood ratio regardless of the training imbalance.
- **Core Assumption:** Resampling (e.g., undersampling) preserves the original class-conditional densities (likelihood-preserving), and posterior estimates are accurate.
- **Evidence Anchors:**
  - [Abstract] "likelihood ratios are derived... prior-invariant."
  - [Section IV.A] Theorem 5 proves transferability of likelihood ratio estimates from associated problems.
  - [Section IV.E] Proposition 16 shows uniform undersampling preserves likelihood ratios in expectation; Proposition 17 warns synthetic oversampling (SMOTE) distorts them.
  - [Corpus] Kernel-density ratio methods estimate likelihood ratios differently and do not leverage Bregman calibration or online adaptation.
- **Break Condition:** Likelihood-distorting resampling (e.g., SMOTE) or miscalibration introduces bias; large calibration errors propagate to likelihood ratios (Theorem 13).

### Mechanism 3: Online Threshold Adaptation Tracks Prior Shifts
- **Claim:** OBIL’s online threshold adaptation tracks deployment prior shifts without labeled data, achieving O(√T log T) regret.
- **Mechanism:** Given a fixed likelihood ratio estimator, the Bayes threshold is Q = Q_C · (1-P₁)/P₁. P₁ is estimated online via an EMA of a combined signal: β·(q̂L/(q̂L+1)) + (1-β)·freq(q̂L>1). The frequency term breaks the feedback loop; a stability clamp limits per-step changes.
- **Core Assumption:** Likelihood ratio error is uniformly bounded (ϵ_LR); true prior varies smoothly (|P₁(t+1)-P₁(t)|≤δ); confident pseudo-labels (γ>0.5) are reliable.
- **Evidence Anchors:**
  - [Abstract] "online algorithm adjusts the decision threshold using unlabeled data."
  - [Section V.B] Algorithm 2 details the update rules and stability mechanisms.
  - [Section VI] Theorem 27 provides the regret bound under smooth drift and a margin condition (A1–A4).
  - [Corpus] Batch shift-correction methods like BBSE exist, but online threshold adaptation with regret guarantees is novel; the corpus does not address this setting.
- **Break Condition:** Calibration error >0.15 makes pseudo-labels too noisy, causing divergence (Figure 6b). Rapid prior drift (δ>0.1) exceeds EMA tracking speed. Covariate shift invalidates the likelihood ratio estimator (Figure 6a).

## Foundational Learning
- **Bayesian Decision Theory:** Bayes-optimal rule, likelihood ratios, prior invariance.  
  *Why needed:* Decouples likelihood estimation from prior, enabling threshold adaptation without retraining.  
  *Quick check:* Given qL(x)=5 and imbalance ratio QP=3, what is the optimal threshold for symmetric costs? (Answer: Q=QP=3; predict class 1 iff qL>3.)
- **Proper Scoring Rules & Bregman Divergences:** Loss functions that yield probability calibration.  
  *Why needed:* Guarantees network outputs estimate true posteriors, allowing valid likelihood ratio extraction.  
  *Quick check:* Why does squared error loss satisfy the Bregman condition? (∂/∂o (½(t-o)²)= -(t-o), so g(o)=1>0.)
- **Class-Imbalance Learning:** Problem definition, evaluation metrics (F1, G-Mean), standard remedies (resampling, cost-sensitive).  
  *Why needed:* To contrast OBIL with existing methods and understand its advantages under shift.  
  *Quick check:* What is the key limitation of SMOTE when the test prior differs from training? (It does not adjust the decision threshold; posterior estimates remain biased by the rebalanced training distribution.)
- **Distribution Shift (Label Shift):** Class-prior changes while class-conditional densities stay fixed.  
  *Why needed:* OBIL specifically addresses label shift; distinguishing it from covariate shift clarifies applicability.  
  *Quick check:* How might you detect label shift using only unlabeled test data? (Test class proportions change significantly while feature distributions remain stable.)
- **Online Learning & Regret Analysis:** Sequential performance relative to an omniscient oracle; sublinear regret as a desideratum.  
  *Why needed:* OBIL’s theoretical guarantee is expressed in regret; understanding this quantifies adaptation quality.  
  *Quick check:* What does O(√T log T) regret imply about average per-step error as T grows? (It tends to zero, so the algorithm asymptotically matches the oracle.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Offline: Balanced subset (undersampling) -> Bregman-trained network (tanh output) -> Ensemble (MC dropout uncertainty) -> Geometric average log-likelihoods -> q̂L(x).  
  Online: Unlabeled x -> q̂L(x) -> Combined signal (β·LR-prob + (1-β)·freq) -> EMA (̂P₁) -> Threshold Q = Q_C·(1-̂P₁)/̂P₁ -> Decision (q̂L > Q).
- **Critical path:**  
  1. For each ensemble member, create a balanced dataset by undersampling majority class to target ˜QP.  
  2. Train network to convergence; verify calibration (ECE<0.05) on a held-out balanced set.  
  3. Deploy: for each x, compute ensemble likelihood ratio; update prior estimate via Algorithm 2; compute threshold; predict.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Ensemble size K: Larger K reduces variance but increases inference cost; diminishing returns beyond K=5 (Table VII).  
  - Loss function: Squared error gives exact Bregman calibration; cross-entropy + temperature scaling is more practical but may leave small calibration gap.  
  - EMA learning rate α: Small α (0.01) robust to noise but slow tracking; large α (0.1) faster but noisier; optimal around 0.01–0.1 (Table IX).  
  - Confidence threshold γ: Higher γ (0.9) yields more reliable pseudo-labels but fewer updates; lower γ increases update frequency but may introduce bias.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Calibration error >0.10 on holdout set → OBIL likely underperforms BBSE; apply temperature scaling or revert to batch methods.  
  - Sudden drop in F1 when test prior deviates → check if prior drift rate δ exceeds ~0.01; if so, consider sliding-window estimation or retraining.  
  - Diverging threshold updates despite stability clamp → reduce α or ∆max; verify likelihood ratio estimator quality.  
  - Performance degradation under feature noise → covariate shift may be present; OBIL is not designed for that regime (Figure 6a).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Calibration verification: On a balanced validation set, plot reliability diagram and compute ECE. Must be <0.05 for safe deployment; if not, recalibrate or retrain with squared error.  
  2. Offline shift simulation: Resample test set to extreme imbalance ratios (e.g., 4× training IR). Compare F1 against baselines (BBSE, LA, RUSBoost). Expect OBIL’s advantage to grow with shift severity (Figure 1).  
  3. Online adaptation test: Simulate a data stream with a step change in prior at t=500. Plot estimated prior vs. true prior and cumulative F1 vs. oracle. Should track with lag ~1/α; if lag is excessive, increase α cautiously.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Multi-class Extension:** How does the multi-class extension of OBIL perform empirically, especially with modern deep architectures? The theoretical framework transfers, but real-world performance on imbalanced multi-class datasets with deep models remains unvalidated.
- **Robustness to Combined Shifts:** How robust is OBIL to simultaneous label shift and covariate shift in real cross-site deployments? The paper isolated label shift via resampling; complex, non-linear covariate shifts (e.g., different imaging devices) are not characterized.
- **Calibration Automation:** Can we automate or improve the calibration verification process for deep networks to ensure OBIL's reliability without requiring a separate holdout calibration set? The dependency on a pre-collected calibration set is a practical bottleneck for streaming/online settings.
- **Abrupt/Adversarial Shifts:** How does OBIL perform under abrupt or adversarial prior shifts that violate the smooth drift assumption (|ΔP|≤δ)? The behavior under sustained high-rate drift, oscillating priors, or malicious manipulation is unknown.
- **Single-Model Efficiency:** Can the ensemble-based likelihood ratio estimator be replaced by a single, computationally efficient model (e.g., Bayesian neural network) without significant performance loss? The ensemble may be over-provisioned; trade-offs between ensemble diversity and per-model calibration are not analyzed.

## Limitations
- The entire mechanism hinges on achieving high calibration quality (ECE<0.05); the exact tolerance threshold is not rigorously quantified, though ECE>0.15 appears catastrophic.
- The online guarantee assumes a static, accurate offline likelihood ratio estimator; covariate shift or concept drift in P(x|y) invalidates this assumption with no proposed mitigation.
- The method is restricted to likelihood-preserving resampling (undersampling); synthetic oversampling (SMOTE) breaks prior invariance, limiting applicability where data retention is critical.
- The theoretical regret bound assumes smooth prior drift; abrupt, volatile, or periodic prior changes may exceed the EMA's tracking capability.
- The empirical evaluation is limited to binary classification with simulated prior shifts; generalizability to multi-class, extreme covariate shift, or real-world non-stationary streams is uncertain.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence:**  
  - Bregman losses produce calibrated posteriors (theoretical Theorem 7, Corollary 8; standard practice).  
  - Likelihood ratios from calibrated posteriors are prior-invariant (Theorem 5, Proposition 16).  
- **Medium Confidence:**  
  - Online tracking and O(√T log T) regret bound hold under specific assumptions (smooth drift, bounded error); violations in practice are not fully characterized.  
  - Empirical superiority under severe shift is demonstrated on selected benchmarks but may not generalize to all domains or multi-class settings.  
- **Low Confidence:**  
  - Robustness to simultaneous label and covariate shift; interactions are not theoretically or empirically analyzed.  
  - Practical deployment without a dedicated calibration set; online calibration methods are unexplored.
