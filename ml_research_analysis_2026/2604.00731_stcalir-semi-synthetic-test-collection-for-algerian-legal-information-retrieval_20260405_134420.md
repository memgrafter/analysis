# STCALIR: Semi-Synthetic Test Collection for Algerian Legal Information Retrieval

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00731
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00731
- **Reference count:** 12
- **Primary result:** Achieves retrieval effectiveness and system ranking concordance comparable to fully human-annotated collections while reducing annotation effort by 99%.

## Executive Summary
STCALIR addresses the scarcity and high cost of manual relevance judgments for test collections in low-resource domains like Algerian legal information retrieval. It proposes a semi-synthetic framework that uses a multi-stage neural retrieval pipeline—bi-encoders for high-recall candidate pooling and cross-encoders for precision re-ranking—to concentrate relevant documents into a top-10 set. Human annotation is restricted to this small set, drastically reducing effort. Validated on the Mr. TyDi benchmark, the resulting semi-synthetic judgments yield strong system ranking concordance (Kendall’s τ=0.89, Spearman’s ρ=0.92) and high hit rate at 10 (≈0.785), demonstrating a viable path for building test collections in resource-constrained settings.

## Method Summary
The STCALIR pipeline consists of five phases. First, a legal corpus is built from Algerian Official Gazette PDFs via OCR (Arabic), manual correction, and hybrid chunking (article-based + semantic). Second, human experts generate topics using a web interface. Third, candidate retrieval uses BM25 plus six bi-encoders fine-tuned on mMARCO in a zero-shot setting; each retrieves top-1000 passages per topic, and the lists are fused via Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) to form a pooled set of 1000. Fourth, five cross-encoders score the pooled passages, and their RRF-fused rankings yield a final top-10 per topic. Fifth, human annotators validate only this top-10 via a web interface to produce the final qrels. This design shifts the annotation burden from examining thousands of candidates per topic to just ten.

## Key Results
- Reduced human annotation workload by **99%** relative to traditional exhaustive judging.
- Achieved **Hit@10 ≈ 0.785** on semi-synthetic judgments, comparable to fully human collections.
- Maintained high system ranking concordance: **Kendall’s τ = 0.89**, **Spearman’s ρ = 0.92** against human judgments from Mr. TyDi.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: High-recall candidate pooling via multi-bi-encoder fusion
Multiple bi-encoders (fine-tuned on mMARCO) retrieve top-1000 candidates per topic in zero-shot. Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) merges these lists, increasing coverage from ~70% (single bi-encoder) to ~80% (pooled). This exploits complementary ranking behaviors to capture a high proportion of truly relevant passages early. The core assumption is that bi-encoders generalize sufficiently to the Algerian legal domain to achieve non-trivial recall (~0.7) without in-domain fine-tuning. Evidence includes the reported average Recall@1000 of ≈0.7 and pooling gains shown in Table 2 and Section 3.3. A break condition occurs if zero-shot Recall@1000 falls below ~0.5, as too many relevant documents would be missing from the initial pool.

### Mechanism 2: Cross-encoder re-ranking elevates relevant documents to top positions
Cross-encoders (fine-tuned on mMARCO) perform fine-grained pairwise scoring on the pooled candidate set. RRF fusion of multiple cross-encoders further refines the ranking, pushing relevant documents into the top-10. This leverages the higher accuracy of cross-encoders for relevance estimation. The core assumption is that cross-encoders maintain strong zero-shot precision (MRR@10 ≈0.24) on the target domain. Evidence shows Hit@10 increasing from 0.654 (pooled bi-encoders) to 0.766 (single cross-encoder) to 0.785 (fused cross-encoders) in Table 2, with at least two truly relevant documents on average in the top-10. If cross-encoder MRR@10 on the pool is <0.15, the top-10 set will contain too few relevant documents, eroding the effort reduction.

### Mechanism 3: Human validation restricted to top-10 preserves system rankings
The final relevance judgment task is collapsed from evaluating up to 1000 candidates per topic to only the top-10 from the fused cross-encoder output. The semi-synthetic Hit@10 ≈0.785 indicates a high concentration of relevant documents in this small set. System-level rankings derived from these sparse judgments remain highly correlated with rankings from full human judgments because IR system comparisons are more sensitive to relative ordering than to exhaustive per-document labels. Evidence includes the 99% reduction in annotation effort alongside Kendall’s τ=0.89 and Spearman’s ρ=0.92. If semi-synthetic Hit@10 falls below ~0.6, the top-10 likely contains too few relevant documents across topics, causing system rankings to diverge significantly (τ/ρ dropping below ~0.7).

## Foundational Learning
- **Cranfield Paradigm (Test Collection Structure):** Understanding the paradigm (static corpus, topics, reusable judgments) is essential to grasp why reducing judgments to top-10 is a valid trade-off and why system ranking correlation is the primary validity metric. *Quick check:* Why does STCALIR prioritize system ranking correlation (Kendall’s τ) over absolute metrics like nDCG when validating its semi-synthetic judgments?
- **Bi-encoder vs. Cross-encoder Architecture Trade-off:** The pipeline’s efficiency hinges on using cheap, high-recall bi-encoders for first-stage retrieval and expensive, high-precision cross-encoders for reranking a small set. This division of labor is key to the 99% reduction goal. *Quick check:* What is the computational complexity difference between bi-encoders (dual-encoder) and cross-encoders (pairwise) that makes this two-stage setup necessary?
- **Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF):** RRF is the default fusion method at both pooling and reranking stages. It is non-parametric, doesn’t require score calibration, and improves coverage and ranking quality. *Quick check:* How does RRF’s score aggregation differ from simple score averaging or Borda count, and why might it be more robust to scale differences between model outputs?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
 Corpus Preprocessing (PDF → OCR → correction → hybrid chunking) → Topic Creation (human via web UI) → Candidate Retrieval (BM25 + 6 bi-encoders → top-1000 each → RRF merge → pooled 1000) → Re-ranking (5 cross-encoders score pool → RRF fuse → top-10) → Human Validation (annotate top-10 via web UI → final qrel)  
  Tools: Pyserini, FAISS, Hugging Face models, custom web UI, RRF implementation.
- **Critical path:** Corpus preprocessing (OCR + chunking) → Topic creation (human bottleneck) → Bi-encoder retrieval (compute-intensive) → Cross-encoder reranking (compute-intensive but on smaller set) → Human validation (smallest time cost).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Open-source models vs. proprietary LLMs: Avoids cost/hallucination but may limit absolute performance.  
  - Cutoff depth (k=10): Drastically reduces effort but relies on cross-encoders to concentrate all relevant docs in top ranks; if relevance is long-tail, some will be missed.  
  - Zero-shot transfer: Eliminates in-domain fine-tuning cost but depends on model generalization (GR metric).
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Bi-encoder recall too low: Check Recall@1000; if <0.6, candidate pool likely missing many relevant docs.  
  - Cross-encoder precision drop: If MRR@10 on pooled candidates is <0.4, top-10 will be noisy.  
  - OCR quality issues: High variance in passage length or nonsensical text chunks indicates poor extraction, corrupting the corpus.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Bi-encoder recall validation:** On a small labeled subset (e.g., 50 Mr. TyDi topics), compute Recall@1000 for each bi-encoder and their RRF fusion. Confirm pooled recall ≥0.75 before full pipeline run.  
  2. **Pooling efficacy test:** For the same subset, compare Hit@10 of the RRF-pooled (1000 docs) vs. BM25-only pool. Expect ≥10% absolute improvement.  
  3. **Cutoff sensitivity curve:** Run full pipeline to get top-k rankings for k=5,10,20,50,100. Plot Hit@k vs. effort reduction (1-k/1000). Identify the "knee" point where Hit@k starts to drop sharply; validate that k=10 is before this knee.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How do OCR errors in the corpus construction phase impact the effectiveness of semi-synthetic relevance judgments?** The framework acknowledges OCR as a dependency but does not quantify error propagation. Resolving this requires controlled experiments injecting synthetic OCR errors into a clean legal corpus, measuring degradation in STCALIR's Hit@10 and Kendall's τ compared to error-free baselines.
- **To what extent can lightweight domain adaptation (e.g., LoRA) improve the bi-encoder and cross-encoder models' performance?** The current pipeline uses models fine-tuned only on mMARCO; potential benefits of legal-specific fine-tuning remain unmeasured. Evidence would come from applying LoRA to the models using a small set of Algerian legal documents, then re-running the pipeline and comparing metrics with the non-adapted version.
- **Does the STCALIR framework generalize to other low-resource legal domains?** Validation is limited to Mr. TyDi and a new Algerian Arabic legal corpus. Domain-specific design choices (e.g., article-based chunking) may not transfer. Resolution requires replicating STCALIR on at least two other legal corpora (e.g., Turkish or French legal texts) and comparing semi-synthetic vs. human judgment concordance.
- **Is Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) the optimal method for merging multi-system retrieval outputs?** RRF is used without comparison to alternatives (e.g., Borda count, weighted score fusion). Evidence would compare RRF with 2–3 alternative fusion techniques using identical retrieval systems, evaluating candidate pool recall@1000 and final Hit@10.
- **Does the semi-synthetic labeling process introduce systematic biases in system rankings?** High Kendall's τ indicates ranking agreement but does not guarantee absence of consistent over/under-ranking for specific system families. Evidence would analyze per-system score residuals (semi-synthetic minus human MRR@10), stratifying by model architecture (e.g., bi-encoder-based vs. BM25), to identify significant bias patterns.

## Limitations
- **Domain mismatch risk:** Zero-shot transfer from English (mMARCO) to Arabic legal may yield uneven recall; average Recall@1000 ≈0.8 could mask topics with very low recall.
- **Long-tail relevance gap:** Hit@10 ≈0.785 implies ~21% of topics have no relevant document in the top-10, potentially causing unstable rankings for those queries.
- **Corpus quality dependency:** OCR and chunking errors in Algerian legal PDFs are not quantified; noisy passages may harm retrieval but impact is unmeasured.

## Confidence
- **High-recall pooling (bi-encoder RRF):** Medium – paper reports pooled Recall@1000 ≈0.8, but no per-topic distribution or failure analysis.
- **Cross-encoder precision:** Medium – improvement to Hit@10 0.785 is shown, but absolute MRR@10 ≈0.24 is modest; zero-shot precision may vary.
- **Top-10 ranking preservation:** Medium-High – τ=0.89, ρ=0.92 on one benchmark are strong, but the small system pool (n=11) limits statistical robustness.

## Next Checks
1. **Recall distribution analysis:** On a held-out topic sample (n≥50), compute Recall@1000 for the RRF-pooled bi-encoders. If >20% of topics have Recall<0.5, the pooling claim fails for a substantial subset.
2. **Cross-encoder precision on pool:** For the same sample, compute MRR@10 and Hit@10 of the fused cross-encoder ranking. If MRR@10 < 0.4, re-ranking may not concentrate relevance sufficiently.
3. **Ranking correlation vs. k sensitivity:** Vary k (5, 10, 20, 50, 100) and compute Kendall’s τ/Spearman’s ρ against full human qrels. If correlation drops sharply below k=15 (e.g., τ<0.8), the 99% effort reduction margin is thinner than claimed.
