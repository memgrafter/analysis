# The Geometry of Alignment Collapse: When Fine-Tuning Breaks Safety

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15799
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15799
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Fine-tuning aligned language models on benign tasks unpredictably breaks safety guardrails due to geometric instability in gradient descent; alignment concentrates in low‑dimensional subspaces with sharp curvature, and curvature coupling causes a quartic scaling law of alignment loss.

## Executive Summary
The paper reveals that the fragility of alignment under fine‑tuning is an intrinsic geometric property of gradient descent, not merely a data‑curation bug. Alignment capabilities are concentrated in a low‑dimensional subspace of the model’s parameters with high curvature (as measured by the Fisher Information Matrix). Although initial fine‑tuning gradients may appear orthogonal to this subspace, the curvature of the fine‑tuning loss induces a second‑order acceleration that steers the optimization trajectory into the sensitive region. This leads to a theoretically derived quartic scaling law for alignment loss. The authors propose the Alignment Instability Condition (AIC) and an empirical Overlap Score that strongly correlates with safety degradation across multiple models and datasets, providing a practical diagnostic for pre‑deployment risk assessment.

## Method Summary
The reproduction plan centers on two main empirical validations. First, compute the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) for a base model on 100 safe reference samples (e.g., BeaverTails). For each transformer block module, gradients are accumulated, flattened, and projected via a fixed Rademacher random matrix (dimension 4096) to obtain a low‑rank approximation of the FIM. Eigenvalue decay is plotted to confirm the low‑rank structure. Second, fine‑tune the base model on a suite of datasets (benign, seemingly benign, harmful) using full fine‑tuning and LoRA. After training, the weight change per module is projected onto the same subspace and used to compute an Overlap Score (OS) — a quadratic form measuring alignment with the high‑curvature subspace. The OS values are correlated with harmfulness scores produced by Gemini‑2.5‑Flash on 520 AdvBench queries. The expectation is a clear separation: harmful and seemingly benign fine‑tune runs yield high OS, while benign runs keep OS low.

## Key Results
- Alignment‑sensitive parameters reside in a low‑dimensional subspace with sharply decaying FIM eigenvalues, confirming a spiked curvature spectrum.
- Curvature coupling causes gradients initially orthogonal to the safety subspace to drift into it quadratically over time, leading to a quartic growth of alignment loss.
- The Overlap Score successfully predicts safety degradation for full fine‑tuning, distinguishing benign from harmful or semi‑benign datasets across LLaMA and Qwen models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Alignment‑sensitive parameters form a low‑dimensional subspace with sharp curvature, making them uniquely fragile.  
**Mechanism:** The Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) for alignment skills exhibits a spiked spectrum; a few top eigenvectors (subspace \(M_i\)) capture most of the curvature. Movement along these directions causes disproportionate alignment loss (quadratic in projection size), while orthogonal directions are locally benign.  
**Core assumption:** Local quadratic approximation of alignment utility holds within KL trust regions.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: "...alignment concentrates in low‑dimensional subspaces with sharp curvature..."  
- Section 3.4: Definition of alignment sensitivity subspaces as top‑\(d\) eigenvectors of \(F_i(\theta^*)\).  
- Corpus: Related work on curvature‑aware methods supports the role of curvature.  
**Break condition:** If the FIM spectrum is flat or the KL trust region is violated, the quadratic approximation fails.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Initial orthogonality between fine‑tuning gradients and the alignment subspace is structurally unstable due to second‑order curvature effects.  
**Mechanism:** Even if \(\|P_i g(\theta^*)\| \approx 0\), the directional derivative \(\nabla g(\theta^*) g(\theta^*)\) — shaped by the fine‑tuning loss curvature — induces acceleration *into* \(M_i\). This "curvature coupling" bends the gradient flow trajectory, causing quadratic growth in the projection \(\|P_i(\theta(t)-\theta^*)\| = \Omega(\gamma t^2)\).  
**Core assumption:** The fine‑tuning loss is twice differentiable; the Alignment Instability Condition (AIC) jointly holds.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: "...curvature of the fine‑tuning loss generates second‑order acceleration that systematically steers trajectories..."  
- Theorem 6.2 proves the quadratic drift bound under AIC's curvature coupling condition.  
- Corpus: Geometric perspectives on loss landscapes support the idea of curved trajectories.  
**Break condition:** Curvature coupling \(\gamma \approx 0\) or early exit from the local quadratic region prevents drift.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Alignment degradation follows a quartic scaling law with training time (\(t^4\)), explaining rapid, sudden failures.  
**Mechanism:** Combining quadratic loss from entering \(M_i\) (\(\Delta u_i = \Omega(\lambda \delta^2)\)) with quadratic drift into \(M_i\) (\(\delta = \Omega(\gamma t^2)\)) yields \(\Delta u_i = \Omega(\lambda \gamma^2 t^4)\). Small \(\gamma\) compounds via fourth power, causing late but sharp collapse.  
**Core assumption:** AIC holds; trajectory remains in the local quadratic regime long enough for the \(t^4\) term to dominate.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: "Our main result establishes a quartic scaling law..."  
- Corollary 6.3 derives \(\Delta u_i = \Omega(\lambda \gamma^2 t^4)\).  
- Corpus: Scaling laws are common, but this specific quartic form is novel.  
**Break condition:** Rapid subspace rotation (Appendix B) or too large step size truncates the quartic regime.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept:** Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) as a Local Curvature Metric  
**Why needed:** The FIM \(F_i(\theta^*)\) defines the alignment sensitivity subspace \(M_i\) (top eigenvectors) and dictates the local utility loss \(\Delta u_i \approx \frac{1}{2} \Delta\theta^\top F_i \Delta\theta\). Understanding its low‑rank structure is central to AIC.  
**Quick check:** If \(F_i(\theta^*)\) has eigenvalues \([100, 50, 0.1, 0.05, ...]\), what does this imply about the alignment‑critical subspace?  
**Answer:** Alignment is fragile only along the first 1‑2 dimensions; other directions are locally safe.

**Concept:** Gradient Flow Trajectory Expansion  
**Why needed:** To see why first‑order orthogonality fails, we must expand \(\theta(t)\) to second order: \(\theta(t)-\theta^* = -t g + \frac{t^2}{2} \nabla g \cdot g + O(t^3)\). The acceleration term \(\nabla g \cdot g\) is the source of curvature‑induced drift.  
**Quick check:** What does \(\nabla g(\theta^*) g(\theta^*)\) geometrically represent?  
**Answer:** The directional derivative of the gradient; it measures how the steepest‑descent direction rotates as we move, i.e., trajectory curvature.

**Concept:** Low‑Rank Adaptation and Parameter Subspaces  
**Why needed:** The paper connects representation‑space low‑rank gradients to weight‑space FIM low‑rank, justifying computation of the FIM via block‑wise projections.  
**Quick check:** If per‑sample gradients \(G_i = \delta_i v_i^\top\) have \(\delta_i\) confined to an \(r\)-dim subspace \(\mathcal{U}\), what is the maximum rank of the average gradient \(\bar{G}\)?  
**Answer:** At most \(r\), because each rank‑1 component lies in \(\mathcal{U} \otimes \mathbb{R}^d\), spanning a subspace of dimension \(\leq r \cdot d\).

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
FIM Estimator → Subspace Identifier → Overlap Score Calculator → Harmfulness Evaluator

### Critical Path
- **Pre‑train:** Compute \(F_i(\theta^*)\) for the base model once using 100 BeaverTails samples.
- **Fine‑tuning:** For each candidate dataset, apply full fine‑tuning or LoRA with given hyperparameters.
- **Post‑process:** For the final checkpoint, compute the weight change \(\Delta W\) per module, project onto the pre‑computed subspace, and calculate the Overlap Score (OS) per block.
- **Safety evaluation:** Generate responses on 520 AdvBench queries, judge with Gemini‑2.5‑Flash to obtain Harmfulness Score (HS).
- **Correlation analysis:** Relate OS to HS; high OS predicts degradation.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Full fine‑tuning vs. LoRA:** Full fine‑tuning shows clear OS‑HS correlation; LoRA introduces "intruder dimensions" that distort the pre‑trained \(M_i\). Recomputing \(F_i\) on the LoRA‑updated model is costly.
- **FIM approximation:** Block‑wise random projection trades accuracy for memory; the projection dimension (4096) must adequately capture the spectrum decay.
- **Single‑skill assumption:** Theory assumes one alignment skill \(S_i\); real models have many. OS should be aggregated across safety‑critical skills.

### Failure Signatures
1. **Flat eigenvalue spectrum:** No spiked eigenvalues → AIC Condition 1 fails → mechanism unlikely.
2. **Low OS but high harmfulness:** Could indicate rapid subspace rotation or that the fine‑tuning task directly modifies alignment (first‑order regime).
3. **Early‑layer OS high, late‑layer low:** Alignment‑sensitive subspaces may be layer‑specific; module‑level granularity needed.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Validate FIM low‑rank:** For each model (LLaMA, Qwen), plot sorted eigenvalues of each module’s FIM on safe data; expect rapid decay (Figure 2, Figure 5).
2. **Correlate OS with harmfulness:** Fine‑tune on curated datasets (benign, seemingly benign, harmful). Compute OS per block and HS; expect Seemingly Benign OS ≈ Harmful and Benign OS lower (Figure 3, Table 1).
3. **Test quartic prediction:** Train on a risky dataset for varying step counts \(t\). Plot alignment loss (e.g., refusal accuracy) vs. \(t\); early stage should follow a \(t^4\) curve before collapse.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can we efficiently compute or estimate the curvature coupling parameter \(\gamma\) for large language models to enable pre‑deployment risk assessment?  
**Basis:** The authors state that computing \(\nabla g(\theta^*)\) remains computationally prohibitive and call for tractable curvature estimation as critical future work (Section 7).  
**Why unresolved:** Current methods require expensive second‑order gradient information; without efficient estimation, predictive diagnostics remain theoretical.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A practical algorithm approximating \(\gamma\) with moderate compute, validated by correlation with safety degradation across diverse models and tasks.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Why does the geometric overlap score fail to predict alignment degradation under LoRA, and how can the theoretical framework be extended to parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning?  
**Basis:** Experiments show LoRA does not exhibit the same clear OS‑to‑HS correlation as full fine‑tuning, hypothesizing "intruder dimensions" that shift top eigenvectors (Section 7).  
**Why unresolved:** The theory assumes full parameter updates; LoRA’s low‑rank structure may alter Fisher geometry or AIC conditions. Understanding this gap is crucial as LoRA is widely used.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical analysis of how LoRA constraints modify AIC or curvature coupling, plus empirical validation with modified overlap metrics accounting for LoRA’s subspace structure.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Can we characterize fine‑tuning tasks by their geometric properties (e.g., Fisher curvature, gradient‑Hessian coupling) to predict which will cause alignment collapse?  
**Basis:** The Alignment Instability Condition provides a theoretical predicate but the paper does not offer a practical method to compute AIC parameters from a dataset (Section 8 suggests estimating \(\gamma\) before training).  
**Why unresolved:** Without translating task data into geometric metrics, the AIC remains theoretical. Task‑dependent variation in degradation is observed but not explained by simple data overlap.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A methodology that takes a fine‑tuning dataset and base model as input and outputs estimates of AIC parameters, validated by predicting degradation on held‑out tasks.

## Limitations
- **Random projection details:** Exact construction of the Rademacher matrix (dense, normalization, fixed seed) is unspecified; OS comparability depends on identical matrices.
- **Block aggregation:** How per‑module eigenvalues/OS are combined into block‑level metrics (simple average vs. parameter‑weighted) is unclear and could shape decay curves.
- **FIM gradient source:** Whether gradients for BeaverTails are taken on prompts only or full prompt+response, and which token’s loss, affects the captured curvature.
- **LoRA OS computation:** Ambiguous whether \(\Delta W\) is the merged effective weight change or the raw low‑rank update; this changes the meaning of OS.
- **Local regime validity:** The quadratic approximation and AIC may fail if training steps are large, learning rate high, or fine‑tuning task far from reference distribution.
