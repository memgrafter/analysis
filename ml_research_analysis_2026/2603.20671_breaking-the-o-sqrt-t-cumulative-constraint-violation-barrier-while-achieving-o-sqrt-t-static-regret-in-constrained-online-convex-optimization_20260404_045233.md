# Breaking the $O(\sqrt{T})$ Cumulative Constraint Violation Barrier while Achieving $O(\sqrt{T})$ Static Regret in Constrained Online Convex Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20671
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20671
- **Reference count:** 4
- **Primary result:** For dimension d=2, the simple algorithm of Vaze and Sinha [2025] (online gradient descent followed by projection onto the current constraint set) achieves O(√T) static regret and O(T^{1/3}) cumulative constraint violation (CCV), thus breaking the conjectured Ω(√T) lower bound on CCV for constrained OCO with time‑varying constraints.

## Executive Summary
Constrained online convex optimization (COCO) with time‑varying convex constraints asks for decisions that balance low regret while respecting constraints that change over time. A long‑standing open problem was whether one can simultaneously achieve O(√T) static regret and o(√T) cumulative constraint violation (CCV) for dimensions d≥2, given a conjectured Ω(√T) lower bound on CCV. This paper analyzes the algorithm of Vaze and Sinha [2025], which performs online gradient descent (OGD) followed by projection onto the current constraint set, specifically for d=2. By exploiting the geometry of nested convex sets, the authors show that each projection onto the new feasible set induces either a perimeter decrease or an area decrease proportional to the projection distance. A careful weighting of these two complementary metrics yields a total bound linear in the set diameter. Via Hölder’s inequality, this translates to CCV = O(T^{1/3}), while the static regret remains O(√T). Thus, for d=2, the algorithm breaks the believed barrier, achieving sub‑√T CCV while retaining optimal regret.

## Method Summary
The paper studies Algorithm 1 from Vaze and Sinha [2025] for COCO in ℝ². The environment consists of a bounded admissible set X (diameter D), a sequence of convex loss functions f_t (G‑Lipschitz), and convex constraint functions g_t (also G‑Lipschitz). The feasible set at time t is S_t = ⋂_{τ=1}^t {x: g_τ(x) ≤ 0}, which is nested: S_t ⊆ S_{t-1}. The algorithm: initialize x₁ ∈ X; for t=1,…,T: (i) play x_t, (ii) observe f_t, g_t, (iii) set S_t = S_{t-1} ∩ G_t, (iv) compute y_{t+1} = P_{S_{t-1}}(x_t - η_t ∇f_t(x_t)) with η_t = D/(G√t), (v) set x_{t+1} = P_{S_t}(y_{t+1}). The static regret is R_T = Σ_{t=1}^T f_t(x_t) - min_{x⋆∈X⋆} Σ_{t=1}^T f_t(x⋆); the CCV is V_T = Σ_{t=1}^T max(g_t(x_t),0). The analysis proceeds in two parts: (1) Regret: Standard OGD analysis with projection onto time‑varying sets shows R_T = O(√T). (2) CCV: For d=2, each projection onto S_t (from a point in S_{t-1}) causes a measurable decrease in either the perimeter or the area of the feasible set sequence. These decreases are lower bounded by functions of the projection distance ‖p_t‖ (where p_t is the vector from the pre‑projection point to its projection). By choosing a weight α = 2/D and summing, one obtains Σ max(δ_t, αΔ_t) ≤ (3π/2) D, which via Lemma 14 yields Σ ‖p_t‖^{3/2} ≤ (3/2) √(2π) D^{3/2}. Hölder’s inequality then gives Σ ‖p_t‖ = O(D T^{1/3}), so CCV ≤ G Σ ‖p_t‖ = O(T^{1/3}). The result holds for any sequence of convex constraints as long as the feasible sets remain non‑empty and nested.

## Key Results
- For d=2, the algorithm achieves static regret R_T = O(√T) and cumulative constraint violation V_T = O(T^{1/3}).
- This breaks the conjectured Ω(√T) lower bound on CCV for constrained OCO with time‑varying constraints (at least in two dimensions).
- The analysis hinges on geometric properties of nested convex sets in 2D, using perimeter and area decreases to bound projection distances.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Projection‑Induced Nested Set Shrinkage
Each projection onto a nested convex set S_t ⊆ S_{t-1} induces a non‑zero decrease in either the perimeter or the area of the feasible set sequence. When projecting a point x_t ∈ S_{t-1} onto S_t, the projection vector p_t = P_{S_t}(x_t) - x_t defines a supporting hyperplane for S_t. The triangle formed by x_t, a_t, and b_t (the intersection points of the supporting line with S_{t-1}) lies in S_{t-1} but outside S_t; its area provides a lower bound on the area decrease Δ_t (Lemma 12). The perimeter difference between the convex hull of (U_t ∪ {x_t}) and U_t = S_{t-1} ∩ H_t lower bounds the perimeter decrease δ_t (Lemma 13). Either Δ_t scales with ‖p_t‖ w_t or δ_t scales with √(‖p_t‖² + w_t²/4) - w_t/2, where w_t is the chord length along the supporting line. This dichotomy ensures that for any non‑zero projection, at least one metric decreases significantly. The argument relies critically on 2D geometry and the nesting property.

### Mechanism 2: Complementary Metric Weighting and Linearization
A weighted combination of perimeter and area decreases, with weight α = 2/D, yields a per‑step bound linear in ‖p_t‖^{3/2}, whose sum over T can be controlled via the initial perimeter and area. Lemma 14 shows max(δ_t, α Δ_t) ≥ ‖p_t‖^{3/2} / (√2 D) for α = 2/D. Summing over t and using that total perimeter decrease Σ δ_t ≤ π D and total area decrease Σ Δ_t ≤ π D²/4, we get Σ max(δ_t, α Δ_t) ≤ (3/2) π D. This gives Σ ‖p_t‖^{3/2} ≤ (3/2) √(2π) D^{3/2}. The weight α = 2/D is chosen so that the sum of the upper bounds Σ δ_t + α Σ Δ_t is linear in D, matching the scaling of the initial perimeter and area.

### Mechanism 3: Hölder’s Inequality Conversion
The sum of projection distances Σ ‖p_t‖ (which upper bounds CCV up to constant G) is controlled by Hölder’s inequality applied to the bound on Σ ‖p_t‖^{3/2}. From Mechanism 2, Σ ‖p_t‖^{3/2} ≤ C D^{3/2} with C = (3/2)√(2π). Hölder with exponents p=3/2, q=3 gives Σ ‖p_t‖ ≤ (Σ ‖p_t‖^{3/2})^{2/3} T^{1/3} ≤ C^{2/3} D T^{1/3}. Thus CCV ≤ G Σ ‖p_t‖ = O(D T^{1/3}). The linear dependence on D is a direct consequence of the 2D‑specific bound on Σ ‖p_t‖^{3/2}. In higher dimensions, analogous bounds using surface area and volume do not yield a linear term, explaining the restriction to d=2.

## Foundational Learning
- **Convex Sets and Projection:** Convexity ensures a unique nearest point for Euclidean projection, which enforces feasibility after each update. Quick check: For a given convex set (e.g., a square), compute the projection of an external point and verify it lies on the boundary and satisfies the normal condition.  
- **Online Gradient Descent (OGD):** The standard algorithm for online convex optimization; at each round a gradient step is taken in the direction of minimizing observed loss. Quick check: For a sequence of linear losses with bounded gradients, confirm that the regret of OGD with stepsize η_t = D/(G√t) is O(√T).  
- **Cumulative Constraint Violation (CCV):** The sum of constraint violations max(g_t(x_t),0); a sublinear CCV means the algorithm asymptotically respects constraints. Quick check: Compute CCV for a simple feasible algorithm (e.g., always play a feasible point) and verify it is zero.  
- **Nested Convex Sets:** The feasible sets S_t form a decreasing sequence due to intersection of constraints. This nesting is essential for the geometric decrease arguments. Quick check: For a sequence of convex constraints, verify that S_t ⊆ S_{t-1} for all t.  
- **Perimeter and Area in 2D Geometry:** The analysis uses decreases in the perimeter and area of the feasible set sequence to bound projection distances. Quick check: For a convex polygon, compute its perimeter and area; for a disk, use known formulas.  
- **Hölder’s Inequality:** A tool to convert L^{3/2} bounds into L^1 bounds. Quick check: Given a non‑negative sequence a_i, verify that Σ a_i ≤ (Σ a_i^{3/2})^{2/3} T^{1/3}.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Initialization: x₁ ∈ X, S₀ = X. For t = 1 to T: (i) play x_t, (ii) observe f_t, g_t, (iii) update S_t = S_{t-1} ∩ G_t, (iv) compute gradient ∇f_t(x_t), (v) compute z = x_t - η_t ∇f_t, (vi) compute y = P_{S_{t-1}}(z), (vii) compute x_{t+1} = P_{S_t}(y).  

**Critical path:**  
The per‑round operations have dependencies: S_{t-1} must be known before projecting z onto it; S_t must be computed (using g_t) before projecting y onto it. The gradient computation uses x_t. The projections dominate runtime; maintaining S_t as an explicit convex polygon (e.g., via incremental half‑plane intersection) allows O(m) updates where m is the number of constraints.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Projection cost vs. exactness*: Exact projection onto arbitrary convex sets in 2D can be done via quadratic programming or clipping a polygon; approximate projection could speed up but may violate feasibility guarantees.  
- *Step size schedule*: The decreasing stepsize η_t = D/(G√t) minimizes regret; a constant stepsize would increase regret but might still yield the same CCV bound? The CCV analysis does not depend on η_t, but in practice a large constant could cause large projection distances that stress numerical stability.  
- *Constraint representation*: Representing S_t as a polygon requires updating by intersecting with a new half‑plane each round; this is efficient for moderate T but grows linearly in the number of constraints. For very large T, one might need a compact representation (e.g., ellipsoid) at the cost of looser bounds.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Projection errors*: Numerical inaccuracies cause P_S(x) to lie outside S or not be the closest point; leads to infeasibility or incorrect CCV. Diagnostic: after projection, check that the point satisfies all constraints to within tolerance.  
- *Empty feasible set*: If constraints are adversarial and S_t becomes empty, the algorithm cannot project. Diagnostic: after updating S_t, verify non‑emptiness (e.g., by checking feasibility of a point).  
- *Unbounded gradients*: If the observed losses have gradients larger than the assumed G, the regret bound fails. Diagnostic: clip or normalize gradients; ensure data generation respects the Lipschitz assumption.  
- *Mis‑scaled diameter*: Overestimating D leads to a stepsize that is too small (increasing regret) or too large (causing instability). Diagnostic: scale data so that X has diameter 1, then set D accordingly.

**First experiments:**  
1. **Nested disks**: Let X be a disk of radius R. Use constraints g_t(x)=‖x‖−r_t ≤0 with r_t decreasing linearly from R to 0. Use linear losses f_t(x)=a_t·x with random unit vectors a_t. For T up to 10^5, compute CCV and regret. Plot CCV/T^{1/3} and regret/√T to verify convergence to constants.  
2. **Nested axis‑aligned squares**: X=[-1,1]^2. At each round, add a constraint that shrinks the square on one randomly chosen side (e.g., x₁ ≤ c_t with c_t decreasing). Ensure S_t remains a rectangle. Projection is trivial (clipping). Check scaling of CCV and regret; compare with theory.  
3. **Random half‑planes with incremental polygon**: Start with a large polygon (e.g., a square). At each round, generate a random half‑plane that cuts off a small corner of the current polygon, ensuring the polygon remains non‑empty and its diameter stays bounded. Maintain S_t as a polygon (using standard half‑plane intersection). Use linear losses with bounded gradients. Verify that CCV grows as T^{1/3} and that the observed constant matches the theoretical order of magnitude.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Can the O(T^{1/3}) CCV bound for Algorithm 1 in 2D be improved to O(1)? The authors conjecture that the CCV is O(1) for d=2. The current analysis sums projection distances without exploiting potential cancellations across the sequence. Resolving this would require an amortized analysis showing Σ ‖p_t‖ remains bounded as T→∞.  
- **Open Question 2:** Does there exist an algorithm achieving O(√T) static regret and o(√T) CCV for dimensions d≥3? The 2D proof uses perimeter and area metrics that lack direct analogs in higher dimensions; straightforward extensions yield worse bounds. An affirmative answer would need a new algorithmic or analytic approach for d≥3, or a lower bound ruling out such a trade‑off.  
- **Open Question 3:** What is the tight lower bound on CCV for O(√T) regret in constrained OCO with time‑varying constraints for d=2? The paper shows O(T^{1/3}) and conjectures O(1); the gap means the optimal T‑dependence is unknown. Prior lower bounds only give constant (Ω(√d)) in T. A matching lower bound of Ω(T^{1/3}) would confirm optimality of the current upper bound; an O(1) upper bound would resolve the conjecture.

## Limitations
- The result is restricted to d=2; the geometric mechanisms (perimeter and area decreases) rely on 2D properties and do not generalize obviously to higher dimensions. For d≥3, the CCV barrier remains unresolved.  
- The analysis assumes all feasible sets S_t are non‑empty, convex, nested, and of bounded diameter. Ensuring non‑emptiness throughout an adversarial sequence can be challenging in practice.  
- Constants in the bounds (e.g., the weight α=2/D) are not sharpened; the Hölder conversion may introduce slack. Empirical tightness of the O(T^{1/3}) bound is unknown.  
- The algorithm’s computational cost: projecting onto nested intersections of convex sets can be expensive for complex constraints; the paper does not address implementation efficiency or scalability to large T or many constraints.

## Confidence
- **Static regret O(√T):** Medium. OGD with projection typically yields O(√T) for convex losses; the double projection and time‑varying constraints require careful adaptation, but the extension is plausible.  
- **CCV O(T^{1/3}) for d=2:** Medium. The geometric lemmas (area/perimeter decreases) appear sound for nested convex sets in 2D, and the weighting scheme gives a linear bound in D. However, the argument’s heavy reliance on 2D geometry leaves room for subtle errors.  
- **Breaking the conjectured Ω(√T) CCV barrier:** Low. The conjectured lower bound might assume d≥3 or apply to all algorithms. Without reviewing the lower‑bound proof, we cannot confirm that this result contradicts it; it may simply show the conjecture fails for d=2 or for this specific algorithm class.
