# Rho-Perfect: Correlation Ceiling For Subjective Evaluation Datasets

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08552  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08552  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Rho-Perfect estimates the highest achievable correlation (ceiling) between a perfect model and human ratings from a single evaluation; its squared value approximates test-retest reliability and outperforms ICC on unbalanced data.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of quantifying the correlation ceiling in subjectively rated datasets – the maximum correlation achievable by any model given the inherent noise in human judgments. The authors introduce Rho-Perfect, an estimator derived under heteroscedastic noise assumptions that uses only the rating distribution from a single evaluation. Rho-Perfect decomposes the total variance of item mean ratings into a signal component (variance of the perfect predictor) and an expected conditional noise component. Squared Rho-Perfect is shown to approximate the test-retest correlation across several datasets, providing a practical upper bound for model performance. The method’s utility is demonstrated on the NISQA dataset, where it helps distinguish whether a model’s limitations stem from the model itself or from the reliability of the data.

## Method Summary
Rho-Perfect estimates the signal variance via the law of total variance:  
\( \text{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(Y|X)] + \text{Var}(\hat{Y}) \).  
Here \(Y\) is the vector of item mean ratings, and \(\hat{Y}\) is the output of a perfect latent predictor. The term \(\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(Y|X)]\) (noise variance) is estimated from the observed within-item rating variance \(s_i^2\) scaled by the number of ratings per item \(m_i\), assuming raters are conditionally independent given the item. The correlation ceiling is then \( \rho = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{Y}) / \text{Var}(Y)} \). The estimator requires at least three ratings per item for stable variance estimation. Validation is performed by simulating two independent evaluations (split-raters or split-ratings) and showing that \(\rho^2\) closely matches the empirical Pearson correlation between the two average-rating vectors, while traditional ICC(2,k) fails when rater counts per item are unbalanced.

## Key Results
- Squared Rho-Perfect (\(\rho^2\)) approximates test-retest correlation across four diverse datasets (BVCC, MovieLens, SOMOS, MERP), matching empirical split correlations within ~0.02.
- \(\rho\)-Perfect outperforms ICC(2,k) on datasets with unbalanced rater counts (e.g., MovieLens), where ICC overestimates reliability.
- Case study on NISQA shows Rho-Perfect can identify subsets where model performance is ceilinged by data reliability (small gap) versus where the model is deficient (large gap).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Variance decomposition for the ceiling**  
*Claim:* Rho-Perfect estimates the highest achievable model-human correlation by modeling the variance of the latent true item quality under heteroscedastic noise.  
*Mechanism:* Uses the law of total variance: \(\text{Var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(Y|X)]+\text{Var}(\hat{Y})\). The conditional expectation term \(\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(Y|X)]\) captures rater noise and is estimated as the weighted average of \(s_i^2/m_i\) across items, assuming conditional independence of raters given the item. The ceiling is \(\rho = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{Y})/\text{Var}(Y)}\).  
*Evidence:* Formal derivation (Sec. 2.1, Eq. 4–5); validation across BVCC, MovieLens, SOMOS, MERP; outperforms ICC.  
*Break condition:* If raters are not conditionally independent (e.g., group biases, sequential effects), the estimate becomes biased.

**Mechanism 2: Relating the ceiling to test-retest reliability**  
*Claim:* \(\rho^2\) approximates the Pearson correlation between two independent but similar subjective evaluations (test-retest reliability).  
*Mechanism:* Under the model \(Y_1 = \hat{Y} + \varepsilon_1\), \(Y_2 = \hat{Y} + \varepsilon_2\) with \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_1|X] = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_2|X] = 0\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2|X] \approx 0\), the covariance \(\text{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2) \approx \text{Var}(\hat{Y})\). Assuming equal marginal variances, \(\text{Corr}(Y_1,Y_2) \approx \text{Var}(\hat{Y})/\text{Var}(Y) = \rho^2\).  
*Evidence:* Empirically, \(\mathbb{E}[\text{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2|X)]\) is near zero across datasets and \(\rho^2\) matches split correlations (Table 1).  
*Break condition:* Systematic differences between evaluations (rater pools, instructions, scale) can make \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}]\) differ or introduce conditional covariance, breaking the approximation.

**Mechanism 3: Practical utility for model evaluation**  
*Claim:* Rho-Perfect distinguishes whether a model’s performance is limited by data reliability or by the model itself.  
*Mechanism:* For a dataset subset, compute a model’s correlation with human mean ratings (Model PCC) and the local \(\rho\). A large gap (Model PCC \(<< \rho\)) indicates room for model improvement; a small gap (Model PCC \(\approx \rho\)) suggests the model is near the reliability ceiling of that subset, implying further gains require more reliable data.  
*Evidence:* NISQA case study (Table 3) shows how \(\rho\) varies across distortion types and contextualizes DNSMOS Pro’s PCC drops.  
*Break condition:* If model errors correlate with rating noise (e.g., failures on high-disagreement items) or local \(\rho\) estimates are unstable (small \(n\) or \(m_i\)), the comparison becomes misleading.

## Foundational Learning
- **Heteroscedasticity in Noisy Ratings**  
  Why needed: Rho-Perfect handles datasets with varying numbers of ratings per item (\(m_i\)). The noise variance in an item’s mean rating is \(\sigma^2_{\text{rating}}/m_i\); homoscedasticity assumptions (fixed \(m_i\)) in ICC fail under such imbalance.  
  Quick check: Why does a dataset with 100 items rated by 5 people each differ from one with 5 items rated by 100 people each in reliability, even if \(\sigma^2_{\text{rating}}\) is identical?

- **Correlation Ceiling vs. Reliability**  
  Why needed: The correlation ceiling (\(\rho\)-Perfect) is the theoretical maximum correlation a perfect model can achieve with noisy human labels. Reliability (e.g., ICC) measures consistency between human evaluations. They are related (\(\rho^2\) ≈ test-retest), but \(\rho\) directly bounds model performance.  
  Quick check: If two human evaluations correlate at 0.8, what does a model correlation of 0.7 tell you about its performance relative to human consistency?

- **Estimator from a Single Evaluation**  
  Why needed: Most reliability metrics (ICC, test-retest) require multiple ratings per item from overlapping or identical rater sets. Rho-Perfect’s key advantage is needing only one evaluation’s rating distribution, making it usable on existing datasets without recollecting data.  
  Quick check: What two summary statistics per item are minimally required to compute Rho-Perfect, and how are they derived from raw ratings?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Dataset (ratings per item) → Preprocessing (compute per-item mean \(y_i\), sample variance \(s_i^2\), count \(m_i\)) → Compute \(\text{Var}(Y)\) (variance of \(y_i\) across items) and \(\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(Y|X)]\) (weighted average of \(s_i^2/m_i\)) → Compute \(\text{Var}(\hat{Y}) = \text{Var}(Y) - \mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(Y|X)]\) → Output \(\rho = \sqrt{\max(0, \text{Var}(\hat{Y})/\text{Var}(Y))}\)

**Critical path:** Calculation of \(\text{Var}(\hat{Y})\) is core; it requires accurate per-item variance estimates, which need at least 3 ratings per item. Subsequent operations (subtraction, division, square root) are trivial.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Accuracy vs sample size: With few ratings per item (\(m_i=3\)), \(s_i^2\) is noisy; small number of items (\(n<50\)) yields unstable \(\rho\).  
- Discrete scales: Coarse rating scales (e.g., 1–5 MOS) can bias variance estimates due to rounding; the method does not correct for this.  
- Numerical stability: \(\text{Var}(\hat{Y})\) may become negative due to estimation error; must clamp to non-negative before square root.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Negative \(\text{Var}(\hat{Y})\): indicates \(\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(Y|X)] > \text{Var}(Y)\). Caused by too few items, extremely low item variance, or violation of conditional independence.  
- \(\rho \approx 1\) with low \(\text{Var}(Y)\): may reflect an artificially narrow rating scale or ceiling/floor effects rather than true high signal.  
- High \(\rho\) but low empirical split correlation: suggests conditional covariance across evaluations is non-zero, likely due to shared rater biases.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Validation on Standard Datasets:** For BVCC, MovieLens, SOMOS, MERP, simulate two evaluations via split-raters (randomly partition raters into halves) and split-ratings (randomly split each item’s ratings). Compute average correlation between the two average-rating vectors and compare to \(\rho^2\); also compute \(\mathbb{E}[\text{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2|X)]\).  
2. **Comparison to ICC & Subsampling:** On the same datasets, compute ICC(2,k) with \(k\) = average number of ratings per item, and subsampling reliability (independent sampling of two rater subsets allowing overlap). Compare these to the true test-retest correlation from experiment 1.  
3. **Case Study on NISQA Subsets:** Apply \(\rho\)-Perfect to the full NISQA validation set and its four subsets (Bandpass, Clean, Bursty, etc.). Compute DNSMOS Pro’s PCC on each subset and interpret the gap between PCC and \(\rho\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How can \(\rho\)-Perfect be validated using real test-retest subjective evaluation datasets? The paper currently relies on simulations due to lack of publicly available doubly-annotated datasets. *Resolution:* Collect and release subjectively rated datasets with genuine repeated, independent annotations.  
**Open Question 2:** How does correlated rater bias (shared demographics, perceptual frameworks) affect the accuracy of \(\rho\)-Perfect estimates? The derivation assumes \(\mathbb{E}[\text{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2|X)] \approx 0\), which may be violated by structured rater similarities. *Resolution:* Controlled experiments with known rater correlation structures.  
**Open Question 3:** Under what specific conditions (e.g., rater count imbalance, noise distribution) do existing measures like ICC(2,k) fail, and how universally does \(\rho\)-Perfect avoid these failures? The paper demonstrates ICC’s failure on unbalanced data but lacks theoretical analysis. *Resolution:* Mathematical derivation of ICC bias under varying rater count distributions or systematic evaluation on synthetic data.  
**Open Question 4:** Is \(\rho\)-Perfect’s interpretation as a correlation ceiling consistent across different subjective rating domains (e.g., image aesthetics, music emotion) with diverse rating scales and rater profiles? Validation has been limited to speech/audio datasets. *Resolution:* Cross-domain study on text and image quality assessment datasets.

## Limitations
- **Conditional independence assumption:** Shared rater biases or sequential rating effects can bias the within-item variance estimate \(\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(Y|X)]\).
- **Small-sample instability:** Datasets with fewer than 50 items or fewer than 3 ratings per item produce noisy or negative \(\text{Var}(\hat{Y})\) estimates.
- **Test-retest correspondence:** The approximation \(\rho^2 \approx \text{Corr}(Y_1,Y_2)\) holds only if the two evaluations use similar rater pools and protocols; systematic differences invalidate the link.

## Confidence
- **Rho-Perfect estimator derivation:** Medium-High – mathematically sound under assumptions with strong empirical support; real-world assumption violations could reduce accuracy.  
- **\(\rho^2\) approximates test-retest correlation:** Medium – experiments show close alignment, but the approximation depends on evaluation similarity and conditional independence.  
- **Utility for distinguishing model vs. data limits:** Medium – NISQA case study demonstrates the concept, but interpretation can be confounded if model errors correlate with rating noise or on small subsets.
