# Multi-Agent Debate: A Unified Agentic Framework for Tabular Anomaly Detection
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.14251
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14251
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: MAD achieves strong detection (PR‑AUC 0.352±0.071, ROC‑AUC 0.936±0.015) and superior calibration (ECE 5.0±1.5) and slice robustness (Gap 0.050±0.024) over 20+ tabular anomaly detection benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Multi‑Agent Debate (MAD) introduces a principled framework for tabular anomaly detection that treats disagreement among heterogeneous detectors as a structured signal rather than noise. A coordination layer aggregates normalized scores, confidence estimates, and structured evidence (e.g., feature attributions) from multiple agents, resolving disputes via a synthesis operator and exponentiated gradient updates. This yields an accurate final anomaly score and an auditable debate trace. Extensive experiments across diverse benchmarks demonstrate MAD’s superior detection performance, calibration, and robustness—particularly under distribution shift, missingness, and rare‑anomaly regimes. The approach unifies ensemble methods with online learning theory and interpretability.

## Method Summary
MAD collects messages from heterogeneous anomaly detectors; each agent emits a normalized score, a confidence value, and evidence (e.g., SHAP attributions). A coordinator synthesizes these into per‑agent bounded losses that penalize confident disagreement and inconsistent evidence. Using exponentiated gradient updates, the coordinator adjusts agent influence over possibly multiple rounds (default T=1). The final anomaly score is a weighted sum of agents’ scores, and the entire debate trace is stored for audit. The system is validated on over 20 benchmarks, outperforming classical, deep, AutoML, and outlier detection baselines in PR‑AUC, ROC‑AUC, Expected Calibration Error, and slice robustness.

## Key Results
- MAD achieves PR‑AUC 0.352±0.071 and ROC‑AUC 0.936±0.015, significantly outperforming stronger baselines.
- Superior calibration: ECE 5.0±1.5.
- Enhanced slice robustness: Gap 0.050±0.024.
- Ablations confirm that dispute‑resolution (confidence + evidence) drives gains, especially under high disagreement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Disagreement among heterogeneous agents is a structured signal about ambiguity and failure modes, not noise to be averaged away. The coordinator’s synthesis operator penalizes confident disagreement unsupported by evidence, down‑weighting unreliable agents via exponentiated gradient updates. This rests on the core assumption that different model families (e.g., tree ensembles, deep networks) exhibit meaningful disagreement under distribution shift, missingness, and rare‑anomaly regimes. Anchors: the paper’s framing of disagreement as a “first‑class signal” and related work. Break condition: if agents are homogeneous or disagreement is minimal (< threshold), MAD reduces to conventional ensembling.

### Mechanism 2
Structured messages (score, confidence, evidence) enable principled dispute resolution beyond scalar aggregation. The synthesis operator combines prediction error (if labels exist), score disagreement weighted by confidence, and evidence inconsistency with consensus; bounded losses ensure EG theory applies. Confidence and evidence provide orthogonal information—high‑confidence claims should be supported by consistent evidence. Anchors: loss definitions (Equations 12‑16) and the distinction from LLM‑based debate. Break condition: if evidence is noisy or missing, performance degrades (ablation evidence shuffle).

### Mechanism 3
Exponentiated gradient updates with bounded losses yield a final anomaly score and an auditable trace, with theoretical regret guarantees. The coordinator updates weights after each round using clipped losses; the trace records messages, losses, and weight evolution. The regret bound holds because losses are clipped to [0,1]. This ties debate to online learning theory. Break condition: if clipping is omitted or log(0) occurs, the bound fails and weights may diverge.

## Foundational Learning
- **Online Learning with Expert Advice (Hedge/EG)**: MAD’s coordination layer aggregates expert agent predictions with regret guarantees, framing debate as a sequential decision problem. *Quick check*: The regret bound (Equation 2) guarantees that MAD’s cumulative loss is at most log N/η + ηT/8 worse than the best fixed agent, regardless of the loss sequence.
- **Confidence Calibration**: Agent confidence modulates dispute and evidence penalties; mis‑calibration misleads Ψ. *Quick check*: MAD’s default confidence (bootstrap variance) estimates model instability, differing from temperature scaling which uses validation post‑hoc.
- **Interpretability via Debate Trace**: The trace records how disagreement was resolved, enabling human audit. *Quick check*: The trace per round includes messages (score, confidence, evidence), synthesized losses, and updated weights.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Agents → Message formation → Coordinator (Ψ) → EG Updater → Output Generator (final score + trace) (with optional T repetitions between Ψ and EG Updater).

**Critical path**  
Input x → Agent scoring & explanation → Message formation → Ψ: loss synthesis & weight update → repeat T rounds → Final aggregation + trace.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Overhead vs robustness: More agents and rounds improve robustness in high‑disagreement regimes but increase inference cost linearly.
- Evidence quality vs cost: Cheap ℓ2‑normalized attributions vs expensive LLM critiques.
- Homogeneity vs debate value: Gains concentrate where disagreement is high; with homogeneous agents, debate adds little.

**Failure signatures**  
- Homogeneous agent pool: low score variance → MAD ≈ mean ensemble.
- Noisy/unstable evidence: near‑zero evidence loss → debate reduces to weighted average.
- Unbounded losses: omitted clipping or log(0) breaks EG theory and causes divergence.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Baseline comparison**: Run MAD vs single agents, mean ensemble, and AutoML/deep baselines on 20+ benchmarks; expect improved PR‑AUC, ROC‑AUC, ECE, Gap.
2. **Disagreement diagnostics**: Bin samples by variance of normalized scores; plot ΔROC/ΔPR vs disagreement; expect positive correlation.
3. **Component ablation**: Remove key MAD components (e.g., set λ=γ=0, drop evidence, disable updates); expect performance drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question**: How to make debate resource usage adapt to per‑input cost and difficulty?  
**Basis in paper**: Explicitly mentioned as future work (“making debate adaptive to cost and difficulty”).  
**Why unresolved**: Fixed T=1 cannot adapt; may waste compute or under‑debate.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: An early‑stopping mechanism based on disagreement, showing efficiency gains with minimal accuracy loss.

### Open Question 2
**Question**: Can agents be automatically selected and pruned to maintain diversity while lowering cost?  
**Basis**: Future work (“automatically designing and pruning agent portfolios”).  
**Why unresolved**: Manual selection may be suboptimal and does not scale.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: An online contribution estimator that prunes low‑value agents, with experiments showing similar accuracy at reduced cost.

### Open Question 3
**Question**: How to learn confidence and evidence that are well‑calibrated and robust?  
**Basis**: Future work (“learning calibrated message channels”).  
**Why unresolved**: Weak/noisy confidence/evidence degrades performance (Tables 4‑5).  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Joint training with calibration losses and evidence stability regularization, testing on calibration error and perturbation robustness.

## Limitations
- Exact confidence and evidence generation procedures are underspecified, hindering replication.
- Final hyperparameter values (λ, γ, η) per dataset after validation tuning are unreported; the perturbation distribution for unsupervised stability loss is unspecified.
- The quantitative breakpoint for “high disagreement” is dataset‑dependent and not extracted, limiting claims about where MAD shines.
