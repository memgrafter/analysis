# Select, Label, Evaluate: Active Testing in NLP

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.21840
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21840
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Annotation cost reductions up to 95% while keeping estimation error within 1% of full-test-set evaluation.

## Executive Summary
Test set annotation remains a major bottleneck in evaluating natural language processing systems. This paper introduces Active Testing, a framework that strategically selects the most informative test samples for labeling under a fixed budget. By iteratively querying an informativeness oracle (e.g., based on model uncertainty or coverage) and applying an unbiased Horvitz-Thompson estimator, Active Testing yields accurate performance metrics with far fewer labeled samples. Empirical results on 18 datasets and four embedding models demonstrate up to 95% cost savings with estimation error below 1% of full-set evaluation. An adaptive stopping criterion further trims the required annotation effort—only 50 samples needed on IMDB compared to a 1000‑sample budget—and proves robust in multilingual and imbalanced settings where the proposed strategies consistently beat random sampling.

## Method Summary
Active Testing operates on an unlabeled test set. First, all samples are encoded using a pretrained embedding model; a predictor (e.g., a classifier or LLM) produces preliminary outputs. The framework then enters an iterative loop: a scoring strategy (Agreement, Surrogate, Uncertainty, Coverage, or Stratified Random) evaluates each sample’s informativeness; the highest‑scoring sample is selected for annotation, and its inclusion probability is updated. After each annotation, the performance metric is recomputed via the inverse probability weighted (IPW) estimator to remain unbiased. An adaptive stopping monitor compares the IPW estimate to a random baseline; when their absolute difference falls below a threshold τ, the process terminates early, otherwise it continues until the annotation budget is exhausted. The entire pipeline is model‑agnostic and works with any embedding and predictor combination.

## Key Results
- Up to **95% annotation cost reduction** across 18 datasets while maintaining estimation error below **1%** of full‑test‑set evaluation.
- **Adaptive stopping** drastically reduces required samples (e.g., **50** on IMDB vs. a **1000**‑sample budget) and remains effective in multilingual and imbalanced scenarios.
- **Active Testing strategies** consistently **outperform random sampling**, particularly on datasets with high class imbalance or language diversity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
#### Mechanism 1: Selective Sample Acquisition
Active Testing reduces annotation costs by selecting a minimal, highly informative subset of test samples. It scores each candidate using heuristics such as attention variance (Agreement) or surrogate model entropy (Surrogate) and picks the top one iteratively under a budget. The underlying assumption is that test samples are not equally informative; a well‑chosen subset can approximate the full‑set evaluation. Evidence appears in the abstract (“selects the most informative test samples… reductions of up to 95%”) and Section 3.2. The approach fails when all samples contribute equally, as seen in homogeneous datasets where gains over random are marginal (Section 5).

#### Mechanism 2: Unbiased Estimation under Selection Bias
To preserve metric accuracy despite non‑random sampling, Active Testing employs the Horvitz‑Thompson inverse probability weighted (IPW) estimator. Each sample’s contribution (e.g., correctness) is divided by its selection probability, yielding an unbiased estimate as long as probabilities are known, non‑zero, and sampling is without replacement. The abstract mentions the unbiased estimator, and Section 3.1 provides formal definitions and proof (Appendix A.2). If probabilities are misestimated or sampling dependencies exist, bias or high variance can undermine reliability.

#### Mechanism 3: Adaptive Stopping for Cost Efficiency
Adaptive stopping eliminates wasted effort by automatically deciding when enough samples have been annotated. The algorithm monitors the absolute difference between the IPW estimate and a simple random baseline; when this gap drops below a threshold τ, it signals that additional annotations are unlikely to change the estimate (Proposition 1). The abstract and Section 3.3 (Algorithm 1) describe this. Poor choice of τ or non‑stationary data may cause premature or delayed termination, harming estimate stability.

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component Map
Unlabeled test set X → Embedding model E → Predictor f → Active Testing strategy (Agreement, Surrogate, etc.) → Sample selection → Annotation (gold label) → Update inclusion probabilities q_i → Compute IPW estimate cM → Compute random baseline cM_random → Adaptive stopping check (|cM − cM_random| < τ) → Repeat until budget B exhausted or stopping condition met.

#### Critical Path
1. Compute embeddings E(X) for all test samples.  
2. Obtain predictions f(X) from the predictor.  
3. Initialize active set A and inclusion probabilities.  
4. Loop: score all unselected samples via chosen strategy; select top‑1; annotate; update probabilities.  
5. Recompute IPW estimate cM and random estimate cM_random.  
6. If |cM − cM_random| < τ, stop; else continue until budget B is reached.  
7. Output final cM and compare to full‑set metric.

#### Design Tradeoffs
- **Strategy complexity vs. computational cost**: Surrogate retrains an auxiliary model each iteration; Uncertainty requires multiple forward passes; Agreement needs an extra attention layer; Random is cheapest but less informative.
- **Estimation error vs. annotation budget**: More samples reduce error but increase cost; adaptive stopping seeks an early stop point where error stabilizes.
- **Bias correction vs. variance**: IPW weights can become large for low‑probability selections, inflating variance; trade‑off between strict unbiasedness and estimator stability.

#### Failure Signatures
- **IPW variance explosion**: Histogram of 1/q_i shows heavy tails; estimates may become unstable or fall outside [0,1].
- **Unreliable adaptive stopping**: Gap |cM − cM_random| fluctuates noisily, causing premature termination or never stopping within budget.
- **Poor minority‑class detection**: Strategies may overlook rare classes if selection scores do not account for class distribution.
- **Embedding‑dependent degradation**: Agreement and Coverage suffer with low‑quality or misaligned embedding models.

#### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline comparison**: Run Agreement, Surrogate, Random, Stratified on AG’s News with Qwen embeddings; plot estimation error vs. budget to verify claimed cost reductions (Section 5, Figure 3).
2. **Stopping validation**: Implement Algorithm 1 on IMDB with τ=0.01; record number of samples annotated and compare estimation error to fixed‑budget runs (Section 5, Table 2).
3. **Multilingual test**: Apply Active Testing on the Multilingual dataset with cost prior p=0.6; assess whether strategies reduce English‑heavy annotation while keeping error <1% (Section 4.2, Figure 6).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Integration with LLM‑based evaluation** – How can Active Testing be combined with LLM judges to further reduce dependence on human‑annotated test sets without compromising accuracy? The paper identifies this as a future direction but leaves the trade‑offs unexplored.
2. **Multi‑criteria sample selection** – Current methods optimize a single informativeness criterion. A principled framework for jointly optimizing uncertainty, diversity, and annotation cost is lacking.
3. **Predicting optimal strategy a priori** – What dataset properties (e.g., imbalance, embedding geometry) determine which Active Testing strategy performs best? Understanding these drivers would allow practitioners to choose the right method without costly trial‑and‑error.

## Limitations
- **Underspecified Agreement strategy**: The implementation details of the lightweight multi‑head attention layer (number of layers, initialization, aggregation) are not provided, making exact replication challenging.
- **Reliance on proprietary predictors**: Experiments use Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Amazon Nova Pro, which may be inaccessible to many researchers and could exhibit different error characteristics than open models.
- **IPW estimator variance**: Inverse probability weights can become extremely large for low‑probability samples, potentially causing unstable estimates; the paper does not discuss mitigation techniques like weight truncation.
