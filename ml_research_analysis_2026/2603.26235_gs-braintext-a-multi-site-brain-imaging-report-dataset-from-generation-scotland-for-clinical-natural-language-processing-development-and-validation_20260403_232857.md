# GS-BrainText: A Multi-Site Brain Imaging Report Dataset from Generation Scotland for Clinical Natural Language Processing Development and Validation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26235
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26235
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Benchmark evaluation of EdIE-R on GS-BrainText shows strong overall micro-F1 (88.82) but substantial performance variation across health boards (86.13–98.13), phenotypes (22.22–100), and age groups (77.60–91.70), highlighting critical generalisation challenges.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces GS-BrainText, a curated multi-site dataset of 8,511 Scottish NHS brain radiology reports, with 2,431 manually annotated for 24 cerebrovascular phenotypes by a clinical team. The dataset addresses the scarcity of expertly annotated, multi-site UK brain imaging data for clinical NLP. A benchmark evaluation of the rule-based EdIE-R system on this gold standard reveals high overall performance but significant variability in effectiveness across different health boards, patient age groups, and individual phenotypes, underscoring major generalisation hurdles for deploying clinical NLP tools in real-world, heterogeneous settings.

## Method Summary
The core method involves creating the GS-BrainText dataset from five Scottish NHS health boards. A clinical team manually annotated 2,431 reports using a detailed schema for 24 cerebrovascular phenotypes, with annotations including entities, modifiers, and relations via the BRAT tool, supported by quality assurance procedures. The benchmark task is document-level phenotype classification. The pre-existing, rule-based EdIE-R NLP system was evaluated as a held-out test on the full manually annotated subset to compute precision, recall, and micro-/macro-averaged F1 scores, with results stratified by health board, patient age group, and individual phenotype.

## Key Results
- EdIE-R achieves a micro-average F1 of 88.82 on the full annotated set, but performance varies dramatically by health board (F1 range: 86.13–98.13).
- Performance differs widely by phenotype, from 22.22 to 100 F1, with extremely low-support phenotypes (e.g., microbleeds, n≤12) yielding unstable estimates.
- System effectiveness increases steadily with patient age, from F1 77.60 for patients under 50 to 91.70 for those 70 and older.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Multi-site data collection with local reporting conventions introduces linguistic heterogeneity that degrades NLP system performance when moving between sites.
- **Mechanism:** Variation in radiologists' descriptive language, abbreviations, and structural reporting habits across NHS health boards creates a distribution shift. A rule-based system optimized on one board's conventions encounters unseen phrasing and pattern combinations in another, leading to missed entity recognition or incorrect modifier attachment.
- **Core assumption:** NLP systems encode implicit site-specific linguistic patterns; performance depends on the match between training/development data patterns and deployment data patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "...substantial variation across health boards (F1: 86.13–98.13)... highlighting critical challenges in generalisation of NLP tools."
  - [section 6.3] "EdIE-R achieves 96.36 on Lothian data similar to what was used in its development, yet performs at 86–89 on data from some other boards... Performance on Fife (98.13) exceeds that on Tayside (86.13) despite similar Tayside data having been used for system refinement."

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Extreme class imbalance in phenotype prevalence leads to unreliable performance estimates for rare conditions and may cause systems to under-fit their patterns.
- **Mechanism:** For phenotypes with very few positive examples (e.g., microbleeds, n=2 or 7), the F1 metric becomes highly volatile and non-generalizable. The system may achieve a perfect score by chance or fail to learn robust features due to insufficient examples.
- **Core assumption:** Performance metrics derived from low-support test sets do not reliably reflect a system's true capability for that phenotype.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 6.2] "Individual results for low-support phenotypes (n<30 in the Total column of Table 2) should be interpreted with caution, as scores may not be reliable estimates of generalised system performance." and "Microbleeds (deep and lobar) achieve perfect scores (F1=100), though their extreme rarity (n=2 and n=7) limits the reliability of these estimates."
  - [section 5.1] Table 2 shows severe imbalance: Small vessel disease (n=545) vs. Microbleed_deep (n=2).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Age-related variation in diagnostic certainty expression (hedging language) reduces NLP system performance on younger patients' reports.
- **Mechanism:** Radiologists use more tentative, speculative language for findings atypical for a patient's age. NLP systems, often trained on more definitive language from older cohorts, may fail to recognize or correctly weigh these uncertain expressions, leading to lower precision or recall for phenotypes in younger groups.
- **Core assumption:** The linguistic strategy of expressing diagnostic uncertainty is systematic and age-correlated, creating a feature distribution a standard NLP pipeline does not handle optimally.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "provides a valuable resource for the study of linguistic variation, diagnostic uncertainty expression and the impact of data characteristics on NLP system performance."
  - [section 5.3] "Casey et al. (2023) previously identified important linguistic characteristics... related to diagnostic uncertainty in radiological reporting. Their analysis showed that scan reports of younger patients frequently employ hedging language, particularly for cerebrovascular findings that are less expected in this age group."
  - [section 6.4] Performance increases with age (F1=77.60 for <50; 91.7 for 70+).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Clinical NLP Evaluation Metrics (Precision, Recall, F1, Micro vs. Macro averaging)
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's core results are presented through these metrics. Understanding macro-F1 (77.47) vs. micro-F1 (88.82) is crucial to interpret how class imbalance skews the overall score and why rare phenotype performance is systematically under-weighted.
  - **Quick check:** If a system has perfect recall for 23 common phenotypes but 0 recall for 1 rare phenotype, micro-F1 would be high (weighted by common class size) while macro-F1 would be low (equal weight per class). Macro-F1 better reflects catastrophic failure on the rare case.
- **Concept:** Document-Level vs. Span-Level Annotation
  - **Why needed here:** The dataset provides document-level phenotype labels derived from underlying text-level annotations. The benchmark task is document classification, not sequence labeling, even though the schema supports finer granularity.
  - **Quick check:** A report could receive a label for `Ischaemic_stroke_deep_old` if it mentions "old deep infarct" without negation, but not if it only mentions "cortical infarct" without "deep." If it mentions "old deep infarct" but later negates it, the label should be absent.
- **Concept:** Generalisation and Domain Shift in Machine Learning
  - **Why needed here:** The paper quantifies generalisation failure across sites and age groups within a single national health system, a concrete case of in-domain vs. out-of-distribution performance.
  - **Quick check:** If EdIE-R was developed on Lothian data, the 8-point F1 drop on Grampian likely stems from a data shift (linguistic/habit differences between boards) rather than model capacity, as the same rules perform well on Fife.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Raw CSV -> Pre-processing & Structuring -> XML with sections -> NER -> Modifier/Negation Detection -> Relation Extraction -> Document Classification -> Phenotype Labels
- **Critical path:** XML conversion -> NER -> Modifier/Negation -> Relation linking -> Phenotype rule application. A failure in any upstream component (e.g., missing an entity) cascades to the final document label.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Rule-Based (EdIE-R) vs. Neural:** Rules offer interpretability and high performance on common patterns (e.g., small vessel disease F1=95.6) but are brittle to linguistic variation (site performance drop). A neural model might generalize better linguistically but requires large, balanced training data across all sites/phenotypes, which is scarce.
  - **Gold-Standard from Pre-annotation:** Using EdIE-R output to speed up annotation creates efficiency but introduces anchoring bias—phenotypes EdIE-R consistently misses may be under-represented in the gold standard.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Site-Specific Drop:** F1 drops >10 points on a specific health board (e.g., Tayside 86.13 vs. Fife 98.13). Signature: Check for unique phrasing/abbreviations in that board's reports.
  - **Phenotype-Specific Collapse:** Very low recall (<30%) on a phenotype with decent prevalence (>20 instances). Signature: Likely a missing or overly restrictive rule in NER or modifier detection for that phenotype's lexicon.
  - **Age Gradient Failure:** Steady decrease in F1 for younger age groups. Signature: Insufficient handling of hedging/uncertainty linguistic markers in the rule set.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Re-run Baseline & Error Analysis:** Execute EdIE-R on the full annotated set. Generate confusion matrices per health board and phenotype. Identify top 3 most frequent error types (e.g., missed entities, incorrect negation, wrong modifier binding).
  2. **Linguistic Variation Probe:** For a low-performance board (e.g., Tayside), extract all false negative reports for a common phenotype like `Small_vessel_disease`. Manually inspect for distinctive local terminology or sentence structures not captured by EdIE-R's patterns.
  3. **Rare Phenotype Stress Test:** Take the 5 phenotypes with <30 total positives (e.g., `Subarachnoid_haemorrhage_aneurysmal`, `Microbleed_*`). Perform leave-one-out cross-validation where each instance is a test fold. Report performance variance to quantify unreliability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How do local reporting conventions across different Scottish NHS health boards impact the generalisability of rule-based clinical NLP systems, and can performance disparities be mitigated through site-specific adaptations?
- **Question 2:** To what extent do age-related variations in diagnostic uncertainty expressions (e.g., hedging language in younger patients) degrade the accuracy of phenotype extraction from brain radiology reports?
- **Question 3:** What are the minimum viable sample sizes and effective methodological approaches (e.g., data augmentation, few-shot learning) for reliably training and evaluating NLP systems on rare cerebrovascular phenotypes?
- **Question 4:** How do longitudinal changes in radiology reporting practices (1994–2021) affect the temporal robustness of NLP systems, and can temporal adaptation techniques improve cross-era generalisation?

## Limitations
- **Annotation bias:** Gold standard derived from EdIE-R pre-annotation may under-represent false negatives, inflating performance, especially for rare phenotypes.
- **Evaluation protocol ambiguity:** Unclear whether reported metrics use a single hold-out test set or cross-validation, hindering direct replication.
- **EdIE-R specification gap:** Exact version, configuration, and rule set used are not provided, risking irreproducible benchmark runs.

## Confidence
- **Dataset existence & scale:** High (clear documentation of sources, size, annotation process).
- **Reported benchmark metrics (F1 scores):** Medium (dependent on unreleased EdIE-R specifics and ambiguous evaluation split).
- **Generalization mechanisms (site/age drift):** Low (proposed but not directly validated; alternative explanations possible).
