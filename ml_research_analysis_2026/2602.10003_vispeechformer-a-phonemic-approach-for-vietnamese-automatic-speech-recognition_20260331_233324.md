# ViSpeechFormer: A Phonemic Approach for Vietnamese Automatic Speech Recognition

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10003
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10003
- **Reference count:** 7
- **Primary result:** Achieves state-of-the-art CER of 11.96% (ViVOS) and 5.30% (LSVSC), WER of 30.49% (ViVOS) and 10.39% (LSVSC), with 27.27% OOV accuracy and competitive inference speed (0.1112 s/sample on LSVSC) using a phoneme-level decoder with fewer parameters.

## Executive Summary
Vietnamese ASR struggles with out-of-vocabulary words and data sparsity when using conventional word, subword, or character-level decoding, failing to leverage the language's phonetic orthography. This paper proposes ViSpeechFormer, a phoneme-level approach employing a custom tokenizer (ViPhonER) that decomposes Vietnamese syllables into initials, rhymes, and tones, and a decoder with three separate feed-forward heads to predict these components. Evaluated on ViVOS and LSVSC datasets, ViSpeechFormer achieves state-of-the-art character and word error rates while demonstrating significantly better generalization on OOV words (27.27% accuracy, nearly double the best baseline) and reduced reliance on word-frequency biases. The model uses fewer decoder parameters and maintains competitive inference speed.

## Method Summary
The method centers on the ViPhonER tokenizer, which deterministically converts Vietnamese text into sequences of phonemic triplets (initial, rhyme, tone) based on the language's regular syllable structure and grapheme-phoneme transparency. The neural architecture uses a Speech Transformer encoder followed by a Transformer decoder. After the decoder layers, three independent feed-forward heads predict the initial, rhyme, and tone components in parallel at each time step. The total loss is the sum of three cross-entropy losses, one per head. During inference, predicted phoneme triplets are mapped back to valid Vietnamese syllables via the inverse of ViPhonER and concatenated into the final transcript. The approach is evaluated on two datasets: ViVOS (15 hours) and LSVSC (100 hours), with transcripts filtered to contain only Vietnamese characters.

## Key Results
- **State-of-the-art accuracy:** Achieves CER of 11.96% (ViVOS) and 5.30% (LSVSC), and WER of 30.49% (ViVOS) and 10.39% (LSVSC), outperforming subword and character-level baselines.
- **Superior OOV generalization:** Correctly generates 27.27% of OOV words, nearly double the best baseline (14.39%).
- **Robustness to frequency bias:** Shows lower Pearson (21.54%) and Spearman (29.54%) correlation between word frequency and correct predictions compared to all baselines.
- **Efficiency:** Uses fewer decoder parameters (2.0M) than most subword baselines and achieves competitive inference speed (0.1112 seconds per sample on LSVSC).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Phoneme-level decoding mitigates data sparsity and OOV problems by composing words from a small, fixed set of phonetic units.** ViPhonER decomposes each syllable into three components (initial, rhyme, tone) using deterministic rules, creating a compact vocabulary of 163 tokens. The decoder predicts these three components in parallel per time step. At inference, predicted phoneme sequences are re-composed into valid syllables via the inverse tokenizer. This allows the model to generate any valid syllable combination present in the language's phonotactic rules, even if the full word was never seen in training. *Evidence:* OOV accuracy of 27.27% vs. max 14.39% for baselines [abstract, Table 5]. *Break condition:* If Vietnamese syllable structure were highly irregular, the compositional generation would produce many invalid syllables.

**Decoupling the prediction of syllabic components into separate feed-forward heads reduces the model's reliance on word-frequency biases.** The three independent heads after shared decoder layers force the representation to disentangle features for initials, rhymes, and tones. This prevents the model from simply memorizing high-frequency whole-syllable patterns, as it must generate valid combinations from separate, lower-frequency phoneme distributions. *Evidence:* ViSpeechFormer has the lowest Pearson (21.54%) and Spearman (29.54%) correlations between train-set word frequency and test-set correctness [Table 4]. *Break condition:* If acoustic cues for the components were extremely overlapping, independent prediction might harm performance by ignoring necessary joint information.

**The small, fixed phonemic vocabulary enables a more parameter-efficient decoder architecture that maintains competitive inference speed.** The vocabulary size (163 tokens) is orders of magnitude smaller than typical subword (5000) or character (hundreds) vocabularies, drastically reducing the size of the decoder's embedding tables and output projection layers. This reduces total decoder parameters (2.0M vs. 2.5–4.3M for subword models) and shrinks the search space during beam search, leading to faster matrix operations. *Evidence:* Decoder parameters are fewer than most baselines [Table 3] and inference speed is competitive [Table 6]. *Break condition:* If the language required a much larger phoneme inventory (e.g., due to allophony), the vocabulary size could approach or exceed subword sizes, negating benefits.

## Foundational Learning
- **Vietnamese Syllable Structure (Initial-Rhyme-Tone):** The entire methodology (tokenizer, decoder, loss) decomposes a Vietnamese "word" (typically one syllable) into these three atomic, meaning-distinguishing components. Without this structure, the phonemic approach is meaningless. *Quick check:* For "mắt" (eye), initial=/m/, rhyme=/ăt/, tone=mid rising /˧˥/.
- **Phoneme vs. Grapheme Transparency:** This property (bidirectional, rule-based mapping between sound and spelling) justifies that a learned acoustic model can reliably map to a small phoneme set and that these phonemes map deterministically back to correct spelling. It makes ViPhonER's rule-based conversion feasible and interpretable. *Quick check:* If the mapping were many-to-many (ambiguous), ViPhonER's deterministic grapheme-to-phoneme conversion would fail or require probabilistic disambiguation, harming reliability.
- **Encoder-Decoder with Multiple Output Heads:** The architecture diverges from a standard single-output-layer decoder by having three parallel FFNs. Understanding this is key to implementing the model and the loss function (sum of three cross-entropy losses). It's a design pattern for multi-component sequence prediction. *Quick check:* Why is the final loss a sum of three losses instead of a single loss over a combined 163-token vocabulary? To enforce decoupled prediction of components and match the three-head structure; a combined vocabulary would collapse the components and defeat the purpose.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Audio Input → Log-Mel Features → Speech Transformer Encoder → Transformer Decoder Layers → Three Parallel FFN Heads (Initial, Rhyme, Tone) → ViPhonER Tokenizer (Inverse) → Vietnamese Syllables → Transcript Output.

**Critical path:**
1. Prepare training data: Convert all training transcripts to sequences of (initial, rhyme, tone) triplets using ViPhonER.
2. Train model: Forward pass (Encoder → Decoder → 3 Heads). Compute three cross-entropy losses against triplet labels. Backpropagate total loss.
3. Inference: Autoregressive loop. Feed previous triplet embedding to decoder. Get three logits. Take argmax for each to get next triplet. Use ViPhonER inverse to convert triplet to a syllable. Append to output. Repeat until EOS.
4. Post-process: Concatenate generated syllables into final text.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Vocabulary size vs. sequence length:** Phonemic vocab (163) is tiny vs. subword (~5000), but output sequence is ~3× longer per word (triplet vs. subword token). Trade-off: smaller softmax vs. more decoding steps.
- **Parameter sharing:** Decoder layers are shared, but FFN heads are separate. Balances component-specific learning with shared contextual representation.
- **Rule-based vs. learned tokenizer:** ViPhonER is deterministic and interpretable but relies on perfect orthographic rules. A learned phoneme tokenizer might be more robust to noise but would lose explicit linguistic structure and valid syllable guarantees.

**Failure signatures:**
- **High PER on rhymes:** Rhyme PER is highest in both datasets (20.52% ViVOS, 7.01% LSVSC), indicating struggle with the complex syllabic nucleus.
- **Invalid syllable generation:** Decoder might predict a triplet that violates Vietnamese syllable structure rules per ViPhonER inverse, producing unpronounceable "words".
- **Tone confusion:** High `PER-t` (13.39% ViVOS, 5.77% LSVSC) leads to homophone errors changing word meaning.
- **Slow inference despite small decoder:** If beam search is too large, exploring many invalid triplets could negate small-vocab advantage.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Tokenizer validation:** Run ViPhonER on a held-out set of real Vietnamese words. Verify 100% round-trip consistency (word → triplets → word). Manually check 50 random outputs for orthographic violations.
2. **Component-wise PER benchmark:** Train ViSpeechFormer and evaluate PER separately for initials, rhymes, tones. Baseline: character-level model. Expect rhyme PER highest, tone PER non-trivial.
3. **Ablation: single vs. multiple heads:** Modify ViSpeechFormer to have a single FFN predicting a combined 163-token index. Train with CE on combined vocab. Compare CER/WER and OOV accuracy to the multi-head version. Prediction: Multi-head should outperform, especially on OOV.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization to other phonetic orthographies:** Can the phoneme-level paradigm be effectively applied to languages beyond Vietnamese, and what modifications are necessary for different linguistic structures? The paper only evaluates on Vietnamese and lacks cross-lingual experiments.
- **Source of OOV improvement:** Does the improved OOV generalization stem from compositional recombination of unseen phonemic components, or from whole-word memorization? The analysis treats OOV words atomically and does not break down by novelty of individual components.
- **Explicit linguistic constraints:** Does explicitly modeling dependencies between Vietnamese syllable components (e.g., which initials can precede which rhymes) within the decoder further improve accuracy? Current three-head design treats components as independent predictions.
- **Robustness to non-standard orthography:** How robust is ViPhonER to dialectal, colloquial, or erroneous Vietnamese spelling that violates strict grapheme-phoneme rules? The datasets used likely adhere to standard orthography; real-world noise is unaddressed.

## Limitations
- Tokenizer assumes perfect grapheme-phoneme transparency; real-world noise (dialects, spelling variants) may break round-trip conversion.
- Separate heads risk ignoring joint acoustic cues among components, potentially harming accuracy on confusable units.
- Reproduction blocked by missing critical hyperparameters (encoder dimensions, decoder FFN size, dropout) and exact dataset splits.
- Inference speed comparison lacks beam search details; reported 0.1112 s/sample may not reflect typical decoding conditions.
- OOV evaluation uses a narrow definition (words absent from training transcripts); real-world OOV includes morphological variants/names not covered by phoneme grammar.

## Confidence
- **Phoneme-level decoding mitigates OOV/data sparsity:** Medium. Strong OOV improvement (27.27% vs. 14.39%) and better CER/WER with fewer parameters are convincing, but tokenizer robustness to non-standard orthography and rate of invalid syllable generation are not thoroughly evaluated.
- **Decoupling reduces training bias:** Medium. Lower correlation coefficients (Table 4) support reduced frequency reliance, but causality is not isolated; ablation (single vs. multiple heads) is missing.
- **Parameter efficiency and speed:** High. Decoder parameters are explicitly smaller (2.0M vs. 2.5–4.3M) and inference speed competitive; the small vocabulary directly enables this.

## Next Checks
1. **Tokenizer stress test:** Run ViPhonER on an authentic noisy Vietnamese text set (social media, dialectal transcripts). Measure round-trip accuracy drop vs. clean test set.
2. **Heads ablation:** Train an ablation model with a single combined FFN (163 classes) and compare OOV accuracy and word-frequency correlation to the multi-head version to isolate the effect of component separation.
3. **Invalid syllable generation audit:** During inference, compute the percentage of predicted phoneme triplets that violate Vietnamese syllable structure rules (per ViPhonER inverse). A high rate indicates decoder phonotactic violations.
