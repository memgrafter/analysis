# Flow-Based Conformal Predictive Distributions

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.07633
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07633
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Nonconformity flows enable efficient, training-free sampling from conformal prediction set boundaries; mixing across confidence levels yields conformal predictive distributions (CPDs) with exact quantile-region coverage and improved distributional fidelity.

## Executive Summary
Conformal prediction provides distribution-free uncertainty quantification but struggles with high-dimensional or structured outputs, where prediction sets are hard to represent and sample from. This paper introduces two key innovations: (1) **nonconformity flows**, a deterministic dynamical system derived from any differentiable nonconformity score that converges exponentially to the boundary of the corresponding conformal prediction set; (2) **conformal predictive distributions (CPDs)**, constructed by mixing boundary measures across confidence levels, whose quantile regions exactly match conformal sets. The approach is training-free, fast (ε-hitting times up to 100 dimensions), and applicable to tasks like PDE inversion, precipitation downscaling, climate debiasing, and hurricane trajectory forecasting. Empirical comparisons show CPDs achieve lower Log Spectral Distance and local MMD than baseline ensembles, Dropout, Flow Matching, and MDNs, while enabling targeted rare-event sampling via the mixing measure.

## Method Summary
Given a trained predictor \(f_\theta\), a calibration dataset \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}\), and a differentiable nonconformity score \(S(x,y)\), the method first computes score thresholds \(\tau_\alpha\) (empirical quantiles) on the calibration scores. To sample from the boundary \(\partial C_\alpha(x) = \{y: S(x,y) = \tau_\alpha\}\) of the conformal set \(C_\alpha(x) = \{y: S(x,y) \le \tau_\alpha\}\), one initializes a point \(y_0\) from a base measure \(\mu_x\) (e.g., empirical distribution of calibration outputs) and integrates the ordinary differential equation
\[
\frac{dy}{dt} = v_\alpha(y) = -\lambda \frac{(S(y)-\tau_\alpha)\nabla S(y)}{\|\nabla S(y)\|^2},
\]
where \(\lambda\) is chosen to achieve an \(\varepsilon\)-hitting time (Corollary 3.2). The terminal point \(y_\infty = \lim_{t\to\infty} \Phi_\alpha(t,y_0)\) lies on \(\partial C_\alpha(x)\). This defines a hitting map \(\Pi_{x,\alpha}(y_0)\). To construct a full predictive distribution, one samples a confidence level \(\alpha\) from a mixing measure \(\pi\) on \((0,1)\) and a base sample \(y_0 \sim \mu_x\), then outputs \(y = \Pi_{x,\alpha}(y_0)\). Optional tangent repulsion (Algorithm 2) may be applied to spread samples along the boundary while preserving the score constraint, followed by a reconformalization step if needed. The resulting CPD \(P_x\) satisfies \(P_x(C_\alpha(x)) = \pi([\alpha,1])\) for all \(\alpha\) (Proposition 3.4), i.e., its quantile regions exactly match the conformal sets.

## Key Results
- CPDs achieve lower Log Spectral Distance (LSD) and local Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) compared to baseline ensembles, Dropout, Flow Matching, and Mixture Density Networks (MDNs) across several challenging tasks.
- The nonconformity flow converges rapidly (ε-hitting times) even in high dimensions (up to 100), as demonstrated on isotropic Gaussian models.
- By varying the mixing measure \(\pi\), CPDs enable targeted sampling of rare events (e.g., extreme hurricane trajectories) while maintaining validity.
- Quantile regions of CPDs coincide exactly with conformal prediction sets, guaranteeing the same coverage property in a distributional form.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Flow convergence to the boundary
Any differentiable nonconformity score \(S\) induces a deterministic flow on the output space whose trajectories converge exponentially to the boundary of the corresponding conformal prediction set. The core ODE is derived by requiring the score error \(\varepsilon(t) = S(y(t)) - \tau_\alpha\) to decay linearly (\(\varepsilon' = -\lambda \varepsilon\)), and choosing the velocity field as the minimum-norm solution that satisfies this constraint: \(v_\alpha(y) = -\lambda (S(y)-\tau_\alpha) \nabla S(y) / \|\nabla S(y)\|^2\). This field is everywhere aligned with \(\nabla S\), so trajectories follow integral curves directly to the level set \(\{S = \tau_\alpha\}\). Convergence is guaranteed under the assumptions that \(\nabla S\) exists almost everywhere and is non-zero in a neighborhood of the boundary (Proposition 3.1). Empirically, smooth scores (e.g., \(\ell_2\), Gaussian NLL) yield fast convergence; non-smooth scores (e.g., \(\ell_1\), kNN) break the mechanism due to vanishing or undefined gradients (Section 4.1).

### Mechanism 2: Mixing across confidence levels yields calibrated CPDs
A conformal predictive distribution (CPD) is defined by mixing the boundary measures \(\nu_{x,\alpha}\) (supported on \(\partial C_\alpha(x)\)) across confidence levels using a mixing measure \(\pi\) on \((0,1)\): \(P_{\text{CPD}}(A) = \int_0^1 \nu_{x,\alpha}(A) d\pi(\alpha)\). By construction, for any \(\alpha\), the probability that a draw from \(P_{\text{CPD}}\) falls into \(C_\alpha(x)\) equals \(\pi([\alpha,1])\) (Proposition 3.4). Thus the quantile regions of \(P_{\text{CPD}}\) exactly match the conformal prediction sets. This lifts the set-valued conformal output into a full distribution without requiring likelihood modeling or transport maps. The flow provides a natural way to generate \(\nu_{x,\alpha}\) via the hitting map: \(\nu_{x,\alpha} = (\Pi_{x,\alpha})_{\#} \mu_x\), where \(\mu_x\) is a base measure (e.g., empirical calibration outputs). Validity holds provided the boundary measures are exact (up to numerical \(\varepsilon\)) and the mixing measure \(\pi\) is chosen appropriately (e.g., uniform for standard coverage, skewed for rare-event emphasis). Evidence of correct calibration and improved distributional fidelity appears in Section 4.2.

### Mechanism 3: Tangent repulsion for geometric coverage
The minimum-norm flow tends to concentrate samples in low-curvature (flat) regions of the boundary, as trajectories converge faster where the gradient is small. To improve coverage, the method adds a deterministic tangential velocity field that repels points along the level set while preserving the score constraint. Given samples already on \(\partial C_\alpha(x)\), pairwise Euclidean repulsion (spring forces) is projected onto the tangent space via \(I - gg^\top/\|g\|^2\), where \(g = \nabla S\). This spreads samples without leaving the boundary; afterwards, a quick reconformalization step (one flow step) can correct any small drift. Empirically, repulsion increases the average nearest-neighbor distance on the boundary by 4–5× (Table 1), indicating better geometric spread. The approach is heuristic but effective; a more principled theory for tangential exploration remains an open question.

## Foundational Learning
- **Split Conformal Prediction**  
  *Why needed*: The entire framework relies on split conformal prediction to obtain nonconformity score thresholds \(\tau_\alpha\) from a calibration set. Understanding its exchangeability assumption and coverage guarantee is essential.  
  *Quick check*: "Given a fixed nonconformity score, what property of the calibration scores guarantees that \(C_\alpha(x) = \{y: S(x,y) \le \tau_\alpha\}\) achieves \(1-\alpha\) coverage?"

- **Dynamical Systems and Flows**  
  *Why needed*: Nonconformity flows are defined as solutions to an ODE; one must grasp flow maps, stability, and attraction to understand the convergence proof (Proposition 3.1).  
  *Quick check*: "If \(y'(t) = v(y(t))\) and we want \(y(t)\) to converge to a level set \(\{S(y)=c\}\), what constraint must \(v\) satisfy along trajectories?"

- **Mixing Measures and Distributional Lifting**  
  *Why needed*: CPDs are constructed by mixing a family of conformal sets indexed by \(\alpha\) using a measure \(\pi\); this lifts the set-valued object into a distribution while preserving exact quantile-region coverage.  
  *Quick check*: "If \(P(C_\alpha) = \pi([\alpha,1])\) for all \(\alpha\) and \(\pi = \text{Uniform}(0,1)\), what coverage does each \(C_\alpha\) have under \(P\)?"

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**
1. Base predictor \(f_\theta\) (MLP, FNO, CNN) trained on \((X,Y)\) to produce point forecasts.
2. Nonconformity score \(S(x,y)\) (e.g., \(\ell_2\), Sobolev, CGT) with differentiable \(\nabla S_y\).
3. Flow integrator: ODE solver (Euler/RK4) implementing \(y' = v_\alpha(y)\) with \(\lambda\) set via \(\varepsilon\)-hitting time.
4. Hitting map \(\Pi_{x,\alpha}(y_0) = \lim_{t\to\infty} \Phi_\alpha(t,y_0)\) (or stop when \(|S-\tau_\alpha|<\varepsilon\)).
5. Base measure \(\mu_x\) (e.g., empirical distribution of calibration outputs) to initialize flow.
6. Mixing layer: sample \(\alpha \sim \pi(0,1)\), then \(y = \Pi_{x,\alpha}(y_0)\) with \(y_0 \sim \mu_x\).
7. Optional tangent repulsion (Algorithm 2) applied post-sampling for boundary spread.
8. Optional reconformalization: inflation \(\eta\) to enforce pointwise risk control.

**Critical path:**
1. Train \(f_\theta\) on training data.
2. Compute calibration scores \(\{S(x_i, y_i)\}\) on a held-out calibration set and determine thresholds \(\{\tau_\alpha\}\) for desired \(\alpha\) levels.
3. For a test input \(x\), choose a mixing measure \(\pi\) (or specific \(\alpha\)), draw \(y_0 \sim \mu_x\), integrate the flow until convergence to obtain a boundary sample \(y_\infty\).
4. Repeat to collect enough samples to approximate \(P_x\). If desired, apply repulsion to the batch of samples.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Score choice*: Smooth scores (e.g., \(\ell_2\), Gaussian NLL) yield stable flows; non-smooth scores (\(\ell_1\), kNN) may fail without smoothing.
- *Base measure \(\mu_x\)*: Using empirical calibration outputs keeps samples on the data manifold but may limit diversity; heavy-tailed or broader \(\mu_x\) can over-disperse boundaries.
- *Mixing measure \(\pi\)*: Uniform \(\pi\) gives standard CP calibration; skewed \(\pi\) (e.g., Beta) emphasizes extreme tails for rare-event sampling but reduces coverage for intermediate \(\alpha\).
- *Integrator*: Fixed-step Euler is simple but requires small \(\Delta t\); adaptive solvers reduce calls but add overhead.
- *Repulsion*: Helps spread samples but adds computation and may need reconformalization to stay exactly on the boundary.

**Failure signatures:**
- *Non-convergence*: Score error \(\varepsilon(t)\) plateaus above tolerance. Diagnose by checking gradient norms \(\|\nabla S\|\) and ensuring score smoothness; reduce step size.
- *Degenerate sampler*: All boundary samples collapse to a small region. Verify base measure \(\mu_x\) diversity; apply repulsion (Algorithm 2).
- *Poor quantile regions*: Empirical coverage of \(C_\alpha\) deviates from \(\pi([\alpha,1])\). Ensure flow hits the boundary to high precision (\(\varepsilon < 10^{-6}\)); apply reconformalization if needed.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Flow convergence test**: High-dimensional isotropic Gaussian (p=10–100) with \(\ell_2\) score; verify \(\varepsilon \to 10^{-6}\) and spectral entropy matches data entropy (Figure 4).
2. **CPD vs baselines**: Compare CPDs (using \(\ell_2\) or composite scores) with Dropout, Ensembles, IQN on PDE inversion and precipitation downscaling using Energy Distance, Log Spectral Distance, and local MMD (Table 2).
3. **Targeted sampling**: Hurricane trajectories with CGT score; sample \(\alpha \in (0,0.1)\) to obtain extreme but geometrically plausible paths (Figure 7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to non-differentiable scores** – How can the nonconformity flow be extended to non-smooth scores (e.g., \(\ell_1\), kNN) while preserving convergence guarantees? The current method requires \(\nabla S\) to exist and be non-zero near the boundary; smoothing or subgradient flows are potential directions.
2. **Systematic tangential velocity design** – Can tangential velocity fields be designed systematically (beyond ad-hoc repulsion) to achieve uniform or otherwise desirable coverage of the conformal boundary \(\partial C_\alpha(x)\) while still converging? A theoretical characterization of the boundary measure induced by the pure gradient flow and a family of principled tangential fields are needed.
3. **Optimal choice of mixing and base measures** – How should the mixing measure \(\pi\) and the base measure \(\mu_x\) be chosen for CPDs to optimize downstream objectives (sharpness, rare-event sampling) while maintaining validity? An optimization framework with constraints and empirical demonstrations would guide practitioners.
4. **Infinite-dimensional output spaces** – Can the method be extended to genuinely infinite-dimensional output spaces (e.g., functions, operators) without relying on finite-dimensional discretization? A gradient flow formulation in a Hilbert space (using Fréchet derivatives) and adaptive basis representations are suggested.

## Limitations
- The flow requires a differentiable nonconformity score with non-vanishing gradient near the boundary; non-smooth scores (e.g., \(\ell_1\), kNN) cause failure, limiting applicability.
- The base measure \(\mu_x\) is typically taken as the empirical calibration distribution, which may not fully cover the test data manifold and can bias CPD samples.
- The mixing measure \(\pi\) is chosen heuristically; no theoretical guidance exists for selecting \(\pi\) and \(\mu_x\) to optimize trade-offs between sharpness, diversity, and validity.

## Confidence
- **Flow convergence for smooth scores**: High (theoretical Proposition 3.1 and empirical Figure 4).
- **Flow convergence for non-smooth scores**: Low (empirical failure in Section 4.1).
- **CPD calibration**: High (theoretical Proposition 3.4) / Medium (numerical approximation via finite \(\varepsilon\) and finite samples may cause small deviations).
- **Tangent repulsion effectiveness**: Medium (empirical Table 1 shows improved spread but no theoretical guarantee of uniformity or coverage preservation).

## Next Checks
1. **Non-smooth score evaluation**: Implement flows with \(\ell_1\) and kNN scores; compare convergence and CPD fidelity against smoothed variants (Huber, kernel smoothing) to identify when smoothing suffices.
2. **Base measure sensitivity study**: For each dataset, vary \(\mu_x\) (empirical Dcal, Gaussian \(N(0,\sigma^2 I)\), uniform in data range); measure changes in CPD sample diversity (spectral entropy) and calibration error. Determine conditions where misalignment harms performance.
3. **Repulsion theoretical validation**: On synthetic level sets (sphere, ellipsoid), apply repulsion with varying step size \(\delta\); check adherence to \(S(y)=\tau_\alpha\) and uniformity of sampled points (nearest-neighbor distance distribution). Propose theoretical bounds on \(\delta\) to avoid boundary exit.
