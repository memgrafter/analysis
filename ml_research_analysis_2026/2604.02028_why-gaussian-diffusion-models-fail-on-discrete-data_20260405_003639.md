# Why Gaussian Diffusion Models Fail on Discrete Data?
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.02028
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02028
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Gaussian diffusion models fail on discrete data because during reverse sampling the noisified distribution becomes multimodal, causing trajectories to enter low-density regions; heuristics like self-conditioning and q-sampling mitigate this, as shown on text, code, and protein generation.

## Executive Summary
Gaussian diffusion models, such as DDPM, are widely used for continuous data but often fail when adapted to discrete domains like text or protein sequences. The paper reveals that during reverse sampling the noisified distribution $p(x_t)$ becomes multimodal, and the DDPM update can place the sample in low-density regions between modes, leading to incorrect generations. A critical interval around $t/T < 0.4$ is identified using a Random Hierarchy Model, where this effect is most pronounced. The authors propose three inference‑time heuristics—self‑conditioning (SC), switching to $q$‑sampling within the critical interval, and Minimum Bayes‑Risk (MBR) resampling—to keep latents in high‑density regions. Extensive experiments on text (ROCStories, Wikipedia), code (CoNaLa), and proteins (SwissProt) verify the analysis and demonstrate that self‑conditioning is essential, while combining it with $q$‑sampling yields substantial gains (e.g., ROCStories Mauve 16 → 82, perplexity 149 → 41). The findings provide a theoretical explanation for diffusion’s discrete‑data struggles and practical mitigation techniques.

## Method Summary
The paper’s analysis centers on a toy Random Hierarchy Model (RHM) of discrete sequences, which allows precise tracking of correctness and density along sampling trajectories. For a standard DDPM reverse update, the authors show that after a certain noising level the marginal distribution $p(x_t)$ becomes multimodal. When the denoising network’s prediction $\hat{x}_\theta(x_t)$ is inaccurate, the DDPM step can land $x_{t-1}$ in a region of near‑zero density, corrupting the sample. This defines a critical interval ($t/T < 0.4$) where such errors dominate. To address the issue, they introduce three heuristics: (1) self‑conditioning, where during training the model receives its previous prediction as extra input, stabilizing consecutive outputs; (2) $q$‑sampling, i.e., drawing the reverse step from the forward process $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$ instead of DDPM during the critical interval; and (3) MBR resampling after a certain timestep to select the highest‑utility candidate. The methods are evaluated on Diffusion‑LM and TEncDM (shallow vs. contextual encodings) across multiple text, code, and protein datasets. Results show self‑conditioning consistently improves correctness, and combining it with $q$‑sampling at $t/T \in [0.3, 0.5]$ yields further gains, albeit with reduced diversity. Reproduction involves replicating the RHM analysis, ablating self‑conditioning on ROCStories, and sweeping $q$‑sampling activation on XSum.

## Key Results
- Self‑conditioning alone boosts ROCStories Mauve from 16 to 71 and perplexity from 149 to 74; adding $q$‑sampling further improves Mauve to 82 and perplexity to 41.
- The failure mode is confined to a critical interval ($t/T < 0.4$) where the noisified distribution becomes multimodal and DDPM trajectories enter low‑density regions.
- Self‑conditioning reduces prediction step sizes, while $q$‑sampling yields higher‑density updates for incorrect predictions; both keep trajectories within high‑density modes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** DDPM trajectories enter low‑density regions between modes during the critical interval ($t/T<0.4$) because the noisified distribution $p(x_t)$ is multimodal and inaccurate predictions cause updates to land in near‑zero density areas.  
**Mechanism:** For discrete data represented as a mixture of delta functions, $p(x_t)$ becomes multimodal from intermediate timesteps onward. When the model’s prediction $\hat{x}_\theta(x_t)$ is inaccurate, the DDPM update (Eq. 4) can place $x_{t-1}$ in a region with near‑zero density, leading to unreliable denoising and corrupted final samples.  
**Core assumption:** The posterior $p(x_0\mid x_t)$ is highly uncertain in the critical interval while the forward marginals are concentrated near data points.  
**Evidence:** Figure 2 shows correctness and density diverging between correct and incorrect trajectories for $t/T\in[0.4,0.5]$; the fraction of same‑mode pairs drops sharply, confirming emergent multimodality.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Self‑conditioning (SC) stabilizes consecutive predictions by feeding the previous prediction as extra input, reducing the distance between them and preventing large jumps that could leave high‑density regions.  
**Mechanism:** During inference, SC passes the previous prediction $\hat{x}_\theta^{t+1}(0)$ as extra context to the model, encouraging gradual changes. Smaller step sizes $\|\hat{x}_\theta(x_t)-\hat{x}_\theta(x_{t-1})\|$ help avoid crossing low‑density barriers between modes.  
**Core assumption:** The model can effectively use its own previous prediction as an informative signal; this assumption fails in purely continuous domains where such conditioning brings little benefit.  
**Evidence:** Section 3.3: Figure 5c shows SC improves correctness in the critical interval; Figure 5b shows SC reduces prediction step sizes.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Switching from DDPM to $q$‑sampling within the critical interval yields updates with higher density under the target distribution and smaller step sizes for incorrect predictions, keeping trajectories within high‑density modes.  
**Mechanism:** $q$‑sampling (Eq. 5) draws $x_{t-1}$ from the forward transition $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$. Compared to DDPM, it has lower marginal variance (Theorem 1) and moves predictors farther from the current sample when they are likely wrong (Figure 4b). This reduces the chance of landing in low‑density regions; Figure 4c shows $q$‑sampling updates have higher density when predictions are incorrect.  
**Core assumption:** The forward process $q(x_t\mid x_0)$ provides a useful reverse update even though it is not a valid reverse kernel; this works particularly well in the multimodal regime of discrete data.  
**Evidence:** Section 3.3: Figure 4 compares single‑step updates; $q$‑sampling improves correctness for incorrect predictions at $t/T<0.4$. Table 1 and Figure 7 show that activating $q$‑sampling near $t/T\approx0.3$–0.5 consistently improves Mauve and perplexity.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Encoder (E) maps token sequences to continuous embeddings $x_0$. Denoising network ($\hat{x}_\theta$) predicts a clean $x_0$ from noisy $x_t$ given timestep $t$ and optional self‑conditioning input (previous prediction). Solver: default DDPM (Eq. 4), optionally switch to $q$‑sampling (Eq. 5) after activation timestep $t_{act}$. Decoder (D) maps final $x_0$ back to tokens via nearest‑neighbor lookup in the embedding table. Self‑conditioning input: during training, with probability $p$ the network receives its previous zero‑conditioning prediction as extra context; at inference it receives the last output except at $t=T$.

E -> $x_0$ -> (forward noising) -> $x_t$ -> $\hat{x}_\theta(x_t, [\text{self-cond}])$ -> (Solver: DDPM or q-sampling) -> $x_{t-1}$ -> repeat until $t=1$ -> $x_0$ -> D.

### Critical Path
1. Sample $t$ uniformly.
2. Generate $x_t$ from $x_0$ using the forward noise schedule.
3. Predict $\hat{x}_\theta(x_t)$ (optionally with self‑conditioning input).
4. If $t \le t_{act}$, use $q$‑sampling to compute $x_{t-1}$; else use DDPM.
5. Repeat until $t=1$, then decode $\hat{x}_\theta(x_1)$ to tokens.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Latent space type:** Shallow, frozen embeddings create isolated modes → require strong heuristics. Contextual encodings (e.g., BERT) produce smoother manifold → DDPM works better, SC/$q$‑sampling give smaller gains.
- **SC probability $p$:** Default 0.5 may mismatch inference (always SC except first step). Increasing $p$ toward 1 improves quality (Table 1) but slightly increases training time.
- **$q$‑sampling $t_{act}$:** Should lie in critical interval (typically $t/T \in [0.3,0.5]$); too early harms early‑stage sampling, too late has negligible effect.
- **Diversity vs quality:** All heuristics sacrifice some diversity for higher correctness; MBR with few candidates can further boost quality at cost of speed.

### Failure Signatures
- High perplexity, low Mauve: Likely SC off or $q$‑sampling mis‑tuned. Check SC activation and $t_{act}$.
- Very repetitive outputs: Possibly over‑use of $q$‑sampling or SC probability too high, causing mode collapse.
- No improvement with SC: Model may already use contextual encodings; try lowering $t_{act}$ or avoid $q$‑sampling.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate RHM:** Implement Random Hierarchy Model with $L=3$, $m=4$, $s=2$, $V=16$, $n=8$. Train a small Transformer diffusion (4 layers, hidden 64). Generate sampling trajectories with DDPM; plot correctness, density, and mode clustering vs. optimal predictions to replicate Figure 2 and identify critical interval.
2. **Add self‑conditioning:** Starting from baseline model, train with SC probability $p=0.5$; measure correctness/Mauve. Then increase $p$ to 0.95 and observe improvement; verify SC reduces prediction distance ($\|\hat{x}_\theta(x_t)-\hat{x}_\theta(x_{t-1})\|$).
3. **Introduce $q$‑sampling:** With SC already enabled, sweep $t_{act}$ across $[0,0.6]$ and record quality metrics. Identify peak region (usually $t_{act}/T\approx0.3$–0.4). Combine SC and $q$‑sampling and report diversity‑quality trade‑off.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Analytical prediction of critical interval:** For a given discrete dataset and noise schedule, can we analytically predict the timestep at which $p(x_t)$ becomes multimodal? The RHM identifies a transition around $t/T=0.4$, but factors controlling onset are not characterized; without a theory, the interval must be tuned per dataset.
- **Optimal SC training probability $p$:** Why does $p=0.95$ outperform $p=0.5$ on multiple datasets? The mechanism and an optimal schedule for $p$ remain unclear; need analysis of prediction magnitudes and variance.
- **Designing inherently continuous latent spaces:** Can we eliminate the need for heuristics by creating latent spaces that are both continuous and semantically meaningful? Current contextual encodings help but still benefit from SC/$q$‑sampling; no method optimizes smoothness of $p(x_t)$ directly.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter details (noise schedule parameters, $q$‑sampling activation step mapping, TEncDM architecture specifics) are not fully disclosed, hindering exact replication.
- The identified critical interval ($t/T<0.4$) may be schedule‑specific; its universality across different noise schedules remains unproven.
- The proposed heuristics reduce sample diversity, and this reduction is not thoroughly quantified across diverse settings.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (multimodality causes low‑density trajectories):** High
- **Mechanism 2 (self‑conditioning stabilizes predictions):** Medium
- **Mechanism 3 ($q$‑sampling keeps updates high‑density):** Medium
