# Diversified Scaling Inference in Time Series Foundation Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17376  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17376  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Diversified inference scaling via tailored input perturbations yields up to 50% performance gains in time series foundation models without any parameter updates, overcoming the saturation of standard best-of-N sampling.

## Executive Summary
Time Series Foundation Models (TSFMs) are large pre-trained models that generate probabilistic forecasts. Standard inference scaling—drawing more samples from the fixed predictive distribution and aggregating (best-of-N)—often saturates quickly because the model's output distribution has limited support and cannot reach high-quality predictions near the ground truth. This paper introduces **diversified inference scaling**: before sampling, the input context is perturbed (e.g., with noise or task-specific transforms) to expand the effective support of the generative distribution. Theoretically, the authors prove that under mild conditions the diversified support strictly contains the standard one, and derive a critical sample threshold $N^*$ beyond which diversified sampling outperforms standard. Empirically, across multiple TSFMs and datasets, proper diversification (within a narrow fidelity band, cosine similarity 0.95–1.00) reduces MSE by up to 50%. Task-specific perturbations consistently beat generic ones. The proposed **RobustMSE** metric measures consistent improvement under fixed compute. The findings establish inference-time design as a crucial, compute-efficient lever for optimizing TSFMs.

## Method Summary
The method operates on frozen TSFMs during test time. Given an input context $\mathbf{X}$, instead of directly sampling forecasts from $P(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{X})$, it first generates $K$ perturbed variants $\mathbf{X}'_k$ using a perturbation distribution $Q(\cdot|\mathbf{X},\pi_S)$ (where $\pi_S$ specifies perturbation type and strength). For each $\mathbf{X}'_k$, it draws $N_k$ stochastic forecast samples (with temperature $\tau>0$) from the TSFM. All $N=\sum N_k$ candidates are aggregated either via Exact Match (selecting the sample with minimal error against a validation target, used in evaluation) or Majority Voting (element-wise median, practical deployment). This process expands the effective support of the predictive distribution $\mathcal{S}_{\text{div}} = \text{support}(\int P(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{X}') Q(\mathbf{X}'|\mathbf{X}) d\mathbf{X}')$, which strictly contains the standard support $\mathcal{S}_{\text{std}} = \text{support}(P(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{X}))$. To quantify consistent gains under a fixed sample budget $N$, the authors propose **RobustMSE**: for each perturbation that improves over standard sampling by less than 20% (deemed “effective”), compute EM/MV over $N=64$ samples and average across multiple trials; the mean score reflects model headroom.

## Key Results
- Standard best-of-N inference saturates rapidly across TSFMs, context lengths, and temperatures due to distributional bias; increasing sample count yields diminishing returns.
- Diversified inference scaling with well-chosen perturbations achieves substantial MSE reductions (up to 50%) without any parameter updates.
- Optimal performance occurs within a narrow fidelity-diversity trade-off band: perturbed contexts must retain high similarity to the original (cosine similarity 0.95–1.00); deviations degrade gains.
- Task-specific perturbations (e.g., based on trend/seasonality decomposition) consistently outperform task-agnostic ones (e.g., Gaussian noise).
- RobustMSE effectively ranks models by their potential under fixed compute; TimesFM and Moirai show lowest RobustMSE, indicating greater headroom.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Standard sampling-based inference scaling fails to adhere to scaling laws due to insufficient exploration of the solution space, leading to rapidly saturating performance gains.  
- **Mechanism:** Repeatedly drawing samples from the fixed predictive distribution $P(\cdot|\mathbf{X})$ refines the empirical approximation of that distribution but cannot expand its support. As $N\to\infty$, the best-of-N loss converges to the infimum of the loss over the support $\mathcal{S}_{\text{std}}$, which may be far from the ground truth $\mathbf{Y}$. Consequently, simply increasing $N$ yields diminishing returns.  
- **Core assumption:** The distribution induced by the original input $\mathbf{X}$ and model configuration $\zeta$ has a limited support that excludes high-quality predictions near $\mathbf{Y}$.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "standard sampling often fail to adhere to scaling laws due to insufficient exploration of the solution space."  
  - [section 3] "standard sampling saturates rapidly due to distributional bias in the fixed predictive distribution"  
  - [corpus] Neighboring work "Towards Neural Scaling Laws for Time Series Foundation Models" focuses on training-time scaling, underscoring that inference scaling is less understood.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Diversified input perturbations expand the effective support of the generative distribution, enabling access to higher-quality predictions that are otherwise unreachable.  
- **Mechanism:** By first sampling perturbed inputs $\mathbf{X}' \sim Q(\cdot|\mathbf{X},\pi_S)$ and then predictions from $P(\cdot|\mathbf{X}')$, the candidate set is drawn from the marginal mixture $P_{\text{mix}} = \int P(\cdot|\mathbf{X}') Q(\mathbf{X}'|\mathbf{X}) d\mathbf{X}'$. Under the mild assumption that $Q$ covers the original input, the support $\mathcal{S}_{\text{div}} = \text{support}(P_{\text{mix}})$ strictly contains $\mathcal{S}_{\text{std}}$. Therefore, as $N\to\infty$, the best-of-N loss from diversified sampling can achieve a lower infimum because the larger set may contain $\mathbf{Y}^*$ with $L(\mathbf{Y}^*,\mathbf{Y}) < \inf_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{std}}} L$.  
- **Core assumption:** The perturbation distribution $Q$ can produce inputs whose predictions are closer to the true target $\mathbf{Y}$ than any prediction from the original input.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "diversified inference scaling via tailored time series perturbations that expand the generative distribution's support."  
  - [section 4.1] Proposition 4.2 (Asymptotic Lower Bound): "Under the mild assumption that the perturbation distribution covers the original input, we have $\mathcal{S}_{\text{std}} \subseteq \mathcal{S}_{\text{div}}$. Consequently, $\lim_{N\to\infty} \text{EM}_N^{\text{div}} \le \lim_{N\to\infty} \text{EM}_N^{\text{std}}$."  
  - [section 5.1] "Introducing input perturbations expands the predictive distribution's support without discarding the original solution capabilities."

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The practical benefit of diversified sampling hinges on a fidelity-diversity trade-off and a critical sample size $N^*$; within an optimal perturbation relevance band (e.g., cosine similarity 0.95–1.00) and with $N > N^*$, diversified sampling statistically outperforms standard sampling.  
- **Mechanism:** Perturbations that are too conservative (high similarity) fail to sufficiently diversify, while overly aggressive ones (low similarity) yield many low-quality samples. The finite-sample analysis models the diversified distribution as yielding a good loss $L_{\text{good}}$ with probability $\rho$ and bad loss $L_{\text{bad}}$ otherwise, leading to a threshold $N^* = \frac{\ln((L_{\text{bad}}-L_{\text{good}})/(L_0-L_{\text{g}}))}{\ln(1/(1-\rho))}$. If $N$ exceeds $N^*$, the probability of sampling at least one good candidate dominates. Empirically, the fidelity-diversity trade-off is observed as a U-shaped curve where MSE drops then rises as perturbation strength increases, with an optimal narrow band.  
- **Core assumption:** The performance of perturbed predictions follows a bimodal distribution: some perturbations yield better-than-baseline forecasts (good), others yield worse (bad).  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "proper diversified scaling yields substantial gains up to 50% without parameter updates" and "optimal within a narrow fidelity-diversity trade-off band (cosine similarity 0.95–1.00)".  
  - [section 4.1] Proposition 4.3 (Critical Sample Threshold): "Diversified sampling outperforms standard sampling in expectation if and only if the sample budget satisfies $N > N^*$."  
  - [section 5.1] "Performance exhibits a clear non-monotonic relationship with perturbation relevance: MSE decreases as relevance increases, reaches an optimal minimum, and then slightly rises. Notably, the best performance consistently occurs within a narrow relevance band, approximately between cosine similarity 0.95 and 1.00."

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Context Extractor -> Perturbation Module -> Parallel Inference Engine -> Aggregator -> RobustMSE Evaluator (optional)

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Extract the most recent $L$ steps of the raw time series as context $\mathbf{X}$.  
  2. Generate $K$ perturbed inputs $\mathbf{X}'_k = \pi_S(\mathbf{X})$ using chosen perturbation(s); total sample budget $N$ is distributed among them.  
  3. For each $\mathbf{X}'_k$, run TSFM inference with temperature $\tau>0$ to produce $N_k$ forecast samples $\mathbf{Y}_{k,j}$ (parallelizable).  
  4. Collect all forecasts into candidate set $\mathcal{Y} = \{\mathbf{Y}_{k,j}\}$.  
  5. Aggregate: for Exact Match, select $\arg\min_{\mathbf{Y}\in\mathcal{Y}} \text{MSE}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y}_{\text{val}})$; for Majority Voting, compute element-wise median across $\mathcal{Y}$.  
  6. Return aggregated forecast.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Perturbation choice:** Task-agnostic (e.g., Gaussian noise) is fast and generic; task-specific (e.g., STL-based) may yield larger gains but require domain analysis.  
  - **Perturbation strength:** Must maintain high fidelity (cosine similarity $\ge 0.95$) to stay in the optimal band; too strong causes degradation. Controlled via noise level $\eta$, insertion length $\ell$, etc.  
  - **Sample allocation $N$:** Larger $N$ improves performance but with diminishing returns; critical $N^*$ from theory suggests a minimum beyond which benefits appear. MV typically converges by $N \approx 32$–$128$; EM may require more.  
  - **Aggregation:** EM is lower-bound but requires ground truth; MV is practical but may be suboptimal if candidate distribution is multi-modal.  
  - **Parallelization:** Inference calls are embarrassingly parallel; batch computations for efficiency, especially with large $N$.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - No improvement or degradation with increased $N$: Perturbations are destructive (outside fidelity band) or model not benefiting from diversity; check cosine similarity of perturbed inputs.  
  - High variance across trials: Perturbations too strong or $N$ insufficient to average out noise.  
  - EM << MV gap: Few very good samples exist but median is poor; consider increasing $N$ or improving perturbation quality.  
  - Convergence at high MSE: Perturbation set misaligned with task; revisit perturbation design.  
  - Computational bottleneck: Latency dominated by perturbed inference; reduce $N$ or use cheaper perturbations.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline and standard scaling:** Run single-sample inference (no perturbation) to get baseline MSE. Then increase sample count $N$ (same input $\mathbf{X}$, $\tau>0$) and observe MSE vs $N$. Expect limited improvement and saturation, confirming need for diversification.  
  2. **Perturbation effectiveness screening:** For each perturbation type, run diversified inference with fixed $N$ (e.g., 128) and record MSE relative to baseline. Classify as effective if MSE reduction $>5\%$ and failure if MSE increase $>20\%$. Vary perturbation strength to locate optimal fidelity band (plot MSE vs cosine similarity).  
  3. **Aggregation and scaling behavior:** Pick best perturbation from experiment 2, vary $N$ (e.g., 2,4,8,32,128), and plot scaling curves for both EM (using validation targets) and MV. Verify diversified surpasses standard beyond certain $N$, and note MV convergence points.

## Limitations
- Task-specific perturbation hyperparameters (e.g., STL period, local window) are unspecified; reproduction requires guesswork or repository inspection.
- The precise list of “effective perturbations” used in RobustMSE is not provided; it depends on a >20% MSE drop filter that must be recomputed per model-dataset pair.
- Decoding settings (e.g., temperature) for models like Moirai and TimeMoE are ambiguous; the paper claims temperature is ineffective but replication details are missing.
- Pre-processing steps (normalization, tokenization) inside each TSFM’s inference pipeline are hidden, potentially affecting reproducibility.
- Theoretical assumptions (perturbation coverage, bimodal loss distribution) may not hold universally, limiting generalizability of the derived threshold $N^*$.

## Confidence

- Diversified expands support: High (rigorous proof plus consistent gains).  
- Threshold & fidelity band: Medium (estimated parameters, narrow band based on limited sweeps).  
- Task-specific > agnostic: Medium (promising but domain-specific results).  
- RobustMSE: Medium (depends on “effective” perturbation definition).
