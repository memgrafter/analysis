# Vichara: Appellate Judgment Prediction and Explanation for the Indian Judicial System

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18346
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18346
- **Reference Count:** 39
- **Primary Result:** A multi-stage LLM pipeline that predicts and explains Indian appellate judgments by decomposing cases into structured "decision points," achieving state-of-the-art macro-F1 scores (81.5 on PredEx, 80.3 on ILDC_expert) and high human-rated interpretability.

## Executive Summary
Vichara is a system designed to tackle the severe backlog of appellate cases in India by automating the prediction of final outcomes (grant/dismiss) and generating legally sound, interpretable explanations. It addresses the complexity of lengthy judgment documents by structuring legal reasoning into discrete, attributable "decision points." These points, combined with a rhetorical analysis of the text, feed into a sequential LLM pipeline that produces IRAC-style explanations. Evaluations on Indian appellate datasets show it outperforms prior specialized legal models in prediction and receives high scores from legal experts for clarity, logical linking, and usefulness.

## Method Summary
Vichara processes an appellate case document through six sequential stages. First, a BiLSTM-CRF model classifies each sentence into one of seven rhetorical roles (e.g., Facts, Rulings). From the 'Facts' sentences, an LLM extracts a structured case context (parties, issue, stances). Next, an LLM extracts structured decision points—each capturing a legal issue, the deciding authority, outcome, reasoning, and a flag indicating if it was made by the present appellate court. All decision points are filtered to retain only those from the present court. Using these filtered points, the final 'Ruling by Present Court' sentence, and the case context, another LLM synthesizes a summary of the present court's final order. This ruling is compared against the appellant's stance (from context) by a predictor LLM to produce a binary outcome. Finally, an explanation generator LLM consumes all prior structured outputs to produce a five-section (Facts, Issues, Law, Reasoning, Conclusion) explanation grounded in the extracted data.

## Key Results
- Achieved state-of-the-art macro-F1 scores on two datasets: 81.5 on PredEx and 80.3 on ILDC_expert, outperforming the INLegalLlama baseline.
- Human evaluation by legal experts rated GPT-4o mini’s explanations highest in Clarity (4.57), Linking (4.96), and Usefulness (4.37) on a 5-point scale.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical role of all pipeline stages; removing the Decision Point Extraction stage caused the largest performance drop (~11 macro-F1 points on ILDC_expert).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Decomposing Judgments into Attributable Decision Points
- **Claim:** Structuring a case into discrete decision points (issue, decision maker, outcome, reasoning, temporal context) isolates the core determinations of the present appellate court, enabling accurate prediction and interpretable explanation by reducing noise from historical rulings.
- **Mechanism:** The pipeline extracts all decisions and then filters strictly to those made by the present court (`present_court_decision=True`). This scopes all downstream reasoning to the relevant judicial act.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] decomposition isolates core determinations; [section 5.3] details schema and filtering; ablation (Table 4) shows largest F1 drop when this stage is removed.
- **Break condition:** Legal reasoning is not modularly segmentable, or LLM fails to attribute decisions correctly.

### Structured IRAC-Style Explanations
- **Claim:** Presenting explanations in a fixed, IRAC-inspired format (Facts, Issues, Law, Reasoning, Conclusion) enhances interpretability and trust for legal professionals by aligning with familiar doctrinal structures.
- **Mechanism:** The final explanation generator is explicitly prompted to organize output into the five sections, grounding each in the previously extracted structured inputs.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] structured format enhances interpretability; human evaluation (Table 2) shows top scores for Clarity, Linking, Usefulness.
- **Break condition:** Target jurisdiction or professional preference does not align with IRAC, or the structure forces artificial segmentation.

### Sequential Multi-Stage Pipeline
- **Claim:** Breaking the complex task into specialized, constrained subtasks with intermediate structured representations yields more reliable outputs than an end-to-end monolithic approach.
- **Mechanism:** Each LLM call operates on a focused, pre-processed subset (e.g., only 'Facts' sentences for context). The stages build upon each other, with clear data contracts (e.g., decision point list).
- **Evidence:** [section 5] describes six stages; ablation (Table 4) shows performance degrades when any stage is removed; author note on synergy.
- **Break condition:** Error propagation between stages becomes severe, or the overhead of multiple LLM calls outweighs benefits.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Rhetorical Role Classification of Legal Sentences**
  - **Why needed:** The entire pipeline starts by labeling sentences (e.g., Facts, Rulings). The accuracy of 'Facts' and 'Ruling by Present Court' labels is foundational; errors here corrupt all downstream data.
  - **Quick check:** Given "The Tribunal's order is set aside," the correct 7-class label is "Ruling by Present Court" (if said by the appellate court) or "Ruling by Lower Court" (if describing the Tribunal). Distinguishing these is critical for extracting the correct final dispositive order.

- **Concept: Appellate Case Structure & "Present Court" Disambiguation**
  - **Why needed:** Vichara must distinguish lower court rulings from the current appellate court's determinations. Its filter for `present_court_decision=True` depends on correctly identifying which court authored which part of the document.
  - **Quick check:** In a Supreme Court document mentioning a Tribunal and High Court, only decisions explicitly attributed to or made within the "Supreme Court" section are considered "present court decisions."

- **Concept: Prompt Engineering for Structured Extraction with LLMs**
  - **Why needed:** Every LLM stage uses prompts to enforce a specific schema (JSON) and constrain outputs (e.g., prevent hallucination of non-existent entities). This ensures consistency for downstream stages.
  - **Quick check:** The Decision Point prompt includes instructions like "Extract only from the provided text" and "If a field is not present, write 'N/A'." These prevent the model from inventing a `decision_maker` or merging multiple issues, which would break filtering and synthesis.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Raw Document -> Sentence Segmenter -> Rhetorical Role Classifier (BiLSTM-CRF) -> Parallel Paths: (Facts -> Case Context LLM) & (All Sentences -> Decision Point LLM) -> Filter Decision Points (present_court_decision=True) + Final Ruling Sentence -> Present Court Ruling LLM -> Judgment Predictor LLM -> Structured Explanation LLM -> Output (prediction, explanation)
- **Critical path:** The filtering step that retains only `present_court_decision=True` decision points is the pivotal operation that scopes the final ruling generation and, consequently, the prediction and explanation to the current appeal's reasoning.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Multi-stage vs. Single Call:** Stages improve accuracy/interpretability (per ablation) but increase latency, cost, and risk of error propagation.
  - **Strict Schema vs. Flexibility:** Enforced JSON fields ensure consistency but may miss nuances not in the 6-field decision point schema.
  - **LLM Choice:** Performance is model-dependent (GPT-4o mini > open models). Using smaller local models reduces cost but may require more prompt tuning.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Early Stage:** Rhetorical classifier mislabels a key fact as 'Argument' -> incomplete/incorrect case context.
  - **Mid-Pipeline:** Decision point extractor misses a key present court decision or mis-flags `present_court_decision` -> corrupted ruling summary.
  - **Late Stage:** Explanation generator ignores inputs, produces generic text -> low human "Linking" and "Usefulness" scores despite correct prediction.
  - **Systemic:** LLM outputs non-JSON or hallucinates entities (e.g., invents parties).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  Validate the Rhetorical Role Classifier on 20 manually annotated sentences from a sample case. Compute per-category F1; low scores for "Facts" or "Ruling by Present Court" invalidate the pipeline's foundation.
  2.  Sanity-check Decision Point Extraction on a short, clear case excerpt. Manually extract 3-4 decision points, compare to LLM outputs. Check for completeness, correct `decision_maker` attribution, and accurate `present_court_decision` flags.
  3.  Run the full pipeline on one ILDC_expert test case. Inspect all intermediates (context, filtered points, present ruling). Verify the final explanation logically uses the filtered points and that the predicted outcome matches a human reading of the dispositive order.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1.  How can Vichara be effectively adapted to legal jurisdictions with different procedural structures and language conventions, such as civil law systems or non-English speaking countries?
2.  What specific methods (e.g., prompt optimization, model distillation) can most effectively reduce the computational overhead of Vichara's multi-stage pipeline while maintaining prediction and explanation quality?
3.  How can the sensitivity of Vichara's LLM-based stages to prompt phrasing be mitigated to ensure consistent and reliable outputs?
4.  Does the binary outcome abstraction (Appeal Granted/Dismissed) in Vichara overlook important nuances in appellate decisions with mixed or partial outcomes, and how would a more granular prediction affect model performance?
5.  How does Vichara's explanation quality and judgment prediction generalize to a wider diversity of case types, court levels, and legal professionals beyond the limited sample in the current study?

## Limitations
- The exact specification and availability of the hierarchical BiLSTM-CRF rhetorical role classifier and the fine-tuned SAT-12L sentence segmenter are unclear, blocking faithful reproduction.
- The ILDC_expert dataset's public accessibility is uncertain, and the specific test split used may not be readily available.
- The pipeline's sensitivity to prompt phrasing and the propagation of errors across sequential LLM stages are acknowledged but not systematically quantified.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Decision Point Decomposition):** High. Strong empirical support from ablation showing the largest performance impact.
- **Mechanism 2 (IRAC-Structured Explanations):** Medium-High. Supported by human evaluation but lacks ablation isolating the format's specific contribution.
- **Mechanism 3 (Sequential Multi-Stage Pipeline):** Medium. Ablation confirms synergy, but risks of error propagation and computational cost are not fully measured.
