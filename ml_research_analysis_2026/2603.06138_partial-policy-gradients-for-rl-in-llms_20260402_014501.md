# Partial Policy Gradients for RL in LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.06138
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06138
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Partial policy gradients (PPG) with K-step lookahead (K=2,3) achieve up to 99.5% persona consistency on Qwen, reducing drift and outperforming base models (79.9%).

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles persona drift in LLM-based role-playing conversations, where models gradually deviate from assigned personas. Existing RL methods like PPO and GRPO suffer from statistical inefficiency due to high gradient variance over long horizons. The authors propose partial policy gradients (PPG), which optimize only a subset of future rewards, yielding simpler policy classes (greedy, K-step lookahead, segment) with lower variance. Both online and offline algorithms are derived. Experiments on Consistent-LLMs across education, therapy, and chatting domains with Qwen, Llama, and Gemma models show that K-step lookahead policies (particularly K=2,3) significantly improve persona consistency—up to 99.5% on Qwen—and stabilize long-horizon behavior by mitigating drift. The work reveals a tradeoff between policy complexity and data efficiency: greedy excels in low-data regimes, while full planning dominates with abundant data. PPG provides a principled framework for temporal credit assignment in LLM RL.

## Method Summary
Partial Policy Gradients (PPG) generalize the standard policy gradient by restricting the set of future rewards that contribute to the gradient at each step. Formally, given a trajectory decomposed into per-step rewards \(r_t\), the total reward \(r(x,\tau_n) = \sum_{t=1}^n r_t\). In PPG, each step \(t\) considers only a subset \(R_t \subseteq [n] \setminus [t-1]\), leading to gradient estimator \(g(B) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_i\sum_t (\sum_{\ell\in R_t} r_{i,\ell}) \nabla\log\pi(a_{i,t}|\cdot)\). Different choices of \(R_t\) define policy classes: greedy (\(R_t=\{t\}\)), K-step lookahead (\(R_t=[t+K-1]\setminus[t-1]\)), segment (predefined segments), and full (\(R_t=[n]\setminus[t-1]\)). The authors provide both online and offline algorithms, with the offline variant using advantage normalization within groups (e.g., same persona/topic) to correct for distribution shift. PPG reduces gradient variance by summing fewer random variables, thus improving statistical efficiency, especially for long-horizon tasks like persona-consistent dialogue.

## Key Results
- K-step lookahead PPG (K=2,3) achieves up to 99.5% persona consistency on Qwen rollouts, compared to 79.9% for the base model.
- Simpler policies (greedy) excel in low-data regimes, while full planning (PG) dominates with abundant data, demonstrating a complexity–efficiency tradeoff.
- PPG stabilizes long-horizon behavior: K-step policies produce smoother persona consistency residuals, reducing both monotonic drift and oscillations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Partial reward subsets simplify policies and reduce gradient variance
**Claim**: Optimizing for a subset of future rewards yields simpler policies that can be learned more reliably because empirical gradient estimates are more accurate.  
**Mechanism**: The total reward is additively decomposed into per-step rewards. For each step \(t\), only rewards in \(R_t\) influence the gradient. Smaller subsets reduce the number of terms summed in the gradient estimator, lowering its variance and improving concentration (Theorem 5). This enables faster and more stable learning.  
**Core assumption**: The reward decomposes as \(r(x,\tau_n) = \sum_{t=1}^n r_t\) with \(r_t \ge 0\), and the chosen subsets \(R_t\) correspond to simpler policy classes (e.g., greedy, K‑step lookahead).  
**Evidence**: Abstract states PPG yields simpler policies with lower gradient variance; Section 3.2 shows PPG is more statistically efficient; Theorem 5 provides variance bounds.  
**Break condition**: If the subset is too small (e.g., greedy), the policy becomes myopic and cannot capture long‑term dependencies, leading to performance plateaus on tasks requiring extended planning.

### Mechanism 2: K-step lookahead policies interpolate between greedy and full planning
**Claim**: K‑step lookahead policies balance planning depth and statistical efficiency, achieving better persona consistency in medium‑data regimes.  
**Mechanism**: Setting \(R_t = [\min\{t+K-1,n\}] \setminus [t-1]\) makes each action affect only the next \(K\) rewards. This creates a policy with bounded horizon \(K\), reducing variance relative to full PG while capturing more future effects than greedy.  
**Core assumption**: There exists an optimal lookahead horizon \(K\) that scales with the amount of training data; intermediate \(K\) values (e.g., 2–3) work best when data is moderate.  
**Evidence**: Abstract reports K=2,3 yields large improvements; Section 5.6 shows greedy excels with low data, K-step dominates with moderate data, and full PG wins with high data; Figure 4 shows optimal \(K\) increases with trajectory count.  
**Break condition**: If \(K\) is too large for the available data, gradient variance increases and performance degrades; if \(K\) is too small, the policy lacks sufficient planning horizon and suffers drift.

### Mechanism 3: Extended temporal scope smooths persona consistency over time
**Claim**: The temporal scope of credit assignment directly influences policy stability; longer horizons (up to an optimal \(K\)) smooth persona consistency across trajectory steps, reducing both drift and oscillations.  
**Mechanism**: The gradient update incorporates rewards over a window defined by \(R_t\). Larger windows allow the policy to anticipate future inconsistencies and adjust current actions, preventing error cascades and promoting stable residuals.  
**Core assumption**: Persona drift arises from error compounding due to myopic credit assignment; extending the horizon mitigates this by aligning immediate actions with nearer‑future rewards.  
**Evidence**: Section 5.5 shows K-step policies maintain stable residuals; Figure 2 displays smoother curves for K=2,3 compared to base (monotonic drift) and greedy (oscillations); qualitative examples (Section G.3) demonstrate 3-step PG maintaining consistency over 60 steps.  
**Break condition**: In some domains, full planning (\(K=n\)) induces oscillations, indicating over‑planning can be destabilizing; the optimal horizon is domain‑specific.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:
Reward decomposer -> Policy type configurator -> Gradient estimator -> Advantage normalizer (offline) -> Training loop -> Evaluation module

**Critical path**:
1. Define per‑step persona consistency reward (LLM judge prompts).
2. Choose policy class (e.g., 3‑Step‑PG) which determines \(R_t\) for all \(t\).
3. Load offline trajectories with context \(x\), actions, observations, and per‑step rewards.
4. Group trajectories (e.g., by persona/topic) for advantage normalization.
5. For each batch, compute advantages \(\tilde{r}_{i,t}\), estimate gradient \(g(B)\), update \(\theta\).
6. Periodically evaluate rollout and step‑level persona consistency on test set.
7. Tune \(K\), learning rate, batch size based on validation performance.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Policy complexity vs. statistical efficiency: Simpler policies (Greedy) have lower variance but insufficient planning; complex policies (full PG) require more data. \(K\) provides a tunable knob.
- Offline vs. online: Offline training is safe and uses existing data but may suffer distribution shift; online interaction could improve adaptation but increases cost and risk.
- Reward granularity: Per‑step rewards must faithfully reflect long‑term persona goals; poor decomposition can mislead learning.

**Failure signatures**:
- Monotonic drift (PC declines with steps) → credit assignment horizon too short or reward design flawed.
- Oscillations (PC alternates high/low) → myopic policy (\(K=1\)) over‑correcting repeatedly.
- High gradient variance / training instability → \(R_t\) too large for available data; consider smaller \(K\) or more aggregation.
- No improvement over Base → check reward decomposition, learning rate, or verify dataset contains consistent examples.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline reproduction**: Train and evaluate all policies (Base, PPO, Full PG, GreedyPG, 2–5‑Step‑PG) on Consistent‑LLMs education domain; report rollout and step‑level PC to replicate Tables 1–3.
2. **Data scaling sweep**: Vary number of training trajectories (50, 500, 5000) and record which policy achieves highest PC; verify that optimal \(K\) increases with data (Figure 3).
3. **Stability analysis**: Compute residual persona consistency across trajectory steps for each policy; produce plots like Figure 2 to visually confirm drift vs. smoothness patterns.

## Limitations
- Reward decomposition sensitivity: per‑step LLM judge may be noisy or biased.
- Limited model/domain coverage: only Qwen, Llama, Gemma in three domains.
- Offline training may introduce distribution shift; online benefit untested.
