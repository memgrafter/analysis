# Heterogeneous computing platform for real-time robotics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.09755
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09755
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** Real-time human-robot collaboration demonstrated via a theremin duet, integrating Loihi2 neuromorphic perception (4 mW hand tracking) with GPU-based cognitive models (Spaun 2.0, LLM).

## Executive Summary
The paper presents a heterogeneous computing platform that combines neuromorphic edge processors with high‑performance GPU clusters to achieve real‑time, energy‑efficient robotics for human‑robot collaboration. A central innovation is the integration of an Intel Loihi2 chip paired with a Dynamic Vision Sensor (DVS) for ultra‑low‑latency perception, and an NVIDIA DGX A100 cluster for cognitively demanding tasks via Spaun 2.0 and large language models. The system successfully drives the Ameca humanoid in a musical duet on the theremin, achieving sub‑millisecond perception latency and milliwatt‑scale power draw at the edge. This demonstration marks the first deployment of a real‑time neuromorphic workload tightly coupled with a large‑scale cognitive model, providing a blueprint for future autonomous robots that must balance responsiveness, computational complexity, and energy constraints.

## Method Summary
The architecture consists of two complementary subsystems. The neuromorphic perception pipeline uses a Prophesee EVK4‑HD DVS camera to stream asynchronous events to an Intel Loihi2 processor, where a deep spiking neural network (trained on the DHP19 dataset) performs hand tracking. The network employs sigma‑delta quantization and dynamic neural fields to maintain temporal precision while consuming only ~4 mW. The cognitive subsystem comprises three NVIDIA DGX A100 units: DGX1 hosts a 13 billion‑parameter LLaMA language model and Whisper speech recognition; DGX2 runs Spaun 2.0 (6.5 million neurons) partitioned across seven GPUs using Nengo‑OCL, alongside audio analysis for theremin note extraction; DGX3 processes stage‑monitoring cameras. A central orchestrator routes data between these components and to the Ameca robot (running Tritium OS), while optional cloud access to GPT‑3.5 augments intent detection. The software stack aligns each computational motif with hardware strengths—event‑driven spiking computation on Loihi2, dense tensor operations on GPUs—and uses standard networking to keep inter‑subsystem latency negligible.

## Key Results
- Demonstrated an interactive system where the Ameca humanoid robot plays the theremin in synchrony with a human.
- Achieved ultra‑low‑power hand tracking: Loihi2 consumes 4 mW vs. 5–10 W for a GPU alternative.
- First integration of a real‑time neuromorphic workload (Loihi2) with a large‑scale cognitive model (Spaun 2.0) on a GPU cluster.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Integrating a Dynamic Vision Sensor (DVS) with Intel Loihi2 enables ultra‑low‑latency, energy‑efficient hand tracking critical for real‑time human‑robot interaction.
- **How it works:** The DVS outputs asynchronous spikes only when pixel brightness changes, eliminating redundant frame‑based data. These events are processed by a spiking neural network on Loihi2, which natively supports event‑driven, asynchronous computation with local memory. This avoids the von Neumann bottleneck and clock‑synchronization overhead, yielding sub‑millisecond latency and very low power (~4 mW).
- **Key evidence:** The paper reports measured power consumption of 4 mW for the hand‑tracking task and sub‑millisecond latency; Section 4.1.4 details the Loihi2/DVS hardware setup.
- **Core assumption:** The hand tracking task can be effectively represented as an SNN that leverages the temporal precision of DVS events, and Loihi2 can execute this SNN in real‑time with sufficient accuracy.
- **Break condition:** If the task requires dense, high‑resolution static scene understanding that benefits from frame‑based data, or if the SNN fails to achieve necessary accuracy, the mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** High‑density GPU clusters (NVIDIA DGX A100) are necessary to execute large‑scale cognitive models (e.g., Spaun 2.0, LLMs) that provide complex reasoning, memory, and natural language capabilities.
- **How it works:** GPUs offer massive parallelism for matrix/tensor operations, ideal for deep learning simulations. Spaun 2.0 (6.5 million neurons) is partitioned across 7 GPUs, and a 13B‑parameter LLaMA LLM runs locally, enabling tasks like conversational dialogue and stateful decision‑making that demand high compute and memory bandwidth.
- **Key evidence:** The paper describes three DGX A100 systems (Section 4.1.3) and the multi‑GPU deployment of Spaun 2.0 (Section 4.2.1); the cluster’s aggregate power (~6.5 kW per DGX) underscores the computational cost.
- **Core assumption:** Cognitive tasks inherently require compute‑intensive models that cannot be efficiently implemented on resource‑constrained neuromorphic edge devices.
- **Break condition:** If neuromorphic hardware scales to support such large models efficiently, or if cognitive tasks can be simplified to lighter models, GPU reliance may diminish. The high power draw may also become unsustainable for edge deployment.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Seamless integration of neuromorphic and GPU subsystems via hardware‑aligned software partitioning and low‑overhead communication minimizes latency bottlenecks, yielding a responsive heterogeneous system.
- **How it works:** Latency‑sensitive perception (hand tracking) is mapped to Loihi2, while compute‑intensive cognition (Spaun, LLaMA) stays on GPUs. Tasks are decomposed to match hardware strengths (e.g., audio processing on DGX2, visual tracking on Loihi2). Communication uses standard network protocols and possibly event‑based standards like AER, keeping inter‑subsystem latency negligible relative to task deadlines.
- **Key evidence:** The paper’s architecture diagram (Figure 5) and Section 3.1 emphasize aligning software with hardware; the theremin demo’s successful synchrony reflects effective integration.
- **Core assumption:** The robotic application can be cleanly decomposed into subtasks with distinct latency/compute requirements, and network latency between subsystems is low enough to not violate real‑time constraints.
- **Break condition:** Excessive data shuffling, network latency spikes, or lack of standardized protocols could break the real‑time guarantees.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
DVS camera (Prophesee EVK4‑HD) → Loihi2 (Kapoho Point) → hand‑tracking data → display; Theremin audio → DGX2 → note extraction → Spaun 2.0 (state machine) → Ameca (Tritium OS); User speech → DGX1 (Whisper → LLaMA) → GPT‑3.5 (cloud) → Spaun; Stage cameras (RGB/depth) → DGX3 → monitoring; All GPU nodes + Loihi2 interconnected via low‑latency network in MDC.

### Critical Path (Theremin Duet Demo)
1. Theremin audio is captured and sent via USB to DGX2, where note pitch and volume are extracted in real time.  
2. Extracted parameters are fed into Spaun 2.0 (running on DGX2), which runs a finite‑state machine to decide the robot’s response based on the interaction scenario.  
3. Spaun outputs motor commands that traverse the network to Ameca’s Tritium OS, which translates them into joint movements.  

Concurrently:  
- User speech is processed by DGX1 (Whisper → LLaMA) for conversation.  
- DVS events are processed by Loihi2 for hand‑tracking visualization.  
- GPT‑3.5 (cloud) detects user intent and can influence Spaun’s state.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Latency vs. accuracy**: Loihi2 hand tracking offers sub‑ms latency but may sacrifice some positional accuracy compared to GPU‑based trackers; acceptable for visualization.
- **Power vs. performance**: Neuromorphic edge (4 mW) vs. GPU cluster (~kilowatts). Task partitioning keeps power‑hungry compute centralized.
- **Partitioning overhead**: Spaun’s split across 7 GPUs introduces inter‑GPU communication; model ran at 45 % of real‑time on the DGX system, indicating potential bottlenecks.
- **Centralized vs. distributed**: Placing heavy compute in a micro‑data center avoids on‑robot thermal constraints but requires a low‑latency, high‑bandwidth network to the robot.

### Failure Signatures
- **Hand‑tracking lag**: Loihi2 stalls → display updates delayed >100 ms.
- **Robot unresponsiveness**: Spaun or network failure → no movement commands.
- **Speech breakdown**: Whisper/LLM errors → conversation halts or nonsense.
- **MDC overheating**: Cooling failure → hardware throttling or shutdown.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Loihi2 hand tracking validation**: Feed synthetic or recorded DVS events of waving hands; verify output coordinates match ground truth with <1 ms latency.
2. **Spaun state machine test**: Run a simple memory task (e.g., digit recall) on the multi‑GPU Spaun; check state transitions and output correctness.
3. **End‑to‑end audio‑to‑robot pipeline**: Play a known theremin note; confirm note extraction on DGX2 → Spaun → Ameca hand movement within 200 ms total latency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
*Question:* What are the essential components and standards for a universal communication protocol that can efficiently integrate neuromorphic and conventional computing hardware in real‑time robotics?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors explicitly state that no standard protocol exists for connecting event‑based sensors and neuromorphic processors to conventional networks, and they outline two key requirements: (1) a raw, asynchronous transport of spikes without timestamps and minimal overhead for local connections, and (2) a safer transport with headers, timestamps, and error correction for longer or less reliable connections.  
*Why unresolved:* The absence of a widely adopted standard hinders interoperability, scalability, and seamless integration of heterogeneous components in robotic systems, limiting deployment of complex applications like the one demonstrated.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Development and community adoption of a protocol specification that satisfies the proposed requirements, validated through cross‑vendor hardware integration and performance benchmarks in real‑time robotic tasks (e.g., the theremin demonstration).

**Open Question 2**  
*Question:* Can vector symbolic architectures (VSAs) be implemented directly on neuromorphic hardware using sparse, binary, event‑driven computation to achieve orders‑of‑magnitude improvements in energy‑delay product compared to rate‑coded implementations on GPUs?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors hypothesize that a direct, sparse, binary, event‑driven implementation of VSA mechanisms should be significantly faster and more energy efficient than the rate‑coded approach used in Spaun (via Nengo on GPUs), but note this has never been demonstrated despite its theoretical potential.  
*Why unresolved:* Current VSA implementations primarily use conventional hardware with rate coding; the lack of direct neuromorphic implementations prevents validation of the predicted efficiency gains and limits the application of VSAs in ultra‑low‑power edge robotics.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A working VSA implementation on a neuromorphic platform (e.g., Loihi2 or SpiNNaker2) benchmarked against GPU‑based VSA/SpAUN implementations on tasks like visual scene recognition or classification, showing substantial reductions in energy consumption and latency while maintaining accuracy.

**Open Question 3**  
*Question:* How do neuromorphic and GPU platforms compare in executing large‑scale cognitive models (e.g., Spaun 2.0) for real‑time human‑robot interaction, considering factors like latency, energy consumption, and model fidelity?  
*Basis in paper:* The paper reports that Spaun 2.0 on an NVIDIA DGX A100 cluster operated at 45 % of real‑time (i.e., slower than real‑time), whereas a SpiNNaker2 implementation is estimated to run in real‑time with 5.4–13.5× lower power. However, no direct head‑to‑head comparison under identical conditions is provided.  
*Why unresolved:* The estimates and indirect comparisons leave open questions about actual real‑time feasibility, energy‑latency trade‑offs, and whether model performance (behavioral/neurological fidelity) is preserved across platforms.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A controlled experiment deploying the identical Spaun 2.0 model (or similar large‑scale cognitive model) on both a SpiNNaker2 system and an NVIDIA DGX A100, measuring wall‑clock latency, power draw, and output consistency in interactive tasks like the theremin duet.

**Open Question 4**  
*Question:* For heterogeneous robotic systems combining edge neuromorphic processors and cloud GPU clusters, what algorithms and frameworks can optimally partition and schedule tasks across the heterogeneous resources to minimize end‑to‑end latency while respecting energy constraints?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors describe a fixed, manually designed partitioning (e.g., hand tracking on Loihi2, high‑level cognition on GPUs) and emphasize the importance of seamless integration to avoid bottlenecks, but they do not address dynamic adaptation or automated resource management.  
*Why unresolved:* Manual partitioning is brittle and may not scale to more complex, multi‑task scenarios or changing environmental conditions; automated methods that account for the distinct computational characteristics (event‑based vs. batch) and energy profiles of neuromorphic and GPU hardware are needed.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A runtime middleware or scheduling framework that dynamically allocates tasks between neuromorphic edge devices and GPU clusters based on real‑time load, latency requirements and energy budgets, demonstrating reduced end‑to‑end latency and energy savings in an interactive robot task like the theremin duet.

## Limitations
- The exact hyperparameters of the sigma‑delta DSNN for the Loihi2 hand tracker are unspecified, hindering precise replication of the perception pipeline.
- Key details of Spaun 2.0’s multi‑GPU partitioning (module allocation, communication topology, synchronization) are omitted, making full system duplication difficult.
- Independent validation is weak: the paper’s reference to neighboring works shows low similarity (average FMR 0.44) and zero citations, indicating limited external corroboration of the heterogeneous approach.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (DVS + Loihi2 hand tracking)**: Medium confidence.
- **Mechanism 2 (GPU cluster for cognitive models)**: Medium confidence.
- **Mechanism 3 (Seamless integration)**: Low confidence.
