# CORE-Seg: Reasoning-Driven Segmentation for Complex Lesions via Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05911  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05911  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** CORE-Seg achieves a mean Dice of 37.06%, outperforming the second-best baseline by 14.89 percentage points, while reducing the failure rate to 18.42%.

## Executive Summary
Complex medical lesion segmentation demands reasoning that goes beyond pattern matching, due to visual ambiguity and heterogeneity. Conventional cascaded approaches suffer from error propagation. CORE-Seg introduces an end-to-end framework that integrates multimodal reasoning with segmentation by leveraging a Semantic-Guided Prompt Adapter to bridge a Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) and the Segment Anything Model (SAM). Trained progressively from Supervised Fine-Tuning to Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with an adaptive dual-granularity reward, the system mitigates reward sparsity and stabilizes learning. The paper also presents ComLesion-14K, the first large-scale Chain-of-Thought benchmark for complex lesion segmentation. On this benchmark, CORE-Seg sets a new state-of-the-art with a mean Dice of 37.06%, a 14.89 percentage point improvement over the prior best, and a failure rate of 18.42%.

## Method Summary
CORE-Seg employs a two-stage progressive training pipeline. **Stage 1 (Supervised Fine-Tuning)** fine-tunes Qwen-VL-2.5-3B (with LoRA), the Semantic-Guided Prompt Adapter, and a SAM-based mask decoder (MedSAM 2) using a composite loss: cross-entropy for text generation (CoT reasoning and answer) and DiceCE for the segmentation mask. The adapter maps the hidden state of a dedicated `<seg>` token from the MLLM into a prompt embedding for SAM, establishing an end-to-end differentiable path. **Stage 2 (GRPO)** optimizes the policy via group-relative reinforcement learning. For each input, multiple completions are sampled; rewards combine format validity, bounding box bipartite matching (IoU × F1), and an adaptive mask reward that switches from GIoU (when Dice < 0.05) to `1 + λ·Dice` otherwise. The GRPO objective maximizes reward while regularizing with a KL penalty against the Stage 1 policy, and the DiceCE loss is retained for segmentation supervision.

## Key Results
- Mean Dice Similarity Coefficient: **37.06%** on ComLesion-14K.
- **14.89 percentage points** improvement over the second-best baseline.
- Failure rate (Dice=0 or invalid format) reduced to **18.42%**.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Semantic-Guided Prompt Adapter mitigates cascading error propagation.** The adapter directly translates the MLLM’s reasoning state (the `<seg>` token’s hidden state) into a visual prompt for SAM, bypassing explicit bounding box reliance. It uses a ResMLP to align the token’s semantic summary into SAM’s feature space, followed by cross-attention with a learnable query to produce a segmentation-compatible prompt. This creates an end-to-end differentiable bridge that fuses linguistic reasoning with visual segmentation. The core assumption is that the `<seg>` token’s representation implicitly encodes a spatial prior robust enough to guide pixel-level segmentation. Evidence includes the abstract’s claim of removing box-based error propagation, detailed adapter architecture in section 4.1.2, and an ablation showing the `<seg>` token outperforms the whole answer hidden state after RL (37.06% vs. 33.01% mDice). A break condition occurs if the `<seg>` token’s representation fails to capture spatial intent due to poor reasoning in SFT, leading to misaligned projections and degraded segmentation, as seen in Stage 1’s lower performance.

**Mechanism 2: Progressive training from SFT to GRPO stabilizes learning.** Stage 1 (SFT) establishes semantic-visual alignment by injecting CoT patterns and linking the `<seg>` token to masks via composite loss. Stage 2 (GRPO) then samples multiple outputs per input, computes group-relative rewards, and updates the policy to maximize reward while regularizing with a KL penalty. This avoids SFT overfitting and enables discovery of better reasoning-segmentation strategies. The core assumption is that SFT provides a sufficient prior for GRPO to explore without collapsing, and the group-relative baseline reduces reward estimation variance. Evidence includes the abstract’s mention of progressive training, formal loss definitions in sections 4.2–4.3, and an ablation where adding Stage 2 boosts mDice from 30.37% (S1 only) to 37.06% and cuts failure rate from 26.02% to 18.42%. Break conditions include SFT overfitting to spurious patterns (e.g., template-like CoT without grounding), which can hinder GRPO recovery, as seen in OOD performance (29.31% mDice on ISPY with S1 alone). Inadequate reward design could also cause policy collapse.

**Mechanism 3: Adaptive dual-granularity reward mitigates reward sparsity.** The reward aggregates three components: format reward (structural validity), bipartite matching reward (box IoU × F1), and an adaptive mask reward. If Dice < 0.05, the mask reward uses GIoU of derived boxes to give a coarse signal; otherwise, it uses `1 + λ·Dice` (λ=2) to emphasize fine mask quality. This transitions from coarse to dense supervision, providing informative gradients even when masks do not overlap. The core assumption is that early in training or on hard samples, pixel-level Dice offers no gradient; a box-level GIoU surrogate encourages spatial alignment. Evidence includes the abstract’s claim of mitigating reward sparsity, reward definitions in section 4.3.1 (Equation 11 and threshold), and an ablation where removing r_mask drops mDice to 26.87% and using only DiceCE without adaptation yields 32.41% vs. 37.06%. Break conditions involve the fixed Dice<0.05 threshold being mismatched for some lesion scales; for very small lesions, GIoU may be noisy, and λ=2 could over-penalize boundary errors. Mis-weighted components might optimize box alignment at the expense of mask detail.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs):** Why needed: The reasoning module is based on Qwen-VL-2.5-3B; understanding its token generation, attention, and LoRA adaptation is key to grasping CoT production and how the `<seg>` token is shaped. Quick check: How does an MLLM generate structured output like `<answer>…<seg></answer>` and what role does the special token play?
- **Segment Anything Model (SAM) architecture:** Why needed: SAM provides the image encoder and mask decoder; the adapter must project features into a space SAM’s decoder can consume. Knowledge of SAM’s prompt-based conditioning (points/boxes vs. dense embeddings) is essential. Quick check: How does SAM’s mask decoder use a prompt embedding to generate a mask, and why might a semantic probe differ from a point/box prompt?
- **Reinforcement Learning for Language Models (GRPO):** Why needed: Stage 2 uses GRPO to optimize the MLLM’s policy for reasoning-segmentation. Understanding group-relative baselines, KL regularization, and reward shaping is necessary to interpret the training objective. Quick check: Why does GRPO omit a separate critic model, and how does the group-relative baseline reduce memory usage compared to PPO?
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting in medical contexts:** Why needed: The dataset and SFT stage require generating interpretable reasoning chains that include normal anatomy description, lesion characterization, and localization logic. This is the "cognitive" target for the model. Quick check: What distinguishes a useful medical CoT from a template-style reasoning trace, and how is logical consistency enforced in ComLesion-14K?
- **Medical image segmentation metrics (Dice, IoU, Failure Rate):** Why needed: These metrics evaluate performance and robustness. The failure rate definition (Dice=0 or invalid format) is critical for assessing clinical reliability. Quick check: Why might a model achieve non-zero Dice but still be clinically unacceptable, and how does the paper’s failure rate capture this?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input (Image I, Query Q) -> Multimodal Reasoning Module (Qwen-VL with LoRA; generates `answer>A</answer>`; A ends with `<seg>`) -> Semantic-Guided Prompt Adapter (ResMLP + cross-attention; takes `<seg>` hidden state → visual prompt ˆP) -> Segmentation Module (SAM image encoder + mask decoder; takes image embeddings + ˆP → mask ˆM).

**Critical path:** The `<seg>` token’s hidden state is the sole link between reasoning and segmentation. Its quality determines ˆP, which conditions SAM’s decoder. End-to-end gradient flow during SFT and RL must preserve this signal.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *End-to-end vs. cascaded:* End-to-end avoids box-error propagation but requires careful alignment of MLLM and SAM feature spaces; cascaded methods are simpler but fragile on complex lesions.  
- *Token choice:* Using the dedicated `<seg>` token sacrifices some contextual richness but yields higher reward variance and better RL convergence versus using the whole answer.  
- *RL reward granularity:* Dual-granularity (box + mask) adds complexity but is necessary to handle reward sparsity; a pure mask reward may fail early.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Format failures:* Missing `<think>`/`<answer>` or `<seg>` → format reward=0, high failure rate.  
- *Reasoning failures:* Hallucinated or irrelevant CoT (Stage 1) → poor `<seg>` representation → low Dice.  
- *Segmentation failures:* Disconnected or empty masks (Dice≈0) → potential reward sparsity if adaptive reward not effective.  
- *Adapter misalignment:* Even with correct CoT, if ResMLP or attention projection is poorly trained, ˆP may be meaningless to SAM.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Adapter validation:** Train only Stage 1 with the adapter vs. a linear projection head. Expect significantly higher mDice with the adapter (reported 30.37% vs. 23.60%).  
2. **Reward ablation:** In Stage 2, disable the adaptive mask reward (use only r_bbox). Expect sharp mDice drop (reported 26.87% vs. 37.06%) and increased failure rate, confirming reward sparsity issue.  
3. **Token probe test:** Compare using `<seg>` token vs. whole answer hidden state as adapter input, both with SFT+RL. Expect `<seg>` to outperform (37.06% vs. 33.01% mDice), validating the design choice.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Extension to 3D volumetric images?** The current framework targets 2D slices and cannot exploit inter-slice context, limiting applicability to volumetric data.  
- **Inference latency reduction?** Explicit CoT generation increases computational cost and slows inference, hindering clinical deployment.  
- **Optimality of fixed Dice threshold?** The adaptive reward uses a static Dice<0.05 threshold; this may not be universal across lesion sizes and modalities.  
- **Why `<seg>` token outperforms whole answer?** Ablation shows `<seg>` yields higher mDice after RL, but the representation differences and design principles for semantic probes remain unexplained.

## Limitations
- Adapter architecture specifics (ResMLP depth, hidden dimensions) are not fully detailed, risking suboptimal alignment.  
- GRPO hyperparameters (KL weight, group size, reward scaling) are unclear and could affect training dynamics.  
- The fixed reward threshold (Dice<0.05) may not suit all lesion scales, potentially causing sparse or noisy gradients.  
- ComLesion-14K is a new benchmark; its CoT quality and difficulty filtering require external validation.

## Confidence
- **Semantic-Guided Prompt Adapter design:** Medium (ablation supports benefit but architecture details missing).  
- **Progressive training (SFT to GRPO):** High (clear ablation gains and well-specified procedure).  
- **Adaptive dual-granularity reward:** Medium (ablation confirms importance but threshold sensitivity unexamined).  
- **Overall results:** Medium-High (strong gains on new benchmark, but failure rate remains 18.42%).

## Next Checks
1. **Adapter architecture ablation:** Systematically vary adapter capacity (linear, 1–3 ResMLP layers) in Stage 1 to measure impact on `<seg>` representation quality (e.g., via probing) and final Dice.  
2. **Reward threshold sensitivity:** Sweep Dice threshold (0.01–0.1) and λ (1–3) on validation set; monitor failure rate and Dice; test responsiveness across lesion sizes.  
3. **Cross-dataset evaluation:** Test the final model on at least two external complex lesion datasets (e.g., ISPY, polyp images) with appropriate prompts; report Dice and failure rate to assess generalization.
