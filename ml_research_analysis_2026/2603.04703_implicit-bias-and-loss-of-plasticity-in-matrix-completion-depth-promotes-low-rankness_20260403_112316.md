# Implicit Bias and Loss of Plasticity in Matrix Completion: Depth Promotes Low-Rankness

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04703
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04703
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Depth in deep linear networks induces coupled training dynamics that promote convergence to near rank-1 solutions for matrix completion, resolving an open question by Menon (2024) and explaining the loss-of-plasticity phenomenon.

## Executive Summary

This paper investigates the implicit bias of deep linear networks in matrix completion. It shows that depth strengthens the bias toward low-rank solutions: networks of depth two can exhibit decoupled dynamics when observations are sparse (e.g., block-diagonal), leading to full-rank solutions, while depth three or more automatically couples the dynamics across factors, driving the solution to near rank-1 as the initialization scale vanishes. This theoretical result resolves an open question and explains why shallow networks suffer from loss of plasticity when pre-trained on sparse data and fine-tuned on augmented data—a phenomenon avoided by deeper networks. Empirical evidence with ResNet/VGG on CIFAR suggests the bias extends to nonlinear architectures.

## Method Summary

The authors analyze gradient flow on deep linear networks factorizing a target matrix as a product of weight matrices. For theory, they consider block-diagonal observation patterns and a deterministic initialization family parameterized by scale α. They formally define coupled (gradients from different observations share parameters) vs. decoupled dynamics. Using singular value evolution equations, they prove that for depth ≥3 with finite initialization rank, dynamics are coupled and the stable rank of the product matrix approaches 1 as α → 0. Depth-2 networks under disconnected observations have decoupled dynamics, yielding full-rank solutions independent of α. The paper also studies loss-of-plasticity: pre-training on sparse data leads to high-rank lazy training in depth-2 models when fine-tuned on augmented data, while deep models retain low-rank bias. Experiments on synthetic and CIFAR data support the theory.

## Key Results

- Depth ≥3 induces coupled training dynamics for generic initialization, leading to convergence to near rank-1 as initialization scale vanishes; depth-2 often yields full-rank under disconnected observations.
- Initialization scale α controls final rank: for coupled dynamics, stable rank → 1 as α → 0; for decoupled, rank is α‑independent.
- Deep networks avoid loss-of-plasticity: warm‑starting from pre‑trained sparse data maintains low‑rank bias, whereas depth‑2 models preserve high‑rank solutions due to lazy training.
- Empirical results with ResNet/VGG on CIFAR show deeper architectures have lower average effective rank across layers, suggesting the bias extends to nonlinear networks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Coupled Training Dynamics Drive Low-Rank Bias
Depth ≥3 networks inherently couple gradients across factor matrices because all layers share interior parameters, regardless of observation connectivity. This coupling aligns singular vectors of the product matrix, leading to low-rank convergence for vanishing initialization scale. In contrast, depth-2 networks under disconnected observations have decoupled dynamics (independent updates per row/column block), preventing alignment.

### Mechanism 2: Deterministic Initialization Scale Controls Rank Emergence
For coupled dynamics, limiting singular values satisfy implicit equations where α multiplies a constant that vanishes as α → 0, forcing all but one singular value to zero. For decoupled dynamics, singular values are α‑independent and converge to multiples of ground truth singular values, yielding full rank.

### Mechanism 3: Loss-of-Plasticity Avoidance via Inherent Low-Rank Bias
Deep networks are already biased toward low-rank even when pre-trained on sparse data because their dynamics are coupled; thus warm‑starting on augmented data does not trap them in high‑rank solutions. Shallow networks may converge to high‑rank zero‑loss solutions on sparse data and then enter lazy training when new data arrives, preserving the high‑rank structure.

## Foundational Learning

- **Matrix factorization as linear networks**: The target matrix is expressed as a product of weight matrices (W = W_L ... W_1). Understanding gradient flow through each factor is crucial for deriving coupled/decoupled dynamics.
  - Quick check: For depth‑2 W = AB, which entries of A and B influence a given observed entry w_{ij}? How does this change for depth ≥3?
- **Gradient flow vs. gradient descent**: The theoretical analysis assumes continuous‑time gradient flow, which simplifies dynamics and enables exact implicit bias characterization. Results may not directly hold for discrete steps.
  - Quick check: Does the implicit bias depend on step size? (Hint: The paper states results for gradient flow and also tests various optimizers experimentally.)
- **Coupled vs. decoupled dynamics**: Coupled means gradients from different observed entries share parameter support, aligning updates across the factorization; decoupled means they affect disjoint parameter sets. This determines whether the product collapses to low rank.
  - Quick check: For a 2×2 matrix with only diagonal observations (w_{11}, w_{22}), are dynamics coupled for depth‑2? For depth‑3?
- **Singular value evolution and stable rank**: The bias is quantified by limiting singular values of the product matrix. Stable rank (sum of squares of singular values divided by square of largest) measures low‑rankness continuously.
  - Quick check: What is stable rank and why is it preferable to numerical rank for capturing low‑rank bias?
- **Lazy training regime**: Occurs when the model is near a zero‑loss solution on a subset, causing tiny gradients and negligible weight updates, preventing adaptation to new data. This explains loss of plasticity in shallow networks.
  - Quick check: If a model already fits a subset perfectly, how does that affect gradient magnitude when training on a larger dataset?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Deep linear network W(t) = W_L(t) ··· W_1(t) with each W_l ∈ ℝ^{d×d}; observed entries Ω ⊆ [d]×[d]; loss ℓ(W; Ω) = ½ Σ_{(i,j)∈Ω} (w_{ij} - w_{ij}^*)²; gradient flow dynamics ˙W_l = -∂ℓ/∂W_l. Tunables: depth L, initialization scale α (and ratio m), observation pattern connectivity.

**Critical path**:
1. Choose observation pattern (block‑diagonal for theory; general connectivity for experiments).
2. Set depth L and initialize weights via deterministic scheme (7) or Gaussian with scale α.
3. Run gradient flow or small‑step gradient descent until loss convergence (e.g., < 1e‑8).
4. Compute stable rank of the final product matrix.
5. For plasticity tests: pre‑train on Ω_pre to convergence; then either warm‑start (continue training on Ω_post) or cold‑start (reinitialize) on Ω_post.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Deeper networks increase compute (more matrix multiplies) but strengthen low‑rank bias.
- Smaller α enhances low‑rank bias for deep coupled dynamics; large α can increase rank.
- For depth‑2, observation connectivity is necessary for low‑rank; for depth≥3, coupling holds almost automatically, making connectivity irrelevant.
- Special diagonal initialization (m → ∞) decouples even deep networks, yielding full‑rank solutions.

**Failure signatures**:
- Deep network yields full‑rank: check for near‑diagonal initialization (large m) or α not small enough.
- Warm‑starting fails to improve rank for depth‑2 but succeeds for depth≥3. If both depths fail, suspect decoupling (e.g., diagonal init for deep net).
- Training divergence: ensure gradient flow convergence; if using discrete steps, learning rate may be too large.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Depth scaling**: Fix α (e.g., 1e‑5) and m (e.g., 10). Increase L on block‑diagonal observations. Expect effective rank to decrease with L. Verify σ₁ >> σᵢ for i≥2.
2. **Initialization sweep**: Fix L≥3, vary α over orders of magnitude. Stable rank should drop as α decreases. Compare to L=2 which should be α‑insensitive.
3. **Warm‑start plasticity**: Pre‑train depth‑2 on diagonal only (high‑rank solution). Then add one off‑diagonal to couple. Warm‑start should preserve high rank; cold‑start should find low rank. Repeat with L≥3 to see low rank persists even after warm‑start.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Gaussian initialization**: Do deep linear networks with standard Gaussian initialization converge to low‑rank solutions for disconnected observations? The current theory covers a specific deterministic family.
2. **Nonlinear networks**: Does depth‑induced low‑rank bias extend to nonlinear neural networks with provable guarantees? Empirical evidence exists but no theory.
3. **General observation graphs**: For arbitrary connectivity patterns, does increasing depth always strengthen the low‑rank bias? The analysis is limited to block‑diagonal patterns.
4. **Mechanism of plasticity avoidance**: What precise mechanism allows deep networks to avoid loss of plasticity? Is low‑rank bias alone sufficient, or does coupling dynamics play an independent role?

## Limitations

- Theory is confined to deep linear networks under gradient flow; extensions to practical settings (discrete steps, nonlinearities) lack rigorous support.
- The analysis relies on block‑diagonal observation patterns; the coupling/decoupling framework for general graphs remains underdeveloped.
- The low‑rank bias is sensitive to initialization (e.g., diagonal initialization decouples even deep networks), limiting its universality.

## Confidence

- **Coupled dynamics drive low‑rank bias**: High
- **Depth ≥3 yields coupling for generic initialization**: High
- **Deep linear networks avoid loss‑of‑plasticity**: High
- **Depth promotes low‑rank in practical deep networks**: Medium
