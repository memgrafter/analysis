# Adapting Rules of Official International Mahjong for Online Players
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.08211
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08211
- Reference count: 23
- Primary result: Identified a first-mover advantage (Position 1 win rate 26.19% vs Position 4's 22.45%) and proposed compensatory points and pattern score adjustments to balance online single-round play.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses fairness issues in online single-round Official International Mahjong by analyzing position-based win rate disparities and scoring imbalances. Using extensive AI self-play (557,056 games), the authors reveal a significant first-mover advantage (3.74% gap between Positions 1 and 4) and overvaluation of frequent patterns like Seven Pairs. They propose two rule adaptations: compensatory points to offset positional bias, and adjusted scores for 11 patterns to align rewards with difficulty. Validation across top competition AIs shows the adaptations restore correct rankings in fixed-position tests. The revised rules are implemented online as MahjongRevised on Botzone.

## Method Summary
The study employs a large-scale AI self-play pipeline, first quantifying positional win rates and average scores using a champion AI. Compensatory points are derived from the average score differences between positions. Pattern frequencies are tallied across 65,536 games, identifying the top 43 most common patterns. Based on frequency and combinatorial analysis, scores for 11 patterns are adjusted: patterns with original scores above 8 points that are frequent are lowered, while 8-point patterns outside the top frequent set are raised. The effectiveness of both adaptations is tested by comparing rankings in fixed-position versus duplicate-format tournaments using four top IJCAI AIs. Finally, the revised rules are deployed in an online environment.

## Key Results
- First-mover advantage quantified: Position 1 win rate 26.19% vs Position 4's 22.45% (3.74% gap).
- Compensatory point offsets (P1: -1.0, P2: -0.4, P3: +0.3, P4: +1.1) neutralize positional bias in multi-AI validation.
- Score adjustments for 11 patterns (e.g., Seven Pairs lowered from 24 to 16 pts) based on frequency; validated by consistency across multiple AIs.
- Revised rules implemented online as MahjongRevised on Botzone.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Compensatory points mitigate first-mover advantage in single-round play by offsetting position-based win rate disparities.
- **Mechanism:** Assign fixed point offsets (P1: -1.0, P2: -0.4, P3: +0.3, P4: +1.1) to each position before final score calculation, mathematically neutralizing the observed 3.74% win rate gap between Positions 1 and 4.
- **Core assumption:** The first-mover advantage is stable across high-level AI self-play and can be counteracted by constant offsets derived from aggregate score differences.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "first-mover advantage... Proposed rule adaptations: (1) Compensatory points..."; [section IV.A] "the first player has about 3.74% higher winning rate... we propose a compensation mechanism..."
- **Break condition:** If the positional win rate differential changes significantly with different AI populations, game variants, or larger-scale self-play, the fixed offsets may no longer balance the game.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Adjusting subgoal scores for tile patterns aligns reward with combinatorial difficulty and observed frequency, preventing high-frequency patterns from being overvalued.
- **Mechanism:** Lower the point value of patterns that appear frequently in AI self-play (e.g., Seven Pairs from 24 to 16 points) and raise points for less frequent patterns initially worth 8 points, based on ranking within the top 43 most common patterns.
- **Core assumption:** A pattern's frequency in strong AI self-play inversely correlates with its true difficulty/combinatorial scarcity, so scoring should reward rarity.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Identified scoring imbalances: frequent high-point patterns... Proposed rule adaptations: (2) Adjusted scores for 11 patterns..."; [section IV.B] "We notice that there are 43 tile patterns worth no more than 8 points... however, Table V shows the most frequently occurred 43 scoring patterns... include patterns up to 24 points..."
- **Break condition:** If frequency distributions shift dramatically across AI architectures or if human play frequency diverges from AI play frequency, the adjustments may misalign with actual difficulty.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Validation across multiple independent AIs ensures that observed imbalances and proposed fixes are not artifacts of a single AI's strategy.
- **Mechanism:** Test the compensatory points and score adjustments using the top 4 IJCAI competition AIs (with different algorithms) in fixed-position experiments, confirming that rankings align with the "correct" duplicate-format baseline only after applying adaptations.
- **Core assumption:** Top-tier AIs represent diverse strategic approaches; consensus among them on positional bias and pattern frequency indicates a systemic game property rather than an AI-specific quirk.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Validated with top 4 IJCAI competition AIs: compensation restored correct rankings..."; [section IV.A & IV.B] "We adopt the top four AI agents... Experiment 2... rankings are inconsistent... Table IV shows... producing a correct ranking..."; "We also investigate... using other AI programs... results in Table VII show consistency..."
- **Break condition:** If the top AI population changes (e.g., new algorithms dominate) and produces different frequency/position data, the adaptations may require re-evaluation.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** AI Self-Play Engine -> Data Pipeline -> Analysis Module -> Rule Adaptation Engine -> Validation Harness -> Online Implementation (MahjongRevised)
- **Critical path:** Self-play data generation → Position/pattern analysis → Adaptation proposal → Multi-AI validation → Rule implementation online.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Champion AI vs. Multi-AI:* Using one champion reduces compute cost but risks AI-specific bias; using multiple AIs is more robust but requires integrating diverse AI APIs and more computation.  
  - *Real-time vs. Batch Analysis:* Real-time adaptation during play is infeasible; all adjustments are based on offline, large-scale data.  
  - *Generalizability:* Adjustments derived from top AIs may not translate to human player balance, as human strategies differ.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Inconsistent rankings after compensation across different fixed-seat permutations.  
  - Pattern frequency rankings differ substantially between champion AI and other top AIs.  
  - Online implementation shows no reduction in position-based win rate disparity in human play compared to original rules.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline Position Bias Quantification:** Run 557,056 self-play games with champion AI in random positions; compute win rates and average scores per position with confidence intervals.  
  2. **Compensation Effectiveness Test:** With top 4 AIs, run duplicate-format (24 permutations) to establish "correct" ranking. Then run fixed-position experiments (no compensation) and observe ranking divergence; apply compensatory points and check alignment with baseline ranking.  
  3. **Pattern Score Adjustment Validation:** Count pattern frequencies across champion AI self-play. Identify top 43 patterns and apply Algorithm 3 to draft new scores. Repeat frequency analysis with 4 other AIs; verify overlap in top 43 patterns and consistency of proposed adjustments. Compare adjusted scores with theoretical combinatorial numbers to check directionality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can a superhuman AI be developed that solves four-player Official International Mahjong, achieving a theoretically optimal policy?
2. How generalizable are the proposed compensatory points (P1: -1.0, P2: -0.4, P3: +0.3, P4: +1.1) across AI agents with substantially different strategies or human players?
3. Do the proposed score adjustments for 11 tile patterns actually improve game health and strategic diversity in online single-round play, or do they create new imbalances?
4. Will human players accept and adopt the MahjongRevised rules (compensation + score adjustments) in practice, and does it improve their perceived fairness and engagement for single-round online play?

## Limitations
- Exact champion AI code and raw game logs are not public, blocking faithful replication.
- Threshold for pattern adjustments (top 43 frequent patterns) is arbitrary and lacks combinatorial justification.
- "Duplicate Format" tournament details are vague, undermining validation reliability.
- Findings may be specific to top-tier AI strategies; human play divergence is unexamined.

## Confidence
- Compensatory points mitigate first-mover advantage: **Medium**
- Pattern score adjustments align reward with difficulty: **Low**
- Multi-AI validation ensures systemic imbalances: **Medium**
