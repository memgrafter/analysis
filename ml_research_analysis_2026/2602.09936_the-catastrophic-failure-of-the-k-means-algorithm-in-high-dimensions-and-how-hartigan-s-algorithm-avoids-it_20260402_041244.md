# The Catastrophic Failure of The k-Means Algorithm in High Dimensions, and How Hartigan's Algorithm Avoids It

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09936
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09936
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Lloyd's k-means suffers catastrophic failure in high-dimensional, high-noise settings, returning its initial partition with no progress, while Hartigan's algorithm avoids this pathology and achieves superior recovery.

## Executive Summary

This paper reveals a fundamental flaw in Lloyd's k-means algorithm under high-dimensional, high-noise conditions. The authors prove that when the data dimension \(d\) is large relative to the number of samples \(n\) and noise variance \(\sigma^2\) (specifically \(d \gtrsim n^3\) and \(\sigma^2 > n\)), essentially every approximately balanced partition becomes a fixed point of Lloyd's update. As a result, Lloyd's algorithm typically halts after its first iteration, outputting its initial partition and failing to recover the true cluster structure. In contrast, Hartigan's algorithm employs a weighted distance metric that eliminates such spurious fixed points with high probability when \(d \gtrsim n\sigma^4\). Through theoretical analysis and extensive experiments on synthetic Gaussian mixture models and real-world datasets, the paper demonstrates Lloyd's severe degradation in this regime, while Hartigan matches or outperforms more sophisticated methods like spectral clustering and SDP relaxations without their computational burdens. These findings challenge the widespread use of Lloyd's method in modern high-dimensional data analysis and highlight Hartigan's as a robust alternative.

## Method Summary

The investigation combines rigorous theory with empirical validation. Theoretically, the authors analyze a two-component Gaussian mixture model with isotropic Gaussian noise, deriving precise conditions for the proliferation of Lloyd's fixed points and the absence of incorrect fixed points for Hartigan. These conditions involve the dimension \(d\), sample size \(n\), noise variance \(\sigma^2\), and signal strength \(\tau^2\). Empirically, they generate synthetic data from this model across a wide range of \(d\) and \(\sigma^2\), and run Lloyd's k-means, Hartigan's k-means, PCA+Lloyd, spectral clustering, and an SDP relaxation. Performance is evaluated using normalized mutual information (NMI) and k-means loss. Additional experiments on real datasets (Olivetti faces and 20 Newsgroups subsets) extend the comparison to practical scenarios. The reproducibility plan includes implementing the algorithms exactly as described, generating data according to the model, and replicating all figures and tables from the paper.

## Key Results

- In regimes where \(d \gtrsim n^3\) and \(\sigma^2 > n\), Lloyd's algorithm almost always returns its initial partition after a single iteration, achieving NMI close to random chance and loss comparable to random assignment.
- Hartigan's algorithm does not exhibit catastrophic failure; it recovers the true clusters with high probability when \(d \gtrsim n\sigma^4\), and its performance is competitive with or superior to spectral clustering and SDP methods, while being significantly more scalable.
- The theoretical analysis shows that the probability of Lloyd's algorithm being trapped in an incorrect fixed point approaches 1 as \(d\) grows, while the probability of Hartigan's algorithm having an incorrect fixed point vanishes exponentially fast in \(d\). The empirical results align with these predictions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Lloyd's Catastrophic Fixed-Point Proliferation:** In high-noise, high-dimensional settings (\(d \gtrsim n^3\)), Lloyd's algorithm traps because nearly every \(q\)-approximately balanced partition is a fixed point. The mechanism: a misassigned sample in a cluster pulls the centroid toward itself via its "self-influence" term (\(\propto 1/|C_j|\)). When noise variance \(\sigma^2\) dominates the signal \(\tau^2\) (\(\sigma^2 > n\)), the expected squared distance difference favors the wrong centroid. The probability that a sample would benefit from moving is shown to be \(\leq \rho_q^{d/4}\) with \(\rho_q < 1\) (Theorem 3.4, Corollary 3.8). This is proven by scaling chi-squared differences and applying Chernoff bounds, then extending uniformly over all balanced partitions via a union bound. Evidence: abstract states that essentially every partition is a fixed point; Section 3.2.1 analyzes single-sample reassignment; Lemmas 3.1–3.2 derive the chi-squared scaling.

**Hartigan's Avoidance via Weighted Distances:** Hartigan's weighted distance \(\Delta_H^2\) rescales squared distances by factors \(|C_j|/(|C_j|\pm 1)\). This weighting amplifies the effect of cluster purity in the expectation, making the sign of \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta_H(x_i, C_j) - \Delta_H(x_i, C_{\bar{j}})]\) independent of \(\sigma^2\) and determined solely by whether the current cluster's purity ratio \(R_\ell^j\) is lower than the alternative's (Observation D.3, Equation 97). In any incorrect partition, at least one sample will have \(R_\ell^j < R_\ell^{\bar{j}}\), making a move beneficial with probability \(1 - \rho_h^{d/4}\) where \(\rho_h < 1\) (Theorem 3.9, Corollary 3.11). Thus spurious fixed points vanish as \(d\) grows. Evidence: abstract claims Hartigan avoids the pathology; Section 3.3 develops the theory; Theorem 3.9 gives the single-sample bound.

**Dimensional Threshold Separation:** The two algorithms exhibit starkly different dimensional thresholds: Lloyd fails with high probability when \(d \gtrsim n^3\) (assuming \(\sigma^2 > n\)), while Hartigan succeeds with high probability when \(d \gtrsim n\sigma^4\). The difference stems from the effective scaling parameters in the distance comparisons: for Lloyd, the relevant parameter \(\alpha\) depends on \(\sigma^2\) and cluster sizes, leading to a bound \(2^n n \rho_q^{d/4}\) with \(\rho_q < 1\) when \(\sigma^2 > n\) (Corollary 3.8). For Hartigan, the parameter \(\eta\) is always \(< 1\), yielding a bound \(2^n \rho_h^{d/4}\) (Corollary 3.12). Consequently, the required \(d\) for Hartigan's success scales only with \(n\sigma^4\), which is much smaller than \(n^3\) when \(\sigma^2\) is not too large. Evidence: Theorems 1.1, 3.8, 3.12; equations (3) and (4); Sections 3.2.3 and 3.3.3.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Chi-squared Distribution Concentration**  
  *Why needed:* The squared distances in Lloyd's and Hartigan's comparisons are scaled chi-squared random variables. Their concentration (Fact A.3) and tail bounds (Lemma 3.3) are crucial for proving that the probability of a beneficial reassignment decays exponentially in dimension.  
  *Quick check:* If \(Y \sim a \chi^2_d\), what is \(\mathbb{E}[Y]\) and how does the variance scale with \(d\)? How does this concentration affect the comparison \(Y_1 - Y_2 < 0\) for \(a_1 > a_2\)?

- **Concept: k-Means Fixed Points and Local Optimality**  
  *Why needed:* The paper defines fixed points of Lloyd's and Hartigan's update rules (Definitions 2.2, 2.3). Understanding that Hartigan's fixed points are a subset of Lloyd's is key to seeing why eliminating spurious Lloyd fixed points implies Hartigan's robustness.  
  *Quick check:* What is the difference between a partition being a fixed point of Lloyd's algorithm vs. Hartigan's? Why might Hartigan have fewer fixed points?

- **Concept: High-Dimensional Geometry ("All Distances Are Equal")**  
  *Why needed:* The failure mechanism exploits that in high dimensions, the difference between scaled chi-squared means becomes negligible relative to concentration, making distance comparisons noisy. This is a precise formulation of the "all distances are equal" phenomenon.  
  *Quick check:* For two random vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) with isotropic Gaussian distributions, how does the variance of their squared distance difference behave as \(d\) increases? Why does this affect Lloyd's assignment step?

## Architecture Onboarding

The experimental and theoretical framework consists of five main components:

Data Generator → Algorithm Implementations → Evaluator → Theory Module → Experiment Harness.

**Critical path:** The theoretical module predicts failure thresholds → these inform the selection of \(d, \sigma^2\) for synthetic data generation → data generator produces samples → algorithm implementations run Lloyd, Hartigan, and baselines → evaluator computes NMI and loss → experiment harness aggregates results across trials and compares to theoretical predictions. Real-data experiments follow the same evaluation pipeline without theoretical prediction.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Lloyd vs. Hartigan: Lloyd has simpler iterations (O(ndK)) but may converge in one step and get stuck; Hartigan performs single-sample moves (O(nd²) per sweep) but converges further and avoids spurious fixed points.
- Scalability vs. optimality: SDP and spectral clustering aim for global or better local optima but scale poorly (SDP O(n³)); Lloyd/Hartigan are scalable but can be trapped; Hartigan offers a better trade-off in high-noise high-dimensional regimes.
- Initialization: Lloyd's performance is highly sensitive to initialization (k-means++ helps but does not prevent trapping); Hartigan is more robust to random balanced partitions.

**Failure signatures:**
- Lloyd: Convergence in exactly 1 iteration (Figure 5), output equals initialization; NMI and loss show no improvement across iterations; particularly severe with random partition initialization.
- Hartigan: May require more iterations but typically improves beyond initialization; in high-dim regimes it recovers ground truth, while Lloyd does not.
- Spectral/SDP: Performance degrades gradually as \(d\) increases or \(\sigma^2\) grows, not as abruptly as Lloyd; however, SDP becomes computationally infeasible for \(n \gtrsim 500\).
- Empty clusters: Lloyd can encounter empty clusters during centroid recomputation; Hartigan's move rule prevents empties if initialized with non-empty clusters.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Synthetic GMM parameter sweep: Vary \(d\) (e.g., 10 to 2000) and \(\sigma^2\) (e.g., 0.5 to 10) for \(K=2,5,10\), run all algorithms with 100 random initializations, compute mean NMI and loss. Replicates Figures 1–6.
2. Single-sample movement probability: For \(n=40, \tau^2=1\), create a balanced ground truth with two clusters, manually move a single sample from cluster 1 to cluster 2 so that its purity ratio in the wrong cluster is 0.25 (vs 0.75 in correct). For varying \(d\) and \(\sigma^2\), run Lloyd and Hartigan and record whether the sample moves back. Compare empirical probability to theoretical \(\rho^{d/4}\) bounds. Replicates Figure 9.
3. Real-data benchmarking: Preprocess Olivetti faces (400 samples, 4096 features, 40 classes) and 20 Newsgroups subsets (200–1000 samples, 5000 features, 2–10 classes) as described. Run all algorithms with 500 initializations (except SDP), select best k-means loss output, compute NMI relative to true labels. Replicates Table 1 and Figure 6.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Transition regime for Lloyd's failure:** How does the fixed-point proliferation weaken as noise or dimension decreases, and can we quantify the trapping probability outside the asymptotic regime (\(d \gtrsim n^3, \sigma^2 > n\))? The paper identifies this as a key open direction (Section 5).
2. **Sharper guarantees for Hartigan:** Can Hartigan's algorithm be given tighter theoretical bounds and recovery guarantees comparable to SDP relaxations? The authors ask whether the current conservative bounds (\(\rho_h\)) can be significantly tightened (Section 5).
3. **Extension to \(K > 2\) clusters:** Does the stark separation between Lloyd and Hartigan observed for two clusters persist for general k-means with \(K > 2\)? The paper suggests the extension appears feasible but remains unproven (Section 5).
4. **EM algorithm behavior:** Does the Expectation-Maximization algorithm for Gaussian mixtures suffer similar catastrophic failure in high-noise, high-dimensional settings? The paper notes EM's similarity to Lloyd and suggests the phenomenon may carry over, but defers detailed analysis (Section 5).

## Limitations

- **Model assumptions:** The theoretical analysis assumes isotropic Gaussian mixtures with balanced clusters and additive Gaussian noise. Real data often violate these assumptions (e.g., manifold structure, heavy tails, cluster imbalance).
- **Asymptotic thresholds:** The conditions \(d \gtrsim n^3\) and \(d \gtrsim n\sigma^4\) are derived asymptotically; finite-sample constants or practical data regimes may require far larger dimensions to observe the predicted effects, or the thresholds may shift.
- **Baseline underspecification:** Key baseline methods (PCA+Lloyd, spectral clustering, SDP) lack detailed implementation parameters (e.g., number of PCA components, kernel bandwidth, solver tolerance), making exact replication challenging and potentially affecting the empirical comparisons.

## Confidence

- **Lloyd's fixed-point proliferation:** High confidence under the Gaussian mixture model; medium confidence for broader data distributions.
- **Hartigan's avoidance:** High confidence under the model; medium confidence in real-world applicability.
- **Dimensional threshold separation:** High confidence in the theoretical derivation; medium confidence due to limited empirical validation across diverse settings.
- **Empirical superiority of Hartigan:** Medium confidence, primarily because of implementation ambiguities in the baselines and the focus on synthetic data.
