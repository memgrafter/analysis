# Neural collapse in the orthoplex regime
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.20587
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20587
- Reference count: 11
- Primary result: In the orthoplex regime (d+2≤n≤2d), softmax codes coincide with spherical codes, hardmax codes are self‑dual, and cross‑entropy minimizers exhibit a temperature‑driven phase transition between low‑ and high‑entropy configurations.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates neural collapse, a phenomenon observed in trained deep networks where features and classifiers become geometrically aligned, in the regime where the number of classes \(n\) exceeds the feature dimension \(d\) but remains at most twice it (\(d+2 \le n \le 2d\)). The authors conduct a theoretical analysis within the unconstrained features model, where features and weight vectors are free unit vectors on a sphere. Their main contributions are threefold: (i) they prove that softmax codes—configurations that maximize the minimum distance of a feature to the convex hull of the other classes—coincide exactly with spherical codes, i.e., those minimizing the maximum inner product between distinct classes; (ii) they establish self‑duality for the hardmax loss, showing that at optimum every feature vector equals its corresponding weight vector; (iii) they characterize the effect of temperature \(\tau\) on the cross‑entropy loss, proving thresholds \(\tau^- < \tau^+\) such that low‑entropy codes (a simplex plus an orthogonal orthoplex) minimize loss for \(\tau < \tau^-\), while high‑entropy codes (mutually orthogonal simplices) are optimal for \(\tau > \tau^+\).

The technical approach hinges on Radon’s theorem and convex geometry to link the two optimality criteria, and on decomposing any orthoplex‑regime code into \(n-d\) mutually orthogonal batches each forming a regular simplex. This decomposition reduces the loss to a sum of functions of the batch dimensions; analyzing the concavity/convexity of these functions yields the temperature‑driven phase transition. The results provide a rigorous foundation for understanding neural collapse beyond the standard simplex case and reveal how temperature influences the geometry of learned representations.

## Method Summary
The study adopts the unconstrained features model, treating the per‑class feature vectors \(h_{k,i}\) and classifier weights \(w_k\) as independent variables constrained to the unit sphere \(S^{d-1}\). The analysis proceeds in three phases. First, within the orthoplex regime (\(d+2 \le n \le 2d\)), it establishes the equivalence \(\delta(X)=1 \iff \alpha(X)=0\) using Radon’s theorem to translate a positive maximum inner product into a violation of the convex‑hull distance condition. Second, for the hardmax loss (\(\tau\to 0\)), this equivalence together with the rattler‑free nature of spherical codes implies that any minimizer must satisfy \(h_{k,i}=w_k\) (self‑duality). Third, for finite temperature, any softmax code can be decomposed into \(l = n-d\) orthogonal batches; each batch is an origin‑centered regular simplex of some dimension \(d_i\). Plugging the optimal intra‑batch geometry into the cross‑entropy loss yields a separable objective \(\sum f_{n,\tau}(d_i)\). The function \(f_{n,\tau}\) is shown to be strictly concave for small \(\tau\) and strictly convex for large \(\tau\), with thresholds \(\tau^-, \tau^+\). Consequently, the discrete optimization over integer dimensions \(\{d_i\}\) favors either an extremely unbalanced allocation (low entropy) or a balanced one (high entropy), depending on \(\tau\). This yields the phase transition described above.

## Key Results
- Softmax codes = spherical codes (\(\delta=1\) iff \(\alpha=0\)) in the orthoplex regime.
- Hardmax codes are self‑dual: \(h_k = w_k\) for all classes.
- For cross‑entropy with temperature \(\tau\), thresholds \(\tau^-(n) < \tau^+(n)\) exist; low‑entropy codes (simplex+orthoplex) are optimal for \(\tau < \tau^-\), while high‑entropy codes (orthogonal simplices) are optimal for \(\tau > \tau^+\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Softmax codes coincide with spherical codes.** In the orthoplex regime (\(d+2 \le n \le 2d\)), a configuration \(X\) of \(n\) unit vectors maximizes the minimum distance \(\delta(X)\) from a point to the convex hull of the others if and only if it minimizes the maximum inner product \(\alpha(X)\). The forward direction uses Radon’s theorem: if \(\alpha>0\) there exists a pair with positive inner product, and the Radon partition of the points (two subsets whose convex hulls intersect) yields a point whose distance to the convex hull of the complement is strictly less than 1 (Lemma 4). Conversely, \(\alpha=0\) implies every point lies in a half‑space defined by the others, forcing \(\delta\ge 1\) (Theorem 2). Rankin’s orthoplex bound guarantees \(\alpha\ge 0\) in general, and \(\alpha=0\) is achievable exactly when \(n\le 2d\). For \(n>2d\), \(\alpha>0\) always, so the equivalence fails.

**Self‑duality of hardmax codes.** Under the same regime, any hardmax code \((W, H)\) that minimizes the hardmax loss satisfies \(h_{k,i}=w_k\) for all classes \(k\) and samples \(i\). This relies on two “rattler‑free” properties of softmax codes: (i) every point lies exactly at distance \(\delta=1\) from the convex hull of the others, and (ii) every point attains the maximum inner product \(\alpha=0\) with some other point. Lemmas 8 and 9 establish these for spherical codes in the orthoplex range. Then Proposition 7 (from prior work) implies that when softmax codes coincide with spherical codes and both are rattler‑free, the only way to satisfy the hardmax optimality conditions is \(h=w\). Thus the feature means equal the weight vectors.

**Temperature‑dependent optimal codes.** For cross‑entropy loss with temperature \(\tau\), the loss can be expressed solely in terms of the dimensions of the orthogonal batch simplices in the code decomposition. The within‑batch contribution takes the form \(f_{n,\tau}(d_i)\), where \(d_i\) is the dimension of the \(i\)th simplex. Lemma 15 analyzes \(f_{n,\tau}\): it is strictly concave for small \(\tau\) and strictly convex for large \(\tau\), with thresholds \(\tau^- < \tau^+\) separating these regimes. In the concave regime (\(\tau < \tau^-\)), the discrete optimization over integer dimensions favors a single large \(d_i\) and all remaining \(d_j = 1\), giving a low‑entropy code: one regular simplex of maximal size plus an orthogonal orthoplex. In the convex regime (\(\tau > \tau^+\)), the optimum spreads dimensions as evenly as possible, yielding high‑entropy codes consisting of mutually orthogonal regular simplices with balanced dimensions. For intermediate \(\tau\), the landscape is more complex and not fully characterized.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Feature extractor \(F: \mathbb{R}^r \to \mathbb{R}^d\) produces last‑layer features; in the unconstrained model we treat the features \(h_{k,i}\) as free unit‑norm variables. Linear head \(L: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^n\) with weight vectors \(w_k\) (\(\|w_k\|=1\)). Softmax with temperature \(\tau\): \(p_k(x) = \exp(\langle w_k, f\rangle/\tau) / \sum_j \exp(\langle w_j, f\rangle/\tau)\). Loss: \(L_{CE} = - \frac{1}{mn} \sum_{k,i} \log p_k(h_{k,i})\).

### Critical path
1. **Unconstrained features model**: Decouple the feature extractor; optimize directly over \(W = \{w_k\}\) and \(H = \{h_{k,i}\}\).
2. **Hardmax limit** (\(\tau \to 0\)): Reduces to maximizing \(\delta(X)\) (softmax code) and the hardmax loss; yields self‑duality.
3. **Orthoplex regime analysis**: Use Radon’s theorem (Section 2) to characterize softmax codes as spherical codes; show no rattlers (Section 3); decompose any code into orthogonal simplices (Section 4).
4. **Finite temperature**: Express loss in terms of batch dimensions \(d_i\); study concavity/convexity of \(f_{n,\tau}\) to derive entropy regimes.

### Design tradeoffs
- **n vs d**: If \(n \le d+1\), simplex geometry; if \(d+2 \le n \le 2d\), orthoplex regime; if \(n > 2d\), spherical codes are more complex and not fully characterized.
- **Temperature**: Low \(\tau\) favors low‑entropy (one large simplex + orthoplex); high \(\tau\) favors high‑entropy (balanced simplices). Intermediate \(\tau\) may have multiple minimizers.
- **Bias terms**: Analysis often removes biases to avoid translation ambiguity; including biases can shift the geometry and potentially alter collapse patterns.

### Failure signatures
- **n > 2d**: Rankin’s bound prevents \(\alpha=0\), so \(\delta<1\) always; softmax codes cannot be spherical; neural collapse may not achieve the neat orthoplex structure.
- **Rattlers present**: If for some class the point is not “tight” in the \(\delta\) condition, self‑duality \(h = w\) may fail; optimization may not converge to zero loss.
- **Intermediate \(\tau\)**: The discrete optimization is non‑convex; gradient‑based training might get stuck in suboptimal codes or oscillate.
- **Non‑balanced classes**: The theory assumes equal class counts; imbalance can change the geometry.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Validate orthoplex geometry**  
   Set \(d=4, n=6\). Train a linear classifier with a ReLU feature extractor (or directly optimize unconstrained features) with a small temperature (\(\tau=0.05\)) until training loss \(\approx 0\). Measure the inner products between weight vectors; expect all near zero (orthoplex). Compute \(\delta\) and \(\alpha\); expect \(\delta\approx 1\), \(\alpha\approx 0\).
2. **Temperature sweep**  
   Same \((d,n)\) but vary \(\tau\) from \(0.01\) to \(10\). For each \(\tau\) after convergence, record the loss and the distribution of simplex dimensions in the configuration (e.g., count how many distinct directions have 3 vs 2 points). Expect low‑entropy (one tetrahedron + antipodal pair) for \(\tau <\) estimated \(\tau^-\), and high‑entropy (two equilateral triangles in orthogonal planes) for \(\tau >\) estimated \(\tau^+\).
3. **Self‑duality check**  
   Under hardmax conditions (\(\tau\) very small or using hardmax loss directly), after convergence compute for each class \(k\) the mean feature \(h_k\) of all training examples in that class. Compare \(h_k\) to \(w_k\) after normalizing; expect \(\|h_k - w_k\| \approx 0\). If significant deviation, investigate presence of rattlers or optimization issues.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Are softmax codes always spherical codes?  
**Basis in paper:** Section 5 directly asks: “Are softmax codes always spherical codes?” and notes the coincidence holds only in specific regimes.  
**Why unresolved:** Complete characterizations exist only for the simplex regime (\(n \le d+1\)) and the orthoplex regime (\(d+2 \le n \le 2d\)). For other ranges, the corresponding spherical codes are not fully known.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Prove that for every \((d,n)\) beyond the orthoplex regime the maximizer of \(\delta(X)\) also minimizes \(\alpha(X)\), or exhibit a counterexample where the two notions diverge.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Is there a generalization of the theory of universal optimality that explains this coincidence?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors explicitly query: “Is there a generalization of the theory of universal optimality that explains this coincidence?”  
**Why unresolved:** Universal optimality is a strong property that has been proved for some spherical codes, but its connection to the softmax objective (maximizing minimum distance to convex hulls) remains unclear.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Show that softmax codes minimize a wide class of potential functions (e.g., \(p\)-th power Riesz energies) for all \(p>0\), or develop a conceptual framework linking the two optimality criteria.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What is the complete structure of softmax codes when the number of classes exceeds twice the feature dimension (\(n > 2d\))?  
**Basis in paper:** The discussion asks: “Is there any hope of determining softmax codes with \(n > 2d\)?”  
**Why unresolved:** The analysis in the paper relies on Radon’s theorem and convexity arguments that are most effective up to \(n = 2d\). For \(n > 2d\), the geometry of feasible codes is less constrained and classical spherical code classifications are largely unknown.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Obtain new necessary or sufficient conditions for \(\delta(X)\) maximization in the overcompleteness regime, and use them to classify softmax codes for selected high \(n\) (e.g., \(n = 2d+1, 3d\), etc.), either analytically or through rigorous numerical optimization.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** How do the minimizers of cross‑entropy loss with temperature \(\tau\) vary when \(\tau\) is in the intermediate range between the derived thresholds \(\tau^-(n)\) and \(\tau^+(n)\)?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors state: “it would be interesting to better understand the cross‑entropy loss minimizers with positive temperatures \(\tau > 0\), and how the optimizers evolve with \(\tau\),” after establishing only the asymptotic regimes.  
**Why unresolved:** Theorem 12 identifies low‑entropy codes as optimal for \(\tau\) below \(\tau^-\) and high‑entropy codes for \(\tau\) above \(\tau^+\), but the landscape for \(\tau \in (\tau^-, \tau^+)\) is not analyzed; the loss function may favor other configurations or exhibit bifurcations.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Perform a full optimization of \(L_{CE}(W,W)\) over \(\mathrm{SMC}(d,n)\) for intermediate \(\tau\), perhaps by solving the reduced discrete problem over simplex dimensions \((d_1,\dots,d_l)\) and showing the optimal partition changes continuously or through phase transitions.

## Limitations
- The analysis assumes an idealized unconstrained feature model (unit‑norm points on a sphere); real networks with finite width, ReLU nonlinearities, and SGD noise may not realize exact spherical geometries.
- Results are restricted to the orthoplex regime (\(d+2 \le n \le 2d\)); for \(n > 2d\), Rankin’s bound forces \(\alpha>0\), so \(\delta<1\) always, and the softmax‑spherical code equivalence fails.
- Self‑duality and the temperature‑driven phase transitions rely on “rattler‑free” configurations and equal class sizes; deviations due to optimization constraints, class imbalance, or biases can break these conclusions.
