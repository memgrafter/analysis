# BXRL: Behavior-Explainable Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23738
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23738
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** Introduces Behavior-Explainable RL (BXRL), formalizing behavior as a differentiable scalar measure over policies to enable gradient-based explanation and adaptation of existing XRL methods.

## Executive Summary
Explainable Reinforcement Learning (XRL) lacks a formal notion of "behavior" as a recurring pattern of actions across episodes, hindering measurement and explanation. This paper proposes Behavior-Explainable Reinforcement Learning (BXRL), treating behaviors as first‑class objects via **behavior measures**: differentiable functions \(m(\pi)\) over policies satisfying action‑based, recurring, graded, and scoped properties. By expressing \(m(\pi)\) as an expectation \(\mathbb{E}_{o\sim D}[g(\pi_\theta(\cdot|o))]\) over a fixed observation distribution \(D\), the measure becomes end‑to‑end differentiable, enabling gradient‑based analysis of contrastive "why" questions (e.g., why does the policy do \(a\) rather than \(a'\)?). The authors show that three existing XRL techniques—data attribution (TracIn‑style), Shapley value attribution (SVERL‑P), and counterfactual policy search (COUNTERPOL)—can be adapted to target any differentiable \(m(\pi)\) by simply substituting the original scalar objective (typically expected return). They release **HighJax**, a JAX port of HighwayEnv, which provides a differentiable environment and utilities to define and compute behaviors. An empirical demonstration defines a "collision" behavior measure \(m_c\) and finds its value rises during PPO training even as the actual collision rate drops, revealing that the agent learns to speed up despite crashing. BXRL thus offers a practical framework for quantifying and explaining agent behaviors.

## Method Summary
BXRL’s core innovation is the formalization of a **behavior measure** \(m(\pi)\) as a differentiable scalar function of a policy. To ensure differentiability, the measure is typically defined as an expectation over a fixed dataset of observations \(D\):  
\(m(\pi_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{o\sim D}[g(\pi_\theta(\cdot|o))]\),  
where \(g\) operates on action probabilities (e.g., probability of a specific action, or a difference of probabilities). This construction allows computation of \(\nabla_\theta m(\pi_\theta)\), which points in parameter directions that increase the behavior. The paper then adapts three XRL methods:  
- **Data attribution (TracIn):** Influence score \(I_i = \langle \nabla_\theta m(\pi_\theta), \nabla_\theta L_{\text{PPO}}(\theta, z_i) \rangle\) attributes each training record’s contribution to \(m(\pi)\).  
- **SVERL‑P (Shapley values):** Replaces expected return with \(m(\pi)\) to compute feature‑level Shapley contributions.  
- **COUNTERPOL:** Optimizes a counterfactual policy \(\pi_\theta^{\text{cf}}\) by minimizing \(|m(\pi_\theta) - m^*| + k\cdot D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta^0 \| \pi_\theta)\) under a KL constraint.  

A minimal reproduction requires: (1) installing HighJax; (2) training or loading a PPO agent; (3) defining a differentiable behavior measure (e.g., the paper’s collision behavior \(m_c\) using six specific observations); (4) implementing one adaptation (e.g., computing gradient inner products for data attribution); and (5) verifying that the output (influence scores, Shapley values, or a counterfactual policy) is plausible.

## Key Results
- Formal definition of behavior measures with four properties (action‑based, recurring, graded, scoped), enabling gradient‑based explanations.
- Theoretical analysis showing three XRL methods can be adapted to any differentiable behavior measure with minimal structural changes.
- Release of **HighJax**, a JAX‑compatible, differentiable version of HighwayEnv that facilitates behavior definition and optimization.
- Empirical case study: a collision behavior measure \(m_c\) increases during PPO training even as the agent’s real collision rate declines, indicating the policy learns to accelerate despite crashes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Gradient‑Based Analysis via Differentiable Measures
Formalizing behavior as a differentiable scalar function \(m(\pi_\theta)\) (e.g., \( \mathbb{E}_{o\sim D}[ \pi_\theta(\text{FASTER}|o) ] \)) allows computation of \(\nabla_\theta m(\pi_\theta)\). This gradient reveals how to adjust policy parameters to increase or decrease the behavior. The core assumption is that the behavior of interest can be captured by a smooth aggregation of action probabilities over a fixed distribution \(D\) independent of \(\theta\). Evidence is provided by the formal definition (Section 5) and concrete examples (Table 2). The approach breaks if the behavior depends on non‑differentiable environmental outcomes (e.g., raw collision counts) or if \(D\) is unrepresentative.

### Contrastive Explanation Measures
Many "why" questions are contrastive (e.g., why \(a\) over \(a'\) in state \(o_P\) vs. \(o_Q\)?). BXRL reduces such questions to explaining a behavior measure of the form \(\pi_\theta(a|o_P) - \pi_\theta(a|o_Q)\) (observation‑contrasting) or \(\pi_\theta(a_P|o) - \pi_\theta(a_Q|o)\) (action‑contrasting). These differences are themselves valid behavior measures, so any BXRL method can attack them. The mechanism assumes the contrast can be expressed as a difference in single‑step action probabilities. Break condition: the contrast involves temporal sequences or multi‑step outcomes not reducible to such a difference.

### Adaptability of Existing XRL Methods
Three XRL methods rely on a scalar differentiable objective and gradients. By substituting their original objective (usually expected return) with an arbitrary differentiable \(m(\pi_\theta)\), they become usable for behavior explanations:
1. **Data attribution:** Replace target \(f(\theta)\) with \(m(\pi_\theta)\) in TracIn‑style influence scores.
2. **SVERL‑P:** Replace expected return in Shapley value computation with \(m(\pi)\); the axioms still apply to the new cooperative game.
3. **COUNTERPOL:** Replace return \(J(\pi_\theta)\) with \(m(\pi_\theta)\) in the counterfactual optimization problem; the KL constraint retains stability.
The core assumption is that the method’s core algorithm depends only on differentiability and gradient access, not on semantics of the objective. Break conditions arise if \(m(\pi)\) has properties that invalidate method assumptions (e.g., high variance, non‑stationarity, or lack of unbiased estimators).

## Foundational Learning
- **MDPs and Policy Gradient Methods**  
  BXRL operates on policies \(\pi_\theta\) defined over Markov Decision Processes. Understanding the policy gradient theorem, advantage estimation, and KL‑divergence constraints (used in PPO and COUNTERPOL) is essential to grasp how parameter changes affect the policy and thus the behavior measure.  
  *Quick check:* How does a KL‑divergence constraint in PPO (\(D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\text{old}} \| \pi_{\text{new}})\)) limit the step size, and why is this relevant when searching for counterfactual policies that change a behavior?
- **Contrastive Explanation (Lipton, Miller)**  
  BXRL explicitly frames explanations as contrastive: "why \(P\) rather than \(Q\)?" The definition of contrastive behavior measures directly builds on this idea. Misunderstanding contrastivity leads to poorly framed behavior measures.  
  *Quick check:* What is the "Difference Condition" for a good contrastive explanation, and how does an observation‑contrasting behavior measure \(\pi(a|o_P)-\pi(a|o_Q)\) satisfy it?
- **Differentiable Programming (JAX)**  
  HighJax and the differentiability of \(m(\pi)\) rely on JAX’s autograd and just‑in‑time compilation. Engineers must understand `vmap`, `jit`, and how to structure computation to maintain end‑to‑end differentiability through the policy network.  
  *Quick check:* Why must the observation distribution \(D\) be independent of policy parameters \(\theta\) for \(m(\pi_\theta)\) to be differentiable with respect to \(\theta\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  HighJax -> Observation dataset \(D\) -> Policy \(\pi_\theta\) -> Behavior measure \(m(\pi_\theta)\) -> BXRL Method Adapter -> Explanation (influence scores / Shapley values / counterfactual policy)  
  (Visualization/TUI assists in defining \(D\) and \(g\) for \(m\).)
- **Critical path:**  
  (1) Define behavior → (2) Implement \(m(\pi)\) as differentiable JAX function → (3) Choose BXRL method and adapt it (substitute \(m\) for original objective) → (4) Run analysis on trained policy → (5) Interpret output.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Choice of \(D\):* A narrow \(D\) (few specific states) makes \(m\) sensitive to local quirks; a broad \(D\) averages across contexts but may dilute signal.  
  - *Method selection:* Data attribution is local to a training round; SVERL‑P offers global feature importance but scales exponentially; COUNTERPOL yields a concrete counterfactual policy but does not decompose causes.  
  - *Computational cost:* Differentiability is cheap; Shapley values are expensive; counterfactual search requires many policy evaluations.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *Non‑differentiable \(m\):* If \(m\) depends on environment rollouts (e.g., \(\mathbb{E}_\tau[\text{collision\_count}]\)), gradients are high‑variance or unavailable. Solution: redefine \(m\) in terms of action probabilities only.  
  - *Mis‑scoped \(D\):* \(m\) measures an unintended variant (e.g., near‑zero for all policies or highly unstable).  
  - *Method‑specific:* All‑zero influence scores → \(\nabla_\theta m\) is small or \(D\) out‑of‑distribution; COUNTERPOL fails to reach target \(m^*\) → KL weight too low or \(m^*\) unreachable.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Reproduce \(m_c\)** from Section 8: Load a pre‑trained PPO agent in HighJax; define \(m_c\) using six cherry‑picked collision scenarios (Appendix B); compute \(m_c\) at training checkpoints; verify it rises as described.  
  2. **Adapt SVERL‑P to \(m_c\):** Compute Shapley values for discrete actions (5) and state features (5×5 matrix) by marginalizing \(m(\pi)\) across feature masks; identify features that increase vs. decrease \(m_c\).  
  3. **COUNTERPOL variant for decreasing \(m_c\):** Set target \(m^* = 0\); solve Eq. (3) with PPO‑style gradient and KL penalty; inspect counterfactual policy \(\pi_\theta^{\text{cf}}\) – it should reduce probability of FASTER/LEFT/RIGHT in \(D\) while minimizing KL from \(\pi_\theta^0\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Implementation and evaluation of BXRL methods**  
   The paper only shows that existing XRL methods can be structurally adapted, but provides no empirical comparison. Open due to lack of standardized implementations, benchmarks, and objective metrics for explanation quality.
2. **User‑study design for XRL**  
   What rigorous user‑study designs and objective success metrics can validate that behavior‑level explanations actually improve human understanding, trust, or decision‑making in safety‑critical RL? The paper calls for objective metrics as a prerequisite for claims of explanatory utility.
3. **Generalization to Behavior‑Explainable AI (BXAI)**  
   Can the BXRL framework be effectively generalized to non‑RL settings (e.g., supervised learning, LLMs)? The Discussion proposes BXAI (e.g., capturing sycophancy in LLMs) but offers no concrete formulation or methods.
4. **Defining measures for complex, multi‑step behaviors**  
   How to robustly define behavior measures for temporally extended behaviors (e.g., overtaking) that involve divergent trajectories, unvisited states, and compositional actions? The paper identifies three core challenges but leaves solutions to future work.

## Limitations
- **HighJax reproducibility unknown:** The environment code is not public‑vetted; bugs could invalidate empirical results.
- **No general framework for complex behavior measures:** Only simplistic examples (e.g., collision) are detailed; scaling to realistic, temporally extended behaviors remains an open problem.
- **Domain‑specific validation:** Adaptations are evaluated solely on HighwayEnv; transfer to other domains and XRL methods is unproven.
