# Fine-Grained Uncertainty Quantification for Long-Form Language Model Outputs: A Comparative Study

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17431  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17431  
- **Reference count:** 30  
- **Primary result:** Claim-response entailment scoring achieves top performance (AUROC up to 0.80) and uncertainty-aware decoding improves factual accuracy by up to +0.18 (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Flash on Bio).

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the challenge of detecting hallucinations in long-form language model outputs, where traditional short-form uncertainty methods fail. The authors propose a unified three-stage taxonomy: decompose responses into fine-grained units (sentences or claims), score each unit via semantic consistency across sampled alternatives, and aggregate scores to the response level. Four scorer families are formalized and evaluated across multiple LLMs and datasets. Key findings show that claim-level scoring—specifically using entailment between sampled responses and original claims—consistently outperforms other methods. Furthermore, uncertainty-aware decoding, which filters low-confidence claims before reconstruction, yields substantial gains in factual accuracy. Efficiency analysis reveals that sampling just five alternative responses captures most of the performance benefit.

## Method Summary
The framework consists of: (1) **Decomposition** into sentences (via SpaCy) or atomic claims (via LLM prompt). (2) **Sampling & Scoring**: Generate *m* alternative responses; for each original unit, compute a consistency score *c* using one of four families: (a) *unit-response*: direct NLI/embedding similarity between each sample and the original unit; (b) *matched-unit*: match each original unit to the best-scoring unit in each sample; (c) *unit-QA*: invert each unit into a question, generate answers for samples, compare to original; (d) *graph-based*: decompose all samples, merge claims into a union set, build a bipartite entailment graph, and compute node centralities. (3) **Aggregation**: average unit scores (*A_avg*) for detection, or apply uncertainty-aware decoding (*A_UAD*) by thresholding unit scores, retaining high-confidence claims, and reconstructing a coherent response. Evaluation uses FactScore (biography and STEM/Geographic QA) with Gemini and GPT-4o models, measuring AUROC, AUPRC, calibration, and accuracy.

## Key Results
- Claim-response entailment achieves the highest AUPRC across all eight model-dataset combinations, with AUROC up to 0.80.
- Claim-level scoring substantially outperforms sentence-level approaches (e.g., AUROC ~0.80 vs. ~0.70).
- Uncertainty-aware decoding yields large factual accuracy gains; for example, it increases accuracy from 0.72 to 0.90 for Gemini-2.5-Flash on Bio.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Fine-Grained Decomposition and Entailment Scoring**  
- **Claim:** Decomposing responses into atomic claims and scoring each via semantic consistency against sampled alternatives yields superior hallucination detection.  
- **Mechanism:** The three-stage pipeline localizes uncertainty to specific factual units. By comparing each claim against multiple sampled responses using NLI-based entailment (*p_entailment*), the method captures semantic agreement on atomic facts more precisely than comparing full sentences or embedding similarity.  
- **Core assumption:** Factual correctness of a long-form response is determined by the correctness of its constituent atomic claims, and semantic consistency (via NLI) across stochastic samples reliably correlates with claim veracity.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "propose a unified three-stage taxonomy..."; [section 3.1] formalization of pipeline; [section 4.2] "claim-response entailment achieves the highest AUPRC in all 8 scenarios" and "claim-level scoring generally yields better results than sentence-level."

**Mechanism 2: Uncertainty-Aware Decoding**  
- **Claim:** Filtering low-confidence claims before reconstruction substantially improves factual accuracy.  
- **Mechanism:** UAD leverages granular claim-level confidence scores. By thresholding and discarding claims with scores below *τ_claim*, the system removes likely hallucinated content while preserving high-confidence facts. This turns uncertainty quantification from a diagnostic into a generative quality control.  
- **Core assumption:** A confidence threshold exists that separates factually correct claims from incorrect ones, and the retained claims are sufficient for coherent reconstruction.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "uncertainty-aware decoding... yields large factual accuracy gains—increasing accuracy from 0.72 to 0.90 for Gemini-2.5-Flash"; [section 3.4] formal definition; [section 4.3] Figure 6 showing consistent improvements.

**Mechanism 3: Sample Efficiency**  
- **Claim:** Sampling just five alternative responses captures most of the performance benefit for fine-grained UQ.  
- **Mechanism:** The consistency signal converges quickly as sample count *m* increases; additional samples provide diminishing returns because the core semantic variance is captured by a small set of stochastic draws.  
- **Core assumption:** The distribution of semantic variations for a given prompt has low variance relative to its mean, or key modes are sampled with few trials.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4] "using just five sampled responses captures most of the performance benefit"; [appendix A] diminishing returns beyond *m*=5; [section 5] computational trade-offs table.

## Foundational Learning

**Semantic Consistency via NLI**  
- **Why needed:** Core unit-level scoring relies on measuring whether a claim from the original response is *entailed* by sampled responses, requiring understanding of Natural Language Inference.  
- **Quick check:** Why might *p_entailment* be preferred over cosine similarity for claim-level scoring in long-form UQ?

**Decomposition Granularity (Sentences vs. Claims)**  
- **Why needed:** The paper's central finding hinges on using *claims* (atomic, verifiable facts) rather than sentences, requiring understanding of the decomposition function *δ_g* and why claims are better units.  
- **Quick check:** How does a sentence containing multiple claims confound sentence-level uncertainty scoring compared to claim-level scoring?

**Aggregation Operators & Uncertainty-Aware Decoding**  
- **Why needed:** Moving from unit-level scores to response-level confidence requires an aggregation function *A*; UAD is a non-standard aggregator that filters and reconstructs.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Input (prompt *x*, original response *y₀*) → Decomposition (*δ_g(y₀)* → units *S_g*) → Sampling & Scoring (generate *m* samples *y_cand*; for each unit *s*, compute *c_g(s; y_cand)* via chosen family) → Aggregation (average *A_avg* or UAD filter/reconstruct *δ_UAD*) → Output (confidence score or reconstructed response).

**Critical path:**  
1. Implement decomposition: sentence tokenizer (SpaCy) or LLM-based claim extractor.  
2. Implement sampling and semantic consistency *η* (NLI model for entailment).  
3. Implement at least one scorer family (claim-response recommended) and aggregation.  
4. Validate on a small labeled dataset (FactScore grades) to compute AUROC/AUPRC.  
5. Implement UAD loop with threshold tuning for generation applications.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Granularity:** Claim-level is more accurate but ~5× more units than sentences, increasing compute. Sentence-level is cheaper but less discriminative.  
- **Scorer family:** Claim-response entailment is best balance of performance and simplicity. Graph-based is competitive but requires decomposing all *m* samples and sequential merging (~3× cost). Claim-QA is generally poor and expensive.  
- **UAD vs. Detection:** *A_avg* is for detection; UAD adds one extra generation per response but enables factual improvement. Trade-off: cost vs. utility.  
- **Sample count *m*:** Diminishing returns after 5; more samples improve signal but increase cost linearly.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Poor calibration:** High ECE (>0.6) for graph-based metrics; scores not usable as probabilities.  
- **Signal inversion:** Negative Spearman correlation (e.g., Betweenness scores) indicates scorer inversely related to factuality.  
- **Claim-QA underperformance:** AUROC often <0.6; likely due to poor question inversion.  
- **Sentence-level ceiling:** Max AUROC ~0.70, significantly lower than claim-level (~0.80).  
- **Cost explosion:** Matched-unit/graph methods on long responses have *O(m·N²)* comparisons, leading to infeasible runtime.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate AUROC/AUPRC tables for one model-dataset (e.g., Gemini-Flash/Bio); verify claim-response entailment achieves highest AUPRC and is within 0.01 of best AUROC.  
2. Sample-size ablation: for claim-response, vary *m* ∈ {1,3,5,10} and plot AUROC/AUPRC; confirm substantial gain from 1→5 and plateau after 5.  
3. UAD threshold sweep: for top scorer, apply UAD across percentile thresholds (10%–90%); plot accuracy vs. threshold; validate large gain (e.g., +0.18) at optimal threshold.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question:** How can fine-grained UQ scorers be better calibrated, given that most evaluated methods exhibit high ECE (>0.1) and poor probability reliability?  
   **Basis:** Authors report most scorers are at best moderately calibrated and rarely attain ECE < 0.1; poor calibration limits utility for risk-sensitive applications.  
   **Why unresolved:** Calibration is critical but existing methods remain uncalibrated; systematic studies on calibration techniques (e.g., temperature scaling) are needed.

2. **Question:** Why do claim-QA scorers substantially underperform compared to claim-response entailment, and how can the question inversion process be improved for atomic claims?  
   **Basis:** Claim-QA achieves low AUROC (<0.6) and is consistently outperformed; authors speculate atomic claims are poorly suited for question-inversion but provide no deep analysis.  
   **Why unresolved:** Question inversion is core to semantic entropy; understanding its limitations could lead to better QA-based UQ.

3. **Question:** To what extent do the conclusions—particularly the superiority of claim-response entailment—generalize beyond the four LLMs and two QA datasets to other architectures and long-form tasks?  
   **Basis:** Authors acknowledge limited evaluation scope (only decoder-only LLMs on QA-style generation); findings may not hold for summarization, creative writing, or other models.  
   **Why unresolved:** Hallucination patterns and UQ effectiveness may vary across tasks and model types; replication on diverse LLMs and tasks is needed.

## Limitations
- High computational cost of graph-based and matched-unit methods; scaling is infeasible for long responses.
- Noisy claim extraction: LLM-based decomposition may produce non-atomic or overlapping claims, affecting unit labels.
- NLI miscalibration: NLI models trained on sentence pairs may produce poorly calibrated entailment probabilities for long premises.
- Domain limitation: Findings are restricted to biographical and STEM/Geographic fact generation; generalization to other tasks is uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1** (claim-level entailment superiority): **High** – Strong internal evidence across 8 model-dataset combinations; depends on NLI model and claim extractor quality.  
- **Claim 2** (UAD factual gains): **Medium** – Consistent improvements shown, but reconstruction could introduce new hallucinations; threshold sensitivity not fully characterized.  
- **Claim 3** (5-sample efficiency): **High** – Diminishing returns clearly demonstrated; likely robust across domains.
