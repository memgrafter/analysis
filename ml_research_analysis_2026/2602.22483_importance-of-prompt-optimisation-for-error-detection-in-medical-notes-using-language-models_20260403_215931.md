# Importance of Prompt Optimisation for Error Detection in Medical Notes Using Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.22483
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22483
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** Automated prompt optimisation (GEPA) boosts error detection accuracy on MEDEC from 0.669 to 0.785 with GPT-5 and from 0.578 to 0.690 with Qwen3-32B, matching or exceeding medical doctor performance and establishing a new state-of-the-art; gains generalise to out-of-distribution data.

## Executive Summary
Medical note errors threaten patient safety, necessitating reliable automated detection. This study evaluates Genetic-Pareto Prompt Optimisation (GEPA) on the MEDEC benchmark, using language models to classify clinical notes for medical errors. GEPA automatically refines prompts through a reflective process and Pareto-based selection, yielding substantial accuracy improvements: GPT-5 accuracy rose from 0.669 to 0.785, and Qwen3-32B from 0.578 to 0.690 on the in-distribution MS-test set. The best GPT-5 result matches or surpasses physician performance and sets a new state-of-the-art. Optimised prompts are short, auditable, and show robustness by improving on an out-of-distribution UW-test set. The work highlights the critical role of prompt engineering for clinical NLP and demonstrates a scalable, privacy-preserving route (via decoupled reflector) to deploy high-performing models.

## Method Summary
The study addresses a binary classification task: detect whether a clinical note contains a medical error (in treatment, management, cause, or diagnosis). The MEDEC dataset provides three splits: MS-train (2,189 notes), MS-validation (574), and MS-test (597), plus an out-of-distribution UW subset (validation 160, test 328). The core method is Genetic-Pareto Prompt Optimisation (GEPA), implemented via the DSPy framework (`heavyAUTO`). GEPA uses two models: an inference model (the target LLM, e.g., GPT-5 or Qwen3 variants) that processes notes with a given prompt, and a separate reflector model (often a stronger model like GPT-5) that analyses the inference model’s errors on minibatches of the training set. For each rollout, textual feedback is generated, stating the classification outcome and providing the actual erroneous sentence and its correction. The reflector consumes this feedback to rewrite the prompt, producing new candidate prompts. A Pareto frontier manager maintains a set of non‑dominated prompts based on their performance on the held‑out MS-validation set, thereby preserving diversity and discouraging overfitting. After optimisation, the best prompt from the frontier is selected and evaluated on MS-test and UW-test. This decoupling allows a powerful reflector to optimise prompts for a smaller, locally deployable inference model, offering a privacy‑preserving alternative to fine‑uning.

## Key Results
- GEPA improved accuracy on MS-test: GPT-5 from 0.669 to 0.785 (+11.6% absolute), Qwen3-32B from 0.578 to 0.690 (+11.2% absolute).
- Optimised GPT-5 (0.785) matched or exceeded medical doctor performance and became the new state‑of‑the‑art on MEDEC.
- Gains generalised to the out‑of‑distribution UW-test for most models, with Qwen3-32B improving from 0.515 to 0.658 (+14.3% absolute).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Pareto Frontier Selection Prevents Overfitting and Promotes Diversity
- **Claim:** The Pareto frontier mechanism in GEPA prevents overfitting to feedback data and promotes prompt diversity, which enhances generalisation to unseen test distributions.
- **Mechanism:** GEPA maintains a set of candidate prompts on a Pareto frontier based on their performance across the validation set. A prompt is retained if it is non‑dominated—i.e., it performs best on at least one validation instance. This explicitly encourages a diverse set of successful strategies rather than converging to a single potentially overfitted prompt.
- **Core assumption:** A diverse set of high‑performing prompts on an unseen validation set is more likely to generalise to a final test set than a single optimised prompt that may overfit the feedback minibatches.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.3 explains that validation‑based frontier maintenance helps preserve diverse strategies and encourages generalisation; Section 5 shows that gains from GEPA on MS‑test generalise to UW‑test for most models; external corroboration is limited.
- **Break condition:** If the validation set distribution is not representative of the deployment distribution, the frontier may select prompts that specialise to validation quirks, harming real‑world performance.

### Mechanism 2: Rich, Natural Language Feedback Encodes Clinical Knowledge
- **Claim:** Providing detailed feedback—including the specific erroneous sentence and its correction—enables the reflector model to infer underlying clinical knowledge gaps and encode them into subsequent prompt revisions.
- **Mechanism:** For each model rollout, GEPA generates a textual critique that goes beyond a scalar accuracy reward. It explicitly states the classification outcome and provides the actual erroneous sentence and its corrected version. The reflector model consumes this detailed feedback to rewrite the inference prompt, targeting the specific type of clinical misunderstanding (e.g., incorrect pharmacotherapy).
- **Core assumption:** A scalar reward signal is insufficient for prompt improvement; concrete, example‑specific corrective feedback is necessary for the reflector to deduce and rectify domain‑specific reasoning errors in the prompt’s instructions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.4 describes how feedback enables the reflector to infer reasoning gaps; Section 5 and Figure 7 show optimised prompts include domain‑specific examples and “conservative” instructions, directly addressing baseline false positives.
- **Break condition:** If the provided corrections are inconsistent, inaccurate, or biased, the reflector may codify these errors into the prompt, propagating harm.

### Mechanism 3: Decoupling Inference and Reflector Models Enables Privacy‑Preserving Transfer
- **Claim:** The decoupling of inference and reflector models allows a high‑capability, potentially expensive or non‑deployable model (like GPT-5) to optimise prompts for a smaller, secure, locally deployable model (like Qwen3), providing a privacy‑preserving route to performance gains.
- **Mechanism:** The reflector operates only during optimisation. It analyses the inference model’s errors on the feedback set and generates new prompt candidates. The final deployed system uses only the optimised prompt with the chosen inference model; the reflector is discarded.
- **Core assumption:** The linguistic and reasoning capabilities of a strong reflector are transferable; it can formulate instructions and heuristics that a weaker inference model can successfully execute.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.5 explains the decoupling; Section 5 and Figure 6 demonstrate that using GPT‑5 as reflector improves performance across most Qwen3 inference models (e.g., +9.8% absolute for Qwen3‑32B). However, for very small models (Qwen3‑0.6B) the benefit reverses.
- **Break condition:** If the capability gap is too large, the reflector may generate prompts beyond the inference model’s capacity, leading to poorer performance.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Inference Model (target LLM) takes Optimised Prompt + Clinical Note and outputs “CORRECT” or a correction. Reflector Model (stronger LLM) ingests Current Prompt + Minibatch Rollouts + Rich Feedback to produce New Prompt Candidates. Feedback Loop/Manager orchestrates rollouts and feedback generation. Pareto Frontier Manager evaluates candidates on MS‑validation and selects non‑dominated prompts. Validation Set (MS‑val) is used exclusively for frontier maintenance.

**Critical path:**  
1. Initialise with seed prompt (P#1).  
2. Run current frontier prompts on random minibatches from MS‑train; for each rollout, generate textual feedback (including erroneous sentence and correction).  
3. Feed feedback and current prompt to Reflector to produce *N* new candidates.  
4. Evaluate all new candidates on full MS‑validation set.  
5. Update Pareto frontier: add non‑dominated candidates, prune dominated ones.  
6. Repeat steps 2–5 until convergence or budget limit.  
7. Select final prompt (e.g., highest validation accuracy) from frontier.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Reflector choice:* Frontier models (GPT‑5) yield larger gains but incur API costs and rate limits; local models reduce costs but may produce smaller generalisation benefits.  
- *Prompt length:* GEPA tends to produce shorter prompts, advantageous for small context windows but possibly sacrificing few‑shot richness.  
- *Validation set size/representativeness:* Small or non‑representative validation sets risk selecting prompts that overfit to narrow distribution, harming out‑of‑distribution performance.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High false positive rate in baseline indicates overly aggressive prompts; optimisation should reduce this.  
- Performance inversion on OOD data (MS improvement but UW degradation) signals overspecialisation, especially for small models.  
- Reflector output truncation (e.g., hitting 32k token cap) may lose revision logic.  
- Compute/time explosion: hundreds of GPT‑5 API calls for numerous model pairs become prohibitively expensive.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. *Baseline establishment:* Implement the exact P#1 prompt and evaluate all target models (GPT‑5, all Qwen3 sizes) on MS‑test and UW‑test with ≥3 seeds; confirm accuracies match Table 1.  
2. *Self‑reflection limit:* For a single model (e.g., Qwen3‑32B), run GEPA using that same model as both inference and reflector; measure MS‑validation gain to establish the maximum improvement without a stronger reflector.  
3. *Cross‑model transfer:* For a smaller model (e.g., Qwen3‑4B), compare two GEPA configurations: self‑reflection vs. GPT‑5 as reflector; assess validation and test gains to test the decoupling hypothesis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Reflector generalisation across datasets:** Does the optimal reflector choice (based on MS‑val performance) hold for other medical error detection datasets or clinical settings? The paper acknowledges that the best reasoning model might differ across datasets, but no systematic cross‑dataset reflector evaluation is provided.
2. **Sensitivity to initial seed prompt:** Could a different starting prompt (e.g., more descriptive or example‑rich) lead to different GEPA outcomes? The study seeds from a specific minimal zero‑shot prompt; influence of initial prompt style remains unexplored.
3. **Source of gains: instruction clarity vs. domain examples:** Are improvements driven mainly by clearer instructions (e.g., “conservative” thresholds) or by incorporation of specific medical examples? The paper lacks ablation to disentangle these factors.
4. **Long‑term maintainability:** How often must GEPA‑optimised prompts be re‑optimised as medical knowledge evolves? The claim of easier updates over fine‑tuning is asserted but not evidenced; lifecycle cost comparison is missing.
5. **Trade‑off between sensitivity and specificity:** Optimisation improves specificity but slightly reduces sensitivity. Does this shift increase false‑negative risk for certain high‑stakes error types (e.g., diagnosis errors)? No error‑category‑wise analysis is provided.
6. **Comparison with multi‑agent or retrieval‑augmented systems:** How does single‑model GEPA compare to more complex architectures (e.g., multi‑agent debate with web retrieval) under identical inference budgets? The paper cites such work but performs no direct head‑to‑head evaluation.
7. **Auditability by clinicians:** Are the optimised prompts truly comprehensible and reviewable by clinical experts? Prompts are short but may contain dense rule combinations; clinician assessment of readability and trustworthiness is absent.
8. **Translation to real‑world clinical settings:** MEDEC uses curated simulated errors; do gains hold on unaudited, real‑world clinical notes with natural error heterogeneity? No prospective validation on live EHR data is reported.

## Limitations
- **Unknown hyperparameters:** GEPA hyperparameters within DSPy’s `heavyAUTO` preset (population size, generations, mutation/crossover rates) are unreported.
- **Undocumented feedback generation:** The exact template and criteria for producing textual feedback (which erroneous sentences are highlighted, phrasing) are not specified.
- **Inference parameter ambiguity:** Sampling settings (temperature, top_p, max_tokens) for baseline and GEPA runs are described only as “default”; precise values are unknown.

## Confidence
- **Accuracy gains:** Medium (internal consistency but limited external validation; reproduction hampered by missing details).
- **Mechanisms (Pareto diversity, feedback richness, decoupling):** Low (claims rely on paper’s own anchors and self‑citations; little independent corroboration or ablations).
- **Out‑of‑distribution generalisation:** Medium (UW improvements are promising but dataset is small; performance drops for smallest models highlight boundary conditions).
- **Prompt auditability claim:** Low (no clinician user study; unverified human comprehension).
