# PETS: A Principled Framework Towards Optimal Trajectory Allocation for Efficient Test-Time Self-Consistency

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.16745  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16745  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** PETS reduces the number of traces needed to reach perfect self-consistency by up to 75% (offline) and 55% (online) compared to uniform allocation, while matching or improving final accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Self-consistency via majority voting over multiple reasoning traces is a powerful way to boost LLM accuracy, but uniformly allocating a fixed sampling budget across questions wastes compute on easy problems that already achieve high agreement quickly. PETS tackles this inefficiency by framing trajectory allocation as an optimization of the self-consistency rate—the probability that a finite-budget majority vote matches the infinite-budget oracle. The framework provides two complementary algorithms: an offline Bayesian adaptive method that treats trajectories as noisy workers and uses an optimistic knowledge gradient to select the next question to sample; and an online method that uses a short warm-up to classify question difficulty, precomputes a greedy budget allocation over discretized difficulties, and then assigns a one-shot budget per question. Across five reasoning benchmarks and multiple LLMs, PETS reduces the number of traces needed to achieve perfect self-consistency by up to 75% (offline) and 55% (online) while maintaining or improving final accuracy, confirming the importance of adaptive budget allocation for sample-efficient test-time scaling.

## Method Summary
PETS addresses test-time self-consistency by explicitly maximizing the self-consistency rate (SC) under a bounded trace budget. For a question with answer distribution θ, SC(θ; B) grows with allocated traces B but with diminishing returns; easy questions saturate quickly, while hard ones require more samples. In the offline setting (all questions known in advance), PETS reduces the problem to a Bayesian multi-armed bandit: it maintains a posterior over θ (and optionally confidence μ) for each question, and at each step selects the question that maximizes the optimistic expected one-step increase in terminal SC (knowledge gradient). This approach adaptively concentrates samples on questions where they most improve overall consistency. In the online setting (questions arrive sequentially), PETS first obtains 4 warm-up traces to estimate difficulty, then maps the question to a discretized difficulty grid based on sorted counts. For each grid j, a Gaussian surrogate ga,b(k) approximates SC(θj; k). Using these, a greedy algorithm optimally allocates a per-question budget by repeatedly assigning 2-trace increments to the grid with highest marginal gain, which is provably optimal under diminishing returns. After allocation, all traces are aggregated by majority (or confidence-weighted majority) voting. Both variants avoid uniform wastage and achieve significant efficiency gains.

## Key Results
- Consistently reduces the number of traces required to reach perfect self-consistency by up to 75% (offline) and 55% (online) versus uniform allocation.
- Matches or improves final accuracy across five reasoning benchmarks and multiple LLMs.
- Combines theoretical grounding (Bayesian optimality and greedy optimality) with practical efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** PETS optimizes test-time compute by allocating sampling budgets based on the self-consistency rate—the probability that a finite-budget majority vote matches the infinite-budget oracle—which varies with question difficulty.
- **Mechanism:** For each question, the self-consistency rate (SC) increases with additional samples but at a diminishing rate; easy questions (with a dominant answer distribution θ) achieve high SC quickly, while hard questions require more samples. PETS allocates more traces to questions where the marginal gain in SC per additional trace is highest, maximizing the aggregate SC under a total budget.
- **Core assumption:** The model's answer distribution θ for a question is stationary and i.i.d. across traces; the population majority y∞ is well-defined and matches ground truth sufficiently for accuracy gains.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] Defines the self-consistency rate as agreement with the infinite-budget majority and states PETS formulates trajectory allocation as an optimization targeting this rate.  
  - [section 2.1] Derives SC(θ; B) as a function of θ and B, showing its dependence on question difficulty.  
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Optimal Self-Consistency..." and "Every Rollout Counts..." corroborate that self-consistency rate is a principled target for allocation. However, direct mechanistic evidence is only from the primary paper.
- **Break condition:** When the population majority y∞ is systematically wrong (i.e., θ poorly aligned with truth), increasing SC may not improve accuracy; allocation-only approaches then plateau.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** In the offline batch setting (all questions known), PETS reduces trajectory allocation to a Bayesian crowdsourcing problem, treating each reasoning trace as a noisy worker label, enabling use of proven adaptive allocation algorithms.
- **Mechanism:** PETS maintains a posterior over each question’s answer distribution θ (and confidence means μ for weighted voting). At each step, it selects the question with the largest optimistic one-step expected improvement in terminal SC (optimistic knowledge gradient), updating the posterior with observed answers and confidences. This balances exploration and exploitation akin to crowdsourcing worker/item assignment.
- **Core assumption:** The Bayesian model (Dirichlet prior for θ, Gaussian prior for μ) accurately captures the trace generation process; the posterior updates are conjugate and reflect true uncertainty.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 3] Explicitly connects to crowdsourcing, models traces as workers, and adopts the Bayesian MDP with OKG policy from Chen et al. [2013].  
  - [abstract] States that offline PETS leverages "rich existing theory" from crowdsourcing.  
  - [section A.2] Provides the full offline algorithm (Algorithm 2) with conjugate updates.
- **Break condition:** Model misspecification (e.g., non-i.i.d. traces, heavy-tailed confidence) degrades posterior quality, leading to suboptimal allocation. The OKG heuristic is not globally optimal and may suffer from approximation bias.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** In the online streaming setting (questions arrive sequentially), PETS estimates question difficulty via a short warm-up, discretizes the difficulty space, and uses a greedy allocation that provably optimizes the expected SC gain per unit budget without revisiting decisions.
- **Mechanism:** From a small training set, PETS learns a prior distribution over question difficulties, discretized into K grids with representative θj. Upon a new question’s arrival, 4 samples classify it into a grid; a precomputed budget Bj (via greedy algorithm) is allocated in one shot. The greedy algorithm iteratively assigns 2-sample increments to the grid with highest current marginal gain R(θj, Bj), which is optimal under diminishing returns.
- **Core assumption:** The warm-up (4 samples) reliably maps questions to difficulty grids; the Gaussian surrogate family ga,b(k) ≈ SC(θ;k) is accurate enough; the marginal gains R(θj, Bj) are diminishing and correctly estimated from prior.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 4.1] Describes the warm-up procedure, discretization, and one-shot allocation.  
  - [section 4.2] Presents Algorithm 1 and Theorem 4.1 proving its optimality under the discretization and diminishing returns property.  
  - [appendix B.2] Proves diminishing returns for binary choice and extends via Gaussian approximation.
- **Break condition:** Warm-up misclassification (e.g., due to low-quality or highly stochastic 4 samples) assigns wrong grid, leading to grossly misallocated budget. The Gaussian approximation fails for highly non-normal SC curves, especially with M > 2 and complex θ.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Self-Consistency Rate
- **Why needed here:** It is the objective function; SC measure defines what "efficient" means—matching infinite-budget majority with finite samples. Understanding SC’s dependence on θ and B is essential to see why adaptive allocation helps.
- **Quick check:** For a binary-choice question with θ = 0.7, does SC increase more from 2→4 samples or from 10→12 samples? Why?

### Concept: Bayesian Adaptive Allocation
- **Why needed here:** Offline PETS relies on maintaining and updating beliefs about θ (and μ) to decide which question to sample next. This is the core of casting the problem as a Bayesian MDP.
- **Quick check:** In the offline MDP, what is the state, action, and reward? How does the posterior over θ evolve after observing an answer?

### Concept: Greedy Algorithm with Diminishing Returns
- **Why needed here:** Online PETS reduces to a knapsack-like allocation over difficulty grids; the greedy algorithm is optimal when marginal gains are diminishing (a property of SC). This mathematical insight enables efficient, provably optimal online decisions.
- **Quick check:** For a grid with θ = 0.9, show that the marginal gain R(θ, B) decreases as B increases (even for B). What is R(θ, 1)?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Offline: Initialization -> [OKG Selector -> Sample -> Question Featurizer (posterior update)] repeated until total budget exhausted -> Voting Engine.  
Online: Precompute (Prior Estimator) -> For each question: Warm-up Encoder (4 traces) -> Grid Assignment -> Greedy Allocator (uses precomputed bpj, aj, bj) -> allocate additional Bj traces -> Voting Engine.

### Critical path
- **Offline flow:** Initialize Dirichlet (or Gaussian) posteriors for each question. For t = 1 to B_total: compute OKG utility R+(S_t) for all questions using current posteriors; pick question q_t with max R+; sample one trace (answer, confidence) from the LLM; update posterior for q_t. After budget, output final decision: argmax of posterior-predicted majority (or confidence-weighted).
- **Online flow:** From a held‑out training set, estimate prior distribution over difficulty patterns and fit Gaussian surrogates (a_j, b_j) for each discretized grid j. When a new question arrives: draw 4 warm‑up traces; compute sorted count vector C(4); map to grid j via a deterministic mapping; retrieve precomputed budget B_j (result of greedy coverage algorithm); sample additional B_j traces; combine with warm‑up; perform majority/confidence-weighted vote.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Offline vs. online:** Offline enjoys Bayes‑optimal guarantees and fine‑grained per‑question decisions but scales poorly with N (many questions) due to recomputing OKG and maintaining posteriors. Online provides cheap one‑shot decisions and scales well but depends on accurate warm‑up classification and precomputed approximations.
- **Confidence weighting:** Adds complexity (need to estimate μ) and can improve accuracy when majority is often correct, but miscalibrated confidences may harm consistency.
- **Grid discretization (online):** Coarser grids reduce computational cost and warm‑up misclassification risk, but lose resolution in adapting to fine‑grained difficulty variations, potentially under‑allocating to borderline questions.

### Failure signatures
- **Offline:** Low consistency despite high budget → posterior estimates stuck in local modes; OKG may over‑exploit uncertain questions. Check if updates are too slow (low trace count) or priors too weak.
- **Online:** Consistency plateaus early for specific questions → warm‑up likely misassigned them to easier grids. Inspect grid counts; if some questions receive far fewer traces than warranted, warm‑up misclassification is probable.
- **General:** When majority is systematically wrong (e.g., dataset bias), SC can be high while accuracy is low. Monitor accuracy vs. consistency divergence; PETS will efficiently reinforce errors.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Offline validation:** Run PETS‑Offline vs. uniform on a small dataset with known θ (simulated). Verify that PETS allocates more samples to questions with intermediate θ (where SC improvement is steepest) and fewer to very easy/hard ones. Check consistency gains align with theoretical marginal gains R(θ, B).
2. **Online warm‑up robustness:** Evaluate PETS‑Online with varying warm‑up sample counts (1, 4, 8). Expect accuracy drops with smaller warm‑ups due to misclassification, especially on questions with borderline count patterns (e.g., (2,1,1,0) vs (2,2,0,0)).
3. **Greedy optimality test:** In the online binary‑choice setting, construct a small instance with 3 difficulty grids and small total budget. Brute‑force all feasible allocations and confirm Algorithm 1 matches the optimum. This verifies the diminishing returns property holds approximately for the fitted Gaussian surrogate.

## Limitations
- The stationary θ assumption may fail if question difficulty changes across traces or if the LLM’s sampling distribution drifts.
- Online warm‑up with only 4 samples is fragile and can misclassify borderline or high‑variance questions, leading to grossly misallocated budgets.
- When the population majority is systematically biased, high self‑consistency does not translate to accuracy gains; PETS may efficiently reinforce incorrect answers.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Offline Bayes‑optimal allocation):** High  
- **Claim 2 (Online greedy optimality under discretization):** Medium
