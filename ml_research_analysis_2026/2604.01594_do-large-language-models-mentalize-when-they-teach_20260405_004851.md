# Do Large Language Models Mentalize When They Teach?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.01594
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01594
- Reference count: 16
- Primary result: Large language models exhibit teaching behavior aligned with a Bayes Optimal Teacher model, suggesting they approximate Bayesian mentalizing in a graph-teaching task.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) mentalize about learners when teaching. In a controlled graph-teaching task, LLMs were simulated as teachers selecting edges to reveal to a learner. Their choices were compared to cognitive models including a Bayes Optimal Teacher (BOT) that performs inverse planning, and simpler heuristic models. Results show most LLMs achieve high teaching performance and their choices are best explained by the BOT model, with performance profiles correlating strongly with human teachers (r ≈ 0.76–0.89). A scaffolding intervention, designed to reduce mentalizing costs, did not improve teaching performance, indicating that LLMs may not process such prompts as intended. The findings suggest that LLMs can approximate Bayesian mentalizing without explicit learner state representations, but the underlying mechanism remains ambiguous.

## Method Summary
The authors created a simulated teaching environment where an LLM acts as a teacher for a learner agent navigating a reward-annotated directed acyclic graph (DAG). The teacher, given the full graph and the learner’s observed partial trajectory, selects one edge to teach to maximize the learner’s future reward. The learner’s knowledge is modeled as known edges; its policy is to choose highest-reward reachable nodes. Teacher choices from multiple LLMs were fitted to three cognitive models: Bayes Optimal Teacher (BOT), Reward Heuristic, and Depth Heuristic, using softmax likelihood and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) for comparison. In a scaffolding manipulation, teachers first performed an auxiliary task (inferring unknown edges or selecting high-reward edges) before the teaching decision, to test whether such prompts improve performance. Primary metrics were Teaching Score (normalized BOT utility) and model fit.

## Key Results
- Most LLMs achieved a mean teaching score of ~0.8 on baseline trials, comparable to human performance.
- The Bayes Optimal Teacher model best explained LLM choices (lowest BIC) across seven models, and graph-wise performance profiles correlated positively with humans (r ≈ 0.76–0.89).
- Scaffolding prompts (inference or reward selection) did not reliably improve test teaching performance; in some cases performance decreased, and model fits did not shift consistently toward heuristic explanations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper evaluates three non-exclusive mechanisms to explain LLM teaching behavior:

1. **Bayesian mentalizing via inverse planning**: LLMs approximate the Bayes Optimal Teacher by internally performing inference over learner knowledge states. Evidence: BOT provides the best fit (lowest BIC) and LLM performance correlates with human teachers. Break condition: If LLMs deviate from BOT on heuristic-divergent graphs, the claim weakens; current tests show alignment but need more diagnostic graphs.

2. **Feature-based heuristic approximation**: LLMs rely on learned graph features (e.g., high rewards, critical junctions) that coincidentally align with BOT on many instances. Evidence: LLMs show sensitivity to graph properties, and authors discuss "high-capacity heuristic approximation". Break condition: If simpler heuristics explain choices better on novel graph families, mimicry is supported; current BIC favors BOT but overlap exists.

3. **Scaffolding decoupling**: The lack of improvement from scaffolding indicates LLMs treat auxiliary prompts as separate subtasks rather than integrating inferred knowledge into teaching decisions. Evidence: Strong auxiliary-step compliance yet no test gain or model shift. Break condition: If future coupling of reasoning to decision improves performance, the decoupling assumption would be falsified.

## Foundational Learning
- **Inverse Planning (Theory of Mind)**  
  Why needed: The Bayes Optimal Teacher model is built on inferring a learner’s knowledge from observed actions. Understanding this is essential to interpret whether LLMs are truly mentalizing.  
  Quick check: How does inferring *why* a learner took a particular path lead to better teaching decisions than simply observing the path?

- **Cognitive Model Fitting & BIC**  
  Why needed: The paper compares LLM behavior to cognitive models using BIC to determine which strategy best explains choices; this is the core analytical method.  
  Quick check: Why might a lower BIC for the BOT model not conclusively prove that an LLM is performing explicit Bayesian inference?

- **Scaffolding as Cognitive Intervention**  
  Why needed: The scaffolding manipulation tests whether external prompts can shift teaching strategies, probing the malleability of LLM tutoring policies.  
  Quick check: What is the key difference between *compliance* with a scaffolding prompt and *integration* of its output into a better teaching decision?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: The system comprises:
  1. Graph Environment Generator (produces DAGs and defines learner optimal policy),
  2. Learner Simulator (hidden from teacher; generates trajectory from partial knowledge),
  3. Teacher LLM Interface (receives full graph, rewards, trajectory; outputs teaching edge; handles scaffolding via Scaffolding Manager),
  4. Scaffolding Manager (injects auxiliary selection steps into training trials),
  5. Cognitive Model Suite (implements Bayes Optimal Teacher, Reward Heuristic, Depth Heuristic),
  6. Fitting Engine (maximizes softmax likelihood of choices under each model; computes BIC),
  7. Metrics Logger (records Teaching Score, choice patterns, best-fitting model).

- **Critical path**:
  Baseline: For each LLM, initialize context → for each trial: present graph/trajectory → query LLM → parse edge → record choice → compute Teaching Score → after all trials: fit all cognitive models → compare BIC.
  Scaffolding: Same as baseline but with training trials modified: LLM first completes auxiliary selection → response stored → then teaching prompt (with history) → same fitting on test trials only.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - LLM decoding: Zero-temperature reduces stochasticity for model fitting but may not reflect typical usage; sampling could introduce noise requiring more simulations.
  - Prompt engineering: Detailed examples and strict output formats increase compliance but may bias toward certain heuristics (e.g., reward focus).
  - Model set: The BOT is computationally heavy; approximate heuristics are simpler. Fitting all models per teacher is costly but necessary for BIC comparison.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Parsing failures: LLM output not matching `(x,y)` pattern; trial discarded → reduces statistical power.
  - Scaffolding non-compliance: LLM ignores auxiliary step or returns invalid format → breaks experimental manipulation.
  - Performance collapse: Teaching Score near zero on test graphs → indicates complete strategy failure or misinterpretation.
  - Heuristic dominance: ∆BIC strongly favors Reward Heuristic on test trials despite training → suggests scaffolding backfired.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Baseline replication: Match human Experiment 1: 40 trials, no scaffolding. Verify LLMs achieve high mean Teaching Score (~0.8) and BOT best fit (lowest BIC).
  2. Scaffolding intervention: Apply 2×3 design (congruent/incongruent training × no/inference/reward scaffolding). Test if Inference scaffolding boosts test performance and shifts model fits toward BOT, as in humans.
  3. Model comparison robustness: Check consistency of best-fitting model across all LLMs and conditions; identify outliers (e.g., models with mixed fits like GPT-o3-mini) for targeted analysis.

## Limitations
- The evidence for mentalizing is correlational; LLMs might approximate BOT behavior via learned heuristics rather than explicit Bayesian inference.
- The interpretation of scaffolding results (decoupled processing) is speculative; alternative explanations like prompt interference or insufficient scaffolding design were not excluded.
- The study’s narrow, deterministic graph-teaching task limits generalizability to natural language or less-structured teaching contexts.

## Confidence
- Mentalizing via BOT alignment: Medium
- Scaffolding decoupling mechanism: Low
- Generalizability beyond narrow task: Low

## Next Checks
1. Diagnostic graph suite: Introduce graph families where BOT and heuristic (e.g., Reward/Depth) predictions diverge maximally. If LLMs remain BOT-aligned on these critical trials, the mentalizing claim strengthens; if they switch to heuristics, the mimicry hypothesis gains support.
2. Ablation prompt study: Systematically remove structured elements from the teacher prompt (e.g., hide full graph, mask learner trajectory). Performance drop would suggest reliance on global graph structure (heuristic); stable performance would support flexible inverse planning.
3. Internal mechanism probe: Use activation/attention analysis or chain-of-thought elicitation to test whether LLM representations differentially encode inferred learner knowledge versus surface graph features when generating teaching choices. Direct evidence of learner-state representation would boost confidence in mentalizing.
