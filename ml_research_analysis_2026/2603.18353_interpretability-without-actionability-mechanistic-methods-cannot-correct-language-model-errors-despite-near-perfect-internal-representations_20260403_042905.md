# Interpretability without actionability: mechanistic methods cannot correct language model errors despite near-perfect internal representations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18353
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18353
- **Reference count:** 27
- **Primary result:** Despite models encoding near-perfect internal hazard knowledge (linear probe AUROC 98.2%), four mechanistic steering methods failed to reliably correct false-negative errors in clinical triage, revealing a fundamental limitation of inference-time interventions to bridge the knowledge-action gap.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether mechanistic interpretability methods can correct dangerous false-negative errors in a safety-critical clinical triage task. The authors find that language models possess near-perfect internal representations of hazard knowledge—detectable with linear probes—but fail to act on this knowledge in their outputs. Four inference-time steering techniques (concept bottlenecks, sparse autoencoder features, activation patching, and a truthfulness separator vector) were tested. None succeeded in reliably and safely converting internal knowledge into correct behavior. The best method (truthfulness separator vector) corrected only 24% of missed hazards at high strength while disrupting 6% of correct detections, leaving 76% of errors uncorrected. The results demonstrate a profound and generalizable "knowledge-action gap" that current mechanistic steering methods cannot bridge, suggesting that post-training inference-time interventions may be insufficient for safety-critical alignment.

## Method Summary
The study evaluated four steering methods on two models (Steerling-8B with a concept bottleneck, and Qwen 2.5 7B Instruct) using 400 clinical vignettes (200 physician-created, 200 real-world Medicaid cases). The task was to correctly identify hazardous patient cases. For each method, the authors identified steering signals (supervised concepts, SAE features, activation differences, or a linear separator vector) and applied additive interventions to model activations during generation, sweeping steering strength (α). Performance was measured by the net correction rate: percentage of false negatives (missed hazards) corrected minus the percentage of true positives (correct hazards) disrupted. All methods were compared against random-direction controls. The baseline models exhibited a large knowledge-action gap: a linear probe achieved 98.2% AUROC for hazard detection, while model output sensitivity was only 45.1%.

## Key Results
- **Concept bottleneck steering** corrected 20% of missed hazards but disrupted 53% of correct detections, performing no better than random perturbation (p=0.84).
- **Sparse autoencoder feature steering** produced exactly zero corrections and zero disruptions despite identifying 3,695 statistically significant hazard-associated features.
- **Activation patching** yielded no net gain at standard strength and required extreme α=5.0 for modest, unreliable improvement.
- **Truthfulness separator vector (TSV) steering** at α=10.0 corrected 24% of false negatives while disrupting only 6% of true positives, but failed to correct the remaining 76% of errors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Concept Bottleneck Steering Failure via Representational Sparsity and Non-Causal Routing
Concept bottleneck steering fails because the bottleneck activations are extremely sparse and do not causally determine downstream representations and outputs. The model's decision relies on distributed, high-dimensional computation in the residual stream; the bottleneck concept activations contribute negligently (99.92% of values <0.01). Overriding near-zero values does not meaningfully alter subsequent transformer layers, which route critical information through pathways bypassing the bottleneck. The core assumption—that human-supervised concepts form a causally sufficient intermediate representation—is false.

### Mechanism 2: Sparse Autoencoder Feature Steering Nullification by Residual Stream Compensation
Amplifying statistically significant SAE features produces zero output change because subsequent transformer layers compensate via the residual stream. Clamping features at layer 14 is neutralized by the forward pass through layers 15–28. The residual architecture allows information to bypass the modified layer, and later layers can reconstruct the original computation from unaffected parts of the stream. The core assumption—that a feature's statistical significance at one layer implies it is causally determinative of the final output—is false.

### Mechanism 3: Partial Bridging via Linear Separator Vectors Amidst High-Dimensional Manifolds
A linear "truthfulness separator vector" (TSV) partially corrects errors at high strength because it captures a meaningful, but incomplete, direction separating correct from incorrect internal states. The TSV lies partially in the subspace distinguishing hazardous from benign cases (cosine similarity 0.50) and separates TP from FN representations (AUROC 0.814). Adding α·TSV nudges the residual stream toward the "correct" manifold. At high α, it overrides some opposing signals, correcting 24% of FNs with low disruption (6%). However, 76% of errors remain because the TSV captures only a projection of the full, high-dimensional set of factors causing the model to fail to act on its knowledge. The core assumption—that the difference between "knows but fails to act" and "acts correctly" is linearly aligned with the hazard detection direction—is incomplete.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Knowledge-Action Gap**
  - **Why needed:** The study quantifies this gap (probe AUROC 98.2% vs. output sensitivity 45.1%). It distinguishes *representational knowledge* (what is encoded linearly) from *operational behavior* (what the model generates).
  - **Quick check:** If a linear probe achieves >95% AUROC on a task but model output accuracy is ~50%, this implies the model's decision policy does not directly leverage its accurate internal representations.

- **Concept: Causal Intervention vs. Correlation**
  - **Why needed:** All four methods found correlations (features, concepts, directions associated with hazards/correctness). The key failure is that these correlations are not *causal levers* for the final output. Engineers must test for causality via intervention (clamping/amplifying) and measure *net correction* (FN fixed minus TP broken), not just feature significance.
  - **Quick check:** How would you design a control to test if a steering method's "corrections" are truly due to targeted causal influence versus general stochastic perturbation?

- **Concept: Residual Stream Dynamics**
  - **Why needed:** The transformer's residual connection is why single-point interventions (SAE, activation patching) can be nullified. It creates a computational "escape hatch" where later layers can ignore or override a perturbed earlier layer. Steering must disrupt the stream's *accumulated state* or alter the *computational rules* (weights) of multiple layers.
  - **Quick check:** Why might clamping a feature at layer 14 of a 28-layer model have no effect on the final output, even if that feature is highly correlated with the target concept?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Text -> Token Embedding -> Transformer Layers 1..L (with residual stream) -> [Optional: Concept Bottleneck Layer] -> Unembedding Layer -> Output Logits -> Autoregressive Generation.
- **Critical path:** Input → [Layers 1..L] (with possible bottlenecks/concepts) → Unembedding → Output. Interventions target: (a) Concept activations (pre-combination), (b) Residual stream (h_ℓ) via hook, (c) Post-unembedding logits (not tested).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Inference-time (cheap, reversible) vs. training-time (reshapes policy). Study shows inference-time fails.
  - Single-layer vs. multi-layer: All interventions single-point; later layers compensate.
  - Strength vs. disruption: Higher α increases correction but risks breaking correct behaviors. TSV best α=10.0 (24% correction, 6% disruption).
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. **Null effect:** Zero corrections and disruptions (SAE steering). Indicates complete compensation or irrelevance.
  2. **Random-like performance:** Correction ≈ disruption (concept steering, TSV low α). Adds noise.
  3. **High disruption:** Corrects some errors but breaks many more (concept steering). Conflicts with correct computation.
  4. **Incomplete bridging:** Net positive correction but vast majority of errors uncorrected (TSV high α). Captures only fragment of needed change.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Establish the knowledge-action gap:** Train linear probes at every layer on the same dataset used for generation. Confirm probe AUROC >> model output sensitivity.
  2. **Baseline steering with random control:** For any new steering vector, run with a random direction of matched norm and same α schedule. A real method must show statistically significantly better net correction than random.
  3. **Dose-response with disruption tracking:** Sweep α from very low to very high. Plot FN correction rate and TP disruption rate separately. Optimal α is where the vertical distance between these curves is maximized (net gain).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What mechanistic properties of autoregressive language models cause the knowledge-action gap? The paper demonstrates the gap and steering failures but does not isolate a primary cause. Evidence to resolve: controlled experiments varying architecture (e.g., residual connections), layer-specific probing to map information flow, and causal mediation analysis.
- **Open Question 2:** Can training-time interventions (RLHF, DPO, representation fine-tuning) bridge the gap where inference-time steering fails? The study evaluated only inference-time methods. Evidence to resolve: fine-tune a model with preference data emphasizing correct triage and re-evaluate the probe-output gap.
- **Open Question 3:** Does the failure generalize beyond clinical triage to other safety-critical tasks and larger model scales? Limitations: evaluation on two models (7B-8B) on a single task. Evidence to resolve: replication on diverse domains (cybersecurity, content moderation) and larger models (>70B).
- **Open Question 4:** Can multi-layer steering overcome residual stream compensation that nullified single-layer interventions? The study tested single-layer only. Evidence to resolve: implement and evaluate coordinated interventions at multiple layers (early, middle, late) and compare net correction against single-layer baselines.

## Limitations
- Model scope limited to 7B–8B LLMs; behavior of larger or differently architected models unknown.
- Task confined to clinical triage; results may not generalize to other safety-critical domains.
- Reproducibility gaps: SAE training details (data preprocessing, convergence) not fully specified; full real-world dataset access may be restricted.

## Confidence
- **Knowledge-action gap exists:** High (directly measured: probe AUROC 98.2% vs. output sensitivity 45.1%).
- **Concept bottleneck & SAE steering failure:** Medium (null/random-like effects consistent with mechanisms but implementation-specific).
- **TSV partial correction:** Medium (dose-response shows net gain; linear assumption likely incomplete).
- **Broad conclusion that inference-time steering is fundamentally limited:** Low (based on four methods; other interpretability or training-time approaches untested).

## Next Checks
1. **Multi-layer steering:** Simultaneously patch residual streams at three or more layers (e.g., early, middle, late) using combined TP-minus-FN directions; compare net FN correction and TP disruption against single-layer baselines.
2. **Causal feature attribution steering:** Replace statistical feature selection with perturbation-based methods (integrated gradients, SHAP) to derive steering vectors; test each against random-direction controls for net gain.
3. **Cross-domain robustness:** Replicate key experiments on at least two additional high-risk tasks (e.g., medical diagnosis, legal risk assessment) and on larger models (>20B parameters) to evaluate generalizability of the knowledge-action gap and steering failures.
