# Training-free Detection of Generated Videos via Spatial-Temporal Likelihoods
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.15026
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15026
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: STALL achieves state-of-the-art zero-shot detection of AI-generated videos with average AUCs of 0.83, 0.80, and 0.85 on VideoFeedback, GenVideo, and ComGenVid benchmarks, respectively, consistently outperforming all prior zero-shot and supervised methods.

## Executive Summary
STALL is a training-free, zero-shot detector for AI-generated videos that fuses spatial and temporal likelihoods within a probabilistic framework. It uses whitened embeddings from a pretrained vision encoder (DINOv3) to score per-frame realism and normalized frame-to-frame differences to capture motion consistency, both modeled as Gaussians via real-video calibration. Final video scores are derived from percentile rankings of these two components, making the method scale-free and eliminating the need for synthetic training data. Across three benchmarks—including a new ComGenVid set featuring videos from Sora and Veo3—STALL achieves superior AUC scores (0.83–0.85), demonstrating strong accuracy, robustness to perturbations, and efficiency.

## Method Summary
STALL operates in two phases: offline calibration and online inference. During calibration, a large set of real videos is used to compute spatial whitening parameters (mean and whitening matrix) from randomly sampled frames, and temporal whitening parameters from ℓ2-normalized inter-frame differences. The distributions of spatial and temporal scores from these real videos are stored. For a test video, each frame is encoded using DINOv3. The spatial score is the maximum negative log-likelihood across whitened frame embeddings. The temporal score is the minimum negative log-likelihood across whitened, normalized frame differences. Both scores are converted to percentiles based on the stored calibration distributions and averaged to produce a final video score, which is thresholded for classification. No synthetic videos or model-specific training are required.

## Key Results
- STALL yields average AUC of 0.83 on VideoFeedback, 0.80 on GenVideo, and 0.85 on ComGenVid.
- It outperforms all prior zero-shot and supervised methods on every benchmark.
- The method is robust to perturbations (e.g., compression, resolution changes) and computationally efficient, requiring only frame-wise encoding.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Spatial Likelihood via Whitened Embeddings
- **Claim**: STALL detects generated videos by scoring each frame against the distribution of real video frames, using a probabilistic Gaussian model on whitened embeddings.
- **Mechanism**: A pretrained vision encoder (DINOv3) maps each frame to a high-dimensional embedding. From a calibration set of real videos, the mean and covariance of these embeddings are computed and a whitening transform is derived. Since whitened embeddings are approximately isotropic Gaussian, the squared norm is proportional to the negative log-likelihood under a standard normal. The spatial score aggregates the maximum log-likelihood across frames (worst-case frame).
- **Core assumption**: Frame embeddings from real videos become spherical Gaussian after whitening; generated frames produce outlying norms.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions likelihood-based scoring; Section 4.1 extends whitened CLIP embeddings to video; D3 uses only temporal cues, highlighting STALL’s spatial component.
- **Break condition**: If the encoder’s frame embedding distribution is not well approximated by a Gaussian even after whitening (e.g., due to encoder bias or mismatched calibration domain), discrimination degrades.

### Mechanism 2: Temporal Likelihood via Normalized Frame Differences
- **Claim**: Motion inconsistencies in generated videos are captured by modeling the directional changes between consecutive frames as uniformly distributed on the sphere, yielding an approximate Gaussian after whitening.
- **Mechanism**: For every pair of consecutive frames, compute the embedding difference Δ. Raw differences are ℓ2-normalized to lie on the unit sphere. High-dimensional uniform directions on the sphere have approximately Gaussian marginals (Maxwell-Poincaré). A whitening transform—estimated from normalized differences of real videos—makes these coordinates standard normal; the squared norm of the whitened vector gives the per-transition temporal likelihood. The temporal score aggregates the minimum log-likelihood across transitions (worst-case transition).
- **Core assumption**: Normalized frame-to-frame transitions from real videos are uniformly distributed on the sphere (or, after whitening, Gaussian). Generated videos produce atypical transition directions.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions normalized differences and motion consistency; Section 4.2 explains uniform directions and Maxwell-Poincaré; D3 uses higher-order differences without normalization.
- **Break condition**: If a generated video’s motion statistics lie within the support of the real-video calibrated distribution (e.g., generator reproduces realistic motion), temporal likelihood may not discriminate.

### Mechanism 3: Zero-Shot, Scale-Free Fusion via Percentile Ranking
- **Claim**: Combining spatial and temporal scores via percentile ranks yields a robust, threshold-free detector that generalizes across generators without any synthetic training data.
- **Mechanism**: The spatial and temporal scores are converted into percentiles based on the calibration set of real videos. The final video score is the average of the two percentiles. This makes the two components scale-free and interpretable: a score of 0.5 means the video is median according to real-video statistics; lower scores suggest artificial content.
- **Core assumption**: The calibration distribution accurately represents typical real video scores; generated videos tend to have lower percentiles in at least one modality.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions percentile ranking combines scores scale-free; Section 4.3 explains scale-free fusion; Table 1 shows STALL never drops below 0.5 AUC while others invert for some generators.
- **Break condition**: If a generated video achieves high spatial and temporal percentiles, it evades detection. Also, small or non-representative calibration sets cause noisy percentiles.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Gaussian Distribution and Negative Log-Likelihood**  
  Why needed: The core score derives from the log-likelihood of a multivariate Gaussian with identity covariance. The squared ℓ2 norm of a standard normal sample is proportional to its negative log-likelihood.  
  Quick check: Derive the closed-form log-likelihood of a vector y sampled from N(0, I_d).
- **Concept: Whitening Transform**  
  Why needed: To turn arbitrary covariance embeddings into an isotropic Gaussian, necessary for the simple likelihood formula. The PCA whitening matrix yields zero mean and identity covariance.  
  Quick check: Given Σ = VΛVᵀ, write the PCA whitening matrix and show it yields identity covariance.
- **Concept: Uniform Distribution on the High-Dimensional Sphere**  
  Why needed: Normalized frame differences are assumed uniform on S^{d−1}, whose marginals become Gaussian in high dimensions (Maxwell-Poincaré lemma), justifying treating each coordinate as Gaussian after whitening.  
  Quick check: State the Maxwell-Poincaré lemma in your own words.
- **Concept: Zero-Shot Detection via Calibration**  
  Why needed: STALL requires no synthetic videos; decision thresholds come from a calibration set of real videos. Understanding how to estimate statistics and percentile ranks is essential.  
  Quick check: How would you detect if a test video is generated using only real video statistics? Explain the role of percentiles.
- **Concept: Percentile Ranking and Scale-Free Fusion**  
  Why needed: Spatial and temporal scores have different distributions; converting to percentiles aligns scales and makes the average meaningful without tuning.  
  Quick check: Why not simply average the raw spatial and temporal scores? What are the pitfalls?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map:
Calibration Module (offline) -> Encoder -> Spatial Scorer & Temporal Scorer -> Percentile Converter -> Fusion

### Critical path:
- Offline: Collect calibration videos → extract embeddings → compute spatial and temporal whitening matrices → compute and store score distributions.
- Online: Load video → decode frames → encode all frames → (spatial): whiten each frame, compute per-frame log-likelihood, take max → (temporal): compute frame differences, normalize, whiten, compute per-transition log-likelihood, take min → convert both scores to percentiles → average → threshold to classify.

### Design tradeoffs:
- Aggregation: Using max for spatial (worst frame) and min for temporal (worst transition) yields lower correlation between branches; mean aggregation is less discriminative.
- Normalization: ℓ2-normalization of differences is essential to obtain a uniform spherical distribution; skipping breaks the Gaussian assumption.
- Encoder selection: Image encoders that work frame-wise are sufficient; video encoders that produce a single embedding discard intra-frame statistics and perform poorly.
- Calibration set size: Performance degrades only below ~5k videos, but more is better. Domain mismatch (e.g., Kinetics vs. web videos) is mostly robust but may drift if domains diverge.
- Frames per second: Calibrated at 8 FPS but robust to variations (2–24 FPS); large mismatches (e.g., 60 FPS) might affect motion statistics.

### Failure signatures:
- Degenerate whitening: Covariance matrices near singular (e.g., calibration size < embedding dimension). Fix: add small regularization to covariance diagonal.
- Extreme OOD test videos: May produce out-of-range percentiles; could also happen for rare real videos if calibration set is small.
- Static or near-identical frames: Temporal branch collapses; STALL falls back to spatial-only. If spatially realistic, may be misclassified as real.
- Generator adaptation: A future generator that explicitly optimizes for high spatial and temporal percentiles could evade detection.
- Domain mismatch: Test videos from domains not in calibration (e.g., surveillance, aerial, medical) may yield miscalibrated percentiles and higher errors.

### First 3 experiments:
1. Gaussianity validation: Run Anderson-Darling and D’Agostino-Pearson tests on (a) whitened frame embeddings and (b) whitened ℓ2-normalized differences from calibration set. Quantify fraction of dimensions passing normality.
2. Calibration size sweep: Evaluate detector with varying calibration sizes (1k, 5k, 10k, full) on all benchmarks. Identify minimum viable size and saturation point.
3. Aggregation ablation: Compare spatial max vs. mean, temporal min vs. mean, and fusion strategies (average percentiles vs. product). Confirm design rationale for worst-case aggregation and simple average.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How does the diversity and size of the calibration set affect STALL's ability to generalize to out-of-distribution real videos, and what is the minimum calibration data required for robust detection?
- Basis: Paper uses V ATEX as calibration and tests robustness to size and source (Fig. 7a, Fig. 6c), but limits are not quantified.
- Why unresolved: Calibration set defines real-data statistics; small or biased sets may cause false positives/negatives on new domains.
- What evidence would resolve: Systematic experiments varying calibration size down to very small numbers and testing on diverse domains, correlated with diversity metrics.

### Open Question 2
Do AI-generated videos consistently violate the Gaussian normality assumption in the whitened embedding space, and can deviations be used to improve detection?
- Basis: Paper validates Gaussian assumption for real data but not for generated; assumes generated videos will have lower likelihood.
- Why unresolved: If generators produce embeddings that are also approximately Gaussian, STALL might fail. Understanding generated embedding distribution is crucial.
- What evidence would resolve: Compute normality tests on embeddings from a wide range of generated videos; if Gaussian, investigate other differentiating features or develop likelihood ratio tests.

### Open Question 3
How does STALL's performance scale with improvements in video generation quality, and can zero-shot detection keep pace?
- Basis: Paper introduces ComGenVid with Sora and Veo3 and shows strong performance but acknowledges rapid development and room for improvement.
- Why unresolved: As generators become more realistic, spatial and temporal artifacts may diminish, reducing STALL's effectiveness.
- What evidence would resolve: Longitudinal studies evaluating STALL on successive generator versions; analysis of persistent artifacts; development of adversarial generators to identify weaknesses.

## Limitations
- Exact DINOv3 variant and input preprocessing are unspecified, risking mismatched embeddings and violated Gaussian assumptions.
- Calibration set composition (size, domain, frame selection) is underspecified, affecting reproducibility and robustness.
- Edge-case handling for static videos (zero frame difference) lacks a clear threshold, potentially causing numerical issues or reliance on spatial-only detection.

## Confidence
- Spatial Likelihood (Whitened Embeddings → Gaussian): Medium (supported by prior work and supplementary histograms, but encoder-dependent).
- Temporal Likelihood (Normalized Differences → Uniform/Spherical → Gaussian): Medium (theoretical basis via Maxwell-Poincaré, empirical confirmation needed).
- Percentile-Based Fusion (Zero-shot, Scale-Free): High (consistent cross-benchmark gains and robustness ablations).
- Reported AUC Scores (0.83–0.85): Medium (results from paper, not yet independently reproduced).
