# Detection of On-Ground Chestnuts Using Artificial Intelligence Toward Automated Picking
## Quick Facts
* **arXiv ID:** 2602.14140
* **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14140
* **Reference count:** 40
* **Primary result:** YOLO models, particularly YOLOv12m, achieved a best mAP@0.5 of 95.1% with significantly faster inference times compared to all RT-DETR variants for detecting on-ground chestnuts.

## Executive Summary
Automating chestnut harvesting requires reliable vision systems to detect nuts on cluttered orchard floors. This study created a dataset of 319 high-resolution images with 6,524 annotated chestnuts and systematically evaluated 29 real-time object detectors (YOLO v11–v13 and RT-DETR v1–v4). YOLO models consistently outperformed RT-DETR variants in both accuracy and speed, with YOLOv12m achieving 95.1% mAP@0.5. For embedded real-time deployment, YOLOv11-s provided the optimal balance of performance and efficiency.

## Method Summary
The authors assembled a custom dataset of 319 RGB images (4032×3024 pixels) depicting chestnuts on orchard ground, annotated with 6,524 bounding boxes using the VIA tool. Images were resized to 1024×1024 for training. A systematic comparison was conducted across 29 real-time detectors: YOLO variants (n/s/m/l/x) from v11 to v13 and RT-DETR variants (R18/R34/R50/R101 backbones) from v1 to v4. Models were trained for 200 epochs using SGD with cosine learning rate decay, automatic mixed precision, and Monte Carlo cross-validation (5 random 70/10/20 splits). Evaluation used standard COCO metrics (mAP@0.5, mAP@[0.5:0.95], precision, recall) on a held-out test set, with inference speed measured on 8× RTX 4090 GPUs.

## Key Results
* YOLOv12m achieved the highest accuracy with mAP@0.5 = 95.1% and mAP@[0.5:0.95] = 78.3%.
* All YOLO models were significantly faster at inference than any RT-DETR variant, with RT-DETR models showing 2–4× higher latency.
* YOLOv11-s offered the best trade-off for real-time embedded deployment, combining high accuracy (mAP@0.5 ≈ 93%) with the lowest inference time among YOLO variants.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not provide an explicit mechanistic analysis of why YOLO models outperform RT-DETR in this task. However, the results suggest that YOLO’s single-stage, anchor-based architecture is better suited for the dense, small-object detection scenario of fallen chestnuts in complex backgrounds. YOLO’s efficient feature aggregation via CSPNet backbones and FPN/PAN necks likely captures fine-grained details more effectively than RT-DETR’s transformer-based decoder, which may struggle with high object density and requires more computational resources. The minimal augmentation needed for RT-DETR may also limit its robustness to the dataset’s clutter and occlusion.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Image Input -> Backbone (CSPNet for YOLO, ResNet for RT-DETR) -> Neck (FPN/PAN for YOLO, Transformer encoder-decoder for RT-DETR) -> Detection Head (anchor-based for YOLO, query-based for RT-DETR)

Critical path: Resize image to 1024×1024 -> Extract multi-scale features via backbone -> Fuse features in neck -> Predict bounding boxes and class probabilities in head.

Design tradeoffs: YOLO prioritizes inference speed and parameter efficiency at a minor cost to fine-grained accuracy; RT-DETR uses attention mechanisms for potentially better global context but incurs high computational overhead and slower training/inference.

Failure signatures: RT-DETR may produce missed detections under heavy occlusion or false positives from debris if augmentation is insufficient; YOLO may generate overlapping predictions in dense clusters if confidence thresholds are too low.
