# JUCAL: Jointly Calibrating Aleatoric and Epistemic Uncertainty in Classification Tasks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.20153
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20153
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: JUCAL reduces NLL by up to 15% and predictive set size by up to 20% versus SOTA; a 5-member ensemble with JUCAL outperforms a 50-member temperature-scaled ensemble, slashing inference costs by up to 10×.

## Executive Summary
JUCAL is a post-hoc calibration method for ensemble classifiers that jointly tunes aleatoric (data noise) and epistemic (model knowledge) uncertainty via two scaling constants. By minimizing negative log-likelihood on a validation set, it separately adjusts the sharpness of individual ensemble members and their diversity. Evaluations on text and image classification tasks show substantial improvements in calibration metrics and efficiency, enabling smaller ensembles to match or exceed larger ones while reducing inference burden.

## Method Summary
JUCAL operates on a pre-trained ensemble and a validation set. It searches a grid of scaling constants \(c_1\) (for aleatoric uncertainty) and \(c_2\) (for epistemic uncertainty) to minimize NLL on the validation set. At test time, each member’s logits are transformed as \((1-c_2)\bar{f}(x)/c_1 + c_2 f_m(x)/c_1\) (where \(\bar{f}\) is the ensemble mean), followed by softmax and averaging. This model-agnostic approach requires only ensemble outputs and labels, making it applicable to any classifier without internal access.

## Key Results
- Reduces negative log-likelihood (NLL) by up to 15% compared to state-of-the-art calibration methods.
- Shrinks predictive set sizes under conformal prediction by up to 20% for the same coverage level.
- A 5-member ensemble with JUCAL outperforms a 50-member temperature-scaled ensemble in NLL and set size, achieving up to 10× lower inference cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Separate Scaling of Aleatoric and Epistemic Uncertainty**  
JUCAL disentangles the two uncertainty types by applying independent scaling constants. Constant \(c_1\) globally scales all logits (temperature scaling), adjusting the confidence of individual members (aleatoric). Constant \(c_2\) scales the deviation of each member from the ensemble mean, controlling diversity (epistemic). The formula \(\bar{p}^{\text{JUCAL}}(x) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \text{Softmax}\left( (1-c_2) \frac{\bar{f}(x)}{c_1} + c_2 \frac{f_m(x)}{c_1} \right)\) embodies this. It assumes ensemble members capture aleatoric uncertainty while their disagreement captures epistemic uncertainty. Evidence: derivation in Section 4.2 and alignment with uncertainty decomposition literature. Break condition: if the ensemble lacks diversity, \(c_2\) has no effect; if members are already aleatorically calibrated, \(c_1\) has little effect.

**Mechanism 2: Joint Optimization via a Proper Scoring Rule**  
The two constants are jointly optimized by minimizing negative log-likelihood (NLL) on the validation set. NLL is a strictly proper scoring rule that uniquely incentivizes reporting the true conditional distribution \(P(Y|X=x)\). By minimizing expected NLL, JUCAL aligns the calibrated predictive distribution with the true distribution, implicitly calibrating both uncertainty components. Assumes the validation set is representative and the ensemble’s raw uncertainty decomposition is reasonable but mis-calibrated. Evidence: use of NLL in Equation (3) and properties in Section I.2. Break condition: a biased validation set or a severely flawed ensemble decomposition may yield poor test calibration.

**Mechanism 3: Model-Agnostic Post-Hoc Calibration**  
JUCAL requires only softmax outputs from ensemble members and validation labels, making it applicable to any pre-trained ensemble without internal model access. It computes \(c_1^*, c_2^*\) via grid search and applies the transformation at test time. This black-box nature allows deployment with heterogeneous models (e.g., transformers, CNNs, tree ensembles). Core assumption: ensemble members produce well-defined logits or probabilities. Evidence: abstract states applicability without internal parameters. Break condition: if outputs are unnormalized or the ensemble is extremely small (size 1), the method is ill-defined or \(c_2\) becomes irrelevant.

## Foundational Learning

**Deep Ensembles and Uncertainty Decomposition**  
Deep ensembles approximate Bayesian posteriors via multiple independently trained models. Disagreement among members estimates epistemic uncertainty; individual softmax outputs capture aleatoric uncertainty. *Why needed*: JUCAL explicitly builds on this decomposition to separately calibrate each component. *Quick check*: In a deep ensemble, what does high disagreement among member predictions indicate about epistemic uncertainty?

**Aleatoric vs. Epistemic Uncertainty**  
Aleatoric uncertainty is irreducible noise inherent in the data (e.g., label noise); epistemic uncertainty is reducible lack of knowledge due to limited data or model capacity. *Why needed*: JUCAL’s core innovation is joint calibration of these two components, which are often conflated by single-parameter methods. *Quick check*: Which type of uncertainty should decrease when more training data is collected?

**Temperature Scaling and Its Limitation**  
Temperature scaling applies a single global scaling factor to logits to calibrate confidence. It adjusts overall sharpness but cannot disentangle aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty in ensembles. *Why needed*: JUCAL extends this with two parameters to separately control each uncertainty source. *Quick check*: Why does a single temperature parameter fail to balance aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty in an ensemble?

**Proper Scoring Rules (NLL)**  
A proper scoring rule incentivizes honest reporting of the true conditional distribution; the true distribution minimizes its expected value. Negative log-likelihood (NLL) is strictly proper. *Why needed*: JUCAL optimizes NLL to align the calibrated predictive distribution with \(P(Y|X=x)\), ensuring conditional calibration. *Quick check*: What property of a scoring rule ensures that the true distribution minimizes its expected value?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Ensemble Members (logits) -> Validation/Calibration Dataset -> JUCAL Engine (Grid Search Module + Transformation Module) -> Calibrated Predictive Distributions

**Critical Path**  
1. Obtain a pre-trained ensemble of classifiers.  
2. Prepare a validation set with ground-truth labels.  
3. Run grid search to find optimal \(c_1^*, c_2^*\) that minimize NLL on the validation set.  
4. For each test input, compute calibrated probabilities using the JUCAL formula with the optimal constants.  
5. Optionally, apply downstream tasks like conformal prediction on the calibrated probabilities.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Grid resolution: Finer grids yield better parameters but increase compute; coarse-to-fine search balances cost and accuracy.  
- Ensemble size: Larger ensembles may require smaller \(c_2\); overly large \(c_2\) can over-disrupt aleatoric estimates.  
- Validation set size: Must be large enough to reliably estimate NLL; small sets risk overfitting the calibration parameters.  
- Black-box vs. white-box: JUCAL uses only outputs; internal model access could enable more sophisticated disentanglement but is not necessary.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Zero ensemble diversity: All members output identical logits, rendering \(c_2\) ineffective. Diagnostic: compute variance of logits across members; should be non-zero.  
- Inverted uncertainty trends: After calibration, aleatoric uncertainty increases with more data, or epistemic uncertainty does not decrease with additional training. Indicates a mis-specified ensemble.  
- Degenerate sets: Predictive entropy near maximal (\(\log K\)) for all inputs, suggesting failed optimization (e.g., \(c_1, c_2\) too large).  
- Conformal coverage mismatch: When using JUCAL with conformal prediction, test coverage significantly deviates from the target, possibly due to non-exchangeable validation and test data.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. **Reproduce Table 2 (Full Datasets, Greedy-50)**: Apply JUCAL to the Greedy-50 ensemble from the FTC-metadataset across six text classification tasks. Compare NLL against pool-then-calibrate and uncalibrated baselines.  
2. **Image Classification (CIFAR-10 with Greedy-5)**: Train a CNN ensemble on CIFAR-10, apply JUCAL, and compare predictive set sizes under Adaptive Prediction Sets (APS) at 99% coverage against baselines.  
3. **Aleatoric-Epistemic Disentanglement (Figure 6)**: Train JUCAL on full vs. mini (10% data) versions of a dataset (e.g., DBpedia). Verify that estimated epistemic uncertainty decreases with more data while aleatoric remains stable, confirming theoretical behavior.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
How does JUCAL perform on other data modalities (e.g., vision-language tasks, time-series) and models (e.g., transformers, foundation models) beyond text and image classification, particularly for conversational AI systems like LLM chatbots? The paper’s experiments are restricted to text and image classification; generalizability to new architectures and multi-modal settings is unexplored.

**Open Question 2**  
Can we develop a standardized interpretation of the absolute values of epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty estimated by JUCAL across heterogeneous datasets and model families to reliably indicate the potential utility of collecting more training data? Absolute magnitudes vary widely without a clear cross-dataset scale, limiting use in data acquisition decisions.

**Open Question 3**  
Does joint calibration via JUCAL maintain its improved conditional coverage under realistic distribution shifts (e.g., covariate shift, domain adaptation), and how does it compare to conformal methods designed for such shifts? The paper relies on i.i.d. test splits; robustness to shift is only qualitatively supported by synthetic figures.

**Open Question 4**  
When the calibration dataset is small, does JUCAL remain stable and effective, or do the two-parameter optimization and ensemble diversity estimates become unreliable compared to single-parameter methods? The paper uses large validation sets (up to 112k examples); performance under small-calibration regimes is unclear.

**Open Question 5**  
Is the mutual information (MI) based decomposition of ensemble predictions into aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty the most appropriate choice for JUCAL's calibration objective, or would alternative decompositions (e.g., based on variance, entropy of ensemble member distributions) yield better performance or interpretability? The paper uses standard MI decomposition but does not justify it over others.

## Limitations
- **Validation set reuse**: The same dataset is used for both greedy ensemble selection and JUCAL calibration, risking overfitting and inflated performance estimates.
- **Undocumented grid search**: Exact ranges and resolutions for \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are not provided; reproduction may miss the true optimum or require arbitrary choices.
- **Ensemble diversity dependency**: JUCAL’s ability to modulate epistemic uncertainty (\(c_2\)) vanishes if ensemble members lack disagreement.

## Confidence
- **Disentanglement mechanism** (separate scaling of aleatoric/epistemic): **Medium**. Theoretically sound but relies on ensemble diversity; break conditions highlighted.
- **Performance improvements** (NLL ↓15%, set size ↓20%, 5‑vs‑50 efficiency): **Medium**. Strong empirical claims exist but are potentially confounded by validation reuse and missing experimental details.
- **Model‑agnostic applicability** (black‑box post‑hoc): **High**. Requires only ensemble softmax outputs; straightforward to implement for any classifier.

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate key results**: Run JUCAL on the published Greedy‑50 text ensembles and a CIFAR‑10 CNN ensemble. Verify NLL reductions and recover reported \(c_1, c_2\) values.
2. **Ablate ensemble diversity**: Construct ensembles with intentionally low disagreement (e.g., identical initialization) and compare JUCAL’s calibration gains and optimal \(c_2\) against diverse ensembles. Expect minimal \(c_2\) effect.
3. **Separate calibration set**: Hold out an independent calibration set (not used for greedy selection) to optimize \((c_1, c_2)\). Compare test‑set NLL with the original shared‑set approach to quantify overfitting magnitude.
