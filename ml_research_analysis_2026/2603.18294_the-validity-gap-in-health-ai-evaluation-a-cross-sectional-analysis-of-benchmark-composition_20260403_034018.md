# The Validity Gap in Health AI Evaluation: A Cross-Sectional Analysis of Benchmark Composition

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18294
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18294
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Health AI evaluation benchmarks suffer from a "validity gap" due to a lack of compositional transparency, risking misleading performance claims for clinical deployment.

## Executive Summary
Health AI benchmarks are routinely used to claim model readiness for clinical use, yet their internal composition—the types of patient queries, clinical contexts, and data sources they contain—rarely aligns with real-world clinical practice. This study analyzes 18,707 queries across six major benchmarks using a 16-dimensional "Query Profile" taxonomy. Results reveal systemic misalignment: chronic disease management represents only 5.5% of queries, safety-critical scenarios like suicide/self-harm are below 0.7%, and objective clinical data is heavily skewed toward wellness wearables (17.7%) versus clinical labs (5.2%) or raw records (0.6%). Vulnerable populations (pediatrics, older adults) are also underrepresented. The authors argue that without standardized profiling—analogous to clinical trial inclusion criteria—benchmark scores may overstate model safety and utility, potentially leading to patient harm upon deployment.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted a cross-sectional analysis of six health AI benchmarks spanning three technological generations (search-based, case-presentation, interactive dialogue). They developed a 16-field "Query Profile" taxonomy to characterize each query across dimensions including clinical context, topic area, intent, objective data presence, and population risk. Using GPT-4.6 and Claude Opus, they automatically tagged all 18,707 queries, achieving high inter-model agreement (Cohen’s κ=0.77 overall) but moderate agreement on subjective dimensions like intent and risk sensitivity (73–88%). Aggregate distributions for each benchmark and the combined corpus were computed and contrasted against expected real-world clinical workload to identify compositional gaps.

## Key Results
- Chronic disease management queries comprised only 5.5% of the total corpus; prevention screening was 2.7%.
- Safety-critical scenarios (suicide/self-harm, acute crisis) were effectively absent, representing <0.7% of all queries.
- Objective clinical data was polarized: wellness wearable data appeared in 17.7% of queries, while clinical lab values (5.2%) and raw structured records (0.6%) were rare.
- Vulnerable populations (pediatric, geriatric) were underrepresented, collectively comprising less than 11% of queries.
- Generational analysis showed newer, data-linked benchmarks still focused on low-risk wellness guidance rather than high-acuity clinical care.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The "validity gap" persists because benchmark development has been driven by technological capabilities (e.g., adding multi-turn dialogue or data streams) rather than a clinically grounded definition of a representative patient encounter. This creates datasets that test structural advances (longer context) while omitting clinical complexity (chronic disease, behavioral crises). Institutional incentives favor large, easily measurable benchmarks over clinically nuanced ones, leading to a selection effect where compositionally narrow datasets become canonical. Additionally, reliance on LLM-based automated annotation, while scalable, introduces methodological validity risks: subjective dimensions like risk sensitivity show only moderate agreement (κ 0.73–0.88), meaning the taxonomy itself may inconsistently frame clinical complexity and mask certain gaps. Together, these factors produce benchmarks that overfit to low-risk, wellness-oriented queries and underrepresent safety-critical and vulnerable-population scenarios.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Query Profile Taxonomy (16-field)**
- **Why needed:** Provides a structured, multi-dimensional schema to decompose a health query into orthogonal facets (context, topic, intent, data), enabling systematic comparison of benchmark composition against real-world clinical needs.
- **Quick check:** Which two taxonomy dimensions most directly capture the "clinical data density" lacking in current benchmarks? (Answer: *Objective Data Present* and *Objective Data Types*)

**Concept: Generational Evolution of Benchmarks (Gen 1/2/3)**
- **Why needed:** Maps benchmarks to three technological stages (search → case presentation → interactive/data-linked), predicting inherent compositional strengths and blind spots (e.g., Gen 3’s focus on wellness despite data integration).
- **Quick check:** Which generation contains the vast majority of high-risk behavioral health queries (suicide/self-harm)? (Answer: None; all generations severely underrepresent them.)

**Concept: External Validity & Generalizability Gap**
- **Why needed:** The core problem—performance on a benchmark may not generalize to target deployment—mirrors clinical trial external validity. The proposed solution (standardized query profiling) directly borrows from clinical trial reporting norms.
- **Quick check:** What key information is routinely reported for clinical trials but rarely for health AI benchmarks? (Answer: Explicit description of inclusion/exclusion criteria for the study population.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Raw Dataset -> Tagger -> Tagged Queries -> Aggregator -> Profile Report -> Comparison Engine

**Critical path:** Tagger fidelity determines downstream validity; validation must occur immediately after tagging.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Automation vs. Nuance: LLM tagging scales but inherits biases on fuzzy clinical boundaries.
- Breadth vs. Depth: 16-field taxonomy covers broadly but may lack domain-specific granularity (e.g., pharmacogenomics).
- Static vs. Dynamic: Snapshot profiles lack longitudinal tracking for multi-turn interactions.

**Failure signatures:**
- Demographic skew: <5% pediatric/geriatric queries when target includes these groups.
- Acuity vacuum: "High Risk" queries <1% or "Triage Disposition" intent near-zero.
- Data poverty: "Objective Data Present" >90% "None" or heavily polarized to one source (e.g., 90% wearables, 0% clinical notes).
- Topic myopia: Single topic area >30% of corpus, indicating non-representative sourcing.
- Validation failure: Low cross-model κ (<0.6) on core clinical dimensions for a new dataset.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replicate Baseline:** Apply the open-source tagging pipeline to one original dataset (e.g., MedRedQA). Verify aggregated profile matches published tables within ±3%.
2. **Profile a Target Dataset:** Generate a Query Profile for a new candidate benchmark. Visually compare "Intent Distribution" and "Objective Data Types" against the paper’s combined corpus. Identify top 3 deviation gaps.
3. **Stress-Test Tagging Ambiguity:** Construct 50 edge-case queries (e.g., vague mood: "anxiety" vs "depression"). Run through tagger. Measure intra-model consistency and inter-model agreement. Document most unstable dimensions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Performance shift across generations:** How do model metrics change when evaluated on high-context, data-rich benchmarks versus current low-context, search-oriented ones? Unresolved: study characterizes composition but not performance differences.
2. **Taxonomy extension for provider-facing AI:** How to adapt the taxonomy for EHR integration, clinical decision support, and provider tasks (e.g., note summarization)? Unresolved: current taxonomy targets consumer-facing queries.
3. **Clinical safety impact:** What is the actual harm rate (e.g., missed diagnoses) from deploying models evaluated on validity-gapped benchmarks? Unresolved: gap established but no direct evidence linking to deployment outcomes.
4. **Adoption barriers:** What practical, cultural, and incentive barriers prevent widespread query profiling adoption? Unresolved: toolkit exists but systemic issues (publication pressure, reviewer enforcement) unaddressed.
5. **LLM annotation reliability:** How valid is LLM-based profiling versus human expert annotation for nuanced dimensions like risk sensitivity? Unresolved: cross-model agreement (κ=0.77) shows consistency but not clinical accuracy; human gold standard lacking.

## Limitations
- **Annotation validity:** LLM tagging shows moderate agreement on subjective clinical dimensions, risking under/over-estimation of gaps.
- **Taxonomy scope:** May omit salient dimensions (e.g., social determinants, pharmacogenomics) and lacks longitudinal tracking.
- **Generalizability:** Analysis covers six English, high-income benchmarks; findings may not apply to non-Western or resource-constrained settings.

## Confidence
- Descriptive gap findings (e.g., chronic disease 5.5%, suicide/self-harm <0.7%): **High** — directly from reported corpus distributions.
- Causal mechanisms (technological generations, incentive structures): **Medium** — plausible inferences from patterns but not causally proven.
- LLM annotation as valid methodology: **Medium** — agreement metrics comparable to human inter-rater reliability but clinical gold-standard validation limited.

## Next Checks
1. **Replication baseline:** Apply tagging pipeline to one original dataset (e.g., MedRedQA) and verify aggregated profile matches published distributions within ±3%.
2. **Clinician gold standard:** Annotate a stratified sample of 200 queries with specialists; compute LLM vs. human agreement to bound tagging error.
3. **Deployment context profiling:** Profile real-world clinical encounter logs (e.g., EHR chat logs) with the same taxonomy; quantify largest compositional deviations from benchmarks.
