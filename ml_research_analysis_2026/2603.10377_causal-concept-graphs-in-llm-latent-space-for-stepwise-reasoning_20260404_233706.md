# Causal Concept Graphs in LLM Latent Space for Stepwise Reasoning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10377
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10377
- **Reference count:** 38
- **Primary result:** Causal Concept Graphs (CCG) achieve a Causal Fidelity Score (CFS) of 5.654 ± 0.625 on GPT-2 Medium across three reasoning datasets, significantly outperforming baselines (p < 0.0001, Bonferroni-corrected).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Causal Concept Graphs (CCG), a method to extract and causally relate interpretable concepts from LLM latent spaces during multi-step reasoning. CCG uses task-conditioned sparse autoencoders with hard TopK sparsity to isolate monosemantic features, then learns a directed acyclic graph over them via differentiable DAGMA optimization. The Causal Fidelity Score (CFS) evaluates the graph by measuring whether interventions on graph-predicted influential nodes cause larger downstream activation changes than random interventions. On ARC-Challenge, StrategyQA, and LogiQA, CCG consistently outperforms tracing and ranking baselines, yielding sparse (5–6% edge density), domain-specific, and stable graphs.

## Method Summary
CCG operates in three stages. First, a task-conditioned sparse autoencoder (SAE) with TopK gating (k=13, 5.1% L0) extracts monosemantic features from mean-pooled residual activations (layer 12, GPT-2 Medium). Second, from the top-64 most active features, DAGMA learns a linear structural equation model (C ≈ CW) with L1 and acyclicity penalties to recover a directed acyclic graph (adjacency W). Third, the Causal Fidelity Score (CFS) quantifies causal fidelity by comparing the mean absolute change in children’s reconstructed activations when ablating high-out-degree nodes versus random nodes, with ratio clipping and flooring for stability. The pipeline runs per dataset with fixed hyperparameters (λ₁=0.02, λ₂=0.05) and is repeated across seeds for stability.

## Key Results
- CCG achieves CFS = 5.654 ± 0.625, significantly higher than ROME-style tracing (3.382 ± 0.233), SAE-only ranking (2.479 ± 0.196), and random baseline (1.032 ± 0.034) (p < 0.0001, Bonferroni-corrected).
- Learned graphs are sparse (5–6% edge density) and domain-specific, with stable structure across random seeds.
- The DAGMA acyclicity penalty is critical: removing it reduces CFS by ~26%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Task-conditioned sparse autoencoders (SAEs) with TopK gating extract monosemantic, task-relevant concepts from LLM residual activations while enforcing a strict, deterministic sparsity budget.
- **Mechanism:** For an activation vector *h*, a linear encoder produces pre-activations; a TopK operator retains exactly *k* largest values (e.g., 13/256) and zeros the rest, yielding sparse code *ĉ*. The decoder reconstructs *h* from *ĉ*. Training minimizes reconstruction loss plus an L1 penalty on *ĉ* and a covariance decorrelation term (β-loss). Neuron resampling prevents dead features.
- **Core assumption:** Concepts active during reasoning are a small, spatially distributed subset; hard sparsity prevents polysemanticity and avoids signal shrinkage from L1.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "task-conditioned sparse autoencoders (with TopK gating, 5.1% L0)"; [section 3.1] "TopK retains exactly k=13 nonzeros per example (5.1% L0)" and "a general-purpose SAE without TopK gating produced 92% activation rate."
- **Break condition:** If reasoning concepts are not sparse (require >50% active features) or if optimal *k* varies widely across tasks, the fixed budget could discard critical signal or retain noise.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** DAGMA-based differentiable structure learning over extracted concept activation matrices recovers a sparse directed acyclic graph (DAG) representing causal dependencies between concepts.
- **Mechanism:** From sparse codes *C* ∈ ℝ<sup>N×K</sup>, select top-*M*=64 active concepts. Optimize weighted adjacency *W* by minimizing ||*C* − *CW*||<sub>F</sub><sup>2</sup> + λ<sub>1</sub>||*W*||<sub>1</sub> + λ<sub>2</sub>*h(W)*, where *h(W)* = tr(e<sup>*W*∘*W*</sup>) − *M* is the DAGMA acyclicity penalty (zero iff *W* is a DAG). Adam optimizes with λ<sub>1</sub>=0.02, λ<sub>2</sub>=0.05.
- **Core assumption:** The linear structural equation model *C ≈ CW* approximates feature interactions in the transformer’s residual stream for selected tasks, and the acyclicity penalty identifies a causal ordering from observational data.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "learn a directed acyclic graph over them via DAGMA-based differentiable structure learning"; [section 3.2] explicit formulation; [section 5.2] shows DAG violation ≈0 and CFS drop when removing acyclicity constraint.
- **Break condition:** If true feature interactions are nonlinear or contain instantaneous mutual causation, the linear SEM and acyclicity constraint may mis-specify the graph. The 5–6% edge density suggests strong sparsity; denser true graphs would be under-recovered with λ<sub>1</sub>=0.02.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The Causal Fidelity Score (CFS) evaluates whether the learned graph identifies causally influential nodes by measuring whether interventions on graph-predicted targets cause larger downstream activation changes than interventions on random nodes.
- **Mechanism:** For node *i*, children *D<sub>i</sub>* = {*j* : *W<sub>ij</sub>* > 0.01}. Ablation effect Δ<sub>i</sub> is the mean absolute change in children’s reconstructed activations *(CW)<sub>·j</sub>* when *c<sub>i</sub>* is set to zero, relative to original run. CFS averages ratio Δ<sub>i(c)</sub>/max(Δ<sub>i(r)</sub>, δ) over *S*=20 high-out-degree targets vs. random targets, with δ=10<sup>-3</sup> floor and τ=10 ceiling.
- **Core assumption:** A node’s causal influence on downstream computation is proportional to the magnitude of change in its children’s activations when ablated; nodes with higher out-degree in the true causal graph will have larger Δ.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Causal Fidelity Score (CFS) evaluates whether graph-guided interventions yield larger downstream activation changes than random ones"; [section 3.3] formal definition; [section 5] histograms show CCG-selected targets yield larger Δ distributions than random (all *p*<0.001).
- **Break condition:** If children activations are insensitive to parent ablation (due to redundancy or inaccurate SEM), Δ may not reflect true causal importance. The τ=10 cap and δ floor make CFS a conservative lower bound; uncapped ratios could be infinite for zero-out-degree random nodes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)**
  - **Why needed:** SAEs extract monosemantic, task-relevant concepts; without them, subsequent graph learning operates on polysemantic or noisy latent dimensions.
  - **Quick check:** Why does TopK gating produce a stable 5.1% L0, and how does that compare to L1-regularized SAEs?
- **Concept: Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) & Causal Structure Learning**
  - **Why needed:** CCG’s core output is a DAG over concepts; understanding acyclicity penalties (e.g., DAGMA’s trace(e<sup>*W*∘*W*</sup>)) is key to learnable gradient-based graph recovery.
  - **Quick check:** What mathematical property of a matrix *W* ensures its associated directed graph is acyclic, and how does DAGMA’s *h(W)* penalty exploit this?
- **Concept: Intervention-Based Evaluation (do-calculus style)**
  - **Why needed:** CFS is empirical validation; understanding that setting *c<sub>i</sub>*=0 is an intervention (*do*(*c<sub>i</sub>*=0)) distinguishes correlation from causal effect.
  - **Quick check:** Why is comparing Δ between graph-selected and random nodes a test of causal fidelity, not just predictive power?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** SAE Trainer -> Concept Selector -> DAGMA Learner -> CFS Evaluator
- **Critical path:** Activation extraction -> SAE training -> Concept selection -> DAG learning -> CFS evaluation. SAE must converge (L0 stable at 5.1%, MSE ↓) before graph learning; DAG violation must be ≈0 before trusting CFS.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **TopK vs. L1:** TopK gives exact sparsity rate and no shrinkage but is non-differentiable; L1 is differentiable but induces magnitude bias. Chose TopK for stable L0.
  - **Linear SEM:** Simplicity and differentiability vs. poor fit for nonlinear transformer computations. Ablation shows DAG constraint matters more than linearity (CFS drop when removing acyclicity).
  - **Single layer (L12):** Simpler graph, but ignores multi-layer reasoning dynamics. Deeper layers had better feature separability, but they prioritized intervention reach.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - SAE: L0 not converging to ~5% (check TopK *k*), high MSE (>0.5), or many dead features (<0.5% fire rate).
  - DAGMA: *h(W)* >> 10<sup>-3</sup> (cycle present), edge density far from 5–6% (λ<sub>1</sub> mis-set), or training loss not decreasing.
  - CFS: Values near 1.0 (graph no better than random), high variance across seeds (>±1.0), or Δ distributions overlapping random.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Train SAE on a small subset (50 examples); plot L0, MSE, total loss over epochs. Verify L0 stabilizes at exactly 5.1% (k=13/256) and MSE plateaus below ~0.5.
  2. After DAGMA training, compute *h(W)* and edge density. Confirm *h(W)* < 10<sup>-3</sup> and density ~5–6%. Visualize top-30 edges for sanity (sparse, no isolated cycles).
  3. Run CFS with a random adjacency matrix (same sparsity) instead of learned *W*. Expected CFS ≈ 1.0. Compare Δ distributions for CCG vs. random; CCG should show right-shift.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can CCG be extended to learn nonlinear causal relationships between concepts, given that transformers perform highly nonlinear computations?
  - **Basis in paper:** Section 7: "It uses a linear SEM, whereas transformer computations are highly nonlinear; extending to nonlinear SCMs is a natural next step."
  - **Why unresolved:** The linear SEM assumption may oversimplify complex, nonlinear interactions, limiting fidelity of recovered graph.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a differentiable nonlinear causal model within DAGMA framework, then evaluate whether CFS or alignment with known circuits improves on reasoning benchmarks.
- **Open Question 2:** Would incorporating activations from multiple layers produce more accurate and comprehensive causal graphs of multi-step reasoning?
  - **Basis in paper:** Section 7: "We extract concepts from a single layer (L12), though reasoning likely spans multiple layers, so multi-layer graphs may better reflect the computation."
  - **Why unresolved:** Reasoning is distributed across layers; a single snapshot might miss critical cross-layer dependencies.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend CCG to jointly model concept features from several layers, compare graph sparsity, stability, and CFS against single-layer baseline, and verify against manually identified circuits.
- **Open Question 3:** Does the CCG methodology scale effectively to larger language models (e.g., GPT-3/4) while retaining interpretability and performance gains?
  - **Basis in paper:** Section 7: "All results are for GPT-2 Medium only, and it remains unclear how the method scales to larger models."
  - **Why unresolved:** Larger models have higher dimensionality, deeper architectures, and more distributed representations, affecting SAE stability, graph sparsity, and computational feasibility.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply full CCG pipeline to models with billions of parameters, report resource usage, graph statistics, and CFS compared to baselines; assess whether concept quality and causal edge recovery degrade or remain robust.
- **Open Question 4:** What is the actual impact of the β-regularization term in SAE training after correcting the correlation computation bug?
  - **Basis in paper:** Section 7: "our β ablation is also confounded by a known correlation computation bug ... we treat this as qualitative evidence only and leave a corrected ablation to future work."
  - **Why unresolved:** The bug (NaNs from zero-variance columns in TopK activation) prevented quantitative assessment of β’s influence on disentanglement and downstream CFS.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Fix the correlation computation (e.g., by excluding zero-variance features), then re-run ablations measuring both correlation metrics and CFS to isolate β’s effect.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter opacity:** Critical training details (learning rates, neuron resampling windows) are underspecified, risking reconstruction fidelity and graph stability.
- **Intervention semantics ambiguity:** CFS computation (global vs. per-example ablation, child set thresholding) is unreported, potentially invalidating the causal metric.
- **Baseline definition gaps:** "ROME-style" and "SAE-only" ranking methods lack precise formulas, making reproduction of comparisons uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Primary outcome (CFS superiority):** High. Statistically robust results (p<0.0001, Bonferroni-corrected) across seeds and datasets; large effect size.
- **Mechanism 1 (TopK SAE monosemanticity):** Medium. Theoretical soundness and reported 5.1% L0 are credible, but lack of direct monosemanticity probes weakens evidence.
- **Mechanism 2 (DAGMA recovers causal DAG):** Low-Medium. Relies on strong assumptions (linear SEM, acyclicity from observational data). DAG violation ≈0 is necessary but not sufficient; no ground-truth validation.
- **Mechanism 3 (CFS as causal fidelity metric):** Medium. Interventional logic is sound, but linear SEM approximation and Δ aggregation may not capture true causal influence in nonlinear transformers.
