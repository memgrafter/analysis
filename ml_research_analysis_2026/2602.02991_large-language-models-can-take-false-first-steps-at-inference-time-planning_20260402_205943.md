# Large Language Models Can Take False First Steps at Inference-time Planning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.02991
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02991
- **Reference count**: 6
- **Primary result**: LLMs' inference-time planning evolves from prior-biased to likelihood-preferred as self-generated context accumulates, due to mismatch between human prompts and the model's internal distribution.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates why large language models (LLMs), despite acquiring sequence-level planning abilities during training, often exhibit short-sighted and inconsistent planning at inference time. It proposes a Bayesian explanation: planning is dynamically shaped by the evolving generative context. Human-written prompts typically differ from the model's internalized language distribution, creating initial uncertainty that biases planning toward the prior. As the model generates tokens, the context gradually shifts toward its in-distribution representation, reducing uncertainty and causing planning to converge toward the likelihood-preferred plan. Two experiments support this: a random-height generation task shows increasing predictability of future tokens with accumulated self-generated context, and a Gaussian sampling task demonstrates a biasing-then-debiasing dynamic when conditioning on external versus self-generated data.

## Method Summary
The study uses two instruction-tuned LLMs (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct) without fine-tuning. In Experiment 1, models generate sequences of integers (simulating random height guesses) from a starting number. Researchers collect hidden-state embeddings for each generated token and use LASSO regression to measure how well embeddings predict numeric values several steps ahead (R²). In Experiment 2, models sample integers from Gaussian distributions. They compare the first sample when conditioning on ground-truth Gaussian samples (Gen I) versus conditioning on the model's own previous samples (Gen II), quantifying bias relative to the true mean. Both experiments track how planning evolves as the context shifts from an external prompt to self-generated content.

## Key Results
- In the random-height task, the predictive power (R²) of embeddings for future numeric values was initially low but increased sharply as more self-generated context accumulated, approaching nearly perfect prediction for some models.
- In the Gaussian sampling task, models conditioned on external Gaussian samples showed significant initial bias (e.g., -9.63 for μ=0 in Llama), while conditioning on self-generated samples markedly reduced this bias.
- Both results confirm the proposed dynamic: planning starts prior-biased due to prompt mismatch and gradually converges toward the likelihood-preferred plan as the context becomes in-distribution.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Initial tokens are planning-prior-biased because human prompts (I_C) are out-of-distribution relative to the model’s internalized language (I_M), creating high uncertainty in the planning-likelihood term.
- Mechanism: The Bayesian decomposition P(s|I, φ) ∝ P(I|s, φ)·P(s|φ) shows that when I_C is unfamiliar, entropy H[P(s|I_C, φ)] increases, causing the domain prior P(s|φ) to dominate early generation.
- Core assumption: Natural language prompts systematically differ from the model’s self-generated distribution; this mismatch is not due to task complexity but representational discrepancy.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Mismatches between human-written prompts and the model's internalized distribution create initial uncertainty, causing planning to be prior-biased."
  - [section 2.1] "H [P(s|I_C, φ)]>H [P(s|I_M, φ)]... the prior term exerts greater influence."
  - [corpus] Corpus shows related work on inference-time planning but no direct citation for the mismatch; this is an inferred assumption from cited distribution shift literature (An et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024).
- Break condition: If prompts perfectly match the model’s internal distribution (I_C ≡ I_M), uncertainty remains low and the planning-likelihood dominates from the first token, eliminating prior bias.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Planning shifts and converges as self-generated tokens (I_M) accumulate in the context, moving from prior-preferred to likelihood-preferred continuations.
- Mechanism: Each generated token is concatenated back into the context, progressively reducing entropy in P(s|context, φ). This “prior-suppressing” process causes the planning-likelihood term to sharpen, aligning the plan with the original query.
- Core assumption: The model’s own generations are in-distribution and thus reliably reduce uncertainty when added to the context.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "As self-generated tokens accumulate, the context shifts toward the model's in-distribution representation... causing planning to converge toward the likelihood-preferred plan."
  - [section 2.2] "The changing context continually adjusts the position as well as extent of the likelihood."
  - [experiment 1] Increasing R² of embeddings predicting future tokens (up to ~1.0 for Qwen) as generation proceeds (Figures 2 & 3).
- Break condition: If the model’s self-generated tokens were themselves out-of-distribution (e.g., due to catastrophic drift), adding them would not reduce uncertainty and convergence would not occur.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The planning process exhibits a biasing-then-debiasing dynamic where initial generations are systematically biased but progressively correct toward the true target distribution.
- Mechanism: The initial prior bias (from Mechanism 1) is counteracted as likelihood gains strength from accumulating I_M, creating a trajectory from biased to unbiased outputs. This implies the model “knows” the correct plan but cannot enact it immediately due to contextual uncertainty.
- Core assumption: The domain prior P(s|φ) is itself unbiased relative to the ground-truth task distribution; initial bias stems solely from the prompt mismatch, not from a flawed prior.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "a biasing-then-debiasing dynamic."
  - [experiment 2] For Gaussian sampling, initial samples conditioned on external Gaussian data show significant bias (e.g., μ=0 → -9.63 for Llama), while samples conditioned on self-generated data show reduced bias (Table 1, Gen I vs Gen II).
  - [discussion] "the model is aware of the correct response sequence from the outset and actively adjusts its biased generation."
- Break condition: If the domain prior P(s|φ) is itself biased (e.g., due to training distribution skew), the “debiasing” phase would converge to a biased equilibrium, not the ground truth.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  Input processor -> Transformer layers -> Context buffer -> Output head

- **Critical path**:
  1. Human prompt I_C enters → high entropy in planning-likelihood → prior-dominated first token.
  2. First token added to context → context partially shifts toward I_M.
  3. Repeat: each new token reduces uncertainty → planning gradually aligns with query intent.
  4. Convergence when context is sufficiently in-distribution.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Greedy decoding (temperature=0) exaggerates planning dynamics but reduces output diversity.
  - Using middle-layer embeddings (vs. final-layer) for planning probes better captures shift (per Experiment 1 layer analysis).
  - Structured numeric tasks isolate planning from semantic confounds but may not generalize to free text.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Large initial bias in outputs (Experiment 2 Gen I).
  - Low R² of embeddings predicting distant future tokens early in sequence (Experiment 1).
  - Over-correction after initial bias (Figure 4).

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Random-height generation**: Track embedding predictability (R²) across sequence positions; verify increase as context grows.
  2. **Gaussian sampling with external vs. self-generated prefixes**: Quantify first-token bias reduction when conditioning on self-generated samples.
  3. **Free-text extension**: Apply similar probing to open-ended generation (as called for in limitations) to test generalizability beyond numeric tasks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What specific factors or properties of the "subtle differences between natural language and the language internalized by LLMs" cause the initial planning likelihood to be high-entropy and prior-biased when conditioning on human-written prompts?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "given the subtle differences between natural language and the language internalized by LLMs, accumulated self-generated context drives a planning-shift during inference." They formalize this as H[P(s|I_C, φ)] > H[P(s|I_M, φ)], meaning higher uncertainty when interpreting human prompts (I_C) vs. model-native prompts (I_M). The mechanism causing this entropy difference is not specified.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper treats the distribution mismatch as the root cause but does not analyze what linguistic, statistical, or representational properties of I_C (relative to I_M) induce the initial high uncertainty. Is it due to token frequency disparities, syntactic structures, semantic gaps, or something else?
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments that systematically vary specific features of prompts (e.g., vocabulary frequency, syntactic complexity, semantic domain) and measure the resulting initial entropy in the planning likelihood term, or directly probe the model's internal representations of I_C vs. I_M to identify the discrepancy.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the predicted planning convergence and recovery from initial bias generalize to unconstrained, open-ended free-text generation beyond the structured numeric tasks used here?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The "Limitations and Future Research" section directly states: "a major limitation of this work concerns the generality of the results; specifically, whether free-text generation exhibits the same planning patterns."
- **Why unresolved:** The two experiments used highly constrained numeric outputs (height estimates, Gaussian samples) to enable clear measurement of planning strength and bias. It is unknown if the same dynamic shift-and-converge pattern occurs in semantically rich, topically diverse, and syntactically varied free-text generation, where planning signals are harder to isolate.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the experimental logic (measuring predictive power of embeddings over future tokens or output distribution bias) in a free-text scenario, perhaps using tasks like story continuation or essay writing where ground-truth "good" plans are harder to define but convergence could still be observed via improving consistency or coherence metrics over time.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do different model architectures (Llama vs. Qwen) exhibit quantitatively different convergence dynamics in Experiment 1 (e.g., Qwen's R² approaches 1.0 while Llama's peaks near 0.8)? Does this relate to their internalized distributions (I_M) or training data?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Figure 3 shows distinct ceiling levels for planning R². The paper attributes convergence to accumulation of I_M, implying the ceiling reflects the strength of the model's inherent planning ability when fully in-distribution. The difference suggests models have different "maximum achievable planning strength" under these tasks, but the cause is not discussed.
- **Why unresolved:** The Bayesian account predicts convergence for all models but does not specify what determines the asymptotic planning strength. It could be differences in training corpus (human vs. synthetic data ratio), model size, or architectural inductive biases affecting how cleanly the planning likelihood sharpens as context becomes I_M.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Correlate convergence metrics (like final R² in Experiment 1) with model metadata (training data composition, size, architecture) across a larger panel of models. Alternatively, directly compare the internalized distributions (I_M) of Llama and Qwen via likelihood modeling or embedding space analysis to see which one better matches the "ideal" in-distribution context for the task.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the precise mechanism behind the over-correction observed in Experiment 2's biasing-then-debiasing pattern? Is it an adaptive adjustment of the prior term, a sharpening of the likelihood, or a form of online error correction?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Discussion notes: "we observe an immediate over-correction following the initial starting bias. These results indicate that the model is aware of the correct response sequence from the outset and actively adjusts its biased generation in a dynamic, self-adaptive manner." However, the Bayesian formulation (Equation 5) describes a gradual shift due to context change but does not explicitly predict over-correction.
- **Why unresolved:** The Bayesian model explains the shift from prior-biased to likelihood-preferred planning as entropy in the likelihood decreases. Over-correction implies the planning term may overshoot the target distribution temporarily, suggesting a more complex dynamics (perhaps involving momentum or adaptive step sizes in the belief update) than a simple entropy reduction.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyze the evolution of the planning likelihood P(I_C^(t)→M | s_t, φ) over time steps in the Gaussian task. Can we directly observe it becoming overly concentrated on values past the true mean before settling? Alternatively, test if introducing small perturbations to the self-generated context alters the tendency to over-correct, probing the stability of the convergence.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can the documented planning shift mechanism be harnessed to improve generation quality, for instance by deliberately steering the context toward I_M earlier or using the initial biased state constructively?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The General Discussion links the finding to self-improvement methods (e.g., self-evaluation, iterative refinement), suggesting the shift might explain why bootstrapping on model outputs helps. However, it does not propose how to actively use the dynamics—e.g., should we avoid early decisions based on the prior-biased plan? Could we design prompts that faster create an I_M context?
- **Why unresolved:** The paper is diagnostic, not interventional. It shows the phenomenon exists but does not test whether controlling the rate of context shift (e.g., by inserting model-native phrases early) leads to better final outputs. The over-correction also hints that the dynamics might be non-monotonic and could be optimized.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Intervention experiments where the generation process is manipulated: (a) prepend a short, model-native "warm-up" sequence to the prompt to accelerate I_M accumulation, measuring if planning converges earlier and outputs improve; (b) use the initial biased generation as a proposal distribution in a sampling method (like MCMC) to see if leveraging the prior leads to better exploration of the true likelihood-preferred sequences.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability**: Experiments use constrained numeric tasks; planning in open-ended text generation remains untested, risking overfitting to structured prompts.
- **Causal attribution**: The Bayesian account posits that human prompts are out-of-distribution relative to the model’s internal distribution, but this is inferred rather than measured; alternative explanations (e.g., decoding artifacts, training data sparsity) are not fully ruled out.
- **Model coverage**: Results are shown for only two instruction-tuned models; scaling trends and architectural dependencies are unknown.

## Confidence
- **Overall claim (planning shifts and converges during inference)**: Medium. Experiments 1 and 2 demonstrate temporal dynamics, but the Bayesian mechanism is a plausible interpretation, not a direct test.
- **Mechanism 1 (initial prior bias due to prompt mismatch)**: Medium. Initial bias is observed, but the assumed mismatch is not quantified; the prior itself could be biased.
- **Mechanism 2 (self-generated context reduces uncertainty)**: High. Experiment 1 directly shows increasing R² as context accumulates, aligning with the prediction.
- **Mechanism 3 (biasing-then-debiasing)**: Medium. Experiment 2 shows reduced bias from Gen I to Gen II, but convergence to ground truth assumes an unbiased prior, which is unverified.

## Next Checks
1. Generalize to free text: Replicate Experiment 1’s R² trajectory in a narrative generation task (e.g., story continuation), using embeddings to predict discourse coherence or next-sentence relevance instead of numeric values.
2. Quantify distribution mismatch: Compute embedding-based distances (e.g., Fréchet distance) between human prompts and model-generated continuations (across many seeds) to test whether initial uncertainty correlates with out-of-distributionness.
3. Test in-distribution prompts: Replace human-written prompts with model-generated prefixes (in-distribution by construction) and verify if the initial planning bias disappears or diminishes, isolating the mismatch effect.
