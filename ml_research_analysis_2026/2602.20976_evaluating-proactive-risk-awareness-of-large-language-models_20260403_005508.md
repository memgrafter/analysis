# Evaluating Proactive Risk Awareness of Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20976
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20976
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs exhibit a significant gap between reactive and proactive safety, with proactive awareness highly sensitive to response length constraints and showing severe blind spots in multimodal conservation scenarios.

## Executive Summary
Current large language model (LLM) safety alignment focuses on reactive refusal of explicitly harmful queries but fails to evaluate proactive anticipation of latent, consequential risks—such as environmental harm—in seemingly innocuous everyday requests. This paper introduces a proactive risk awareness framework and the Butterfly dataset, a benchmark of 1,094 queries simulating ordinary activities with hidden ecological impacts, grounded in Chinese environmental law. The authors evaluate five state-of-the-art LLMs across response length, language, and modality conditions. Results reveal that proactive awareness is substantially weaker than reactive safety, collapses under short-response constraints, exhibits similar fragility across English and Chinese, and displays severe blind spots when processing images of protected species. These findings expose a critical alignment gap for systemic risks and highlight the verbosity-dependency of proactive reasoning.

## Method Summary
The Butterfly dataset is constructed by first extracting 70 harmful behaviors from 36 Chinese environmental laws. Using a GPT-based pipeline and two-stage verification (automated consistency checks and human review), the authors generate 1,068 textual queries that appear benign but imply ecological harm. Additionally, they curate a protected species subset: 26 query templates paired with images of 285 first-class protected animals and plants. Five LLMs (GPT-5, Gemini-2.5, Doubao-seed-1.6, Qwen3-Max, Deepseek-Chat) are evaluated under three factors: response length (full vs. short, induced by prompt), language (English vs. Chinese), and modality (text vs. image). Each model response is automatically classified by GPT-5 into semantic categories (Harmful, Warning, Alternative, Blind, Others) based on behavior adoption and warning alignment. Four metrics are computed: Proactive Rate (ProR), Harmful Adoption Rate (HAR), General Warning Rate (GR), and Blind Spot Rate (BR). A system prompt intervention (“Be aware of potential consequences…”) is also tested to assess whether explicit instructions improve awareness.

## Key Results
- Proactive awareness drops sharply under short-response constraints, while harmful adoption and blind spots increase (e.g., GPT: ProR 0.85→0.71).
- Visual species queries yield lower ProR than textual ones, with weak coupling between species recognition and risk warnings.
- Reactive safety (explicit harm refusal) consistently outperforms proactive awareness, indicating a fundamental alignment gap for latent risks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper identifies three mechanisms that explain the observed limitations in LLM proactive risk awareness:

**Response Length Constraints Collapse Proactive Reasoning**  
Proactive risk identification and communication require verbosity to articulate latent impacts and safe alternatives. Under token constraints, the model’s output distribution shifts toward fulfilling the surface intent, suppressing the longer explanatory chains needed for proactive warnings. This is evidenced by the systematic decline in ProR and increase in BR under short conditions across all models.

**Cross-Lingual Transfer of Proactive Fragility**  
The patterns of proactive fragility under brevity and the baseline gap with reactive safety hold across English and Chinese, suggesting the issue is not language-specific but reflects a universal misalignment. The underlying representation of systemic risks appears under-trained compared to reactive harm recognition.

**Modality-Induced Blind Spots in Species Protection**  
When species are presented as images without explicit names, proactive awareness plummets. The model’s safety triggers rely on lexical patterns (species name + activity) and are not robustly activated by visual cues alone, revealing a decoupling between perception and policy reasoning.

### Reactive vs. Proactive Safety Alignment
- **Why needed:** Distinguishes between refusing explicit harmful queries (reactive) and anticipating harm from innocuous ones (proactive), which is central to the paper’s evaluation framework.
- **Quick check:** Provide an example of a Butterfly dataset query that might trigger reactive refusal but slip past proactive awareness.

### Legal/Regulatory Grounding of Harm
- **Why needed:** Using Chinese environmental laws provides concrete, objective harm definitions, avoiding subjective model‑centric judgments and ensuring evaluation validity.
- **Quick check:** Why is it acceptable to use a single jurisdiction’s laws to assess a general ecological awareness capability, despite cultural differences?

### Response Taxonomy (SafeAlt, WarnIntel, WarnGeneral, Blind)
- **Why needed:** The core metrics (ProR, HAR, GR, BR) are derived from these semantic categories; misunderstanding them leads to misinterpretation of results.
- **Quick check:** Classify: “You should use eco‑friendly fireworks to minimize pollution.” (Options: SafeAlt, WarnIntel, WarnGeneral, Blind).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Query Generator → Harm Behavior & Impact Mapper → Model Inference Engine → Auto-Annotator → Metrics Calculator  
(Protected Species Asset Library branches into Model Inference for multimodal queries)

**Critical path**  
Scenario generation → Harm mapping → Model response generation → Automatic classification → Metric computation.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Legal grounding vs. generalizability: Using Chinese laws yields concrete harm definitions but may not generalize globally; a worldwide system would need multi‑jurisdiction harmonization.
- Bilingual coverage vs. scope: Testing EN/ZH is feasible but not exhaustive; expanding to more languages increases evaluation cost quadratically.
- Multimodal test vs. complexity: Raw image inputs probe realistic blind spots but introduce noise from variable species recognition; a controlled test with both images and names would be less realistic.

**Failure signatures**
- Metric-level: ProR << ReR; BR spikes under short responses; GR surges after system prompt intervention.
- Category-level: ProR very low for “everyday care” species actions (e.g., feeding, watering) compared to commercial/hunting actions.
- Modality-level: For identical species queries, ProR(image) < ProR(text).

**First experiments**
1. **Length Stress Test:** Run full Butterfly dataset (EN) on all models with default and “response in short” prompts; measure changes in ProR and BR.
2. **Reactive Baseline:** Query models with direct statements of harmful behaviors (e.g., “How to illegally dump waste?”) and compare ReR to ProR.
3. **System Prompt Intervention:** In the short-response condition, add the provided system instruction; track effects on ProR, GR, and HAR.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can LLMs be optimized to retain proactive environmental risk awareness when generating concise responses, which are common in real-world deployments?
- **Basis in paper:** The results show a “substantial drop in Proactive Rate” and increase in “Blind Spot Rate” under short-response constraints across all models, indicating proactive awareness is “highly verbosity-dependent.”
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies this critical dependency but does not investigate architectural, training, or inference‑level modifications that could mitigate it, leaving a practical barrier for deployment unaddressed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments ablating model components (e.g., attention heads), fine‑tuning with length‑penalized objectives on latent‑risk scenarios, or evaluating decoding strategies (e.g., constrained generation) to measure their effect on ProR under fixed brevity.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why do current LLMs exhibit a consistent gap between reactive safety (refusing explicit harmful behaviors) and proactive awareness (anticipating latent ecological risks), and what alignment techniques can bridge this gap?
- **Basis in paper:** The analysis experiment finds “proactive awareness is substantially weaker than reactive safety” across models, with higher “Reactive Environmental Reminder Rate (ReR)” when harmful behaviors are explicitly stated.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper documents the disparity but does not analyze whether it stems from training data imbalances (overexposure to explicit harm vs. latent risk), deficiencies in causal or counterfactual reasoning, or misaligned reward models.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative fine‑tuning studies where models are exposed to curated datasets of latent ecological risks, paired with probing of internal representations during inference to identify signals of risk anticipation, or reinforcement learning from human feedback specifically targeting proactive warnings.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What mechanisms cause multimodal LLMs to show reduced proactive species protection awareness when processing images compared to text, and how can this be improved?
- **Basis in paper:** The multimodal experiments reveal “visual modality consistently yields lower ProR” and that “species identification and conservation awareness are weakly coupled,” with low warning rates even when species are recognized.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper notes this blind spot but does not explore whether the vision encoder fails to extract ecologically relevant features, if the language model ignores visual cues during risk reasoning, or if training data lacks multimodal ecological context.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Error analyses of vision‑language attention patterns on protected species images, controlled tests with synthetically manipulated visual inputs (e.g., highlighting ecological attributes), or multimodal fine‑tuning on datasets explicitly linking species imagery to legal/ecological consequences.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent do the findings on proactive environmental awareness generalize beyond the China‑centric legal framework and bilingual (English/Chinese) settings considered in this study?
- **Basis in paper:** The limitation section acknowledges that generalizability to other “languages, cultural contexts, and broader categories of environmental or social harm remains an open question,” and the dataset is grounded in Chinese regulations.
- **Why unresolved:** Environmental harms and legal definitions vary culturally; models trained or evaluated on this dataset may not transfer to regions with different ecological priorities or regulatory systems, limiting the global applicability of the framework.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication studies in diverse cultural and regulatory contexts (e.g., EU, US, or Global South) using locally sourced environmental laws and scenarios, measuring consistency in ProR and failure modes across these settings.

## Limitations
- **Dataset reconstruction risk:** The Butterfly dataset is not public; rebuilding it from the described pipeline may yield a different distribution and lacks the original protected species images.
- **Annotation ambiguity:** The auto‑annotator’s prompt and exact “short response” constraint are underspecified, threatening measurement consistency.
- **Scope constraints:** Evaluation covers only five models, two languages, and Chinese environmental law; applicability to other architectures, languages, or harm domains is uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Response Length Collapse):** High (consistent, significant results across models).
- **Mechanism 2 (Cross‑Lingual Transfer of Proactive Fragility):** Medium (patterns within EN/ZH clear, but universality untested).
- **Mechanism 3 (Modality‑Induced Blind Spots):** Medium (strong within‑paper evidence but no external validation).

## Next Checks
- **Release & Replicate:** Obtain the Butterfly dataset from the authors and re‑run the core length‑stress experiment on at least two models to confirm the ProR drop and BR increase.
- **Human‑Annotation Audit:** Conduct a detailed human review (n ≥ 500) of the GPT‑5 annotations, measuring agreement on critical distinctions (e.g., WarnIntel vs. WarnGeneral, Blind). Refine definitions if agreement < 85%.
- **Modality Isolation Test:** For the species subset, run three conditions: (a) text‑only with species name, (b) image‑only without name, (c) image + name. Measure ProR and species identification accuracy to directly quantify the perception‑policy decoupling.
