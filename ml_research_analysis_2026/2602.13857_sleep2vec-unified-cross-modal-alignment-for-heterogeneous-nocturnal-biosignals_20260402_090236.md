# sleep2vec: Unified Cross-Modal Alignment for Heterogeneous Nocturnal Biosignals
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13857
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13857
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Foundation model for polysomnography that handles arbitrary missing modalities; achieves state-of-the-art sleep staging (88.6% accuracy, 0.84 Cohen’s κ, 79.5% Macro‑F1) and strong cross‑cohort generalization (APPLES: 78.4% accuracy, 0.69 κ).

## Executive Summary
sleep2vec is a foundation model for heterogeneous nocturnal biosignals (polysomnography) that learns a unified representation across nine modalities. It employs modality-specific tokenizers to map raw signals at their native sampling rates into temporally aligned embeddings, which are processed by a shared modality‑agnostic RoFormer backbone. Pre‑training uses a metadata‑aware DASH‑InfoNCE contrastive objective that weights negative samples by demographic and acquisition similarity to mitigate cohort‑specific bias. Independent timestep masking (15%) further encourages cross‑modal complementarity. The model accepts any subset of modalities at inference, making it robust to sensor dropout. Evaluated on five public cohorts, sleep2vec surpasses prior foundation models on sleep staging and clinical prediction tasks, and demonstrates strong generalization to an external dataset (APPLES).

## Method Summary
sleep2vec is pre‑trained on 42 249 overnight recordings from five public cohorts (SHHS, WSC, MrOS, MESA, HSP). Each 30‑second epoch of a modality is fed through a lightweight MLP tokenizer to produce a fixed‑dimensional embedding; nine tokenizers yield temporally aligned sequences. A RoFormer backbone (12 layers, hidden 768, 16 heads) processes the sequence with a prepended `[CLS]` token. During pre‑training, 15% of timesteps are independently masked with modality‑specific tokens. The backbone output at each timestep is projected into a 128‑D space via a shared three‑layer MLP, and a DASH‑InfoNCE loss is computed between two randomly chosen modalities within the batch. Negative samples are weighted using age (Laplace kernel, σ=20), gender, and site coefficients, and a margin reduces the influence of same‑subject negatives. For downstream tasks (sleep staging, clinical prediction), the projection head and masking are removed. A learnable gating mechanism fuses the modality‑specific RoFormer outputs; LoRA is applied for sleep staging while the backbone is frozen for clinical prediction. The model can handle any subset of the nine modalities without retraining.

## Key Results
- State‑of‑the‑art sleep staging on SHHS full channels: 88.6% accuracy, 0.84 Cohen’s κ, 79.5% Macro‑F1.
- Strong cross‑cohort generalization on APPLES (unseen during pre‑training/fine‑tuning): 78.4% accuracy, 0.69 κ.
- Robustness to arbitrary missing modalities: graceful degradation when any single modality is excluded (leave‑one‑out analysis).
- Ablation studies confirm DASH‑InfoNCE and 15% masking both contribute significantly to performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** The DASH-InfoNCE objective improves cross-cohort generalization by reducing cohort-specific bias in contrastive learning.  
**Mechanism:** The loss dynamically weights negative samples based on demographic (age, gender) and acquisition (site) metadata. An age kernel decreases weight with increasing age difference; gender and site coefficients penalize mismatches; a small constant retains same-subject-night negatives while a margin reduces their influence.  
**Core assumption:** Physiological similarity correlates with demographic and site similarity; thus demographically similar negatives are harder and more informative for learning general representations.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract explicitly states metadata is used to “dynamically weight negatives and mitigate cohort-specific shortcuts.”  
- Section 3.3 details the weighting formula (Eq. 4) and margin modulation (Eq. 5).  
- Ablation in Table 8 shows DASH‑InfoNCE outperforms vanilla InfoNCE on cross‑site APPLES evaluation.  
**Break condition:** If metadata is missing or unreliable; if physiological state does not correlate with demographic factors; if weighting destabilizes optimization by over‑penalizing negatives.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Modality-specific tokenizers enable unified processing of heterogeneous sampling rates while preserving temporal alignment for contrastive learning.  
**Mechanism:** Each modality uses a lightweight MLP tokenizer to project a 30‑second native‑rate segment into a fixed‑dimension embedding. High‑rate signals (EEG, etc. at ~128 Hz) and low‑rate signals (SpO₂, etc. at ~4 Hz) produce temporally aligned sequences for the shared backbone.  
**Core assumption:** PSG channels are temporally synchronized recordings of the same underlying physiological state; aligning at 30‑second epochs (standard AASM epoch) is valid.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract mentions “modality-specific tokenizers and a modality‑agnostic RoFormer backbone to align embeddings at each timestep.”  
- Section 3.2 describes tokenizer architecture and native sampling rates.  
- Table 3 confirms typical sampling rates for each channel.  
**Break condition:** If recordings are not properly synchronized; if tokenizer capacity is insufficient; if sampling rate disparity is too extreme for a fixed 30‑second window.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Independent timestep masking with a modality-agnostic backbone yields robust representations that generalize to arbitrary missing modalities and sensor dropout.  
**Mechanism:** During pre‑training, each timestep in a segment has a 15% chance to be replaced by a modality‑specific mask token. The backbone must rely on other modalities at the same or adjacent timesteps to reconstruct or align the masked content, encouraging cross‑modal complementarity.  
**Core assumption:** Within a short epoch (30 sec), physiological events manifest across multiple modalities; missing information in one channel can be inferred from others.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract highlights robustness to “arbitrary modality subsets and sensor dropout.”  
- Section 3.2 specifies independent time‑step masking (15% probability, modality‑specific mask token).  
- Tables 9 and 10 (ablation) show 15% masking outperforms 0% and 30% on downstream staging.  
**Break condition:** If modalities are redundant rather than complementary; if masking rate is too high/low; if backbone lacks capacity to model dependencies.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cross‑modal contrastive alignment**  
  *Why needed:* PSG modalities have distinct statistical distributions and noise characteristics. Contrastive learning aligns them into a shared space without requiring synchronized labels for every channel, enabling a unified representation.  
  *Quick check:* Do t‑SNE plots ([section 4.1] Figure 3) show that embeddings cluster by subject/physiological state rather than by modality after pre‑training?

- **Metadata‑aware regularization**  
  *Why needed:* Pre‑training data spans multiple sites and decades; without correction, the model may learn site‑specific artifacts or demographic shortcuts instead of general physiology. Metadata weighting forces consideration of true physiological similarity.  
  *Quick check:* Does performance on a new site (APPLES) degrade less when using DASH‑InfoNCE vs. vanilla InfoNCE? ([Table 8])

- **Modality drop‑in/drop‑out capability**  
  *Why needed:* Real‑world deployments have variable sensor availability (wearable vs. full PSG). The model must accept any subset of the nine modalities at inference without retraining a specialized pipeline.  
  *Quick check:* Does the leave‑one‑out analysis ([Figure 4]) show graceful degradation when any single modality is excluded, rather than catastrophic failure?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Raw PSG modalities (EEG, EOG, EMG, ECG, Nasal Airflow, ABD/Thor, SpO₂, IBI, Respiratory) → modality‑specific tokenizers (MLP per modality) → feature embeddings (temporally aligned sequences) → modality‑agnostic RoFormer backbone (with `[CLS]`) → timestep hidden states.  
Pre‑training: hidden states → shared projection head (3‑layer MLP) → 128‑D alignments → DASH‑InfoNCE loss.  
Fine‑tuning: remove projection head; gated fusion of modality‑specific RoFormer outputs; task‑specific heads (per‑timestep for sleep staging, `[CLS]` for clinical prediction).  

**Critical path:**  
1. Segment recordings into non‑overlapping 30‑second epochs.  
2. Tokenize each epoch with its modality‑specific MLP → embedding sequence.  
3. Pre‑training: prepend `[CLS]`; apply independent timestep masking (15%); feed to RoFormer.  
4. Project each timestep; compute DASH‑InfoNCE loss across modality pairs within batch, using metadata‑weighted negatives and same‑subject margin.  
5. Inference: no masking; fuse modalities via learned gating; feed to task head.  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Tokenizer simplicity: MLP is lightweight but may lose modality‑specific patterns; a CNN could improve feature extraction but adds parameters and overfitting risk.  
- Masking rate: Fixed 15% works well; too low under‑regularizes, too high corrupts signal. Fixed across modalities ignores signal‑to‑noise ratio differences.  
- Metadata weighting: Requires reliable metadata; heuristic coefficients (γ, δ) may need tuning for new cohorts; adds computational overhead.  
- Fusion strategy: Gating assumes linear combination; attention could capture interactions but may overfit with limited downstream data.  

**Failure signatures:**  
- t‑SNE shows embeddings clustering by modality (not subject) → tokenizers or contrastive loss ineffective.  
- Large performance drop on new site (APPLES) when metadata not used → weighting insufficient or metadata mis‑specified.  
- Leave‑one‑out ablation shows catastrophic drop for a channel → model over‑relied on that channel; masking too low or contrastive objective weak.  
- Training instability (NaNs) → masking too high, negative weighting too aggressive, or learning rate issues.  

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Alignment sanity check: t‑SNE of `[CLS]` and timestep embeddings pre/post training to confirm cross‑modal and temporal coherence.  
2. Modality importance: Leave‑one‑out on SHHS sleep staging to identify critical channels and ensure robustness to missing sensors.  
3. Scaling verification: Vary model size and pre‑training data fraction to confirm performance gains and diminishing returns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can the fixed negative weighting in DASH‑InfoNCE be replaced with a learnable, data‑driven mechanism to improve cross‑modal alignment?  
2. What is the functional form of the scaling law relating downstream performance to model size and number of modalities for PSG foundation models, and does it hold beyond the studied regime?  
3. Does sleep2vec generalize to pediatric populations and non‑Western cohorts, given current evaluation on adult/Western datasets?  
4. Can a hybrid contrastive‑generative pre‑training objective combine benefits of both to achieve greater robustness to sensor dropout?

## Limitations
- Exact pre‑training schedule and handling of missing modalities during pre‑training are unspecified, hindering exact replication.  
- Aggregation method for whole‑night clinical predictions is not described, possibly affecting reported performance.  
- Limited external validation: only one out‑of‑domain cohort (APPLES) tested; broader generalizability remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- DASH‑InfoNCE effectiveness: Medium  
- Modality‑specific tokenizers enabling unified processing: High  
- Independent timestep masking for robustness to sensor dropout: High  
- Overall performance and cross‑cohort generalization: Medium  

## Next Checks
1. Replicate DASH‑InfoNCE vs. vanilla InfoNCE on an independent public cohort (e.g., MESA) to verify that metadata weighting consistently reduces site‑specific bias and improves cross‑site sleep staging performance.  
2. Evaluate real‑world missing‑modality robustness by applying the pre‑trained model to a dataset with naturally limited sensors (e.g., home sleep apnea testing with only EEG, ECG, SpO₂) and compare degradation to controlled leave‑one‑out results.  
3. Ablate tokenizer complexity: replace the MLP tokenizer with a lightweight 1D CNN or add handcrafted spectral features to assess whether the simple tokenizer is near‑optimal or if more sophisticated feature extraction yields gains.
