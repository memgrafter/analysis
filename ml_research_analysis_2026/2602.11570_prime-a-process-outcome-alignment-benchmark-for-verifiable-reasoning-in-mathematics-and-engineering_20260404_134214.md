# PRIME: A Process-Outcome Alignment Benchmark for Verifiable Reasoning in Mathematics and Engineering

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11570
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11570
- **Reference count:** 34
- **Primary result:** PRIME introduces a benchmark and process-aware RLVR method that yields substantial gains (8-9% absolute) on challenging math benchmarks by detecting and eliminating reward hacking via flawed reasoning.

## Executive Summary
PRIME addresses a critical flaw in existing outcome-only verifiers used for reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR): they incorrectly reward correct answers derived from flawed reasoning ("lucky guesses"). To solve this, the authors construct a high-quality benchmark of 2,530 college-level STEM problems, each annotated with both outcome correctness and process-outcome logical consistency. The benchmark is filtered to retain only "hard-to-verify" samples where verifiers disagree, ensuring it targets subtle reasoning errors. Evaluation shows current verifiers often fail to identify flawed derivations. Using verifiers selected based on PRIME scores to train policies with a process-aware reward function—which only rewards trajectories with both correct answers and sound reasoning—leads to significant improvements on AIME and Beyond-AIME benchmarks for Qwen3-14B-Base (absolute gains of up to 9.12%). Moreover, verifier accuracy on PRIME strongly predicts downstream RLVR performance (R² > 0.92), validating PRIME as a reliable selection tool.

## Method Summary
The authors first curate a large corpus of STEM problems, then apply a multi-stage filtering pipeline: (1) remove unverifiable or incorrect problems using automated checks; (2) generate diverse responses from multiple LRMs; (3) use a proxy verifier (GPT-OSS-120B) to compute a consensus score over multiple trials, retaining only samples where the consensus is strictly between 0 and 1 (i.e., verifier disagreement) to isolate "hard-to-verify" instances; (4) have expert annotators label each retained sample for outcome accuracy and overall process-outcome consistency. This yields the PRIME benchmark. For RLVR, they train policy models (Qwen3-8B/14B-Base) using PPO with a reward function that is the logical AND of outcome correctness and process consistency as judged by a selected verifier. Verifiers are chosen based on their Overall Accuracy on PRIME. Downstream performance on challenging math benchmarks is measured to assess the effectiveness of the process-aware approach and the predictive power of PRIME scores.

## Key Results
- Process-aware RLVR with PRIME-selected verifiers yields absolute gains of 8.29% on AIME24, 9.12% on AIME25, and 7.31% on Beyond-AIME for Qwen3-14B-Base.
- Verifier accuracy on PRIME (Overall label) shows a strong linear correlation (R² > 0.92) with downstream RLVR performance, confirming PRIME's utility for verifier selection.
- Current state-of-the-art verifiers frequently fail to detect correct-outcome/flawed-process samples, highlighting the benchmark's difficulty and the need for process-aware supervision.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Difficulty Filtering Isolates "Lucky Guesses"
The PRIME construction pipeline uses a two-stage filter to select samples that are intrinsically hard to verify. After generating multiple responses per problem, a proxy verifier (GPT-OSS-120B) runs k=8 trials. Samples with a consensus score strictly between 0 and 1 (i.e., non-unanimous verdicts) are retained as "Hard-to-Verify." This precisely captures cases where the reasoning contains subtle errors that confuse verifiers, including "lucky guesses" (correct outcome but flawed process). The core assumption is that verifier disagreement indicates underlying complexity or deception. Break condition: if the proxy verifier achieves perfect unanimity (all correct or all incorrect), the sample is deemed "Easy-to-Verify" and dropped because it fails to discriminate verifier capability.

### Mechanism 2: Process-Aware Reward Prevents Reward Hacking
The reward function for RLVR is defined as R(x,y,a*) = I(O) · I(C), where I(O) indicates outcome correctness and I(C) indicates process consistency (as judged by a verifier). This logical AND ensures that a trajectory receives positive reward only if both the final answer is correct and the derivation is logically sound. Consequently, "lucky guess" trajectories (O=True, C=False) are rejected, preventing the policy from learning to produce flawed reasoning that coincidentally yields correct answers. The mechanism assumes the existence of a verifier capable of reliably assessing process consistency. If no such verifier exists or process labels are too noisy, the reward collapses to outcome-only, losing its benefit.

### Mechanism 3: PRIME Scores Predict RLVR Effectiveness
Verifiers are ranked by their Overall Accuracy on PRIME (which penalizes lucky guesses by requiring both correct outcome and sound process). Those with higher PRIME scores provide more accurate process-outcome supervision during RLVR, leading to policies that learn robust reasoning paths and achieve higher scores on held-out challenging benchmarks. The strong linear correlation (R² > 0.92) between a verifier's PRIME score and the downstream policy's average performance demonstrates that PRIME is a reliable predictor of verifier utility. The underlying assumption is that the skills required to judge process-outcome consistency on PRIME transfer directly to the skills needed to generate useful reward signals during RLVR on related STEM problems. If the distributions are misaligned, the correlation would weaken.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Raw STEM Corpus → Verifiability & Correctness Filter → Response Generator → Difficulty Filter → Expert Annotator → (PRIME Benchmark & Evaluation Harness). Parallel path: Selected Verifier (from Evaluation) → RLVR Trainer → Policy Model.

**Critical path:**  
Benchmark construction pipeline: Raw Corpus → Filter → Response Gen → Difficulty Filter → Expert Label → Benchmark.  
RLVR pipeline: Selected Verifier → RLVR Trainer → Policy Model (with reward from verifier's process-outcome judgment).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Cost vs. Quality: Expert labeling is expensive but ensures reliability for the final 2,530 samples.  
- Generality vs. Specificity: Filtering uses a single proxy verifier, potentially biasing the "hard" set toward that model's failure modes.  
- Metric Focus: Evaluation uses Overall label, deliberately ignoring pure outcome accuracy.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low inter-annotator agreement among experts on Overall labels.  
- Proxy verifier consensus scores cluster at 0 or 1 for most samples, indicating difficulty filtering failure.  
- No linear correlation (R² near 0) between PRIME scores and downstream RLVR gains.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate difficulty filtering: For 100 problems, generate 5 responses each, run a proxy verifier 8 times, and verify the consensus score distribution matches the "Hard-to-Verify" retention criterion (0 < consensus < 1).  
2. Lucky Guess Subset Test: Evaluate a held-out set of correct-outcome/flawed-process samples. A process-aware verifier should score low on Overall (since label=False) while an outcome-only verifier scores high.  
3. Correlation Sanity Check: Using 3-5 verifiers with known PRIME scores (e.g., CompassVerifier-32B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B), run minimal RLVR (10 steps) on a small WebInstruct-verified slice and check if higher PRIME scores correlate with initial downstream gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How can the process-aware RLVR paradigm be effectively scaled to larger language models (>100B parameters)?** The current experiments are limited to 8B/14B models; scaling may introduce instability, reward degradation, or prohibitive costs.  
- **Does training a specialized process-aware verifier from scratch yield better performance than repurposing generalist models?** The authors used existing high-performance models; it is unknown whether a tailored verifier would be more accurate, robust, or cost-effective.  
- **What explains the strong linear correlation (R² > 0.92) between verifier accuracy on PRIME and downstream RLVR performance?** The correlation could be incidental or driven by confounding factors like model size; the underlying mechanism is unclear.

## Limitations
- The core claims rely on unreported details: exact RLVR hyperparameters (learning rate, KL penalty), the source and preprocessing of the `WebInstruct-verified` dataset, and full evaluation protocols for downstream benchmarks.
- Potential bias from using a single proxy verifier (GPT-OSS-120B) for difficulty filtering may limit the generalizability of the "hard" sample set.
- Closed-source model scores in the paper are not reproducible without API access, hindering full verification of the reported verifier rankings.

- Mechanism 2 (Process-aware reward prevents reward hacking): Medium  
- Mechanism 3 (PRIME scores predict RLVR performance): High
