# A Kolmogorov-Arnold Surrogate Model for Chemical Equilibria: Application to Solid Solutions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15307
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15307
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** KANs reduced absolute error by 62% and relative error by 59% compared to MLPs for cement hydration, and achieved near 1×10⁻³ median errors for solid solutions with 87–93% computational speedup.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) as surrogate models to replace computationally expensive geochemical solvers (specifically GEM-Selektor) in reactive transport simulations. KANs employ learnable spline-based activation functions on network edges, in contrast to the fixed activations used in standard multilayer perceptrons (MLPs). The authors evaluate KANs on two geochemical benchmarks: cement hydration and radium incorporation into barium/strontium sulfate solid solutions. Results show that KANs significantly outperform MLPs in predictive accuracy while using fewer trainable parameters, and they accelerate evaluation by 87–93% relative to the reference solver. The study highlights the potential of KANs for accelerating large-scale reactive transport modeling, though challenges remain for dynamic integration and broader generalizability.

## Method Summary
Training data were generated by sampling input conditions (bulk compositions, temperature) via Sobol sequences and solving equilibrium with GEM-Selektor. Inputs and outputs were log-transformed and scaled (min-max or standardization). Two model families were compared: MLPs with fixed activations (e.g., Mish) and KANs with B-spline activations on each edge. Hyperparameters (depth, width, spline order, grid size) were selected via Bayesian optimization (Optuna) on validation sets. Models were trained with mean squared error (MSE) loss using the Adam optimizer and a learning rate scheduler. Performance was assessed on held-out test sets using RMSE and RRMSE, and evaluation speed was measured against GEM-Selektor on a fixed CPU.

## Key Results
- For cement hydration (50k samples), KANs reduced absolute error by 62% and relative error by 59% compared to MLPs, while using fewer trainable parameters.
- For binary and ternary radium-bearing sulfate solid solutions (262k–524k samples), KANs achieved median prediction errors near 1×10⁻³ with no individual output error exceeding 10%.
- Surrogate model evaluation was 87–93% faster than the reference GEM-Selektor solver.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Learnable spline activations enable adaptive nonlinear shaping
KANs apply a parameterized univariate function (B-spline) to each edge, allowing the network to tailor nonlinear responses per connection. In contrast, MLPs use identical fixed activations (e.g., SiLU) at every neuron. This edge-wise flexibility aligns with the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem, which suggests that compositions of univariate functions can approximate any continuous multivariate function. The paper demonstrates that for chemical equilibrium mappings, splines provide a more expressive basis than fixed functions, yielding higher accuracy with fewer parameters. Evidence: The abstract and Section 3.2 contrast the architectures; Table 2 shows KANs outperforming MLPs with equal or fewer parameters. Potential break condition: If the target function has sharp discontinuities or phase boundaries that require extremely fine spline grids, the approach may overfit or become unstable.

### Mechanism 2: Parameter efficiency through distributed representational capacity
By placing expressive univariate functions on edges, KANs distribute modeling capacity across connections. A single spline can capture complex local relationships, whereas an MLP neuron must first aggregate weighted inputs before a fixed nonlinearity, often requiring wider layers to achieve similar expressivity. This yields better parameter efficiency for the geochemical tasks. Evidence: The abstract states KANs achieve "higher accuracy with fewer trainable parameters"; Section 4.1 (Table 2) shows a KAN with ~1.5×10⁵ parameters outperforms an MLP with the same count, and a smaller KAN (5.6×10⁴) approaches MLP performance. Break condition: For very high-dimensional inputs (p >> 100), the number of edges may grow large, potentially exceeding MLP parameter counts if layer widths are not constrained.

### Mechanism 3: Heterogeneous sensitivity capture reduces outlier errors
Geochemical equilibrium responses exhibit variable sensitivity across the input domain (e.g., steep gradients near phase boundaries, flat regions elsewhere). KANs' edge-wise splines can adapt to this heterogeneity, learning strong interactions only in relevant subregions while maintaining simplicity elsewhere. This leads to more uniform error distribution and fewer high-magnitude outliers compared to MLPs. Evidence: Section 4.2 notes KANs have "fewer high-error predictions"; Section 4.3 and 4.4 report median errors near 1×10⁻³ and zero predictions exceeding 10% error for solid solutions. Break condition: If the response is globally linear or separable, KANs' added flexibility may introduce unnecessary variance, especially under noisy training data.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Theorem**
  - *Why needed here:* The KAN architecture is explicitly grounded in this theorem, which guarantees that any continuous multivariate function can be represented as a finite composition of continuous univariate functions and addition. Understanding this basis explains why placing learnable 1D functions on edges is a viable alternative to MLPs.
  - *Quick check:* Does the theorem guarantee that a deep network of spline-based univariate functions can approximate any multivariate function? (Answer: The theorem guarantees existence of *some* continuous univariate functions, not specifically splines; however, splines are dense in the space of continuous functions, so they can serve as a practical basis.)
- **Concept: Spline Interpolation & B-splines**
  - *Why needed here:* KAN activation functions are B-splines defined by degree and knot count. These hyperparameters control smoothness and flexibility, directly affecting accuracy vs. overfitting trade-offs.
  - *Quick check:* How does increasing spline degree affect representation of sharp derivatives? (Answer: Higher degree yields smoother splines with more continuous derivatives, potentially struggling with sharp cusps unless knot density is increased.)
- **Concept: Geochemical Equilibrium via Gibbs Energy Minimization**
  - *Why needed here:* The surrogate must predict outcomes (species concentrations, phase amounts) of Gibbs energy minimization given bulk composition and conditions (T, P). The mapping is highly nonlinear due to phase transitions, activity models, and solid-solution mixing.
  - *Quick check:* Why does temperature as an input increase surrogate difficulty? (Answer: Temperature affects equilibrium constants and activity coefficients nonlinearly, expanding input variation and requiring the model to learn thermodynamic relationships.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Inputs -> Preprocessing (log transform + min-max scaling) -> KAN Core (series of KANLayer modules) -> Output Layer (linear projection) -> Postprocessing (inverse transform)

### Critical Path
1. **Data generation**: Use GEM-Selektor with Sobol sampling over input ranges to produce input-output pairs (X, Y).
2. **Preprocess**: Apply log1p to inputs/outputs as needed, then scale to zero mean/unit variance or min-max.
3. **Model selection**: Run Optuna to search hyperparameters (hidden_layers, hidden_dim, spline_order, spline_grid_size) within bounds per case.
4. **Training**: Train with MSE loss using Adam, batch size ~192, and a learning rate scheduler (reduce by factor after plateau). Train up to 100–200 epochs.
5. **Evaluation**: Compute RMSE/RRMSE on test set; time 5k forward passes vs. GEM-Selektor.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Training time vs. inference speed**: KANs train 3–4× slower than MLPs but offer large speedups over the reference solver; acceptable if many evaluations are needed.
- **Spline complexity**: Higher spline order and grid size improve accuracy but increase parameters and training time. Paper uses order 8–10, grid 10–12 for radium cases.
- **Dataset size**: Larger datasets reduce error but require expensive GEM-Selektor calls. Paper shows error decreases with up to 524k points for ternary systems.
- **Parameter count vs. accuracy**: KANs need fewer parameters than MLPs for same accuracy; prefer smaller KAN if memory constrained.

### Failure Signatures
- **Training divergence**: Loss spikes to NaN. Mitigation: lower learning rate, increase batch size, clamp spline values.
- **High test errors (>10% on many outputs)**: Likely underfitting (increase hidden_dim/spline_order) or insufficient data (increase Sobol samples). Check if errors concentrate near phase boundaries.
- **Slow convergence (>100 epochs)**: May indicate coarse spline grid; try increasing grid_size.
- **Inconsistency across seeds**: Large variance suggests sensitivity to initialization; increase spline_order or hidden_dim.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate cement benchmark**: Use the 50k dataset from Zenodo with train/val/test split 40k/5k/5k. Train MLP (5 layers, 192 neurons, Mish) and KAN (5 layers, 40 neurons, order 8, grid 12). Verify RMSE reduction ~60% and RRMSE ~59% as in Tables 2–3.
2. **Dataset size scaling for mechanical mixing**: Train KANs on 2^15, 2^16, 2^17 point sets (32k, 64k, 131k). Plot median error vs. log2(N); confirm error decreases and high-error predictions drop toward zero.
3. **Binary SS with temperature**: Train KAN on 262k-point (Ba,Ra)SO4 dataset including temperature. Ensure all output RRMSE < 2e-3 and zero predictions >10% error. Then ablate temperature (remove T from inputs) and retrain; expect significant accuracy drop, confirming temperature's role.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Integration into reactive transport simulations**: How can KAN surrogates be used without violating mass conservation and accumulating errors over time? The paper only evaluated static predictions; dynamic integration remains untested.
- **Physics-informed training**: Can penalty terms for charge balance or direct Gibbs energy minimization enforce thermodynamic consistency during training? The authors propose but do not test such constraints.
- **Scalability of training time**: How does KAN training scale with larger datasets (e.g., millions of points) or higher-dimensional geochemical systems? The paper reports slower training than MLPs but only up to ~524k points.
- **Generalizability across geochemical systems**: Do KANs outperform MLPs on other common benchmarks (e.g., carbonate systems, clay sorption) beyond cement and sulfate cases? The study is domain-specific.
- **Error accumulation in iterative simulations**: Can KANs maintain low errors when embedded in reactive transport models where outputs feed into subsequent steps? The paper cautions that one-time accuracy does not guarantee stability over many iterations.

## Limitations
- Incomplete reporting of hyperparameters and data split seeds hinders exact replication.
- The thermodynamic solver configuration (GEM-Selektor version, database, tolerances) is not fully specified, potentially affecting target data.
- Results are limited to two geochemical application areas; generalizability to other systems (e.g., gas phases, redox) is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Accuracy & parameter efficiency:** Medium confidence (compelling comparisons but missing full hyperparameter details).
- **Robustness (low max error):** High confidence (clear metric: no predictions >10% error for solid solutions).
- **Speedup vs. GEM-Selektor:** High confidence (timing measurements are straightforward and consistent).
