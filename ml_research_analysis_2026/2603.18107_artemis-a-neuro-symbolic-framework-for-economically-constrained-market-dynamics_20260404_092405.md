# ARTEMIS: A Neuro Symbolic Framework for Economically Constrained Market Dynamics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18107
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18107
- **Reference count:** 34
- **Primary result:** ARTEMIS achieves state-of-the-art directional accuracy on financial forecasting tasks—64.96% on DSLOB (synthetic crash) and 96.0% on Time‑IMM (temperature)—while providing interpretable trading rules through a differentiable symbolic bottleneck.

## Executive Summary
ARTEMIS is a neuro‑symbolic framework designed to overcome the lack of interpretability and economic grounding in deep learning for finance. It combines a Laplace Neural Operator (LNO) encoder to process irregularly spaced market data, a neural stochastic differential equation (SDE) that models continuous‑time latent dynamics, and two physics‑informed regularizers—a Feynman‑Kac PDE residual that enforces local no‑arbitrage conditions and a market price of risk penalty that bounds the instantaneous Sharpe ratio. End‑to‑end training yields high directional accuracy while respecting fundamental economic principles. On four benchmark datasets, ARTEMIS sets new records: 64.96% on the synthetic crash dataset DSLOB and 96.0% on Time‑IMM. Ablation studies confirm that each component—especially the physics losses—contributes substantially to performance; removing both physics losses drops accuracy below random. The framework also integrates conformal prediction for uncertainty quantification and a differentiable symbolic bottleneck that distills complex dynamics into sparse, human‑readable formulas. A noted limitation is higher computational cost (~3× vs. LSTM) and underperformance on long‑sequence volatility‑focused tasks like Optiver, indicating boundary conditions for future refinement.

## Method Summary
The core architecture begins with a Laplace Neural Operator encoder that directly ingests irregular observations \(x(t_i)\) and outputs a continuous latent function \(z_{\text{enc}}(t)\). The value at \(t=0\) initializes a neural stochastic differential equation (SDE) (\(dz = \mu_\theta dt + \sigma_\phi dW\)), simulated via Euler‑Maruyama on a regular grid. The drift \(\mu_\theta\) and diffusion \(\sigma_\phi\) (factorized as \(L_\phi D_\phi\) for positive definiteness) are neural networks. Training minimizes a composite loss: forecast MSE plus three regularizers – (i) the Feynman‑Kac PDE residual (computed with an auxiliary network \(V_\psi\)) that penalises deviations from the no‑arbitrage PDE along the SDE path, (ii) a hinge‑style market price of risk penalty \(\max(0, \|\sigma^{-1}\mu\|^2 - \kappa^2)\) that bounds the instantaneous Sharpe ratio, and (iii) a consistency loss aligning SDE states with encoder outputs at the grid points. After convergence, the encoder and SDE are frozen and a differentiable symbolic bottleneck distills the mapping from inputs to predictions into a sparse linear combination of predefined basis functions (moving averages, ratios, differences, etc.) using a Gumbel‑Softmax relaxation and an \(L_1\) sparsity penalty. Finally, a conformal layer builds calibrated prediction intervals from residuals on a held‑out set, optionally feeding into a Kelly‑criterion portfolio allocator.

## Key Results
- State-of-the-art directional accuracy: 64.96% on DSLOB (synthetic crash), 96.0% on Time‑IMM (temperature); competitive on Jane Street but lower on Optiver (RankIC –0.0555) due to long sequences and volatility‑focused target.
- Ablation on DSLOB: removing the PDE loss reduces directional accuracy to 50.32%; removing both physics losses reduces it to 41.77% (below random), demonstrating the dramatic impact of economic regularisation.
- The symbolic bottleneck yields interpretable closed‑form rules with high fidelity; on validation sets the RankIC between neural and symbolic predictions exceeds 0.9, while sparsity (most weights near zero) is achieved via \(L_1\) regularisation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Physics‑informed regularisation** forces economically plausible latent dynamics. The Feynman‑Kac PDE residual penalises any SDE‑induced evolution of a learned derivative pricing function \(V_\psi\) that deviates from the martingale condition required by the Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing. The market price of risk (MPR) penalty bounds the squared instantaneous Sharpe ratio \(\|\sigma^{-1}\mu\|^2\) by a threshold \(\kappa^2\), preventing the model from fitting unrealistically profitable but spurious patterns. Ablation evidence on DSLOB shows that removing either component drastically harms directional accuracy, confirming that soft economic constraints guide learning toward robust, arbitrage‑free representations. A break condition arises if the latent dimensionality is too low or \(\kappa\) is set too restrictively, making the constraints unsatisfiable and hindering convergence.

**Differentiable symbolic bottleneck** distils the complex SDE‑based predictor into an interpretable, closed‑form expression. After pretraining, a layer with a predefined library of primitive features (e.g., moving averages, ratios, differences) learns sparse weights via a student‑teacher distillation loss and an \(L_1\) penalty. The Gumbel‑Softmax relaxation makes the discrete selection differentiable, enabling end‑to‑end learning of a compact formula that mimics the frozen neural model’s outputs. This bridges performance and interpretability without requiring manual rule extraction. Potential failure includes insufficient basis expressiveness or an improperly annealed temperature, which can lead to fidelity loss or non‑convergent selection.

**Laplace Neural Operator (LNO) encoder** processes irregularly sampled market data in continuous time, avoiding interpolation artefacts. The LNO defines a kernel integral \(z(t) = \int \kappa(t-s)x(s)ds + b(t)\), where the kernel is parameterised in the Laplace domain as a sum of rational functions (\(\hat{\kappa}(\omega) = \sum A_k/(\omega - \lambda_k)\)) for efficiency and causality. Given discrete irregular observations, the integral is approximated with a Riemann sum, producing a latent function evaluated on a regular grid for the SDE solver. This preserves the true temporal structure of asynchronous events like limit order book updates. The approach is particularly beneficial for high‑frequency financial data but adds overhead for already regular sequences; the kernel capacity is limited by the number of poles \(K\), and highly uneven observation gaps may bias the Riemann approximation.

## Foundational Learning

### Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs)
The latent dynamics are modeled as \(dz = \mu dt + \sigma dW\). Mastery of Itô calculus, the Euler‑Maruyama scheme, and the distinction between drift and diffusion is essential. *Quick check:* Why is the diffusion term \(\sigma_\phi\) parameterised as a product \(L_\phi D_\phi\), and what property does this guarantee for the MPR penalty?

**Answer:** The factorization \( \sigma_\phi = L_\phi D_\phi \) (with \( L_\phi \) lower triangular having positive diagonal entries and \( D_\phi \) diagonal with positive entries) ensures that \( \sigma_\phi \) is positive definite. This guarantees that the instantaneous Sharpe ratio \( \|\sigma^{-1}\mu\|^2 \) is well‑defined, because \( \sigma_\phi \) is invertible and its inverse is also positive definite. Without positive definiteness, \( \sigma_\phi \) could be singular, making the inverse unstable or undefined and causing the MPR penalty to be numerically ill‑posed. The positive‑definite factorization thus stabilises the training of the MPR regulariser.

### Feynman‑Kac Theorem & PDE Residuals
The no‑arbitrage constraint is enforced by penalising the residual of the PDE that a derivative price \(V\) should satisfy under the risk‑neutral measure derived from the learned SDE. *Quick check:* How does minimising the squared PDE residual encourage the drift \(\mu\) and diffusion \(\sigma\) to be economically plausible?

**Answer:** Minimising the squared PDE residual drives the auxiliary network \(V_\psi\) and the SDE parameters \(\mu, \sigma\) toward a consistent solution of the Feynman‑Kac relationship. This relationship mathematically expresses that \(V\) is a martingale under the risk‑neutral measure derived from the SDE. When the residual is zero, the dynamics of \(V\) along the SDE path exactly match the expectation under the risk‑neutral measure, which in turn requires that the drift and diffusion are arbitrage‑free (i.e., there exists an equivalent martingale measure). By penalising deviations, the optimisation indirectly forces \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) to satisfy the no‑arbitrage condition, making them economically plausible.

### Differentiable Symbolic Regression
The symbolic bottleneck uses a library of basis functions and a Gumbel‑Softmax relaxation to select a sparse linear combination that approximates the neural model’s output. *Quick check:* What is the purpose of the \(L_1\) penalty \(\|w\|_1\) in the distillation loss, and how does the Gumbel‑Softmax temperature \(\tau\) affect sparsity?

**Answer:** The \(L_1\) penalty on the symbolic weights \(w\) encourages sparsity, driving most weights to zero and yielding a compact, interpretable expression that uses only a few basis functions. The Gumbel‑Softmax relaxation provides a differentiable approximation to discrete weight selection; the temperature \(\tau\) controls the smoothness of the approximation. A high \(\tau\) yields near‑uniform soft weights (less sparse), while a low \(\tau\) approximates a one‑hot selection (sparser). During training, \(\tau\) is typically annealed from high to low, gradually enforcing sparsity while maintaining gradient flow.

### Conformal Prediction
This provides distribution‑free prediction intervals by calibrating quantiles on held‑out residuals; an adaptive rolling window accommodates non‑stationary financial data. *Quick check:* Why is an adaptive rolling window used for conformal quantiles in financial time series, and what coverage guarantee is lost as a result?

**Answer:** Financial time series are non‑stationary; the distribution of errors can shift over time due to market regime changes. An adaptive rolling window uses only the most recent residuals for calibration, making the quantile estimates more relevant to the current distribution. However, the standard conformal prediction guarantee (exact finite‑sample coverage under the assumption of exchangeability) relies on the calibration data being identically distributed to the test data. Using a rolling window breaks strict exchangeability because older residuals may come from a different distribution; thus, the guarantee becomes only approximate in practice, and coverage may degrade if the non‑stationarity is severe or the window size is poorly chosen.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
The framework comprises:
1. **Laplace Neural Operator (LNO) encoder** – maps irregular input function \(x(t)\) to a continuous latent function \(z_{\text{enc}}(t)\).
2. **Neural SDE dynamics** – simulates \(z_{\text{sde}}(t)\) from \(z_{\text{enc}}(0)\) using drift \(\mu_\theta\) and diffusion \(\sigma_\phi\) networks via Euler‑Maruyama.
3. **Physics‑informed losses** – compute the Feynman‑Kac PDE residual (with auxiliary network \(V_\psi\)) and market price of risk (MPR) hinge penalty along the trajectory.
4. **Consistency loss** – aligns \(z_{\text{sde}}(t_j)\) with \(z_{\text{enc}}(t_j)\) at regular grid points.
5. **Forecasting head** – linear layer on the terminal SDE state \(z_{\text{sde}}(T)\) to predict target \(\hat{y}\).
6. **Symbolic bottleneck** (two‑phase) – after pretraining, a differentiable symbolic layer with a predefined basis library learns sparse weights \(w\) to produce interpretable approximations of \(\hat{y}\) via distillation.
7. **Conformal layer** (post‑training) – builds calibrated prediction intervals using adaptive rolling‑window quantiles of calibration residuals.

Data flow: *Input* → LNO → \(z_{\text{enc}}(0)\) + grid → SDE simulation → losses → backpropagation; after pretraining: freeze nets → train symbolic layer → calibrate conformal intervals.

### Critical Path
Raw irregular data are fed into the LNO encoder, which outputs a continuous latent function. The latent value at time 0 initializes the SDE, and its values on a regular grid guide the Euler‑Maruyama simulation. At each step, the drift \(\mu_\theta\) and diffusion \(\sigma_\phi\) networks are evaluated. The SDE trajectory is used to compute four losses: the forecast MSE, the PDE residual (by differentiating \(V_\psi\) along the path), the MPR penalty (based on \(\sigma^{-1}\mu\)), and the consistency (squared distance between SDE and encoder states). All losses are weighted and summed, then backpropagated to update the LNO, SDE, \(V_\psi\), and forecast head. Once training converges, these networks are frozen and the symbolic bottleneck is trained to mimic the frozen model’s predictions using a student‑teacher loss plus \(L_1\) sparsity. Finally, a held‑out calibration set’s residuals are used to compute quantiles for conformal prediction intervals, optionally feeding into a Kelly‑criterion portfolio layer.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Directional vs. Point Accuracy:** The stochastic SDE and physics regularisation improve directional metrics (e.g., DirAcc) but typically worsen point‑wise errors like RMSE.
- **Interpretability vs. Performance:** The symbolic bottleneck yields human‑readable formulas but may slightly reduce accuracy if the fixed basis library cannot fully capture the neural model’s behavior.
- **Computational Cost:** SDE simulation (especially with backprop through Euler‑Maruyama) and PDE residual computation increase training time by ~3× compared to an LSTM baseline.

### Failure Signatures
- **SDE instability:** NaN or infinite values in latent trajectories during training, often caused by too large integration step size \(\Delta t\) or poorly scaled network outputs.
- **PDE loss stagnation:** The PDE residual does not decrease, suggesting the auxiliary network \(V_\psi\) lacks capacity, collocation sampling is inadequate, or the loss weight \(\lambda_1\) is too low.
- **Symbolic collapse:** All symbolic weights \(w\) become zero, indicating the \(L_1\) penalty \(\lambda_4\) is too high or the distillation loss is insufficient.
- **Conformal miscalibration:** Prediction intervals exhibit under‑coverage (e.g., nominal 90% intervals contain <85% of true values) because the calibration window does not reflect test‑time distribution shifts.

### First Three Experiments
1. **Pretraining sanity check** – Train the core model (LNO + SDE + forecast head) with only forecast and consistency losses on a small synthetic dataset. Verify that SDE trajectories remain smooth (no exploding norms) and that validation loss decreases steadily.
2. **Component ablation on DSLOB** – Starting from the pretrained model, sequentially disable (a) the PDE loss (\(\lambda_1=0\)), (b) the MPR loss (\(\lambda_2=0\)), and (c) both physics losses. Measure directional accuracy on the DSLOB test set; expect a drop from ~65% to ~50% (PDE removed) and to ~42% (both removed), confirming the contribution of the physics regularisation.
3. **Symbolic bottleneck distillation** – Freeze the fully pretrained model. Train the symbolic layer with distillation loss plus \(L_1\) sparsity, using Gumbel‑Softmax with an annealing temperature schedule. Monitor the sparsity of \(w\) (fraction below a small threshold) and the RankIC between the frozen model’s predictions and the symbolic predictions on a validation set; target RankIC >0.9 and >80% sparse weights.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Optiver performance and long‑sequence volatility tasks**  
The paper explicitly reports underperformance on the Optiver dataset (negative RankIC and 45.82% DirAcc) and attributes it to long sequence length, volatility‑focused target, and limited feature set. However, it does not propose or evaluate specific architectural or regularisation modifications to handle these conditions. Resolving this would require controlled experiments that isolate sequence length, target type, and feature dimensionality; test higher‑order SDE solvers, volatility‑specific physics constraints, hierarchical encoders, and multi‑task learning.

**Computational efficiency**  
ARTEMIS incurs ~3× longer training than LSTM due to SDE simulation and PDE backpropagation, yet no efficiency optimisations are explored. The relative cost of each component and whether approximations (adaptive SDE steps, sparse PDE collocation, model distillation) can reduce overhead without sacrificing accuracy remain unknown. Evidence would come from profiling runtime, benchmarking potential speed‑ups, and reporting the resulting speed‑accuracy trade‑off.

**Cost of interpretability**  
The symbolic bottleneck is a post‑hoc distillation; its impact on predictive accuracy is not quantified. The fixed basis library might be too restrictive, leading to an unmeasured accuracy‑interpretability trade‑off. What evidence would resolve this: direct comparison of the full neural model vs. the distilled symbolic model on directional metrics; ablations with end‑to‑end training of the symbolic layer and with expanded basis libraries; measuring fidelity (e.g., RankIC) versus accuracy across datasets.

**Generalisation beyond finance**  
Success on the non‑financial Time‑IMM dataset suggests the framework may apply to other domains with irregular sampling and underlying physical laws. However, the economic regularisers (no‑arbitrage PDE, Sharpe bound) are finance‑specific—it is unclear whether substituting domain‑appropriate constraints would yield similar benefits. To resolve, apply ARTEMIS to multiple physical‑science benchmarks (e.g., fluid dynamics, reaction‑diffusion) with the relevant PDE constraints and evaluate improvements in directional prediction and interpretability.

## Limitations
- Critical hyperparameters (latent dimension, loss weights, SDE steps) and auxiliary network \(V_\psi\) architecture are not fully specified, preventing exact replication.
- The synthetic DSLOB dataset used for key results is not publicly available, forcing reproducers to approximate its generation and potentially introducing subtle discrepancies.
- The model incurs high computational overhead (~3× training time vs. LSTM) and may not scale to very long sequences or real‑time applications; the symbolic bottleneck’s interpretability benefit may come at an unquantified accuracy cost.

## Confidence
- **Physics‑informed regularisation substantially improves directional accuracy:** High confidence. The ablation study on DSLOB demonstrates a dramatic drop in directional accuracy from 64.96% to 50.32% when the PDE loss is removed, and to 41.77% when both physics losses are removed. These precisely quantified, large‑magnitude effects strongly support the claim.
- **Symbolic bottleneck yields interpretable rules with minimal accuracy loss:** Medium confidence. The paper reports a high RankIC (>0.9) between neural and symbolic predictions, but does not quantify the absolute directional accuracy of the symbolic model or perform an ablation comparing it to the full neural model. The potential accuracy trade‑off remains untested; fidelity depends on the expressiveness of the fixed basis library.
- **LNO encoder effectively handles irregular sampling:** Low confidence. While the LNO is designed for irregular data, the paper does not compare against a baseline that first resamples to a regular grid (e.g., via interpolation). The claimed advantage is theoretical and not validated empirically in the experiments; any superiority must be inferred from design rather than direct measurement.
