# Optimized Certainty Equivalent Risk-Controlling Prediction Sets
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13660
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13660
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: OCE-RCPS provides high-probability guarantees on OCE risks (CVaR, entropic); on polyp segmentation it achieves average satisfaction rates 0.83 (CVaR) and 0.93 (entropic) with α=0.2, δ=0.2, significantly outperforming baselines (<0.65).

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Optimized Certainty Equivalent Risk-Controlling Prediction Sets (OCE‑RCPS), a framework for constructing prediction sets with high‑probability guarantees on tail risk measures such as Conditional Value‑at‑Risk (CVaR) and entropic risk. By leveraging upper confidence bounds from the Waudby‑Smith and Ramdas sequential testing procedure, OCE‑RCPS selects a hyperparameter λ that ensures the optimized certainty equivalent (OCE) risk of the resulting prediction sets does not exceed a user‑specified threshold α with probability at least 1−δ. The method decouples the optimization of the risk‑aversion parameter t on a held‑out dataset from the calibration of λ using an independent dataset, preserving the validity of the probabilistic guarantee while improving efficiency.

Experiments on a large polyp segmentation dataset (n=1781) demonstrate that OCE‑RCPS achieves high satisfaction rates—0.83 for CVaR and 0.93 for entropic risk when α=0.2, δ=0.2—while a baseline (OCE‑CRC) fails to meet the target (0.65, 0.58). OCE‑RCPS maintains reliable coverage across a range of δ values (0.6–0.9), albeit with a moderate increase in prediction set size to uphold the guarantee. These results establish OCE‑RCPS as a robust tool for risk‑aware prediction in safety‑sensitive applications.

## Method Summary
OCE‑RCPS constructs prediction sets Γ_λ(x) that are monotonic in λ (e.g., via thresholding a scoring function). The core idea is to control the OCE risk, defined as ROCE(λ)=inf_t R(λ,t) where R(λ,t)=t+(1/|D|)∑ϕ(ℓ_i−t) for a bounded loss ℓ and convex transformation ϕ. To select λ satisfying Pr[ROCE(λ)≤α]≥1−δ, the method first optimizes the auxiliary parameter t* on a separate dataset D_opt by minimizing the empirical R(λ,t) for a candidate λ. Then, on an independent calibration set D_cal, it applies the Waudby‑Smith and Ramdas sequential upper confidence bound (UCB) to derive a valid high‑probability upper bound ˆR⁺(λ,t*,δ) on the surrogate risk R(λ,t*) for each λ. Exploiting the monotonicity of the sets and loss, OCE‑RCPS selects the smallest λ̂ such that ˆR⁺(λ′,t*,δ)≤α for all λ′≥λ̂. This selection rule guarantees that the final predictor Γ_λ̂(x) meets the risk constraint with probability at least 1−δ.

The method is instantiated for false negative rate (FNR) loss in medical segmentation, using a pre‑trained PraNet model to obtain pixel‑wise scores and constructing Γ_λ(x) as the set of pixels with score ≥ 1−λ. The loss ℓ(y,Γ_λ) is 1−|y∩Γ_λ|/|y|, which is bounded by 1. The WSR UCB dynamically updates a multiplicative process over the calibration examples, yielding a data‑dependent bound that is both valid and relatively tight. By decoupling t* optimization (D_opt) from λ selection (D_cal), OCE‑RCPS avoids double‑dipping and retains theoretical soundness while improving set efficiency.

## Key Results
- OCE‑RCPS achieves average satisfaction rates 0.83 (CVaR) and 0.93 (entropic risk) with α=0.2, δ=0.2 on the polyp dataset; OCE‑CRC baseline fails (0.65, 0.58).
- Across δ∈[0.6,0.9], OCE‑RCPS consistently meets the 1−δ coverage target, whereas baselines deteriorate.
- Prediction set sizes increase moderately compared to uncontrolled methods but remain within acceptable bounds for practical use.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Valid high‑probability UCB on surrogate risk
**Claim:** OCE‑RCPS provides a high‑probability upper bound on the OCE risk ROCE(λ) by constructing a Waudby‑Smith and Ramdas (WSR) upper confidence bound (UCB) on the surrogate risk R(λ,t) for a fixed t.
- **Mechanism:** For a chosen parameter t, the method computes an empirical estimate ˆR_cal(λ,t) on the calibration data D_cal. It then applies the WSR sequential testing algorithm (Eq. 12–13) to derive a data‑dependent UCB ˆR⁺(λ,t,δ) that satisfies Pr[R(λ,t) ≤ ˆR⁺(λ,t,δ)] ≥ 1−δ. Since ROCE(λ) ≤ R(λ,t) always holds (Eq. 11), this UCB also validly bounds ROCE(λ) with the same probability (Lemma 1).
- **Core assumption:** The WSR procedure assumes i.i.d. calibration data and bounded loss values ℓ(y,Γ_λ(x)) (≤1 for FNR). Boundedness is stated in the paper (Page 3).
- **Evidence:** Abstract emphasizes UCBs for reliability; Section IV.B derives ˆR⁺ and the guarantee (Eq. 14); monotonicity conditions from Section II underpin the logic.
- **Break condition:** Guarantee fails if data are not i.i.d. or loss is unbounded without normalization.

### Mechanism 2: Monotonic selection guarantees risk control
**Claim:** The selection rule λ̂ = inf { λ : ˆR⁺(λ′,δ,t) ≤ α for all λ′ ≥ λ } (Eq. 16) ensures that the final prediction set Γ_λ̂(x) satisfies ROCE(λ̂) ≤ α with probability ≥ 1−δ.
- **Mechanism:** The rule finds the smallest λ such that the UCB on R(λ′,t) (and thus on ROCE(λ′)) is below α for all larger λ′. Combined with the pointwise UCB guarantee, this yields that ROCE(λ̂) ≤ α holds with high probability over the calibration draw (Theorem 1). The monotonicity of Γ_λ(x) (Eq. 2) and the loss monotonicity in set inclusion (Eq. 3) are essential.
- **Core assumption:** Prediction sets must be monotonic in λ (Eq. 2) and loss must be monotonic in inclusion (Eq. 3). These are required in Section II.
- **Evidence:** Section II defines monotonicity; Section IV.C states and sketches Theorem 1 proof; abstract mentions guarantees for miscoverage and FNR.
- **Break condition:** Violating monotonicity (non‑nested sets or non‑monotonic loss) breaks the guarantee.

### Mechanism 3: Optimizing t on held‑out data improves efficiency
**Claim:** Optimizing the auxiliary parameter t on a separate held‑out dataset D_opt (Eq. 8) improves the tightness (efficiency) of the final prediction set Γ_λ̂(x) without compromising the high‑probability guarantee.
- **Mechanism:** The OCE risk ROCE(λ)=inf_t R(λ,t) is defined via an infimum. A poor fixed t yields a loose upper bound R(λ,t), leading to conservative (large) sets. By selecting t* that minimizes the empirical estimate ˆR_opt(λ,t) on independent data D_opt, the surrogate bound becomes tighter, allowing the UCB‑based selection to pick a smaller λ while still satisfying ˆR⁺(λ′,δ,t*) ≤ α. The independence of D_opt from D_cal preserves the validity of the final UCB argument.
- **Core assumption:** D_opt must be independent of D_cal to avoid double‑dipping; Section IV.B explicitly requires this.
- **Evidence:** Section IV.B states the necessity of independence; Section V.A reports splits (|D_opt|=200, |D_cal|=800); the methodology implements separate optimization and calibration phases.
- **Break condition:** Using overlapping data for D_opt and D_cal invalidates the theoretical guarantee. Overfitting t* to D_opt could degrade efficiency.

  *Why needed:* OCE‑RCPS extends RCPS, which itself extends standard CP from marginal coverage to controlling expected loss with high probability. Understanding RCPS is prerequisite for grasping why controlling OCE risk (a tail risk) with high probability is novel and necessary for safety‑critical applications.

- **Concept: Optimized Certainty Equivalent (OCE) Risk Measures**  
  *Why needed:* OCE‑RCPS targets OCE risks (e.g., CVaR, entropic risk) via the family ROCE(λ)=inf_t E[ϕ(ℓ−t)]. One must understand OCE to see why controlling only expected risk (as in RCPS) is insufficient for tail risk sensitivity and how t acts as a risk‑aversion dial.  
  *Quick check:* Why is controlling the OCE risk (e.g., CVaR) considered more safety‑critical than controlling the average risk?

- **Concept: Sequential Testing & Upper Confidence Bounds (UCBs)**  
  *Why needed:* The core technical tool is the WSR UCB, which provides a valid, anytime‑valid high‑probability upper bound on a stochastic process (here R(λ,t)) from sequential data. This is the engine that converts the calibration sample into a guarantee.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Pretrained model → scoring function s(y|x) → (with λ) Γ_λ(x) → loss ℓ(y,Γ_λ) → data split → D_opt → t optimiser → t*; D_cal → WSR UCB engine (using t*) → ˆR⁺(λ,t*,δ) for all λ → λ selector → λ̂ → predictor Γ_λ̂(x)

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Split available labeled data into D_opt (size 200) and D_cal (size 800) independently.  
  2. On D_opt, for a grid of t values, compute empirical R(λ,t) for a grid of λ (or a candidate λ); choose t* that minimizes ˆR_opt(λ,t).  
  3. On D_cal, using t* and the WSR method, compute the UCB ˆR⁺(λ,t*,δ) for every λ in the grid (by iterating through examples and updating the multiplicative process).  
  4. Find the smallest λ̂ such that ˆR⁺(λ′,t*,δ) ≤ α for all λ′ ≥ λ̂ (checking monotonicity).  
  5. Deploy the predictor that outputs Γ_λ̂(x) = {y: s(y|x) ≥ 1−λ̂}.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Set size vs. guarantee strength:* Tighter risk control (smaller α or higher 1−δ) forces larger λ̂ and thus larger sets.  
  - *t optimization scope:* Searching t over a wider range may find a tighter surrogate but increases compute and risk of overfitting D_opt.  
  - *UCB tightness:* The WSR bound is valid but may be conservative; alternative UCBs could be substituted but would require re‑proving validity.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *Symptom:* Empirical satisfaction rate falls significantly below 1−δ.  
    *Likely causes:* (i) D_opt and D_cal not independent (data leakage) invalidating t*; (ii) violation of monotonicity conditions (Eq. 2,3); (iii) unbounded loss without clipping, breaking WSR assumptions.  
  - *Symptom:* Prediction sets are excessively large.  
    *Likely causes:* Overly conservative UCB due to poor η_j schedule; t* too large from overfitting D_opt.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **UCB coverage validation:** Using synthetic bounded loss data, run many trials of D_cal sampling for fixed λ,t. Verify that Pr[R(λ,t) ≤ ˆR⁺(λ,t,δ)] ≈ 1−δ across several δ levels.  
  2. **t* optimization vs. fixed t:** Compare OCE‑RCPS with t* from Eq. 8 against using a heuristic fixed t (e.g., median of training losses). Measure median relative set size at same (α,δ); expect t* to yield smaller sets.  
  3. **Monotonicity stress test:** Implement a non‑monotonic Γ_λ (e.g., selecting a random λ% of pixels). Run OCE‑RCPS and observe that the empirical violation rate exceeds δ, confirming the necessity of monotonicity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How can OCE‑RCPS be extended to handle distribution shifts between calibration and test data?  
  *Basis:* The conclusion states "future directions include extending OCE‑RCPS to distribution‑shift settings". It remains unresolved because current theory assumes i.i.d. data; shifts may violate assumptions.

- **Question 2:** How effective is OCE‑RCPS in other safety‑critical domains like autonomous driving?  
  *Basis:* Conclusion mentions applying the framework to domains such as autonomous driving. It is unresolved; the paper only evaluates medical segmentation, and other domains have different data types, losses, and constraints.

- **Question 3:** Does the choice of upper confidence bound (WSR UCB) affect OCE‑RCPS efficiency, and can alternative UCBs improve prediction set size?  
  *Basis:* The paper adopts the WSR UCB without comparison; alternative UCBs (e.g., Hoeffding, Bernstein) might yield tighter bounds and smaller sets.

- **Question 4:** How sensitive is OCE‑RCPS to the split between optimization and calibration datasets?  
  *Basis:* The paper uses fixed split sizes (|D_opt|=200, |D_cal|=800) without sensitivity analysis. The trade‑off between accurate t* estimation and tight UCB construction remains unexplored.

## Limitations
- The exact procedure for selecting t* in Eq. 8 is ambiguous; it is unclear whether t* is optimized per λ or globally.
- Details of the WSR UCB’s "online Newton step scheme" for the η_j schedule are missing, hindering exact replication.
- Evaluation is limited to a single dataset and model; generalizability to other tasks and domains is unknown.

## Confidence
- Theoretical guarantee: High (proof aligns with WSR bound; assumptions clear)
- Empirical results: Medium (single dataset, unclear t* optimization, potential overfitting)
- Robustness across δ: Medium (only a narrow range tested, moderate set‑size increase not fully explained)
