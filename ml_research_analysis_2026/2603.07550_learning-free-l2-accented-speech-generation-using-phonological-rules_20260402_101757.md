# Learning-free L2-Accented Speech Generation using Phonological Rules
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.07550
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07550
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Applying phonological rules to American English phonemes and using a target-language speaker embedding in a pretrained multilingual TTS model yields a significant accent shift (e.g., Indian-accented probability from 58.9% to 86.4%) while maintaining speech quality (UTMOS ~4.1–4.3).

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a learning-free method for generating L2-accented speech without retraining. By applying deterministic phonological transformation rules to American English phoneme sequences and conditioning a pretrained multilingual TTS model (Kokoro-82M) on a speaker embedding from the target L1 (Spanish or Hindi), the approach achieves explicit phoneme-level accent control. The rules capture systematic L1 interference at the segmental level, while the speaker embedding injects prosodic and rhythmic characteristics. Evaluations show accent strength increases substantially (target accent probability rises to 51.6% for Spanish and 86.4% for Indian), human listeners rate accent accuracy at 75.7%, and speech quality remains stable. Ablation studies reveal that preserving natural phoneme durations (i.e., not forcing alignment to American English timing) further enhances accent perception, especially for Indian-accented speech.

## Method Summary
The method processes input American English text through a frontend that produces US phonemes (IPA). A rule engine then applies L1-specific phonological transformations to these phonemes, producing a target-accent variant (Spanish or Indian). For synthesis, the transformed phoneme sequence is fed into the pretrained multilingual TTS model Kokoro-82M along with a speaker embedding extracted from recordings of a native speaker of the target L1. The model generates mel-spectrograms which are converted to waveform by a vocoder. An optional duration alignment step (forcing US durations onto the transformed sequence) is ablated to assess rhythmic contributions. The entire pipeline requires no model retraining or accented training data.

## Key Results
- Vox-Profile accent classification shows target accent probability increases from 26.6% (speaker embedding alone) to 51.6% (with rules) for Spanish, and from 58.9% to 86.4% for Indian.
- Human evaluation reports 75.7% perceived accent accuracy for both accents when rules are applied.
- Speech quality metrics (UTMOS ~4.1–4.3) remain stable relative to baseline, while ASR-based WER/CER increase due to inherent accent bias in the ASR system.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Applying L1-specific phonological transformation rules to American English phoneme sequences systematically converts them into target-accent (Spanish or Indian) variants at the segmental level.
- **Mechanism:** Deterministic substitution, insertion, or deletion operations on IPA symbols based on documented L1 phonotactic constraints (e.g., mapping English /v/ to Spanish /b/ or Indian /w/, epenthesizing /s/ clusters for Spanish). These rules modify the canonical pronunciation representation before synthesis.
- **Core assumption:** Accented speech arises from predictable, rule-governed phonological interference from the speaker’s L1, which can be captured by discrete phoneme mappings without modeling gradual or probabilistic deviations.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract and Section 2.1 provide rule design details; Tables 1–2 show rule application examples; neighboring work on accent disentanglement supports plausibility.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Conditioning the pretrained multilingual TTS model on a speaker embedding from the target L1 injects accent-specific prosodic, rhythmic, and timbral characteristics, complementing the segmental modifications.
- **Mechanism:** The embedding, extracted from native L1 recordings, carries latent information about L1-influenced rhythm, intonation, and voice quality. The model fuses the target pronunciation (from rules) with L1-derived prosody to generate acoustics.
- **Core assumption:** Speaker embeddings in multilingual TTS models encode language and accent information orthogonally to phoneme content, allowing cross-lingual accent transfer when paired with non-native phoneme sequences.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract, Section 2.2, and related literature on cross-lingual speaker embeddings.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Preserving natural phoneme duration patterns (i.e., not force-aligning to American English timing) strengthens perceived accent, particularly for syllable-timed L1 influences (e.g., Indian English), by allowing rhythmic transfer.
- **Mechanism:** The TTS model’s duration predictor generates phone durations conditioned on the phoneme sequence and speaker embedding. When the phoneme sequence is accented, the predicted durations naturally reflect the target L1’s rhythm. Forcing alignment overrides this, diminishing accent perception.
- **Core assumption:** L2 accent perception includes rhythmic transfer from L1, realized through durational patterns, which can be controlled via the duration modeling pathway.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract, Section 2.3, and Table 3 (higher Indian accent probability without alignment).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Phonological rule formalism (feature geometry, context-sensitive mappings)**  
  *Why needed:* The entire transformation depends on correctly defining and applying context-dependent IPA substitutions (e.g., word-initial /v/ → /b/ for Spanish). Misapplication could produce non-linguistic outputs.  
  *Quick check:* For the Spanish rule “Initial Consonant Substitution /v/ → /b/”, would this apply to “very” (/ˈvɛri/) in all contexts? Intervocalically it may not apply.

- **Concept: Multilingual TTS conditioning architecture (text → phonemes → speaker embedding → acoustic model)**  
  *Why needed:* Understanding how phoneme sequence and speaker embedding jointly control output is crucial to predict how modifications affect accent; the mechanism relies on the model’s ability to decouple content and speaker/accent.  
  *Quick check:* If you input Spanish phonemes with an American English speaker embedding, what accent would you expect the Kokoro-82M model to produce, and why?

- **Concept: L1 phonological interference patterns (specific to Spanish and Indo-Aryan languages)**  
  *Why needed:* The rule sets are derived from documented L1→L2 transfer effects (e.g., Spanish speakers lack /θ/, Indian languages have retroflexes). Without this linguistic knowledge, rules would be arbitrary and unlikely to produce recognizable accents.  
  *Quick check:* Why does the Indian rule set include “Dentalization of Fricatives /T, D, ð/ → /t̪, d̪, l̪/”? This reflects the absence of dental fricatives in many Indian languages and their replacement with dental stops/laterals.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Front-end text processor -> Phonological rule engine -> Duration controller (optional) -> Multilingual TTS backbone (Kokoro-82M) -> Speaker embedding bank

Critical path: Input text -> US phonemes -> apply accent rules -> (optional duration alignment) -> feed (phonemes + target L1 speaker embedding) to TTS -> accented speech.

Design tradeoffs:
- Rule coverage vs. complexity: More rules improve accent shift but risk over-constraining or non-words; Rule 5 (Vowel Simplification) most impactful for Spanish, Rule 1 (Retroflexion) for Indian.
- No retraining vs. limited control: Zero data/training cost, but relies on pretrained model’s latent accent knowledge; insufficient if model hasn’t learned strong L1 accent characteristics.
- Natural timing vs. intelligibility: Preserving natural durations boosts accent perception but may increase ASR WER; alignment yields more American-like timing but weaker accent.

Failure signatures:
- Low target-accent probability in Vox-Profile (<70% for IN, <50% for SP) despite rule application.
- High WER/CER not accompanied by accent shift (distortion, not accent).
- Human evaluation: speech perceived as “American” or “incomprehensible” rather than target-accented.
- Using only speaker embedding without rules yields low accent probability (e.g., SP: 3.7%, IN: 58.9%), confirming embedding alone insufficient.

First three experiments:
1. **Baseline establishment**: Synthesize with US speaker embedding only (no rules) to measure native accent probability and quality (UTMOS ~4.38, WER ~3.42). This shows the model’s default output.
2. **Rule application test**: Apply full rule set for SP or IN with corresponding speaker embedding. Measure accent probability shift (e.g., IN: 58.9% → 86.4%) and quality stability (UTMOS ~4.16–4.25). Validates core mechanism.
3. **Duration alignment ablation**: For IN condition, compare with/without phoneme-level duration alignment. Expect higher IN probability without alignment (93.1% vs. 86.4%, Table 3). Validates rhythmic contribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can accent-specific evaluation be performed without relying on proxy labels in existing accent classification systems like Vox-Profile?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors use Vox-Profile with "Romance" and "South Asia" as proxies for Spanish- and Indian-accented English because the system lacks explicit labels for these accents.
- **Why unresolved:** Proxy labels may not accurately capture the specific phonological features of the target accents, potentially conflating different accent characteristics and limiting precise assessment.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a dedicated accent classification model trained on labeled Spanish- and Indian-accented English speech, or creation of a benchmark dataset with ground-truth accent annotations for evaluation.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent can the proposed phonological rule framework generalize to L2 accents beyond Spanish and Indian English, such as Mandarin- or Arabic-accented English?
- **Basis in paper:** The study focuses only on two L2 accents derived from specific L1 phonological systems, leaving untested the applicability of the rule-based approach to other L1-L2 pairs with different phonotactic constraints.
- **Why unresolved:** Different L1 languages influence L2 pronunciation in distinct ways; rules derived from Spanish and Hindi may not transfer directly to other language families without modification, and the paper does not explore this boundary.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic replication of the methodology for additional L2 accents, with rule sets designed based on the L1 phonology, followed by objective and subjective evaluations to measure effectiveness.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can explicit modeling of L1-specific rhythmic patterns (e.g., syllable-timing vs. stress-timing) enhance the perceived authenticity of synthesized L2-accented speech beyond current duration alignment experiments?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper analyzes the effect of removing duration alignment to preserve timing but does not incorporate formal rhythmic models that encode L1-specific temporal patterns; the observation that Indian-accented speech benefits from non-aligned timing suggests rhythmic transfer is significant but not explicitly modeled.
- **Why unresolved:** Rhythmic differences are noted as influential, but the current approach merely preserves or removes duration alignment rather than actively modeling and injecting L1 rhythmic templates into the synthesis process.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Designing rhythmic transformation rules that modify phoneme durations based on L1 rhythmic class (e.g., applying syllable-timing patterns for Hindi-influenced English) and comparing perceived accent strength and naturalness against the baseline duration alignment method.

## Limitations
- **Phoneme set mismatch:** The paper uses IPA symbols in rules, but Kokoro-82M's exact input phoneme alphabet is undisclosed. A mismapping could break rule application entirely.
- **Rule coverage & generalizability:** Fixed rule sets may not capture all L1 interference patterns (e.g., vowel quality shifts, connected speech processes). Their effectiveness for broader speaker populations or other accents is untested.
- **Speaker embedding transfer limits:** The assumption that a Spanish/Hindi speaker embedding cleanly transfers L1 prosody to English is not probed; it may instead encode L1 language characteristics that interfere with English synthesis.

## Next Checks
1. **Verify phoneme-level implementation:** Replicate the exact IPA transformations from Table 1 for a held-out word list, then inspect Kokoro-82M's inference logs or use a phoneme recognizer on its outputs to confirm the model actually receives the intended transformed phoneme sequence.
2. **Probe speaker embedding transfer:** Synthesize Spanish (or Hindi) text using a Spanish/Hindi speaker embedding and US phonemes (no rules). Measure accent probability. If it remains high (>70%), the embedding alone may be sufficient, contradicting the paper's "embedding alone insufficient" finding.
3. **Isolate rhythmic effect quantitatively:** For Indian-accented outputs (rules + target embedding), extract fundamental frequency (F0) and phone duration statistics. Correlate these with human perception scores of "accent strength" and with Vox-Profile probability. A strong correlation would validate that preserved timing drives the perceptual effect beyond segmental changes alone.
