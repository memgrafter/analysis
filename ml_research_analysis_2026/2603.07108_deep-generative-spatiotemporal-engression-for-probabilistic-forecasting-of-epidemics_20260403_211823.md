# Deep Generative Spatiotemporal Engression for Probabilistic Forecasting of Epidemics

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.07108  
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07108  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Deep generative spatiotemporal engression (MVEN, GCEN, STEN) with pre‑additive noise injection yields state‑of‑the‑art probabilistic forecasts for epidemics, backed by theoretical guarantees and explainable spatial contributions.

## Executive Summary
Probabilistic forecasting of epidemics in space and time is essential for public health decision‑making, yet traditional point forecasts fail to quantify uncertainty. This paper introduces deep generative spatiotemporal engression frameworks—MVEN, GCEN, and STEN—that address this gap by integrating pre‑additive noise injection into lightweight LSTM‑based architectures. The noise, added to either raw inputs or spatial embeddings, enables the model to generate an ensemble of forecasts that approximates the full predictive distribution. The frameworks are evaluated on six diverse epidemic datasets (tuberculosis, influenza, COVID‑19, dengue, chickenpox) and consistently outperform state‑of‑the‑art temporal and spatiotemporal benchmarks (e.g., DeepAR, GpGp) in both point accuracy and probabilistic metrics such as CRPS and Pinball loss, while producing well‑calibrated prediction intervals. The authors provide theoretical guarantees of geometric ergodicity and asymptotic stationarity, ensuring long‑term stability of closed‑loop forecasts. Moreover, the STEN model offers explainability by quantifying the contributions of spatial lags. The approach is computationally efficient, validated for low‑frequency data, and the code is publicly available.

## Method Summary
The proposed method builds on engression: a noise tensor η is added to covariates before a nonlinear transformation. Three variants are presented:  
- **MVEN:** no spatial component; noise added to raw historical incidence.  
- **GCEN:** spatial module uses graph convolution with a static adjacency matrix derived from Haversine distances; noise is injected into the graph‑convolved embeddings.  
- **STEN:** spatial module computes multi‑scale neighborhood influences via precomputed inverse‑distance weight matrices, combines them with learned linear transforms, applies ReLU, then adds noise.  

All variants share a configurable multi‑layer LSTM that processes the perturbed sequence and a final dense layer that outputs a q‑step forecast for all locations. Training minimizes the **energy score loss**, a proper scoring rule that balances median accuracy and ensemble sharpness. For each batch, a tiny ensemble (M=2) is generated by sampling independent noise tensors; the gradient of the energy score w.r.t. this ensemble drives backpropagation. Hyperparameters (embedding dimension, LSTM hidden size, depth, dropout, learning rate, and for STEN also maximum lag L and decay exponent α) are tuned per dataset and horizon via Optuna (100 trials) to minimize validation CRPS. Data are split chronologically with horizon‑specific validation lengths; the final model is trained on the union of training and validation sets. At inference, a large ensemble (M=100) is produced to estimate the predictive distribution, yielding point forecasts (median) and quantiles (e.g., 95% PI). Models are compared against a broad set of baselines (DeepAR, MAF, Transformer, STGCN, DiffSTG, etc.) using point metrics (SMAPE, MAE, RMSE) and probabilistic metrics (CRPS, Pinball, Winkler score), with calibration assessed via probability integral transform (PIT) histograms. The paper also establishes theoretical properties of spatiotemporal engression, proving geometric ergodicity and asymptotic stationarity under mild assumptions (bounded activations, finite noise moments, contractive forget gate), and confirms empirically that closed‑loop simulations yield stationary trajectories with exponential decay of coupling.

## Key Results
- Consistent state‑of‑the‑art performance: The models surpass strong temporal and spatiotemporal baselines on all six epidemic datasets in both point and probabilistic metrics (CRPS, Pinball), with particularly large gains on long‑horizon forecasts.
- Reliable uncertainty quantification: Calibrated prediction intervals (uniform PIT) and superior Winkler scores demonstrate that the ensemble‑based approach yields well‑calibrated, sharp predictive distributions.
- Theoretical and practical stability: Proofs of geometric ergodicity and empirical stationarity (>92% KPSS pass) guarantee non‑explosive closed‑loop dynamics, while STEN’s explainable spatial lags offer insights into disease spread.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Pre‑additive noise injection (engression) enables model‑intrinsic probabilistic forecasting by transforming a simple noise distribution through learned nonlinear spatial‑temporal mappings, producing diverse, dynamically plausible forecast ensembles.
- **Mechanism**: In training, a noise tensor η is sampled from a pre‑defined distribution (e.g., standard Gaussian) and additively combined with either raw input sequences (MVEN) or spatial embeddings (GCEN/STEN). The perturbed input Z′ = Z + η is propagated through LSTM layers. The final dense layer outputs a single forecast trajectory. This process is repeated M times with independent noise samples during both training (minimizing energy score loss) and inference (to generate an ensemble). The energy score loss (Eq. 3) balances accuracy (pulling ensemble median toward ground truth) and sharpness (spreading ensemble members apart), allowing the model to approximate the full predictive distribution.
- **Core assumption**: The spatiotemporal data generating process can be represented as a nonlinear transformation of an input that itself incorporates latent stochasticity, with the injected noise distribution sufficiently rich to cover residual uncertainty.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "integrate pre‑additive noise injection into lightweight LSTM‑based architectures, enabling intrinsic uncertainty quantification through ensemble sampling."
  - [section 3]: "The engression framework… assumes that noise η is added directly to the covariates X before they are passed through a nonlinear transformation g, represented as Y=g(X+η)."
  - [corpus]: Neighbors like EnTransformer and RIPCN also explore deep generative architectures for uncertainty, but engression’s pre‑additive formulation is a distinct mechanism for endogenous uncertainty.
- **Break condition**: If the noise distribution does not match the true residual distribution (e.g., heavy‑tailed series with Gaussian noise), calibration deteriorates. Evidenced by S‑shaped PIT curves (overconfidence) or excessively wide intervals, indicating mismatch in learned heteroscedasticity.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Spatial embedding modules (graph convolutions in GCEN, STAR‑layer in STEN) transform raw observations into locally‑informed representations that capture spatial dependencies, improving forecast accuracy by leveraging known diffusion patterns.
- **Mechanism**: GCEN applies graph convolution operations using a static adjacency matrix A (derived from Haversine distances with a Gaussian kernel threshold). Each node aggregates features from its neighborhood. STEN computes multi‑scale spatial lags W^l Y_t (l=0..L) via a precomputed inverse‑distance weights matrix, then combines them with learnable linear matrices Φ_l. The resulting embedding Z_t encodes both the current state and the weighted influence of neighbors at different lags.
- **Core assumption**: The spatial connectivity matrix (A or W) accurately reflects the disease transmission network; nonlinear transformations can effectively capture spatial heterogeneity.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.1]: For GCEN: "graph convolution operations that propagate and transform node features across the graph… hi,(k)_t = f(k)_t( Θ(k)_t mean_{j∈N(i)} hj,(k-1)_t + B^(k)_t hi,(k-1)_t )."
  - [section 4.2]: For STEN: " eZ_τ = ReLU( sum_{l=0}^L (W^l Y_τ) Φ^l )… This allows the model to learn the relative importance of information from different spatial scales."
  - [section 7.2]: Learned spatial lag importances (Fig. 11) show disease‑specific patterns (e.g., first‑order neighbors dominant for Belgium COVID‑19).
- **Break condition**: Misspecified adjacency (e.g., using only contiguity when mobility drives transmission) degrades performance. Evidenced by weaker results on Colombia dengue (high spatial noise, Fig. 4 E) where spatial modules offer little gain over MVEN.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Under mild assumptions (bounded activation, finite noise moments, contractive forget gate), the closed‑loop spatiotemporal engression process is geometrically ergodic and asymptotically stationary, ensuring long‑term forecasts are stable and free from explosive behavior.
- **Mechanism**: The system is modeled as a homogeneous Markov chain S_t = (y_t, h_t, c_t) with transition F (Eq. 5). The proof uses Tweedie’s drift criterion: a Lyapunov function V(s) = 1 + ∥s∥ satisfies E[V(S_t) | S_{t-1}=s] ≤ (1-δ) V(s) + const for s outside a small set G, given ∥F(z)∥ ≤ a∥z∥ + b with a<1. Assumptions (A1)-(A6) ensure irreducibility and drift.
- **Core assumption**: Network weights remain bounded (achieved with bounded activations like tanh/sigmoid and weight constraints), noise has finite moments (Gaussian/uniform), and the forget gate is contractive (∥f(u,v)∥_∞ < 1).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 5.1]: Theorem 2 states: "Suppose Assumptions (A1)-(A6) hold. Then the GCEN and STEN processes… are geometrically ergodic."
  - [section 5.3]: Simulations show 92%+ KPSS stationarity pass rates and exponential coupling decay (γ ≈ -0.55).
  - [corpus]: No direct neighbor addresses ergodicity; this proof is novel for this architecture class.
- **Break condition**: Violated assumptions (e.g., unbounded ReLU activations, exploding gradients) would break the drift condition, potentially causing numerical divergence or non‑stationary forecasts.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Pre‑additive (engression) vs post‑additive noise models**  
  - *Why needed here*: Explains how the model learns full predictive distributions rather than just a conditional mean. The pre‑additive approach propagates uncertainty through the network, making the forecast distribution state‑dependent and naturally generating ensembles.  
  - *Quick check question*: In the lag plot (Fig. 1A), why does a single y_{t-1} value correspond to multiple y_t values, motivating pre‑additive noise?

- **Concept: Energy score loss as a proper scoring rule for distributional regression**  
  - *Why needed here*: The energy score (Eq. 3) is the differentiable loss used to train the generative architecture. Its two terms reward accuracy (mean close to observed) and sharpness (ensemble spread). This is central to making the model probabilistic without post‑hoc calibration.  
  - *Quick check question*: Which term in the energy score loss penalizes mode collapse (all ensemble members too similar)?

- **Concept: Geometric ergodicity of Markov chains**  
  - *Why needed here*: Provides theoretical assurance that long‑horizon forecasts are not arbitrarily dependent on initial hidden states. This stability guarantee is essential for trustworthy epidemic forecasting, especially under low‑frequency data.  
  - *Quick check question*: What function V(s) is used in the drift condition to prove geometric ergodicity (Theorem 1)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  - **Input layer**: 3D tensor Y ∈ ℝ^{T×N×D} (D=1 for incidence).  
  - **Spatial module**:  
    - GCEN: Graph convolution layers with adjacency A (Haversine + Gaussian kernel).  
    - STEN: STAR‑layer that computes W^l Y_t for l=0..L and combines via learnable Φ^l.  
    - MVEN: No spatial module; directly uses raw sequences.  
  - **Noise injection**: Add η ~ Uniform (Belgium) or Gaussian (others) to spatial embeddings (GCEN/STEN) or raw inputs (MVEN). Shape matches embedding dimension (D′ for GCEN/STEN, D for MVEN).  
  - **Temporal module**: LSTM (configurable depth 1–5, hidden size 2–100, dropout 0–0.5). Processes perturbed sequence of length p.  
  - **Forecast head**: Dense layer maps final hidden state to q‑step forecast for all N nodes.  
  - **Ensemble generation**: Repeat forward pass M times (training M=2, evaluation M=100) with fresh noise samples; stack outputs along first dimension.

- **Critical path**:  
  1. Extract lookback window Y_{t‑p+1:t} (shape: batch × p × N × D).  
  2. Compute spatial embeddings Z = f_spatial(Y, adjacency) → shape (batch, p, N, D′).  
  3. Sample η ∈ ℝ^{p×N×D′} (or concatenate as alternative).  
  4. Compute Z′ = Z + η (or [Z∥η]).  
  5. Run LSTM on reshaped Z′ (p, batch·N, net_dim) → final hidden H_t (batch·N, D_hidden).  
  6. Apply dense: Ŷ = Dense(H_t) → reshape to (batch, q, N, D).  
  7. Repeat steps 3–6 M times to build ensemble.  
  8. Training: minimize energy score between ensemble and ground truth; backpropagate.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - **GCEN vs STEN**: GCEN uses data‑driven graph convolutions → better for complex nonlinear spatial patterns; STEN uses explicit lag matrix → provides explainable spatial importance scores (Fig. 11) and is preferable when interpretability is critical.  
  - **Noise distribution**: Uniform for series with localized periodicity (Belgium COVID‑19); Gaussian for noisier series (most others). Chosen via validation.  
  - **MVEN**: Use when spatial signal is weak or absent (e.g., no location metadata). Faster training, but may underfit if spatial dependencies exist.  
  - **Ensemble size M**: Larger M improves uncertainty calibration but increases inference time; training with M=2 is sufficient (per Kraft et al.).

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - **Calibrated but wide intervals**: High empirical coverage (≈1.0) with poor Winkler score → ensemble spread too large. Check noise variance hyperparameter; consider reducing via validation CRPS.  
  - **Divergent closed‑loop forecasts (testing ergodicity)**: During simulation (Sec 5.3), if coupling distance does not decay exponentially, weights may violate bound assumptions (A6) → add gradient clipping or weight regularization.  
  - **Spatial module overfitting**: If GCEN significantly underperforms MVEN on a dataset with suspected weak spatial signal (e.g., Colombia dengue, Table 10), likely adjacency misspecification → try k‑NN graph or learn adjacency.  
  - **Training instability**: Energy score non‑convex; use Adam with LR ∈ [1e‑5, 0.1] and early stopping on validation CRPS.

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Replicate paper’s simulation**: Run closed‑loop GCEN (p=1, q=1) on random graph (N=10–60) with Gaussian noise. Compute KPSS pass rate and coupling decay (Fig. 2). Should see >90% stationarity and negative γ ≈ -0.5.  
  2. **Single‑node MVEN sanity check**: Train MVEN on Japan TB Node 27 (Osaka) with p=6, q=6. Generate 100‑member ensemble; verify median aligns with test set and ensemble spread captures observed variability (similar to Fig. 10E‑F).  
  3. **Spatial importance ablation**: For STEN on USA ILI, vary max_spatial_lag L=0,1,2,3 and compute Φ_l Frobenius norms (Eq. in Sec 7.2). Confirm l=1,2 dominate (Fig. 11C) and that removing higher lags degrades CRPS.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can extreme value theory be integrated into spatiotemporal engression to improve forecasting of epidemic peaks?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors identify "diminished capacity to accurately forecast extreme peaks" as a limitation and suggest integrating extreme value modeling as a future direction.
- **Why unresolved**: Extreme peaks are critical for public health preparedness, but current models tend to under‑predict them due to the energy score loss and the generative noise structure not focusing on tail behavior.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop a hybrid engression model that incorporates an extreme value component (e.g., a conditional generalized Pareto tail) into the predictive distribution. Evaluate using tail‑sensitive metrics (e.g., high‑quantile Pinball loss, CRPS for exceedances) on multiple epidemic datasets with documented extreme outbreaks. Superior performance on these metrics would demonstrate resolution.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How can exogenous determinants such as climate, mobility, or demographic factors be effectively incorporated into the spatiotemporal engression framework?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors note that the study "exclusively relies on historical epidemic incidence data, which omits the influence of exogenous determinants" and that the architecture is generalizable to include them.
- **Why unresolved**: Exogenous drivers are known to modulate transmission dynamics. Ignoring them may limit forecast accuracy and prevent the model from capturing causal effects needed for intervention planning.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Extend the input tensor `Y` to include exogenous covariates, retrain the models on the same datasets enriched with relevant features, and compare probabilistic forecast metrics (CRPS, Winkler score) against the baseline absence‑of‑exogenous‑variables version. Significant improvements across multiple datasets would validate the approach.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can spatiotemporal engression models be combined with mechanistic compartmental models (e.g., SEIR) to enhance predictive accuracy and interpretability?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors propose that "future research... integration with compartmental models... would likely enhance predictive accuracy and provide more robust contextual information."
- **Why unresolved**: Purely data‑driven models lack explicit representation of disease transmission mechanisms, which can be valuable in low‑data regimes and for scenario analysis (e.g., evaluating interventions).
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Design a hybrid architecture where engression predicts latent parameters (e.g., transmission rate, recovery rate) of a compartmental model, or where compartmental constraints regularize the network. Assess the hybrid against standalone engression and baseline mechanistic models on accuracy, calibration, and interpretability (e.g., ability to simulate counterfactual policies). Consistent gains would resolve the question.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Is the choice of the L2‑based energy score loss optimal for all spatiotemporal epidemic distributions, or would alternative distance measures (e.g., copula‑based scores) improve robustness?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors remark that "the discrepancy between the target and the functional space is sensitive to the choice of the distance metric. Copula‑based density regression might be viewed as an alternative."
- **Why unresolved**: Different underlying distributions (e.g., heavy‑tailed, multimodal) may respond better to non‑Euclidean metrics. The L2 norm might penalize certain forecast errors asymmetrically, affecting calibration.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replace the energy score with a copula‑based proper scoring rule (or other metrics like the maximum mean discrepancy) while keeping the model architecture fixed. Compare forecast distributions using multiple proper scores (CRPS, energy score variants) and calibration measures (PIT histograms) across a diverse suite of epidemic datasets. If an alternative metric yields stricter adherence to uniformity in PIT and lower scores, the question would be positively answered.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter ambiguities: The paper does not clearly specify key settings such as lookback length (p), adjacency construction thresholds (σ, ϵ), graph convolution depth (K), or training details (batch size, weight decay), making exact replication difficult.
- Noise distribution choice is heuristic (Gaussian vs. Uniform) and may not match the true residual distribution, risking suboptimal calibration for series with heavy tails or heteroscedasticity.
- The theoretical ergodicity assumptions require bounded activations, but the STEN model uses ReLU, which is unbounded and thus potentially violates the conditions, leaving the guarantee on shaky ground.
