# Learning Complex Physical Regimes via Coverage-oriented Uncertainty Quantification: An application to the Critical Heat Flux
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.21701  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21701  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Coverage-oriented UQ enables accurate and regime-aware CHF prediction, with aleatoric uncertainty effectively signaling regime transitions.

## Executive Summary
Scientific machine learning often struggles with multi-regime physical systems where dynamics change across input space. This paper uses critical heat flux (CHF) prediction as a benchmark to compare post-hoc uncertainty quantification (split conformal prediction) with coverage-oriented learning, where uncertainty is modeled jointly via Bayesian heteroscedastic regression and quality‑driven losses. On a filtered U.S. NRC dataset, ResNet models achieve ~10% RMSPE. Coverage‑oriented methods match this accuracy while reshaping internal representations to capture distinct physical regimes. The predicted aleatoric uncertainty dynamically increases in the transition zone (outlet quality X∈[0.25,0.5]), flagging regime changes without supervision. Conformal prediction provides ~95% coverage; end‑to‑end UQ yields adaptive, physically consistent uncertainty with higher informativeness (UQF up to 74%) and similar coverage. Bayesian analysis confirms aleatoric uncertainty dominates total variance, highlighting the importance of modeling regime‑dependent stochasticity.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates two UQ strategies for CHF prediction: (1) post‑hoc split conformal prediction applied to a deterministic model’s residuals, offering distribution‑free coverage guarantees; and (2) coverage‑oriented learning, where a Bayesian neural network with heteroscedastic output is trained end‑to‑end using a loss combining negative log‑likelihood and coverage regularization. Both approaches use a ResNet backbone to process input features (geometry, operating conditions). The coverage‑oriented method explicitly predicts mean and variance of CHF; Bayesian posterior inference captures epistemic uncertainty, while the variance head models input‑dependent aleatoric noise.

## Key Results
- **Accuracy and regime representation**: ResNet models attain ~10% RMSPE; coverage‑oriented learning improves internal representation, enabling capture of distinct regimes.  
- **Adaptive uncertainty**: Aleatoric uncertainty increases in the transition zone (X∈[0.25,0.5]), effectively flagging regime changes without supervision.  
- **UQ quality**: End‑to‑end UQ yields adaptive uncertainty with high informativeness (UQF up to 74%) and ~95% coverage; aleatoric uncertainty dominates total variance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Coverage‑oriented learning jointly optimizes prediction and uncertainty, forcing the network to internalize features correlated with both target value and its variability. Heteroscedastic variance allows the model to assign higher uncertainty to regions with inherent stochasticity, such as regime transitions. Bayesian inference captures epistemic uncertainty due to limited data, while quality‑driven losses ensure calibrated coverage. This combination reshapes the latent space, making regimes more separable and enabling uncertainty to serve as an unsupervised indicator of physical boundaries.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Input features → ResNet backbone → [Mean head, Variance head] → Loss (Negative Log‑Likelihood + coverage regularization) → Parameter updates (via variational inference).

**Critical Path**  
1. Preprocess and normalize the filtered U.S. NRC CHF dataset.  
2. Forward pass through ResNet to obtain mean and variance predictions.  
3. Compute loss: NLL using predicted Gaussian distribution plus optional coverage penalty.  
4. Backpropagate gradients and update model parameters (and posterior approximations).  
5. Evaluate RMSPE, coverage, and uncertainty metrics on held‑out data.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Bayesian vs. deterministic: Bayesian captures epistemic uncertainty but adds computational overhead; deterministic is faster but may be overconfident.  
- Heteroscedastic vs. homoscedastic: Heteroscedastic enables regime‑dependent uncertainty but requires careful regularization to avoid variance overfitting.  
- End‑to‑end UQ vs. conformal: End‑to‑end yields adaptive, often sharper intervals; conformal provides guarantee but may produce conservative sets.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Coverage below nominal level (e.g., <95%) indicates mis‑calibration of predicted variance.  
- Absence of an uncertainty peak in the transition zone suggests the model fails to detect regime change.  
- High epistemic uncertainty across all inputs may signal underfitting or data scarcity in key regimes.  
- Numerical instability (e.g., negative variances) if the variance head is unconstrained.

**First Experiments**  
1. Train a deterministic ResNet with MSE loss (baseline).  
2. Train a deterministic heteroscedastic model (predicting mean and variance, optimized with NLL).  
3. Train the full Bayesian heteroscedastic model with coverage‑oriented loss; compare uncertainty behavior, coverage, and informativeness against the previous two.

## Limitations
- **Dataset limitation**: Results are based on a single filtered U.S. NRC dataset; generalizability to other geometries or boiling regimes is unclear.  
- **Representation analysis**: The claim of regime‑capturing representations lacks quantitative disentanglement metrics (e.g., linear probing accuracy).  
- **Method comparison**: No evaluation against alternative UQ techniques (deep ensembles, MC dropout) limits the strength of superiority claims.
