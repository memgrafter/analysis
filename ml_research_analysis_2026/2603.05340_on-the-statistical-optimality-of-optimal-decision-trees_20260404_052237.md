# On the Statistical Optimality of Optimal Decision Trees
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.05340  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05340  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Sharp oracle inequalities and minimax optimal rates for optimal decision trees over the newly introduced Piecewise Sparse Heterogeneous Anisotropic Besov (PSHAB) function class.

## Executive Summary
Optimal decision trees obtained by empirical risk minimization (ERM) lack rigorous statistical guarantees despite recent computational advances. This paper closes the gap by establishing sharp oracle inequalities that bound the excess risk of an ERM tree with \(L\) leaves by the sum of an approximation error (the risk of the best tree with at most \(L\) leaves) and a variance term of order \(L \log(nd)/n\) under sub‑Gaussian noise. To capture high‑dimensional structure, the authors introduce the Piecewise Sparse Heterogeneous Anisotropic Besov (PSHAB) space, which allows for varying smoothness, sparsity patterns, and anisotropy across regions. Over this class, ERM trees attain minimax optimal rates that adapt to these features up to logarithmic factors. Under heavy‑tailed noise, the rates degrade but remain non‑trivial, highlighting sensitivity to tail behavior. These results provide the first principled foundation for non‑dyadic optimal trees.

## Method Summary
A minimum viable reproduction plan is provided to complement the theory. First, obtain an exact ERM tree solver—using CORELS for classification or the `optimal‑trees` library (or a custom MIP with Gurobi/CPLEX) for regression—that supports a maximum leaf budget \(L\). Second, construct a synthetic target function belonging to the PSHAB class, e.g., a piecewise constant function on a fixed partition of \([0,1]^2\) with known bounds and sparsity. Third, generate training sets of size \(n\) with sub‑Gaussian noise, fit constrained ERM trees for various \(L\), and evaluate excess risk on a large hold‑out set, averaging over multiple splits. This verifies the predicted scaling: in the variance‑dominated regime (\(L\) at least the true complexity), excess risk decays as \(O(L \log(nd)/n)\) when increasing \(n\); in the approximation‑dominated regime (\(L\) below the truth), excess risk decreases with \(L\) according to the approximation rate. The plan also notes pitfalls such as solver optimality gaps and the need to respect boundedness assumptions.

## Key Results
- **Oracle inequalities**: For any \(L\)-leaf tree, the constrained ERM estimator’s excess risk is bounded by the best approximation error plus a variance term \(O(L \log(nd)/n)\) under sub‑Gaussian noise.
- **Minimax optimality over PSHAB**: Rates adapt to sparsity, anisotropy, and spatial heterogeneity, achieving the optimal balance between approximation (\(L^{-2\alpha/s}\)) and estimation (\(L \log(nd)/n\)) up to logarithmic factors.
- **Heavy‑tailed robustness**: Under heavier noise, the same method yields suboptimal but still non‑trivial rates, demonstrating sensitivity to tail behavior.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The theoretical guarantees rely on a novel uniform concentration inequality that controls the supremum of empirical risk deviations over all tree partitions with at most \(L\) leaves. The key tool is empirically localized Rademacher complexity, which adapts to the complexity of the PSHAB function class and the data‑dependent geometry of the tree. This yields sharp oracle inequalities separating approximation error (best \(L\)-leaf tree in the class) and estimation error (stochastic fluctuations). By balancing these components and exploiting the PSHAB structure—which encodes sparsity, anisotropy, and piecewise smoothness—the authors derive minimax optimal rates for both regression and classification. For heavy‑tailed noise, truncation arguments extend the analysis at the cost of a slower rate.

## Foundational Learning
- **Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)**: Core principle that the estimator minimizes average loss on the training data under a leaf constraint. *Quick check*: Given a loss and data, define empirical risk and state that the ERM tree minimizes it over all trees with ≤ \(L\) leaves.
- **Oracle Inequalities**: Finite‑sample bounds linking the excess risk of the ERM tree to the best approximation error by a tree of similar complexity. *Quick check*: Write the generic form: \(\mathbb{E}[R(\hat f)] - R(f^*) \le C \cdot \inf_{f \in \mathcal{F}_L} (R(f)-R(f^*)) + \text{variance term}\).
- **Empirically Localized Rademacher Complexity**: A refined version of Rademacher complexity that focuses on functions with small empirical error; it adapts to the anisotropic Besov structure and yields tight uniform convergence. *Quick check*: Recall the definition of Rademacher complexity and explain how localization (e.g., via a weighted norm) yields data‑dependent bounds.
- **PSHAB Spaces**: Generalization of anisotropic Besov spaces to piecewise definitions, allowing different smoothness levels, sparsity patterns, and anisotropy across regions. Captures realistic high‑dimensional signals. *Quick check*: Describe that a PSHAB space is specified by a tree‑based partition \(\mathcal{P}^*\), local smoothness \(\alpha_b\), sparsity sets \(S_b\), and anisotropy dimension \(s\); the Besov norm controls approximation.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data Generator → ERM Tree Solver → Risk Evaluator

**Critical path**:
1. Install and configure an exact ERM tree solver (e.g., CORELS, optimal‑trees, or a custom MIP).
2. Implement a synthetic data generator for a target PSHAB function plus noise.
3. For each \((n, L)\) pair, fit the constrained ERM tree and compute test excess risk; repeat over random splits.
4. Analyze scaling: compare empirical excess risk against theoretical expressions \((L\log(nd))/n\) and \(L^{-2\alpha/s}\).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Exact vs approximate*: MIP solvers guarantee optimality but may time out on larger instances; heuristics (e.g., CART) violate theory.
- *Choice of \(L\)*: Must explore both \(L <\) true leaves (approximation regime) and \(L \ge\) true leaves (variance regime) to see the full trade‑off.
- *Boundedness \(M\)*: Theory assumes outcomes bounded by \(M\); if \(M\) is set too low, the fitted leaf values may be clipped, altering the estimator.
- *Noise type*: Sub‑Gaussian vs heavy‑tailed distributions require different \(K\) assumptions and affect the variance term.

**Failure signatures**:
- Solver reports a non‑zero optimality gap (e.g., > 1%), indicating suboptimal trees.
- Test error increases with \(L\) for small \(L\) where approximation error should dominate—possible mismatch between synthetic target and PSHAB assumptions.
- Fitted leaf means far exceed the prescribed bound \(M\), violating the boundedness assumption.
- No visible logarithmic dependence on dimension \(d\) when increasing \(d\)—entropy constants may dominate or sample size too small.
- In the approximation regime, excess risk does not decrease with \(L\)—target may be outside PSHAB or solver not finding better approximations.

**First experiments**:
1. *Variance scaling*: Fix \(L\) larger than true leaves (e.g., \(L = 10\)), vary \(n \in \{200, 500, 1000, 2000\}\). Plot excess risk vs \(1/n\); expect linear trend with slope proportional to \(L\log(nd)\).
2. *Approximation scaling*: Fix large \(n\) (e.g., 2000), vary \(L\) from 1 to 16. For a piecewise constant target with true leaves = 4, expect a U‑shaped curve: risk decreases as \(L\) approaches 4 (reducing approximation error), then increases beyond 4 (variance dominates).
3. *Logarithmic dimension effect*: Fix \(n\) and \(L\), increase \(d\) (e.g., 2, 5, 10). Excess risk should increase roughly as \(\log d\); computing excess risk divided by \(\log(nd)\) should be approximately constant.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Achieve minimax optimal rates for ERM trees under heavy‑tailed noise. *Basis*: Section 8 identifies the gap. *Why unresolved*: Current analysis relies on sub‑Gaussian tails; trees are sensitive to outliers. *What evidence would resolve*: A robust ERM variant (e.g., median‑of‑means leaf averages) with a uniform concentration proof matching the sub‑Gaussian rate.
- **Open Question 2**: Prove minimax lower bounds for classification over PSHAB spaces with general smoothness parameters \(p\) and \(q\). *Basis*: Remark 6.21 notes the lack of lower bounds beyond \(p = q = \infty\). *Why unresolved*: Entropy computations for anisotropic Besov spaces with finite \(p,q\) require mixed‑norm chaining beyond Hölder‑based methods. *What evidence would resolve*: A matching lower bound (or counterexample) for a range of \((p,q)\) via metric entropy or Fano inequalities.
- **Open Question 3**: Extend the uniform concentration framework to CART and Random Forests. *Basis*: Conclusion suggests potential for tighter generalization bounds. *Why unresolved*: CART and Random Forests are not global ERM; greedy splits and randomness break the uniform convergence over all partitions. *What evidence would resolve*: Oracle inequalities (or minimax rates) for CART/Random Forests using an empirically localized Rademacher complexity that accounts for algorithmic data‑dependence, possibly via symmetrization over split randomness.

## Limitations
- The uniform constants in Theorems 6.1 and 6.14 are not quantified, preventing precise numerical verification.
- Exact ERM trees are NP‑hard; mixed‑integer programming solvers may not achieve certified optimality for moderate \(n\) or \(d\), limiting empirical validation to small scales.
- Constructing smooth PSHAB target functions that also satisfy sparsity and anisotropy constraints for testing approximation rates is non‑trivial; the simple constant target does not probe that regime.
