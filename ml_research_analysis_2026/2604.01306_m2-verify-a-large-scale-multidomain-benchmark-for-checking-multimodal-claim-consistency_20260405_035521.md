# M2-Verify: A Large-Scale Multidomain Benchmark for Checking Multimodal Claim Consistency

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.01306
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01306
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: M2-Verify introduces a 469K-instance multimodal benchmark; SOTA models achieve 85.8% Micro-F1 on low-complexity medical tasks but drop to 61.6% on high‑complexity anatomical shifts.

## Executive Summary
M2-Verify is a large‑scale benchmark for multimodal scientific claim verification, comprising 469K instances across 16 domains (medical from PubMed, general science from arXiv). The dataset construction uses a calibrated pipeline with domain‑specific perturbations and three‑phase expert validation to ensure high quality. Evaluation of state‑of‑the‑art vision‑language models (VLMs) reveals a significant performance gap: top models score 85.8% Micro‑F1 on low‑complexity medical perturbations but only 61.6% on high‑complexity challenges like anatomical location shifts. Models also hallucinate in generated explanations. Ablation studies confirm that both figures and captions are essential, and supervised fine‑tuning yields large improvements, highlighting the dataset’s diagnostic utility and learnability.

## Method Summary
The task is multimodal scientific claim consistency verification: given a scientific figure, its caption, and a claim, determine whether the claim is supported or refuted by the evidence. M2‑Verify provides 469K instances across 16 domains. The dataset is built via a four‑stage pipeline: (1) collect figure‑caption pairs from source corpora where authors draw conclusions; (2) extract supported claims using domain‑specific LLMs (MedGemma for medical, Gemma‑3 for general science); (3) generate refuted claims via curated perturbation taxonomies (7 medical types, negation for general); (4) generate grounded explanations through constrained rationalization, followed by LLM‑judge filtering. Quality is assured by a three‑phase expert audit: blind inter‑annotator agreement, alignment validation, and statistical reliability checks. Evaluation uses zero‑shot prompting of VLMs to produce binary verification decisions and explanations, with primary metrics Micro‑F1 and Macro‑F1. Fine‑tuning experiments employ LoRA (r=32, α=64, dropout=0.05, lr=5e‑5) with an image resolution cap of 401,408 pixels.

## Key Results
- SOTA models (e.g., Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B) achieve 85.8% Micro‑F1 on low‑complexity medical perturbations but degrade to 61.6% on high‑complexity anatomical shifts.
- Ablation studies confirm that both figures and captions are essential; removing either modality causes severe performance drops.
- Supervised fine‑tuning on M2‑Verify yields substantial improvements, demonstrating the dataset’s learnability and diagnostic value.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark’s effectiveness stems from three design mechanisms.

**Mechanism 1: Calibrated, expert‑validated pipeline with domain‑specific perturbations**  
The four‑stage data construction (collection, claim extraction, perturbation, explanation) creates a realistic, high‑cognitive‑load verification task. A curated perturbation taxonomy (medical: 7 types; general: negation) systematically probes specific reasoning deficits. Expert audits across three phases (blind IAA, alignment, statistical reliability) ensure label‑instance alignment and explanation quality (e.g., 84.9% agreement on medical). The core assumption is that human‑verified, perturbation‑controlled instances can isolate model weaknesses (e.g., spatial, numerical). A break condition would be implausible contradictions; the paper mitigates this via a hardness selection strategy and expert validation.

**Mechanism 2: Performance cliff between low‑ and high‑complexity perturbations**  
The medical taxonomy introduces a controlled complexity gradient. All evaluated open‑source VLMs degrade sharply from ~85% to ~62% Micro‑F1 on high‑complexity perturbations (e.g., anatomical location shifts). This gap persists for top models, indicating that current architectures struggle with subtle, high‑level semantic shifts requiring deep domain knowledge. The variance across perturbation types reveals model capability boundaries rather than dataset noise; balanced sampling and expert curation address potential distribution biases.

**Mechanism 3: Strict necessity of multimodal fusion**  
Ablation studies show that removing either images or captions leads to substantial, often asymmetric, performance degradation. For advanced VLMs (GPT‑4o‑mini, Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B) both modalities are essential, confirming that claim‑evidence relationships encode unique information in each modality. However, a prior model (MOCHEG) performed worse multimodal than text‑only on general science, hypothesizing “representation interference” when integrating diverse scientific imagery. The pipeline’s claim‑extraction filtering (discarding instances solvable by caption alone) ensures the task genuinely requires joint reasoning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cross‑Modal Grounding**: Aligning claim concepts with image regions and caption text. *Why needed*: The task requires distributed evidence mapping; failure to localize spatially causes errors on location shifts. *Quick check*: Why would a model fail a “location shift” perturbation if it correctly describes the image? (It may not spatially localize the referenced anatomy relative to the figure’s coordinate system.)
- **Controlled Perturbation Taxonomy**: Systematic set of claim modifications creating graded difficulty. *Why needed*: To diagnose *why* models fail, not just that they fail; taxonomy yields fine‑grained error categories. *Quick check*: How does a “Certainty Shift” (e.g., “suspicious for” → “diagnostic of”) test reasoning beyond simple negation? (It challenges nuanced medical semantics and evidential strength.)
- **Expert‑Validated Ground Truth**: Human‑verified labels and explanations. *Why needed*: Scientific verification is subjective; high inter‑annotator agreement (65.4% blind IAA) sets a realistic human ceiling. *Quick check*: Why is blind IAA lower than alignment agreement (84.9%)? (Blind IAA measures independent annotation consistency; alignment reflects consensus after discussion, which is higher.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data Pipeline (collects figure‑caption pairs, extracts claims, applies perturbations, generates explanations) → Evaluation Harness (loads dataset, runs models with fixed prompts, computes metrics) → Analysis Modules (perturbation complexity grouping, domain breakdown, modality ablation, fine‑tuning).

**Critical path**: Data collection must precede claim extraction; claim extraction must precede perturbation; all must complete before the three‑phase expert audit; the final gold set validates the pipeline’s statistical reliability before model evaluation.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Scale vs. Quality*: 469K instances with only ~1.5K expert‑reviewed; mitigated by statistical audit.
- *Perturbation Subtlety vs. Solvability*: Harder perturbations are more diagnostic but risk being unsolvable; mitigated by hardness selection and expert agreement checks.
- *Generalization vs. Domain Specificity*: Separate medical/general subsets with distinct taxonomies maximize relevance but complicate unified model development.

**Failure signatures**:
- Performance collapse on high‑complexity medical perturbations (e.g., Anatomical Location Shift) near random (50%) indicates a fundamental lack of cross‑modal reasoning for fine‑grained visual details.
- Domain‑specific underperformance in abstract sciences (e.g., physics, math) suggests deficiency in interpreting non‑standard, diagrammatic visual evidence.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Modality Necessity Check**: Run ablation (remove images or captions) on a baseline model. If text‑only performs comparably, the task may have shortcuts; revisit claim‑extraction filtering.
2. **Perturbation Sensitivity Probe**: Evaluate a model on each perturbation type individually. Identify categories causing the largest drops to target architectural improvements (e.g., spatial reasoning modules).
3. **SFT Data Efficiency Curve**: Fine‑tune a model (e.g., InternVL3‑8B) on increasing fractions of M2‑Verify‑Med; plot Micro‑F1 vs. data size to assess learnability and potential saturation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do residual generation biases in the automated pipeline affect its reliability as a proxy for human scientific reasoning? A larger‑scale, fine‑grained human audit across pipeline stages would be needed.
2. Why do VLMs exhibit pronounced failures on high‑complexity perturbations (anatomical shifts) and abstract domains (math/physics), and what specific architectural or reasoning deficits cause this? Controlled probing experiments isolating factors like spatial resolution or symbolic reasoning integration could provide evidence.
3. What modifications to multimodal fusion architectures (e.g., MOCHEG) are necessary to prevent “representation interference” when integrating diverse scientific imagery? Detailed ablation of fusion mechanisms and analysis of internal representations are required.
4. How can generation of faithful, non‑hallucinated scientific explanations be systematically improved, given the gap between high verification accuracy and low explanation faithfulness? Analyzing explanation generation conditioned on correct/incorrect predictions and training ablations with rationale‑specific losses could help.
5. Will extending M2‑Verify to dynamic visual modalities (videos, interactive 3D) and multilingual contexts reveal new classes of multimodal failures, or can static‑image‑trained models generalize? Creating such extensions and evaluating transfer would provide evidence.

## Limitations
- Expert audit covers only ~1.5K of 469K instances; potential systematic errors across the full benchmark remain undetected.
- Perturbation “hardness selection” is automated; subtle lexical or visual shortcuts could bias high‑complexity evaluations.
- Explanation quality relies on LLM‑judge without human validation; may overestimate faithfulness.
