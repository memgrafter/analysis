# CDH-Bench: A Commonsense-Driven Hallucination Benchmark for Evaluating Visual Fidelity in Vision-Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27982
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27982
- **Reference count:** 20
- **Primary result:** Vision-language models systematically override anomalous visual evidence with learned commonsense priors, leading to high collapse rates in multiple-choice QA (up to 85.85%) and persistent failures on counting anomalies.

## Executive Summary
CDH-Bench introduces a benchmark to evaluate *commonsense-driven hallucination* (CDH) in vision-language models. CDH occurs when models ignore counterfactual visual evidence and default to expected semantic priors. The dataset consists of 300 counterfactual–commonsense image pairs across three anomaly types (counting, relational, attribute), each evaluated via binary and multiple-choice QA. Testing eight frontier VLMs reveals substantial accuracy drops on counterfactual images (average 16.39% in binary QA, 25.20% in MC) and high commonsense collapse rates, especially for counting anomalies. Even large-scale or reasoning-oriented models show limited improvement, highlighting CDH as a fundamental reliability gap in multimodal understanding.

## Method Summary
Benchmark construction starts by generating counterfactual images containing a single, visually explicit anomaly (e.g., a blue banana) using a text-to-image model. For each counterfactual, a matched normal counterpart is created by minimally altering the prompt. Two question formats are produced per pair: (1) binary QA that explicitly contrasts the anomaly (e.g., “Does the image show X and not Y?”) and (2) multiple-choice QA containing the correct counterfactual answer, the commonsense alternative, and two distractors. Evaluation runs on 600 images, computing Counterfactual Accuracy (CF-Acc), Commonsense Accuracy (CS-Acc), accuracy drop (CFAD), Relative Prior Dependency (RPD), and for MC, Commonsense Collapse Rate (CCR). Category-level breakdowns isolate failure modes by anomaly type.

## Key Results
- Average CFAD: 16.39% (binary QA), 25.20% (MC)
- Commonsense Collapse Rate (CCR) reaches up to 85.85% in MC, indicating strong prior normalization.
- Counting anomalies, especially body part counts, are the most persistent; even large reasoning models show little mitigation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** CDH arises from a failure in arbitration between a visual perception channel and a commonsense-prior channel, leading to prior-driven normalization.
- **Mechanism:** When visual evidence conflicts with high-probability commonsense priors learned during training, the model’s generation smooths the anomalous evidence toward the semantic prior, effectively “correcting” the image to match expectation.
- **Core assumption:** VLMs internalize two competing signals—image-grounded features and distributional priors—and generate via a process that can suppress anomalous but correct visual signals.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “models override visual evidence and outputs the commonsense alternative… generation can behave like semantic smoothing.”  
  - Section 2: “VLMs implicitly arbitrate between… a visual perception channel… and a commonsense-prior channel.”
- **Break condition:** If the visual evidence is made irreducibly salient or the prior is weakened (e.g., via adversarial prompt engineering or architectural changes), collapse rates (CCR) should decrease.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Task format directly modulates CDH severity; multiple-choice QA amplifies prior-driven collapse by making the commonsense alternative an explicit competitive choice.
- **Mechanism:** In binary QA, the question text itself contains the conflict, possibly allowing models to “avoid” choosing by answering “no” more cautiously. In MC QA, the commonsense answer is a selectable option, forcing a direct competition that triggers stronger prior reliance.
- **Core assumption:** The presentation of alternatives changes the decision landscape, making prior-based selection more likely when the correct answer is counterfactual.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section 3.2: “Multiple-choice QA… makes the commonsense alternative explicit and places it in direct competition.”  
  - Section 5.3: Average CFAD increases from 16.39% (QA) to 25.20% (MC); CCR up to 85.85% in MC.
- **Break condition:** If models are trained or prompted to treat multiple-choice options with equal skepticism (e.g., via uncertainty regularization), the MC advantage for priors should diminish, narrowing the QA-MC gap.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Scale and reasoning-oriented decoding improve but do not eliminate CDH; the hardest subcategories (e.g., biological part counting) remain persistently vulnerable.
- **Mechanism:** Larger capacity and test-time reasoning provide more computational resources to reconcile conflict, but the fundamental architectural bias toward commonsense distributions persists, especially where fine-grained perception is required.
- **Core assumption:** CDH reflects a deeper inductive bias in training data and objectives, not merely a lack of parameters or reasoning steps.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section 5.4: Instruct series shows monotonic CF-Acc gains with scale; Thinking series improves CF-Acc but not uniformly on CCR/RPD.  
  - Section 5.3: Counting anomalies show highest CFAD across models; “the hardest subcategory-level bottlenecks remain visible even in large-scale systems.”
- **Break condition:** If a model architecture explicitly separates and constrains the prior channel (e.g., via modular routing or contrastive visual grounding), persistent subcategory failures should reduce.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multimodal Arbitration Failure**  
  *Why needed here:* Understanding CDH requires seeing VLMs not as pure perception engines but as systems that must weigh visual evidence against parametric priors. This concept explains why “what is shown” can be overridden by “what is known.”  
  *Quick check question:* If a VLM sees a blue banana and is asked “What color is the banana?”, what two channels are in conflict, and which one likely wins in a standard model? Why?

- **Counterfactual Evaluation Design**  
  *Why needed here:* CDH-Bench’s paired design (counterfactual vs. commonsense images) is key to unambiguous error attribution. Without matched controls, we couldn’t distinguish “I don’t see the extra finger” from “I see it but refuse to believe it.”  
  *Quick check question:* Why is a benchmark that only uses *normal* images insufficient to detect CDH? What would a false negative look like?

- **Collapse-Oriented Metrics (CCR, RPD)**  
  *Why needed here:* Accuracy alone masks the *nature* of failure. CCR specifically measures if errors revert to the commonsense alternative, which is the signature of CDH. RPD normalizes degradation by model capability.  
  *Quick check question:* A model has CS-Acc=90% and CF-Acc=80%. Another has CS-Acc=70% and CF-Acc=60%. Which shows stronger relative prior collapse? How does RPD clarify this?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Image Generation Pipeline → Pair Construction Engine → Task Format Renderer → Evaluation Harness → Analysis Suite

**Critical path:**  
Prompt design for anomaly generation → image synthesis & human verification → pair matching → question generation → model inference → metric calculation → categorical breakdown. The most failure-prone step is **image generation**; if the anomaly isn’t rendered unambiguously, the benchmark loses diagnostic power.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Anomaly salience vs. realism:* Making the anomaly “necessary” in the prompt can produce unrealistic artifacts. Tradeoff: use redundant, explicit language at the cost of some naturalness.  
- *Binary vs. MC QA:* Binary QA gives cleaner conflict isolation; MC QA better simulates real-world decision pressure but introduces confounds (distractor quality, positional bias).  
- *Automation vs. human verification:* Full automation risks invalid pairs; human verification ensures quality but limits scale (fixed at 300 pairs).

**Failure signatures:**  
- High **CCR** in MC QA (close to 100%) indicates systematic collapse to priors.  
- **Negative CFAD** (e.g., Gemini-3.1-pro-preview in QA) may indicate conservative/refusal behavior on *both* conditions, not true robustness.  
- Disproportionately low CF-Acc in **counting anomalies**, especially body parts, signals a specific perceptual bottleneck.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Replicate the QA vs. MC CFAD gap:** Run any VLM on both formats; confirm CFAD increases in MC and CCR is high. Validates the core amplification mechanism.  
2. **Subcategory stress test:** Isolate the worst-performing subcategory (e.g., “Body Parts” counting). Does the model fail on *all* items there, or just a subset? Checks if failure is systematic or noise.  
3. **Scale/reasoning ablation (Qwen-style):** Within one model family (e.g., Qwen3-VL), compare Instruct vs. Thinking at fixed scales (2B, 4B, 8B, 32B). Does Thinking consistently reduce CCR? This tests the reasoning-style hypothesis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does increasing model scale or employing reasoning-oriented inference consistently mitigate commonsense-driven hallucination (CDH) across diverse VLM architectures, given the mixed scaling trends observed in the Qwen3-VL family?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicit (Section 5.4): “A key question is whether model scale and reasoning style mitigate commonsense-driven hallucination under counterfactual visual evidence.” Evaluation of Qwen3-VL Instruct and Thinking yields non-monotonic improvements and inconsistent effects on CCR/RPD.
- **Why unresolved:** Study limited to a single model family. It is unclear whether patterns generalize to other architectures (e.g., GPT, Gemini, LLaVA) with different training objectives, fusion mechanisms, or data distributions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Cross-family evaluation of multiple VLM architectures at various scales (e.g., 2B, 8B, 32B) and inference styles (standard vs. reasoning-oriented) using a unified CDH-Bench protocol. Analysis should separate scale effects from architectural design choices and training data, with statistical tests for interaction effects.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why are counting anomalies, particularly those involving body parts (e.g., finger counts), disproportionately persistent across models and task formats compared to relational and attribute anomalies?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred: Table 1 and Figures 3–6 show counting anomalies yield the lowest CF-Acc and highest CFAD for many models, especially in MC (e.g., kimi-k2.5: counting CFAD 46.00% vs. attribute 25.00%). Paper notes “counting anomalies are the most persistent” but provides no explanatory analysis.
- **Why unresolved:** Paper categorizes anomalies but does not investigate the cognitive or architectural factors driving this disparity. Potential reasons: finer-grained visual perception demands, stronger canonical priors for body part counts, or difficulty in spatial discretization. Without targeted experiments, the relative contribution remains unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled subcategory ablation on CDH-Bench: vary count magnitude, part salience, and prior strength (e.g., common vs. rare counts). Include model probing (attention maps, region-level accuracy) to test if models fail to perceive anomalies or override them. Compare with non-body counting (e.g., object counts) to isolate body-part-specific effects.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** At which stage of the VLM pipeline does CDH primarily manifest—during visual perception (feature extraction), multimodal reasoning (integration), or final decision generation (prior bias in decoding)?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred: Benchmark’s paired design attributes errors to prior-driven overrides but examines no internal model states. Appendix case study shows inconsistent behavior (e.g., Qwen3-VL-32B-Thinking correct on CF MC but incorrect on binary QA), suggesting task-dependent failure modes that may arise at different stages. Theoretical framing mentions “visual perception channel” vs. “commonsense-prior channel” but provides no empirical dissection.
- **Why unresolved:** Current evaluation relies solely on input-output accuracy; no introspection (saliency, layer-wise probes) identifies where visual evidence is lost or overridden. Without this, mitigation strategies lack targeted direction.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyze model internals: compute alignment between image regions containing anomalies and textual answer probabilities; use faithfulness metrics (perturbation tests) to measure reliance on visual vs. textual context; compare representations in vision encoder, fusion layers, and LM decoder across CF vs. CS images. Ablate components (e.g., freeze vision encoder) to localize failure.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can training interventions explicitly designed to improve visual fidelity under conflict—such as counterfactual data augmentation, contrastive learning on anomaly images, or reinforcement learning with fidelity rewards—reduce CDH without compromising general VLM performance?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred: Paper introduces CDH-Bench as a diagnostic but does not explore mitigation beyond evaluating off-the-shelf models. Scale study implies scaling alone is insufficient, suggesting a need for novel training approaches. Authors state CDH-Bench can “guide the development” of more robust models but offer no empirical training results.
- **Why unresolved:** No experiments on model adaptation are presented. It is unknown whether models can learn to prioritize anomalous evidence through fine-tuning or architectural changes, and what trade-offs with standard benchmarks (e.g., VQAv2) might arise.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Fine-tune baseline VLMs on CDH-Bench training split (or synthetically expanded counterfactual data) using objectives that penalize commonsense collapse (contrastive losses between CF and CS representations, RLHF with fidelity rewards). Evaluate on held-out CDH-Bench and diverse standard benchmarks to measure generalization and trade-offs. Ablate augmentation strategies to identify effective methods.

## Limitations
- **Benchmark validity**: Counterfactual images must be unambiguously anomalous; imperfect generation can produce weak anomalies, leading to spurious results.
- **Model access**: Key proprietary models (Gemini, Doubao, GPT-4.1) are version-sensitive and access-limited, hindering exact reproduction and possibly forcing reliance on proxy models.
- **Scope**: CDH-Bench tests static single-image QA only; it does not address video, multi-turn dialogue, or generative tasks, so generalizability beyond this controlled setting is unknown.

## Confidence
- **High:** Existence of CDH phenomenon; robustness of metric calculations (CFAD, CCR) across the dataset; counting anomalies being the most persistent subcategory.
- **Medium:** The arbitration failure mechanism (visual vs. prior channel conflict) as root cause; role of task format (MC amplifying collapse) as primary driver; scale/reasoning providing incremental but incomplete mitigation.
- **Low:** Specific architectural or training interventions to fix CDH; generalizability of findings to non-QA VLM tasks; whether CDH is purely a data-distribution artifact vs. an inherent architectural bias.

## Next Checks
1. **Image verification pipeline:** Conduct a human study (n=50–100) where annotators rate each counterfactual image for anomaly clarity and naturalness. Correlate human scores with model CCR to confirm benchmark integrity.
2. **Family-scale ablation:** Within the Qwen3-VL family (2B/4B/8B/32B, Instruct vs. Thinking), run full CDH-Bench. Test if CCR monotonically decreases with scale and if Thinking reduces CCR more than Instruct at equivalent scale.
3. **Adversarial prompt stress test:** For the hardest subcategory (body part counting), design prompts that force explicit enumeration (e.g., “List every finger you see”) or suppress commonsense priors (“Ignore typical human anatomy”). Measure CCR drop; failure to reduce would challenge the arbitration mechanism.
