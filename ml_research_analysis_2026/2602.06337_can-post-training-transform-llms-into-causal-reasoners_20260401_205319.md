# Can Post-Training Transform LLMs into Causal Reasoners?
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06337
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06337
- Reference count: 16
- Primary result: Post-training with GRPO on a 14B model achieves 93.5% accuracy on the CaLM benchmark, surpassing much larger models like OpenAI o3 (55.4%) and DeepSeek-R1-671B (57.0%).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether post-training can equip large language models with reliable causal reasoning abilities. The authors introduce CauGym, a dataset of seven causal inference tasks (ATE, CDE, ETT, NDE, NIE, PN, PS) with five complementary test sets probing generalization, internalization, and robustness. Using DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B as the base model, they evaluate several post-training methods: SFT, DPO, KTO, PPO, and GRPO. Results show that online reinforcement learning, particularly GRPO, dramatically improves performance, achieving 93.5% average accuracy on the CaLM benchmark—a gain of up to 42.5% over the cold-start baseline—and maintains robustness under rephrased questions, omitted instructions, confounders, and noisy data. The study provides the first systematic evidence that targeted post-training can transform smaller LLMs into effective causal reasoners, even outperforming far larger proprietary and open models.

## Method Summary
The study employs a two-stage training pipeline. Stage 1 (cold-start) performs supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on a curated set of 3,500 CauGym training samples for three epochs using LoRA for parameter efficiency. Stage 2 applies various post-training methods to the cold-start checkpoint: additional SFT, offline preference optimization (DPO on 3,500 preference pairs, KTO on 7,000 samples with a balanced 1:1 ratio), or online reinforcement learning (PPO and GRPO on 3,500 questions). All post-training runs also last three epochs. The reference model for generating reasoning traces and preference pairs is DeepSeek-R1-0528-671B. Evaluation covers nine test sets—five CauGym robustness splits plus CaLM lite, Math500, Minerva Math, and AMC2023—with answers parsed as JSON and scored by exact match.

## Key Results
- GRPO achieves 93.5% accuracy on the CaLM benchmark, far exceeding OpenAI o3 (55.4%) and DeepSeek-R1-671B (57.0%).
- Post-training improves overall causal inference accuracy by up to 42.5% relative to the cold-start baseline.
- Online RL methods (PPO, GRPO) show strongest and most robust performance across rephrased questions, omitted instructions, deconfounding, and noisy data, while preserving general math abilities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The authors attribute the superior performance of online RL, particularly GRPO, to its on-policy data collection and group-relative reward normalization. These mechanisms provide dense, stable feedback that encourages the model to not only mimic correct reasoning traces but also internalize causal inference rules. The combination of a final-answer reward (for correctness) and a format reward (for JSON output) shapes behavior precisely. Unlike offline methods (DPO, KTO) that rely on static preference pairs, online RL continuously interacts with the environment, allowing the model to explore and refine its reasoning under diverse perturbations, which is essential for generalizing to the challenging CauGym test sets. The cold-start SFT stage seeds the model with basic chain-of-thought skills, which are then sharpened by RL alignment.

- **Post-Training Methods (SFT, DPO, KTO, PPO, GRPO)**  
  - *Why needed*: Align a pre-trained model to downstream tasks where ground-truth outputs or preference pairs are available; different methods offer trade-offs in sample efficiency and stability.  
  - *Quick check*: What is the key difference between offline preference methods (DPO) and online RL (PPO)?  

- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**  
  - *Why needed*: A variant of PPO that normalizes advantages within a group of sampled responses, reducing variance and improving sample efficiency; crucial for robust learning in this study.  
  - *Quick check*: How does group-relative normalization affect policy gradient updates compared to standard PPO?  

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Base model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B) → Cold-start SFT (LoRA) → Post-Training module (select one: SFT/DPO/KTO/PPO/GRPO) → Evaluation suite (CauGym, CaLM, Math500, etc.)

**Critical Path**  
1. Generate CauGym training questions and corresponding reasoning traces using the reference model (DeepSeek-R1-0528-671B).  
2. Perform cold-start SFT for 3 epochs to instill basic CoT skills.  
3. For each post-training method, load the cold-start checkpoint and train for 3 epochs using method-specific data and objectives.  
4. After training, evaluate on all nine test sets by prompting with a basic CoT template and parsing the JSON answer.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- **SFT vs RL**: SFT is simpler and more stable but limited to imitation; RL (PPO/GRPO) can surpass the reference behavior but requires careful reward design and more compute.  
- **Offline (DPO/KTO) vs Online (PPO/GRPO)**: Offline methods use a fixed preference dataset and are less prone to training collapse, but lack on-policy adaptation; online methods continuously generate new responses, improving robustness at the cost of instability.  
- **LoRA Efficiency**: Parameter-efficient fine-tuning reduces memory usage but may restrict the model's capacity to fully adapt to complex causal reasoning; full fine-tuning could yield higher gains but is more resource-intensive.

**Failure Signatures**  
- **Reward mismatch**: If the format reward is too strict or the JSON parsing fails, the model receives no signal, leading to stagnant or random performance.  
- **Overfitting to CauGym training distribution**: Large performance drop on rephrased/omitted tests indicates the model memorized surface patterns rather than internalizing causal rules.  
- **KL divergence explosion**: In online RL, an excessively high KL penalty (β) or large policy updates can cause the model to deviate too far from the reference, resulting in unstable training or collapse.

**First Experiments**  
1. **Cold-start baseline replication**: Train only the cold-start SFT on CauGym (3 epochs) and evaluate on CaLM lite; expect ~51% accuracy to confirm correct setup.  
2. **Single GRPO run with reward logging**: Implement GRPO for 1 epoch on a small subset (e.g., 500 questions) while printing sample rewards; verify that format reward correctly identifies valid JSON and that final-answer reward matches ground truth.  
3. **SFT vs GRPO comparison**: On a held-out set of 200 CauGym questions, compare cold-start SFT and GRPO post-training after 1 epoch; GRPO should show a noticeable lift in accuracy (>10%) to validate the benefit of online RL.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What specific mechanisms within online reinforcement learning (GRPO) drive its superior performance in learning causal inference compared to offline methods (DPO, KTO) and SFT?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper shows GRPO achieves 93.5% average accuracy across tasks, while DPO reaches 52.4% and SFT 70.2%. The authors claim online RL is "crucial for teaching them to apply these rules," but do not explain why.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not analyze whether the advantage stems from on-policy data collection, iterative feedback, group-relative reward normalization, or other factors. Without this, the design of future post-training methods remains guided by empirical results rather than theory.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Ablation studies comparing GRPO with variants that alter on-policy sampling, advantage estimation, or reward structures. Representation analysis (e.g., probing for causal concept encodings) during training, and comparing GRPO to PPO to isolate the effect of group-relative rewards.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can models post-trained on CauGym's synthetic, binary-variable causal tasks generalize to real-world causal inference problems with continuous variables, larger graphs, and unmeasured confounding?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Training and test data rely on 10-node DAGs with binary variables generated by single-layer perceptrons. The authors state the models "are not intended to serve as general-purpose causal inference systems," implying limited transferability.
- **Why unresolved**: The robustness tests (redundant/insufficient) only add/remove conditions within the same synthetic framework. Real-world challenges—non-linear relationships, missing variables, continuous outcomes—are not addressed. It remains unclear if the learned causal principles are robust beyond the training distribution.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Evaluation on real-world causal benchmarks (e.g., from health or economics) with continuous variables and potential unmeasured confounding. Alternatively, constructing a more diverse synthetic suite that includes continuous treatments/outcomes, larger graphs (e.g., 50 nodes), and complex noise distributions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Do high scores on internalization tests (CauGym-omitted, -deconfounding) truly indicate understanding of causal theorems, or are models exploiting dataset-specific shortcuts?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Internalization tests show online RL methods maintain performance while others drop dramatically, suggesting "understanding of the underlying causal structure." However, these tests still use similar 10-node DAGs and probability templates.
- **Why unresolved**: Models might learn heuristics tied to the specific structure of synthetic problems (e.g., recognizing confounder patterns) rather than general theorem application. Without adversarial testing, we cannot rule out shallow pattern matching.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Developing internalization tests with drastically varied problem presentations (e.g., different node labeling, narrative styles, or embedding in natural stories) and edge cases where standard heuristics fail. Human expert evaluation of reasoning traces for logical consistency, or automated verification via theorem provers.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: How does the scale and architecture of the base language model affect post-training gains for causal reasoning, and what is the scaling trend?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Main experiments use a 14B Qwen model; appendix tests on 7B and 8B models show GRPO improves them but with lower absolute scores (e.g., 86.1% vs. 93.5%). No systematic study across a wide size range or diverse architectures is provided.
- **Why unresolved**: The interaction between model capacity and post-training efficiency is unknown. It is unclear whether larger models inherently need less tuning, or whether certain architectural features (e.g., attention depth) are more conducive to learning causal principles.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematically post-training models of varying sizes (1B to 100B+) and architectures (decoder-only, encoder-decoder) with identical GRPO recipes. Measuring sample efficiency, generalization, and transfer to unseen causal tasks would reveal optimal scaling regimes and architectural choices.

## Limitations
- **Training hyperparameters**: Exact LoRA configuration (target modules, learning rate, batch size) for cold-start and subsequent steps is not reported, hindering precise replication.
- **Reward implementation**: Details of online RL rewards (JSON parsing regex, scaling between final-answer and format rewards) are missing, making it difficult to reproduce the exact training signal.
- **Data reproducibility**: Random seeds for the 3500 training samples and the construction of the 7000 KTO samples (especially negative selection) are unknown, leading to variability in replication.

## Confidence
- **Post-training improves causal inference (up to +42.5%)**: Medium – based on reported benchmark numbers but without full training logs or variance across seeds.
- **GRPO surpasses much larger models (o3, DeepSeek-R1-671B)**: High – direct tabulated results provided, though baseline model and evaluation details could still hide subtle mismatches.
- **Robustness across rephrased/omitted/noisy tests**: Medium – claims rely on test-set design described, but exact perturbation methods and sample counts need verification.

## Next Checks
1. **Reward function audit**: Implement a minimal GRPO training run and log rewards for a batch of rollouts to confirm that format reward correctly validates JSON output and that final-answer reward scales appropriately with ground truth.
2. **Data split verification**: Regenerate the CauGym training set using the authors’ scripts with a fixed seed (e.g., 42) and validate the size, task balance, and the positive/negative ratio in KTO data.
3. **Cold-start baseline replication**: Before any post-training, run cold-start SFT (3 epochs, LoRA) and confirm accuracy on CaLM lite is within ±2% of the reported ~51% to ensure correct model, tokenizer, and evaluation setup.
