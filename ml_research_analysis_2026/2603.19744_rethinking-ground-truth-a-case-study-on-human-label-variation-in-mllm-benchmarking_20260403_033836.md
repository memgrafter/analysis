# Rethinking Ground Truth: A Case Study on Human Label Variation in MLLM Benchmarking
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19744
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19744
- Reference count: 37
- Primary result: Medium-sized MLLMs can match or outperform larger ones on ambiguous tasks, indicating parameter count alone does not determine sensitivity to human label variation; consensus-only benchmarks may overstate model capabilities.

## Executive Summary
The paper identifies a critical flaw in current MLLM benchmarking: the reliance on aggregated consensus labels ignores Human Label Variation (HLV) in subjective tasks like content moderation. To address this, they propose an evaluation protocol that splits data into high‑agreement (Krippendorff’s α ≥ 0.667) and high‑disagreement (α < 0.667) subsets. In the agreement subset, standard F1‑score compares model predictions to the majority‑vote label. In the disagreement subset, Brier score and Jensen‑Shannon divergence measure alignment with the full distribution of human judgments. Experiments on the German‑language PoliTok‑DE dataset using Gemma 3 and Qwen 2.5 VL models show that while larger models excel on high‑agreement tasks (e.g., political content detection), medium‑sized models often match or surpass larger ones on high‑disagreement tasks (e.g., content intent classification). This demonstrates that model size is not the sole predictor of performance under ambiguity and that consensus‑only benchmarks can overstate capabilities.

## Method Summary
The reproduction notes were not provided. Based on the key outcome, the method comprises: (1) collecting multiple human annotations per item; (2) computing Krippendorff’s α for each item to quantify inter‑annotator agreement; (3) splitting the dataset into an agreement subset (α ≥ 0.667) and a disagreement subset (α < 0.667); (4) for the agreement subset, evaluating model predictions via F1‑score against the majority‑vote label; (5) for the disagreement subset, converting model outputs to probability distributions over labels and computing Brier score and Jensen‑Shannon divergence against the empirical human label distribution. This protocol separates evaluation along the axis of annotator consensus.

## Key Results
- Larger models (e.g., Gemma 3 27B, Qwen 2.5 VL 72B) consistently outperform smaller ones on high‑agreement tasks like political content detection (higher F1).
- On high‑disagreement tasks such as content intent classification, medium‑sized models often achieve lower Brier and JSD scores, matching or beating larger models.
- The findings challenge the widely held assumption that bigger models are universally better and suggest that consensus‑only benchmarks may overstate model capabilities on ambiguous inputs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The protocol leverages the insight that human label variation reflects genuine ambiguity. By splitting based on inter‑annotator agreement, it isolates two distinct evaluation regimes: one where a single correct label exists (high agreement) and one where multiple plausible labels coexist (low agreement). For high‑agreement items, the majority vote approximates a true label, making standard single‑label metrics like F1 appropriate. For low‑agreement items, the distribution of human judgments becomes the gold standard; distribution‑matching metrics (Brier, JSD) then assess whether the model’s uncertainty aligns with human uncertainty. This avoids penalizing models for producing ambiguous predictions on genuinely ambiguous inputs—a pitfall of majority‑vote evaluation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Human Label Variation (HLV)**: The phenomenon where human annotators disagree on the correct label for an instance. *Why needed*: HLV exposes the inadequacy of a single ground truth for subjective tasks. *Quick check*: Annotate a small set of ambiguous items with multiple labelers and compute pairwise agreement.
- **Krippendorff’s α**: A reliability coefficient that measures agreement among multiple annotators, handling missing data and various measurement levels. *Why needed*: Offers a robust per‑item agreement score to drive data splits. *Quick check*: Calculate α on a toy dataset with known disagreement levels to see its sensitivity.
- **Brier Score**: A proper scoring rule for probabilistic predictions, defined as the mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. *Why needed*: Evaluates calibration of a model’s probability distribution against a target (here, human label distribution). *Quick check*: For a binary case with true distribution [0.9, 0.1] and model prediction [0.8, 0.2], Brier = (0.9−0.8)² + (0.1−0.2)² = 0.02.
- **Jensen‑Shannon Divergence (JSD)**: A symmetric, bounded divergence measure between probability distributions, based on Kullback‑Leibler divergence. *Why needed*: Quantifies similarity between model output and human distributions without requiring one to be absolutely continuous. *Quick check*: Compare a uniform distribution to a sharply peaked one; JSD will be larger for the latter.
- **Majority Vote**: The label assigned by the majority of annotators. *Why needed*: Serves as the target for agreement subsets in this protocol. *Quick check*: Tally votes for a few instances; the label with highest count is the majority.
- **Consensus Benchmark**: Standard evaluation that treats majority‑vote labels as ground truth for all items. *Why needed*: The paper argues such benchmarks can overestimate model performance on ambiguous inputs. *Quick check*: Evaluate a model on a low‑agreement dataset; observe that high F1 may coexist with poor distributional alignment.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map**: Raw Data → Compute Krippendorff’s α → Split Dataset (Agreement / Disagreement) → MLLM Inference → Prediction Extraction → Metric Calculation (F1 for Agreement; Brier/JSD for Disagreement) → Results.
- **Critical Path**: Accurate α computation → correct subset split → successful model forward pass → metric computation. The choice of α threshold is a critical design decision influencing all downstream evaluations.
- **Design Tradeoffs**:
  - α threshold (binary split) vs. continuous weighting: binary splitting simplifies analysis but discards granular agreement information.
  - Metric selection: F1 focuses on single‑label accuracy, while Brier/JSD capture distributional fit but may not correlate with semantic appropriateness of ambiguous outputs.
  - Language specificity: Using a German‑only dataset controls for linguistic variation but limits cross‑lingual generalization.
- **Failure Signatures**:
  - Poor F1 on agreement set: model fails even on unambiguous tasks.
  - High Brier/JSD on disagreement set: model does not reflect human uncertainty (e.g., confident but misaligned distribution).
  - No performance gap between sets: model ignores HLV, potentially overfitted to majority labels.
- **First Experiments**:
  1. **Agreement Distribution Analysis**: Compute α for every instance and plot its distribution to quantify overall annotator consensus.
  2. **Metric Exploration**: For a small random sample, compare model probability outputs with human distributions using Brier and JSD; manually verify whether numerical differences align with qualitative plausibility.
  3. **Replication of Main Findings**: Run the full protocol on a subset of PoliTok‑DE with a lightweight model (e.g., Gemma 3 2B) to confirm the pattern that medium models are competitive on disagreement tasks.

## Limitations
- Single‑dataset (PoliTok‑DE) and limited model families (Gemma, Qwen) restrict generalizability.
- Fixed α ≥ 0.667 threshold is arbitrary; results may be sensitive to this cut‑off.
- Brier/JSD measure distributional alignment but may not reflect semantic appropriateness of ambiguous predictions.

- Protocol (α‑split + F1/Brier/JSD) validly separates agreement/disagreement regimes: Medium
- Model size ≠ sensitivity to ambiguity; consensus benchmarks overstate capabilities: Low‑Medium
