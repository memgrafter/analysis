# Automated evaluation of LLMs for effective machine translation of Mandarin Chinese to English

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09998  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09998  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** DeepSeek achieves the highest semantic fidelity in Chinese-English translation, with a 7.02 percentage point advantage over Google Translate in classical literature.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces an automated framework to evaluate large language models (LLMs) on Chinese-English translation across news, classical literature, and modern fiction. The framework combines BERT-based semantic similarity (cosine similarity of multilingual embeddings) and sentiment analysis to compare outputs from Google Translate, GPT-4, GPT-4o, and DeepSeek against expert human translations. Results reveal a clear performance hierarchy: news translation is nearly solved with uniform high scores, while literary translation—especially classical texts—remains challenging. DeepSeek consistently outperforms other systems in preserving semantic fidelity and cultural nuances, though all LLMs over-amplify positive emotions in literary works, losing subtle neutrality. The study highlights the limitations of current automated metrics for culturally dense translation tasks.

## Method Summary
The evaluation pipeline processes source texts and reference translations, generates candidate translations via standardized API calls, and computes two primary automated metrics: paragraph-level semantic similarity using cosine similarity of MPNet embeddings, and sentence-level sentiment distributions using a fine-tuned multilingual BERT model (trained on the SenWave dataset). Additional n-gram analysis compares lexical and stylistic patterns. Scores are aggregated by chapter and genre to rank system performance. The approach enables large-scale, reproducible assessment but relies on a single expert reference per text and a sentiment model trained on modern social media data.

## Key Results
- Translation difficulty increases across genres: news (0.947–0.953 similarity), modern fiction (0.735–0.798), classical literature (0.699–0.769).  
- DeepSeek achieves the highest semantic fidelity, leading Google Translate by 7.02 percentage points in classical literature.  
- All LLMs over-amplify positive emotions in literary texts, reducing neutral sentiment compared to expert translations; DeepSeek’s sentiment distribution deviates least from the expert benchmark.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Semantic fidelity is measured via cosine similarity of multilingual sentence embeddings (MPNet).  
- **Mechanism:** Bilingual sentence pairs (human reference vs. LLM output) are encoded into fixed-dimensional vectors; the cosine between them estimates semantic alignment under the hypothesis that similar meanings map to nearby vectors in a shared embedding space.  
- **Core assumption:** MPNet embeddings capture sufficient cross-lingual equivalence such that vector proximity correlates with human-judged quality for the tested genres.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] mentions "BERT-based semantic similarity (cosine similarity)"; [section 3.3] specifies MPNet; [section 4.3] presents cosine scores as main results.  
- **Break condition:** Fails for passages with multiple valid translations (e.g., poetic ambiguity) where the single reference is not the only correct rendering, as noted in [section 5.2].

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Emotional tone preservation is evaluated by classifying sentence-level sentiment with a fine-tuned BERT model and comparing polarity distributions.  
- **Mechanism:** Each translated sentence is labeled with one of nine emotions from the SenWave-adapted taxonomy; these are aggregated into positive/neutral/negative and compared to the expert translation’s distribution.  
- **Core assumption:** The sentiment model’s categories and polarity mappings remain valid for both modern and classical Chinese texts despite being trained on contemporary multilingual tweets.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.3] describes the BERT-based sentiment stage; [section 4.2] shows distribution charts; [section 5.2] acknowledges dataset origin issues.  
- **Break condition:** Classification accuracy collapses for classical rhetorical devices (e.g., irony, restrained humor) absent from the training corpus, leading to systematic over-identification of categories like "Humour".

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The observed hierarchy of translation difficulty (News < Modern Fiction < Classical Literature) is linked to increasing cultural entrenchment, historical linguistic forms, and stylistic complexity, which amplify performance gaps between systems.  
- **Mechanism:** Genres serve as proxies for challenge vectors: News (standardized terminology, low cultural load), Modern Fiction (contemporary vernacular, narrative complexity), Classical Literature (archaic grammar, allusions, poetic form). Performance gaps widen as these challenges intensify.  
- **Core assumption:** Selected texts (*Global Times*, *Red Sorghum*, *Dream of the Red Chamber*) are genre-representative, and semantic similarity reliably reflects handling of cultural nuance and classical references.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] states the difficulty ordering; [section 3.1] justifies text selection; [section 4.3] tables show narrowing gaps in simpler texts; [section 4.4] links low scores to cultural/poetic failures.  
- **Break condition:** Causality is associative, not proven; an LLM’s architectural advantage might align only with the specific test passages rather than the abstract genre definitions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: BERT & Transformer Bi-Directional Context**  
  **Why needed here:** The entire evaluation relies on contextual embeddings from models like BERT/MPNet for both semantic similarity and sentiment analysis. Understanding bidirectional context is key to grasping why these models capture nuanced meaning better than n-gram models.  
  **Quick check question:** How does bi-directional context in BERT help distinguish the meaning of a Chinese word with multiple segmentations (e.g., "美国会" as "美国/会" vs. "美/国会") compared to a unidirectional model?

- **Concept: Cosine Similarity for Semantic Equivalence**  
  **Why needed here:** It is the primary quantitative output metric. One must understand that a high cosine score is an *estimate* of semantic closeness, not a direct measure of translation quality, based on the hypothesis that similar meanings yield similar vectors.  
  **Quick check question:** If two translations have identical meaning but use radically different vocabulary (e.g., synonyms), would you expect a high or low cosine similarity? What does this imply about the metric's sensitivity to paraphrasing?

- **Concept: Sentiment Analysis as a Proxy for Tone Preservation**  
  **Why needed here:** It provides the secondary evaluation dimension. Emotion labels are a coarse-grained tool to detect if a translation has shifted emotional tone (e.g., neutral literary prose becoming overly positive), a critical failure mode for literary translation.  
  **Quick check question:** Why might a sentiment classifier trained on social media tweets fail to correctly identify the tone in a sentence from an 18th-century Chinese novel?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Manager -> Translation API Client -> Text Preprocessor -> N-Gram Analyzer -> Embedding Engine -> Sentiment Classifier -> Similarity Scorer -> Stats & Visualizer -> Result Aggregator

**Critical path:**  
1. Prepare text chapters (preserving paragraphs).  
2. Call all translation APIs with standard prompt; store outputs.  
3. Compute embedding vectors for each chapter/system pair.  
4. Calculate cosine similarity between system output and expert reference.  
5. Run sentiment classifier on all sentences; aggregate polarity by chapter.  
6. Perform n-gram extraction on pre-processed texts.  
7. Compile chapter-level metrics; aggregate by text type and system.  
8. Validate against known high/low similarity examples from Appendix.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Single reference simplifies evaluation but cannot account for valid translation variance; multi-reference would be more robust but costlier.  
- Paragraph-level semantic similarity preserves context but may dilute local errors; sentence-level sentiment captures fine-grained emotion but may misrepresent overall tone.  
- Fixed sentiment taxonomy (9 categories from Twitter data) is pragmatic but misaligned with classical literary emotions, adding systematic noise.  
- Cosine similarity is cheap and automated but conflates semantic equivalence with factual/idiomatic accuracy.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Cultural/idiomatic collapse: Very low cosine scores (<0.3) for passages with classical idioms or poetry (Appendix Tables A.1, A.5).  
- Polarization of nuance: All LLMs show higher positive and lower neutral sentiment than expert translation for classical literature, signaling "simplification" of ambiguous tones.  
- Inconsistent character rendering: N-gram analysis shows unsystematic handling of proper names/kinship terms (e.g., *Red Sorghum*'s "Uncle Arhat" variably translated).  
- Refusal/omission: Some LLMs refuse profanity, leading to incomplete outputs and artificially low similarity.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Reproduce News Semantic Baseline:** Run pipeline on *Global Times*; verify all systems achieve ~0.95 cosine similarity and inter-system spread <0.6% (Table 5).  
2. **Isolate Classical Literature Gap:** Run pipeline on *Dream of the Red Chamber*; verify DeepSeek's lead (~0.77) over Google Translate (~0.70) and the performance drop magnitude from news (~24.8 pp). Examine a low-score verse (Table A.1) to confirm failure on classical rhetoric.  
3. **Sentiment Polarization Test:** On *Dream of the Red Chamber*, extract sentences classified as "neutral" by expert; re-run sentiment analysis on LLM versions. Quantify drop in neutral classification rate and rise in positive classification.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
How can automated evaluation frameworks be designed to systematically assess the preservation of culturally specific nuances, classical allusions, and figurative expressions in literary translation, which current semantic similarity metrics fail to capture?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors state that "maintaining cultural details, classical references and figurative expressions remain an open problem for all the models" and note that their lowest-similarity examples often involve "content requiring cultural-contextual knowledge beyond linguistic competence." They also observe that DeepSeek shows "systematic superiority" in classical literature but still fails on passages like classical idioms and poetry.  
*Why unresolved:* Current metrics (cosine similarity, BERT-Score) measure lexical/semantic alignment with a single reference but cannot quantify whether a translation preserves culturally embedded meaning that may not align word-for-word with the expert reference. The paper shows high scores for simple passages but catastrophic failure on culturally dense ones.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A dedicated test suite of culturally loaded passages (e.g., classical idioms, historical metaphors, poetic allusions) with multi-dimensional annotations: (1) binary judgment on whether the cultural referent is correctly conveyed, (2) a graded scale for cultural fidelity, and (3) comparisons against multiple expert translations to account for interpretive variation. Success would be demonstrated by a metric that correlates with these annotations better than existing semantic similarity scores.

**Open Question 2**  
To what extent do variations among multiple expert human translations for the same literary text represent acceptable interpretive diversity versus genuine errors, and how can evaluation metrics distinguish between these to set meaningful thresholds for machine translation quality?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors acknowledge a key limitation: "Our research only uses one expert translation per text, which implies that a single expert translation is the correct translation... which can be a problematic assumption concerning literary translations." They cite Howard Goldblatt's admitted 13% abridgment of *Red Sorghum* and note that older translations like Joly's for *Dream of the Red Chamber* reflect historical standards.  
*Why unresolved:* Using a single reference translation treats all deviations as errors, but literary translation often involves legitimate stylistic and interpretive choices. The paper's framework cannot differentiate between a "creative but valid" translation and a "bad" translation, penalizing both equally. This is especially critical for classical literature with multiple accepted interpretations.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Compiling a dataset containing 3–5 high-quality, published expert translations for key literary passages. Analyzing the distribution of semantic similarity scores among these "gold standard" variations would establish a baseline of acceptable divergence. Machine translations scoring within this distribution could be considered plausibly adequate, while those falling far outside could be flagged for probable error.

**Open Question 3**  
Can sentiment analysis models be effectively adapted to handle the implicit, ambiguous, and historically situated emotional expressions found in classical Chinese literature, moving beyond the modern social media-trained models that currently over-polarize sentiment?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors report that all LLMs "over-amplify positive emotions" in literary texts and lose nuanced neutrality. They explicitly state that their sentiment model, trained on the SenWave dataset (modern multilingual tweets), may be unsuitable: "The emotion recognition model... can return incorrect results for classical literature where the emotional tests do not cover the same patterns" and note the "over-capture of the humour sentiment."  
*Why unresolved:* The nine-category sentiment scale (derived from Twitter data) lacks emotional states relevant to classical literature (e.g., melancholy, nostalgia, restrained讽刺 *fengci*). The forced mapping into positive/neutral/negative further flattens subtlety. The paper shows LLMs simplify ambiguous classical expressions into clear positive/negative labels.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Developing a classical Chinese-specific emotion lexicon or fine-tuning a sentiment model on a curated corpus of classical Chinese texts annotated for literary emotions by humanities scholars. A successful model would reduce the "positive amplification" artifact and better match expert translations' neutral sentiment retention (e.g., ~30% in *Dream of the Red Chamber* vs. 21–25% in current LLMs).

**Open Question 4**  
What architectural or training differences in DeepSeek lead to its systematically superior performance in preserving cultural subtleties and semantic fidelity in complex literary texts, and can this be isolated to specific capabilities like handling classical grammar or cultural knowledge?  
*Basis in paper:* DeepSeek consistently outperforms others, with its advantage *amplifying* with text complexity: only 0.59 percentage points ahead in news but 7.02 in classical literature. The authors note DeepSeek "demonstrated better performance in preserving cultural subtleties and grammatical rendering" and showed "unique sensitivity to social hierarchy" in *Dream of the Red Chamber* bigrams and "explicit military terms" in *Red Sorghum*.  
*Why unresolved:* The paper is comparative and descriptive, not ablative. It cannot pinpoint whether DeepSeek's edge comes from: (a) richer training data with more classical/literary Chinese, (b) superior handling of long-range dependencies for narrative cohesion, (c) better few-shot prompting inherent to its architecture, or (d) less aggressive safety/post-processing that avoids refusing culturally sensitive content (e.g., profanity in *Red Sorghum*).  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments comparing DeepSeek's internal representations (e.g., attention patterns on classical idioms vs. other models) and systematic error analysis across failure modes (e.g., classical poetry, kinship terms, profanity). Ablation studies on model size, training data composition, and prompting strategies would isolate the contributing factor. Success would be a replicable methodological insight that improves other models' literary translation.

**Open Question 5**  
How do machine translation systems perform on maintaining narrative coherence, character voice consistency, and cross-paragraph stylistic continuity in long-form literary works, and how does this relate to their paragraph-level semantic similarity scores?  
*Basis in paper:* The paper evaluates at the paragraph/sentence level and notes large inter-chapter variation (e.g., up to 9.77 percentage points in *Red Sorghum*). The authors explicitly state a future need: "The quality of literary translation cannot be evaluated in terms of cross-paragraph coherence... beyond the capabilities of our analysis at the paragraph level." High semantic similarity on isolated passages does not guarantee a coherent whole (e.g., inconsistent character naming as seen in GPT-4's fragmented "uncle luohan/luo/ luo han" in Table 3).  
*Why unresolved:* Current automated metrics are local. A translation could score perfectly on each paragraph yet fail to maintain a consistent narrative voice, character identity, or tonal progression across chapters. The paper's highest-similarity examples are simple, discrete actions; the lowest involve complex cultural/narrative context that requires long-range understanding.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Designing a longitudinal evaluation protocol for chapter-by-chapter translations of novels. Metrics would include: (1) consistency of named entity translation (e.g., does "Commander Yu" remain constant?), (2) stylistic similarity analysis across chapters (e.g., lexical density, sentence complexity profiles), and (3) human evaluation of narrative flow and voice preservation. Correlating these longitudinal metrics with the existing paragraph-level semantic scores would reveal if high local fidelity predicts global coherence.

## Limitations
- Single expert reference per text ignores valid translation variance, potentially biasing similarity scores.
- Sentiment model trained on Twitter (SenWave) is domain-mismatched for classical Chinese, causing misclassifications (e.g., over-identification of "Humour").
- MPNet variant and pooling method are unspecified, hindering reproducibility and score comparison.
- Inferred difficulty hierarchy is correlational; no causal proof links genre features to performance gaps.

## Confidence
- **Framework validity:** Medium (semantic similarity is a proxy; sentiment analysis noisy).  
- **DeepSeek’s superiority:** Medium (gap is large but may not generalize beyond test passages).  
- **Difficulty ordering:** High for observed ranking (News < Modern Fiction < Classical); Medium for causal explanation (genre features cause gaps).

## Next Checks
1. **Multi-reference test:** Add at least two expert translations for a subset of classical works; recompute cosine similarity to gauge sensitivity to reference choice.  
2. **Domain-adapted sentiment:** Fine-tune sentiment classifier on a labeled classical Chinese corpus and compare polarity distributions to the original model.  
3. **Embedding ablation:** Repeat evaluation with alternative multilingual embeddings (e.g., LaBSE, XLM-R) to confirm that the system ranking is not MPNet-specific.
