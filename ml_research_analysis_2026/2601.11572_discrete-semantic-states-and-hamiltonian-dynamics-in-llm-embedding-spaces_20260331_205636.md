# Discrete Semantic States and Hamiltonian Dynamics in LLM Embedding Spaces

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.11572  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11572  
- **Reference count**: 0  
- **Primary result**: Cosine similarity relates to perturbations via `SC = -1 + ½∑Δᵢ²`; Hamiltonian matrix is rank-1; a non-zero minimum similarity `ε` exists.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the discrete geometric structure of large language model (LLM) embedding spaces under L2 normalization using a Hamiltonian formalism borrowed from quantum mechanics. The authors model semantic transitions as perturbations from maximally dissimilar states and derive an exact quadratic relationship between cosine similarity and the squared perturbation magnitude. They show that the transformation (Hamiltonian) matrix is rank-1, implying a single dominant semantic mode, and that a non-zero minimum transformed cosine similarity `ε > 0` emerges, analogous to zero-point energy. Numerical experiments on a small set of prompts using a Gemma 300M model confirm the theoretical predictions, including the equality of direct and indirect cosine similarities via Householder reflections.

## Method Summary
The study uses L2-normalized sentence embeddings from a Gemma 300M model (via Sentence Transformers). The core method defines a Hamiltonian matrix `H` such that the target embedding `b = Ha`, subject to the constraint that `H` preserves the L2 norm of the source (`∑(∑ᵢ hᵢⱼ aⱼ)² ≈ 1`). In the experiment, three prompts (“quick brown fox”, “a fast brown animal”, “a fast brown fox”) are encoded into vectors `vec1`, `vec2`, `vec3`. Householder reflections are built to map `vec1→vec2` (`H12`) and `vec2→vec3` (`H23`). The indirect transformation `vec3_indirect = H23(H12 vec1)` yields a cosine similarity between `vec1` and `vec3_indirect` that matches the direct cosine similarity between `vec1` and `vec3`, demonstrating consistency. The norm-preservation constraint is verified for each `H`.

## Key Results
- Cosine similarity between an L2-normalized embedding and its perturbed maximally dissimilar state satisfies `SC = -1 + ½∑Δᵢ²`, with numerical validation `SC_direct = 0.8761` vs. `SC_indirect = 0.8761`.
- The Hamiltonian matrix `H'` (in the dominant perturbation basis) is rank-1, with eigenvalues exactly `{1,0,…,0}`, indicating a single dominant semantic mode.
- A non-zero minimum transformed cosine similarity `ε > 0` exists (`S'ᶜ,min = ε`), proportional to the square of the dominant eigenvector amplitude, serving as an analogue of zero‑point energy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Cosine similarity as a quadratic perturbation
The L2 normalization constraint (`‖a‖=‖b‖=1`) forces the perturbation vector `Δ = b + a` to satisfy `∑aᵢΔᵢ = ½∑Δᵢ²` (Eq. 15). Substituting into the cosine formula `SC = a·b = a·(-a+Δ) = -1 + ∑aᵢΔᵢ` gives `SC = -1 + ½∑Δᵢ²` (Eq. 17). This links a semantic shift measured by `Δ²` to the geometric constraint. The relation holds exactly when `b` lies on the normalized manifold and the perturbation model `b = -a + Δ` is obeyed; it breaks otherwise.

### Mechanism 2: Rank‑1 Hamiltonian structure
The Hamiltonian `H` (or `H'` in a rotated basis) is defined by `b = Ha` and the norm constraint. Under the perturbation ansatz with all dimensions perturbed proportionally (`Δᵢ = vᵢΔ`), `H'` becomes proportional to the outer product `vvᵀ/‖v‖²` (Eq. 94). Such a matrix is rank‑1. Its trace equals 1 (Eq. 95), forcing eigenvalues `{1,0,…,0}`. Thus only the eigenvector direction `v/‖v‖` carries non‑zero weight, meaning all semantic transformations align with this dominant mode. The rank‑1 conclusion depends on a dense perturbation structure; multiple independent active modes would raise the rank.

### Mechanism 3: Minimum similarity (zero‑point energy)
For any normalized state `a`, the transformed cosine similarity under `H'` is `S'ᶜ = ⟨a|H'|a⟩`. Decomposing `|a⟩` into components along the dominant eigenvector `|1⟩` and an orthogonal remainder `|δ⟩` yields `S'ᶜ = A₁²`, where `A₁` is the amplitude of `|1⟩`. Because embeddings live on a discrete sphere and cannot achieve perfect anti‑alignment (i.e., `b ≠ -a` exactly due to normalization and discrete steps), `A₁` cannot be zero; therefore `S'ᶜ` has a positive minimum `ε`. This `ε` acts as a zero‑point energy—the unavoidable baseline alignment with the dominant semantic mode. This mechanism requires the eigenvalue spectrum of `H'` to be exactly `{1,0,…}`; otherwise `S'ᶜ` could vanish.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Embedding vectors (L2-normalized) → Perturbation model (`Δ` from `b ≈ -a + Δ`) → Hamiltonian `H` (subject to norm constraint) → Unitary transformation `U` to eigenbasis of `H'` → Dominant eigenvector `|1⟩` and amplitude `A₁` → Minimum transformed cosine similarity `ε`.

### Critical path
1. Obtain L2‑normalized embeddings `a` and `b` from the LLM.  
2. Compute perturbation `Δ = b + a` (since `b ≈ -a + Δ`).  
3. Construct unit vector `v` with components `vᵢ = Δᵢ/Δ` (`Δ = ‖Δ‖`).  
4. Assemble rank‑1 Hamiltonian `H' ∝ vvᵀ` and verify constraint `C ≈ 1`.  
5. Diagonalize `H'` via unitary `U` to identify dominant eigenvector and eigenvalue `1`.  
6. Compute `A₁ = ⟨a|1⟩` and `ε = A₁²`; compare across semantic states.

### Design tradeoffs
- **`H` vs. direct `v` estimation**: Using a full Hamiltonian (e.g., via linear regression) allows flexibility but may introduce non‑physical off‑diagonals; the `vvᵀ` form is tightly constrained by the perturbation model.
- **Unitary vs. general transformations**: Householder reflections guarantee norm preservation but are synthetically constructed; a more general `H` that only approximately satisfies the constraint might better capture natural semantics.
- **Interpretation of `|1⟩`**: The dominant eigenvector is mathematically defined but its semantic meaning is unclear; it could be an artifact of the specific `Δ` direction rather than a universal concept.

### Failure signatures
- Constraint `C` deviates significantly from `1` (e.g., `C > 1.001` or `< 0.999`), indicating `H` does not preserve L2 norms.
- Eigenvalue spectrum of `H'` shows more than one value well above numerical noise, contradicting rank‑1.
- For semantically dissimilar pairs, computed `ε` is not consistently positive, or `S'ᶜ` can approach zero, undermining the zero‑point analogue.
- The quadratic relation `SC = -1 + ½∑Δᵢ²` fails numerically, suggesting `b` is not well approximated by `-a + Δ` with normalized `a, b`.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Validate Eq. 17**: Generate random unit vectors `a` and construct `b = -a + Δ` such that `b` is also unit norm. Compute `SC` directly and via `-1 + ½∑Δᵢ²`; expect match to machine precision.
2. **Test rank‑1 on real embeddings**: Use a Sentence Transformer model to encode diverse sentences. For each pair `(a,b)`, compute `Δ`, form `H' ∝ vvᵀ`, and verify eigenvalues are `1` and near‑zeros.
3. **Probe zero‑point energy**: Collect many antonym word pairs, compute `ε = A₁²` for each. Verify `ε > 0` consistently and explore its distribution across model scales and depths.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**: Can the proposed hallucination mitigation strategies (eigenvector distribution regularization, symmetry enforcement) be implemented and shown to reduce hallucinations in LLMs?  
*Basis*: Section VII states that further research is needed to validate these strategies and develop practical algorithms.  
*What evidence would resolve*: Controlled fine‑tuning experiments with the regularization terms, evaluated on hallucination benchmarks (e.g., TruthfulQA, factuality tasks).

**Open Question 2**: Do real LLM embedding spaces exhibit the parity and rotational symmetries hypothesized in the Hamiltonian analysis?  
*Basis*: Section VIII notes the authors do not assert that specific symmetries are necessarily present; their analysis reveals no contradictions that would preclude their existence.  
*What evidence would resolve*: Statistical tests on large sets of embeddings to check invariance of cosine similarity under sign flips of single dimensions (parity) and under swaps of paired dimensions (rotational).

**Open Question 3**: How does the rank‑1 structure of the Hamiltonian matrix relate to the internal mechanics of Transformer models, particularly attention mechanisms?  
*Basis*: Section VIII lists exploring the relationship between attention weights and Hamiltonian elements as a key future direction.  
*What evidence would resolve*: Empirical studies correlating Hamiltonian matrix entries (e.g., off‑diagonal elements) with attention weight matrices for identical prompts across layers and architectures.

## Limitations
- The analysis assumes strict L2‑normalization and the perturbation model `b ≈ -a + Δ`, which may not hold for all LLM embeddings without explicit normalization.
- The experimental validation uses a single non‑standard Gemma 300M model and only three prompts, limiting generalizability and potentially capturing artifacts.
- The Hamiltonian construction via Householder reflections is artificially constrained and may not reflect natural semantic transitions in real embeddings.

## Confidence
- **High**: Underlying mathematical derivations (e.g., Eq. 17, rank‑1 form of `H'`) are sound given the paper’s assumptions.
- **Medium**: Empirical demonstration that direct and indirect cosine similarities match for the specific three‑prompt, Householder‑based experiment is reproducible.
- **Low**: Broad claims about discrete semantic states, universal rank‑1 structure, and zero‑point energy analogy extend beyond the limited evidence and remain speculative.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the key numerical match using public sentence‑transformation models (e.g., `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`) with a large and diverse set of sentence pairs.
2. Fit a general linear transformation `H` to real embedding triples `(a→b→c)` and measure the eigenvalue spectrum; test whether the single‑dominant‑eigenvalue pattern persists beyond the rank‑1 `vvᵀ` construction.
3. For many antonym pairs, compute `ε` across model scales and layers; assess whether `ε` is reliably positive and whether it correlates with model size or layer depth.
