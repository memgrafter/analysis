# Dementia-R1: Reinforced Pretraining and Reasoning from Unstructured Clinical Notes for Real-World Dementia Prognosis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.03018
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03018
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Two-stage RL framework achieves state-of-the-art dementia prognosis (F1 77.03%, AUROC 84.02% on AMC) using a 7B model.

## Executive Summary
Dementia-R1 introduces a two-stage reinforcement learning framework for longitudinal dementia prognosis directly from unstructured clinical notes. The method first performs a Cold-Start pretraining stage where the model learns to predict verifiable clinical indices (MMSE, GDS, CDR) from historical notes, using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with dense, verifiable rewards. This stage builds a foundation for temporal reasoning before fine-tuning on the final binary prognosis task with sparse rewards. Evaluated on real-world Korean hospital cohorts (Asan Medical Center, Haeundae Paik Hospital) and the ADNI benchmark, Dementia-R1 achieves state-of-the-art performance, including an F1 score of 77.03% and AUROC of 84.02% on the Asan Medical Center cohort, AUROC of 78.37% on an independent Parkinson’s disease dementia cohort, and the highest AUROC among LLM baselines at 83.17% on ADNI, all while using a 7B parameter model that rivals much larger general-purpose LLMs. The approach demonstrates improved temporal robustness and cross-disease generalization.

## Method Summary
Dementia-R1 employs a two-stage reinforcement learning pipeline. Stage 1 (Cold-Start) constructs a pretraining dataset by extracting structured clinical indices (MMSE, GDS, CDR) from future visits using an auxiliary LLM. The base model (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B) is trained with GRPO to predict these indices from the preceding note history. The reward is binary based on whether the prediction falls within a clinically defined tolerance. Stage 2 initializes from the Stage 1 checkpoint and fine-tunes on the binary dementia prognosis task using the same GRPO algorithm but with a sparse correctness reward. During inference, the model receives a concatenated sequence of historical clinical notes and generates a reasoning process followed by a boxed answer (0 or 1). The training uses a group size of 8, 5000 steps, and a maximum completion length of 2000 tokens. Patient-level splits ensure no data leakage between stages, with Stage 2 training sets balanced and test sets reflecting natural prevalence.

## Key Results
- F1 77.03% and AUROC 84.02% on the real-world Asan Medical Center cohort.
- AUROC 78.37% on an independent Parkinson’s disease dementia cohort.
- AUROC 83.17% on the ADNI benchmark, the highest among LLM baselines.
- Achieves competitive performance with only a 7B model, outperforming many larger general-purpose LLMs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Cold-Start RL with Verifiable Rewards Mitigates Sparse Reward Problem
**Claim:** Pre-training the model to predict clinically established indices (MMSE, GDS, CDR) provides dense, verifiable supervision that stabilizes reinforcement learning and improves longitudinal reasoning before the final binary prognosis task.
**Mechanism:** Direct RL on the binary dementia label suffers from sparse rewards; the model receives feedback only at the end. By inserting a Cold-Start stage where the model forecasts intermediate scores from historical notes, the RL algorithm (GRPO) receives more frequent, granular rewards. This creates a curriculum: the model first learns to interpret symptom trajectories via index prediction, then transfers these reasoning primitives to the final diagnosis.
**Core assumption:** Predicting verifiable clinical indices requires integrating temporal information from notes, thereby learning transferable skills for prognosis.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] “pre-trains the model to predict verifiable clinical indices (MMSE, GDS, CDR) before fine-tuning on the final binary prognosis, enabling explicit reasoning over non-monotonic symptom trajectories.”
- [section 3.1] “we construct a pre-training dataset by extracting verifiable clinical indices via an auxiliary LLM… The resulting dataset D<sub>pre</sub> = {(H<sub>&lt;t</sub>, s<sub>t</sub>)} trains the model to forecast s<sub>t</sub> from preceding history.”
- [section 4.8.1] Figure 7 shows that adding Stage 1 improves convergence stability and peak AUROC across all cohorts.
- [corpus] Neighboring work (e.g., “Evaluating GPT’s Capability in Identifying Stages of Cognitive Impairment”) investigates LLM detection of cognitive stages but does not use RL with intermediate verifiable rewards for longitudinal prognosis.
**Break condition:** If extraction of clinical indices is highly inaccurate (<90% slot-level), the reward signal becomes noisy and Stage 1 may degrade performance. The paper reports 98.5% audit accuracy (Appendix A.13), but residual errors could still limit gains.

### Mechanism 2: Longitudinal Reasoning Forced via Index Forecasting
**Claim:** Forcing the model to predict structured clinical scores from unstructured notes compels it to internalize temporal patterns of cognitive decline; this generalizes to prognosis even when explicit scores are absent at inference.
**Mechanism:** During Stage 1, the model must synthesize information across visits to forecast a future score. This process teaches it to recognize progressive, fluctuating, or stable trajectories. At Stage 2, the same reasoning ability is applied to the binary label, with ablation studies showing the model retains strong performance even when all clinical indices are masked at inference (Figure 10), indicating it has internalized longitudinal cues beyond explicit score copying.
**Core assumption:** Skills acquired from predicting indices transfer to the final prognosis task because both require understanding symptom evolution.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [section 1] “standard supervised training lacks explicit annotations for symptom evolution, while direct Reinforcement Learning (RL) is hindered by sparse binary rewards.”
- [section 4.8.3] Under Note Only, Index Only, and Note+Index settings, the full input performs best, but note-only performance remains substantial (Figure 9), suggesting the model uses longitudinal cues beyond explicit scores.
- [appendix A.12] Temporal density ablation shows that even at 0% index density (all indices masked), the model retains 78.50% AUROC on AMC, demonstrating internalized reasoning.
- [corpus] “MINDSETS: Multi-omics Integration with Neuroimaging for Dementia Subtyping” uses multi-omics but does not address unstructured note reasoning; no known work uses clinical index forecasting as RL pre-training for dementia.
**Break condition:** If notes lack sufficient temporal detail (e.g., infrequent visits, sparse symptom descriptions), the model cannot learn meaningful trajectories, leading to poor long-horizon performance (as seen in Haeundae cohort with lower index density, Figure 21).

### Mechanism 3: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) Stabilizes Learning with Coarse Rewards
**Claim:** GRPO, which normalizes advantages within a sampled group of outputs, enables effective RL even with binary or thresholded rewards (like correct/incorrect index prediction) by reducing variance without a separate value network.
**Mechanism:** In standard RL, sparse rewards cause high variance in policy gradients. GRPO estimates a baseline from the average return of the current group (G=8), making the advantage relative. This is particularly suitable for Stage 1 where rewards are binary (within tolerance) rather than continuous. The clipping mechanism further stabilizes updates. Stage 1 provides a strong initialization, making Stage 2’s binary reward learnable despite its sparsity.
**Core assumption:** Group-based baseline estimation remains reliable if the group size is sufficient and the initial policy is not random.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [section 3.2] “GRPO… estimates the baseline from a group of outputs without a separate value function… A<sub>i</sub> = [R<sub>cold</sub>(o<sub>i</sub>) − mean(group)] / std(group).”
- [section 4.8.1] Figure 7: “GRPO w/o Stage 1” (direct RL on Stage 2) shows slower convergence and lower peak AUROC compared to Dementia-R1 (two-stage).
- [appendix A.14] Training uses per-device batch size 1, gradient accumulation 2, group size G=8, 5000 steps.
- [corpus] DeepSeek-R1 introduced GRPO for math reasoning; this paper adapts it to clinical longitudinal tasks. No corpus paper directly evaluates GRPO for clinical notes.
**Break condition:** Very small group sizes (<4) would yield noisy baseline estimates, harming stability. The paper uses G=8; deviating significantly may degrade performance. Additionally, if Stage 1 pre-training is poor, the policy may not generate sufficiently diverse outputs for meaningful group normalization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards**  
  **Why needed here:** Direct RL on the final binary dementia prognosis provides only a sparse reward at the end of the reasoning process. Intermediate clinical index predictions offer denser, verifiable feedback that can guide the model during training.  
  **Quick check:** How does the tolerance-aware reward design (±2 for MMSE) help accommodate noise in clinical practice? (Answer: It accounts for minimal detectable change and minor extraction errors, avoiding penalizing clinically acceptable predictions.)

- **Concept: Cold-Start Pre-training (Curriculum for RL)**  
  **Why needed here:** Dementia prognosis requires complex longitudinal reasoning; starting directly with the high-stakes binary reward leads to poor signal. Cold-Start pre-training acts as a curriculum, building foundational trajectory interpretation skills before the final task.  
  **Quick check:** What would likely happen if you fine-tuned the model with full notes and binary reward from random initialization, skipping Stage 1? (Answer: Slower convergence, higher risk of local optima, especially difficulty capturing non-monotonic patterns.)

- **Concept: Temporal Reasoning from Unstructured Clinical Narratives**  
  **Why needed here:** Cognitive decline is documented in free-text notes over time; the model must infer trends (improvement, decline, fluctuation) from narrative descriptions, not just structured scores.  
  **Quick check:** Why is predicting future clinical indices a more suitable pre-training objective than simply predicting the final label? (Answer: It requires the model to process the entire history, compare across visits, and output a numeric score, thereby practicing temporal integration.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input: longitudinal clinical notes H (<t) and optionally extracted clinical indices s<sub>t</sub> from earlier visits → Extraction pipeline: auxiliary LLM (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct) translates Korean notes to English and extracts MMSE/GDS/CDR for Stage 1 only → Training Stage 1 (Cold-Start): base LLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B) + GRPO. Input: notes before cutoff. Output: reasoning + predicted index. Reward: 1 if prediction within tolerance (δ) → Training Stage 2 (Task Fine-tuning): initialize from Stage 1 checkpoint. Same GRPO setup. Input: notes before final assessment. Output: reasoning + binary prognosis (0/1). Reward: 1 if correct diagnosis → Inference: feed concatenated notes; model generates <think> and \boxed{0/1} → Evaluation: compute AUROC, F1, AUPRC; slice by time-gap buckets; compare against LLM and RF baselines.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Build extraction pipeline: ensure <2% slot error via human audit.  
  2. Construct Stage 1 dataset: patient-level split, token cap (≤8000), task-specific sampling (MMSE/GDS/CDR).  
  3. Train Stage 1: GRPO with group size G=8, 5000 steps, bfloat16, learning rate 1e-5 (typical).  
  4. Train Stage 2: same hyperparams, different reward; ensure test patients from Stage 2 are never seen in Stage 1.  
  5. Evaluate: report AUROC/F1 over 5 seeds; conduct temporal bucket analysis; ablation studies.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Indices:** MMSE (fine-grained, abundant), GDS (coarser, stable), CDR (sparser). Paper used all combined; ablation suggested GDS-only sometimes better on AMC/Haeundae but not universal.  
  - **Tolerance (δ):** MMSE uses ±2 (clinically accepted minimal detectable change); coarser scales (GDS, CDR) use exact match to avoid ambiguous boundaries.  
  - **Backbone:** Qwen2.5-7B used; paper also tests Llama-3.1-8B with same gains. Framework is backbone-agnostic but requires sufficient capacity for reasoning over long contexts.  
  - **Token limit:** 8000 tokens; longer histories truncated, possibly losing early visits. Could impact performance on dense histories.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Stage 1 index prediction accuracy <50% on test set → Stage 2 likely ineffective.  
  - Stage 2 AUROC <0.75 on AMC → model not learning longitudinal patterns; check if Stage 1 was trained properly.  
  - Performance drop in longest time bucket (≥24m) >10% compared to shortest → insufficient temporal generalization.  
  - Cross-disease transfer (HPH) AUROC <0.70 → overfitting to AMC style; may need domain adaptation.  
  - Large gap between Note+Index and Note-only performance (>15% AUROC drop when indices masked) → model relying on index shortcuts rather than narrative reasoning.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Ablation: No Cold-Start.** Train GRPO directly on Stage 2 from base model; expect lower AUROC and higher variance (Figure 7).  
  2. **Index masking at inference.** Remove all clinical indices from input; if model uses them as shortcuts, AUROC should drop dramatically on cohorts with dense index measurements (AMC), but less so on ADNI where indices are part of linearized input.  
  3. **Backbone generalization.** Replace Qwen2.5 with Llama-3.1-8B; run all configurations. Expect Dementia-R1 to remain best among Llama variants (Table 19), confirming framework robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can Dementia-R1 be validated and adapted to ensure robust performance across diverse demographic groups, languages, and clinical documentation styles beyond the current Korean hospital cohorts?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "all unstructured clinical data originate from a single nation and ethnic group. This may limit the model’s ability to generalize to other demographics, languages, or documentation styles. Future validation on multi-national and multi-ethnic datasets is necessary."
- **Why unresolved:** The model has only been evaluated on Korean data; biases or linguistic/cultural mismatches could impair real-world deployment globally.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Multi-center studies involving datasets from multiple countries and languages; systematic measurement of performance variation across demographic and linguistic subgroups.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the impact of translating Korean clinical notes to English on the model’s ability to capture subtle linguistic cues of cognitive decline, and how can an end-to-end native-language pipeline be realized?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "linguistic limitations may arise from the translation process. Converting Korean notes into English might result in the loss of subtle nuances, such as syntax errors, which are important for assessing cognitive decline. Future work should apply our method directly to native-language texts."
- **Why unresolved:** The current preprocessing relies on translation, which may discard clinically relevant linguistic patterns; direct processing of Korean notes remains unevaluated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train and evaluate Dementia-R1 directly on original Korean clinical notes; compare performance against translated versions; develop language-specific tokenization or models.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent do extraction errors from auxiliary LLMs (used for clinical index annotation) affect the reinforcement learning training dynamics, and how can the framework be made robust to such noise?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "our framework relies on the performance of the auxiliary Large Language Models (LLMs)… any extraction errors or hallucinations could introduce noise into the reinforcement learning process. A human expert audit (Appendix A.13) confirmed 98.5% slot-level accuracy, suggesting that this noise is negligible in practice, though residual errors merit attention in future deployments."
- **Why unresolved:** Although the error rate is low, the RL process may amplify certain errors; the tolerance-aware reward mitigates but does not fully address this.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments injecting realistic extraction errors; analysis of performance degradation; design of noise-robust RL objectives or confidence-weighted rewards.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Do the observed rankings of pretraining targets (e.g., GDS vs. MMSE) and the monotonic relationship between intermediate supervision density and final prognosis performance hold when ablations are conducted at full dataset scale?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "the pretraining target ablation and density ablation experiments were each conducted on a fixed subsample of ∼1,000 examples due to computational cost, which may limit the generalizability of the observed rankings across indices and density levels. Future work with full-scale ablations could yield more definitive conclusions."
- **Why unresolved:** Current findings are based on small subsamples; trends might change with larger data or different cohorts.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Run pretraining target and density ablations on the entire available pre-training datasets (e.g., all 32k AMC samples) to confirm stability of GDS advantage and density-performance curve.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How can the Cold-Start RL strategy be extended to diseases that lack standardized numerical clinical indices, thereby enabling verifiable rewards for longitudinal reasoning?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "our approach relies on quantifiable clinical indices (e.g., MMSE) for rewards. This limits immediate application to diseases that lack standardized numerical records. Extending this framework to conditions with subjective or qualitative markers remains a challenge for future work."
- **Why unresolved:** Many important clinical conditions (e.g., psychiatric disorders) rely on narrative assessments without numeric scores; the current reward design is inapplicable.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Exploration of alternative reward sources such as expert-derived ordinal scales, natural language inference over clinical notes, or self-supervised prediction of future visit content; case studies on non-quantified diseases.

## Limitations
- Incomplete methodological details (extraction prompt, GRPO hyperparameters, patient splits, evaluation token mapping) hinder full replication.
- Reward noise from auxiliary LLM extraction errors and context truncation may destabilize training and limit performance.
- Generalization to other demographics, languages, and documentation styles remains unvalidated.
