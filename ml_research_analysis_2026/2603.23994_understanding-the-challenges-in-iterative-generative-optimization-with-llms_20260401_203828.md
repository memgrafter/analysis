# Understanding the Challenges in Iterative Generative Optimization with LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.23994
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23994
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LLM-based generative optimization is highly sensitive to three hidden design choices—starting artifact, credit horizon, and experience batching—with no universal settings; engineers must make task-specific decisions to achieve good performance.

## Executive Summary
LLM-based generative optimization, where an LLM iteratively improves a system (e.g., code or prompts) based on execution feedback, promises to automate the development of AI agents but remains under-adopted due to hidden design complexities. This paper systematically investigates three key choices that engineers must make: (1) the initial system decomposition (starting artifact), (2) the length of the execution trace used for feedback (credit horizon), and (3) the number of independent trials batched per optimizer update (experience batching). Controlled experiments across three domains—ML pipeline generation (MLAgentBench), Atari game playing, and prompt optimization (BBEH)—show that each factor significantly influences performance and is highly task-dependent. For instance, modular many-function initialization outperforms monolithic one-function in ML tasks; both one-step and multi-step credit horizons dominate in different Atari games; and batch size optimization yields mixed benefits, with larger batches sometimes causing meta-overfitting. The findings highlight the absence of a universal recipe and call for more principled guidelines to make LLM optimizers practical.

## Method Summary
The study employs the OptoPrime optimizer (Claude Sonnet-3.5) within a generative optimization loop that iteratively revises a trainable artifact. Three case studies isolate specific design decisions: (1) Starting artifact: comparing one-function vs. many-function code decompositions on Spaceship Titanic and Housing Price datasets, optimizing toward Kaggle leaderboard percentiles over 20 steps. (2) Credit horizon: comparing one-step (immediate reward) vs. multi-step (full episode rollout) on eight Atari games, measuring final game scores. (3) Experience batching: comparing batch sizes of 1, 3, and 5 concatenated traces on eight BBEH tasks, tracking validation accuracy across 15 updates. All experiments use execution-based feedback, a memory of 5 previous traces, and natural-language templates to present performance metrics and suggestions to the optimizer. Random seeds (3 per condition) are used to assess variability.

## Key Results
- **Starting artifact:** Many-function initialization dramatically improves performance over one-function (Spaceship Titanic: 86.6% vs 72.7% Kaggle percentile; Housing Price: 75.6% vs 54.6%), demonstrating that the initial code structure strongly determines reachable solutions.
- **Credit horizon:** The optimal trace length is task-specific: multi-step (full rollout) yields higher scores in 4/8 Atari games (Pong, Breakout, Space Invaders, Asterix), while one-step (immediate reward) is superior in the remaining 4 (Freeway, Enduro, Q*bert, Seaquest).
- **Experience batching:** Optimal batch size varies across BBEH tasks; larger batches accelerate early learning but do not guarantee better final generalization. In Boardgame QA, optimization even harms performance (meta-overfitting), dropping accuracy from 0.371 to ~0.28.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The choice of initial system decomposition (starting artifact) significantly influences the final performance of LLM-based generative optimization, with modular many-function initialization sometimes enabling higher solution quality than a monolithic one-function approach.
- Mechanism: The optimizer revises only the components exposed in the starting artifact; a modular design provides a more structured hypothesis space, potentially guiding the LLM toward effective intermediate representations.
- Core assumption: Assumption: The LLM optimizer can successfully modify any trainable component of the initial system, and the search space defined by that system is sufficiently expressive to contain high-performing solutions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "Different starting artifacts determine which solutions are reachable in MLAgentBench."
  - [section 4]: Figures 4a–b and Table 1 show Spaceship Titanic: many‑function (86.6 % percentile) vs. one‑function (72.7 %); Housing Price: many‑function (75.6 %) vs. one‑function (54.6 %).
  - [corpus]: Corpus evidence is weak; related works (e.g., VeRO, EPOCH) study agent optimization broadly but do not isolate the effect of starting artifacts.
- Break condition: If the initial artifact’s modularization is misaligned with the task structure or overly constrains editable code, the optimizer may fail to discover high‑performing solutions regardless of update steps.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The length of the execution trace provided to the LLM optimizer (credit horizon) is a critical design choice; both one‑step (immediate reward) and multi‑step (full rollout) traces can succeed depending on the task, but mismatched horizons degrade performance.
- Mechanism: Short horizons provide frequent updates with potentially noisy or myopic feedback; long horizons give accurate credit assignment but reduce update frequency and increase context demands. The optimal horizon depends on the alignment between immediate rewards and long‑term outcomes.
- Core assumption: Assumption: The trace includes sufficient information for the optimizer to make beneficial revisions, and the optimizer can correlate actions with eventual outcomes within the provided horizon.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "truncated traces can still improve Atari agents."
  - [section 5]: Figure 5 shows multi‑step outperforms one‑step in 4/8 games (Pong, Breakout, Space Invaders, Asterix) while one‑step is better in the other 4 (Freeway, Enduro, Q*bert, Seaquest).
  - [corpus]: "Learning Game‑Playing Agents with Generative Code Optimization" (paper ID 52808) also uses full rollouts but does not compare horizons; evidence is indirect.
- Break condition: When the task requires long‑term planning and immediate rewards mislead (e.g., Space Invaders), short horizons may lead to suboptimal policies; conversely, tasks where immediate feedback is reliable may suffer from unnecessary delay with long horizons.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The number of independent execution traces batched together per optimizer update (experience batch size) impacts convergence speed and final generalization; optimal batch size is task‑dependent and larger batches do not guarantee better final performance.
- Mechanism: Larger batches aggregate diverse feedback, potentially stabilizing the update direction and accelerating early learning; however, they may also blend conflicting signals or cause meta‑overfitting to the batch distribution. Smaller batches yield noisier updates but may explore more diverse revisions.
- Core assumption: Assumption: The optimizer can synthesize information from multiple traces to produce a single update that improves performance across the underlying distribution.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "larger minibatches do not monotonically improve generalization on BBEH."
  - [section 6]: Table 2 shows varying optimal batch sizes (1, 3, 5) across tasks; Figure 7 shows larger batches often learn faster but plateau early; Boardgame QA exhibits meta‑overfitting (performance degrades from 0.371 to ~0.278).
  - [corpus]: Corpus includes AceGRPO and EPOCH that address multi‑round optimization but not directly batch size; evidence is limited.
- Break condition: If batch size is too large, the optimizer may overfit to the aggregated feedback and lose generalization; if too small, the signal may be too noisy to make consistent progress.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Training Data → Execution Engine (runs Current System) → (Input, Output) → Feedback Oracle → (Input, Output, Score) traces → Learning Context Builder (applies credit horizon, batches) → Prompt → LLM Optimizer → Edit Instructions → Update Applier → Updated System.

### Critical path
1. Define initial system and mark optimizable components.
2. For each iteration: sample a batch of inputs → execute current system on each → record (input, output, feedback) traces → apply credit horizon truncation → concatenate traces into one learning context → call LLM optimizer → apply proposed edits to system → repeat.

### Design tradeoffs
- Starting artifact: Modularity vs simplicity; many‑function gives clearer interfaces but may over‑specify structure.
- Credit horizon: Short traces → frequent updates but risk myopia; long traces → accurate credit but slower iteration and larger context.
- Batch size: Larger → stable gradient‑like signal, faster early learning but possible meta‑overfitting; smaller → noisy but diverse, may generalize better.

### Failure signatures
- No improvement across iterations (optimizer stuck).
- Validation performance diverges from training (meta‑overfitting), as in Boardgame QA.
- Erratic performance swings indicating unstable updates.
- Early plateau at sub‑optimal level (e.g., one‑function Spaceship Titanic).

### First 3 experiments
1. Starting Artifact Replication: Implement both one‑function and many‑function initializations for the Spaceship Titanic task (or a simplified regression task) and verify that the many‑function version achieves higher validation F1 after 20 steps.
2. Credit Horizon Calibration: Choose a single Atari game (e.g., Pong), run both one‑step and multi‑step configurations for 5 trials, and confirm that multi‑step yields higher final scores (or observe the task‑dependent pattern).
3. Batch Size Sweep: On a BBEH task like Boolean Expressions, run batch sizes 1, 3, and 5; plot validation accuracy curves to reproduce the finding that optimal batch size is task‑specific and larger batches may plateau early.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Can we discover universal "default" starting artifacts or modularization patterns that reliably enable successful optimization across diverse agentic tasks?  
**Basis in paper:** The conclusion states: "We conclude that the lack of a simple, universal way to set up learning loops across domains is a major hurdle for productionization and adoption." It also conjectures that we may eventually discover such artifacts, analogous to how Transformers became a broadly useful inductive bias.  
**Why unresolved:** The study found that optimal starting points (e.g., one-function vs. many-function for ML pipelines) are task-dependent and dramatically affect reachable solutions. No single initialization strategy worked best across the studied tasks.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic large-scale empirical studies across a wide variety of agentic domains (code generation, reasoning, robotics) comparing a standardized set of initialization patterns (monolithic, modular, hierarchical, etc.), measuring success rates, sample efficiency, and final performance to identify if any patterns consistently outperform others or if domain-specific heuristics are necessary.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** What theoretical or heuristic principles can guide the selection of credit horizon (trace length) for multi‑step generative optimization, given the observed trade‑off between update frequency and long‑term credit assignment?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors frame the credit horizon problem and state: "there is no universal answer to 'how long should the credit horizon be?' The agent engineer must consider the causal structure of the task." They note the parallel to horizon debates in RL but point out the lack of analogous guidance for LLM optimizers.  
**Why unresolved:** Experiments in Atari showed that multi‑step (full rollout) optimization outperformed one‑step in 4 out of 8 games, while one‑step was better in the other 4. The optimal choice depended on the game's strategic structure (e.g., immediate vs. delayed consequences).  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Developing a taxonomy or predictive model for tasks that estimates the "effective horizon" of reward signals. This could involve: (a) analyzing the temporal credit assignment structure of tasks (e.g., using causal analysis); (b) empirical studies measuring the correlation between immediate-step improvements and end-of-episode performance; (c) designing adaptive mechanisms that dynamically adjust the credit horizon during optimization based on learning progress.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Why does experience batching (minibatch size) exhibit such strong task‑dependent effects on generalization and convergence in generative optimization, and can we predict the optimal batch size from task characteristics?  
**Basis in paper:** The BBEH case study concludes: "the optimal number of triplets is different for each task... Larger batches can speed up early learning but they do not reliably improve final generalization." This mirrors batch size effects in SGD but without clear heuristics.  
**Why unresolved:** Results varied: some tasks (e.g., Disambiguation QA) performed best with batch size 1, others (e.g., Geometric Shapes) with batch size 3, and others (e.g., Dyck Languages) with batch size 5. Additionally, meta-overfitting was observed in Boardgame QA (optimization degraded performance). The underlying reasons for these differences are not explained.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying task properties (e.g., dataset size, noise level, task complexity, diversity of reasoning patterns) and measuring how they interact with batch size. Theoretical analysis of how LLM optimizers aggregate conflicting evidence across batched traces (e.g., is there an "effective gradient" noise scale?). Alternatively, developing adaptive batching strategies that adjust batch size based on validation performance or diversity metrics.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** What causes meta‑overfitting—where optimization on a small training set hurts generalization to unseen examples—and how prevalent is it across different agentic optimization setups?  
**Basis in paper:** In the BBEH experiments, the paper notes: "For Boardgame QA, optimization degrades performance from 0.371 (unoptimized) to ~0.28" and suspects "a type of meta‑overfitting: the optimizer finds prompts and code that perform well on the 15 training examples but fail to generalize." Similar overfitting was observed in the MLAgentBench learning curves (Figure A3).  
**Why unresolved:** The phenomenon is identified but not systematically studied. It is unclear whether meta‑overfitting is more severe for certain types of tasks (e.g., small datasets, high‑dimensional prompt spaces), credit horizon configurations, or batching strategies. No mitigation strategies (e.g., stronger regularization, validation‑based early stopping, diversity‑promoting objectives) are explored.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Extending the experimental framework to include a larger, more diverse suite of tasks with known generalization difficulties, measuring the gap between training‑set and test‑set performance across optimization iterations. Investigating factors like the size of the training set, the complexity of the optimizable artifact (e.g., prompt length vs. code), and the nature of the feedback (e.g., sparse vs. dense). Testing intervention strategies such as explicit regularization terms in the feedback, dropout‑like random perturbations, or using a separate validation set for checkpoint selection more aggressively.

## Limitations
- Insufficient detail on prompt templates, LLM hyperparameters, and data splits limits reproducibility and sensitivity analysis.
- Findings are restricted to a narrow set of domains and a single LLM/optimizer, raising concerns about generalizability to other tasks or models.
- The study lacks formal theoretical analysis and does not investigate mitigation techniques for failure modes such as meta‑overfitting.

## Confidence
- Starting artifact impact: Medium
- Credit horizon: Medium
- Experience batching: Medium
