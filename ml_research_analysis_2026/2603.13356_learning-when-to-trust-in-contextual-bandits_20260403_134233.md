# Learning When to Trust in Contextual Bandits

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.13356
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13356
- Reference count: 9
- Primary result: CESA-LinUCB achieves near-zero latent regret in settings with 80% adversarial evaluators, while baselines incur linear regret.

## Executive Summary
Contextual sycophancy occurs when evaluators provide truthful rewards in benign contexts but strategically biased rewards in critical contexts, causing standard contextual bandit algorithms to suffer from objective decoupling. This paper introduces CESA-LinUCB, a method that learns a high-dimensional trust boundary for each evaluator using epistemic forecasting, trust-weighted ridge regression, and sparse axiomatic checks for ground truth. Theoretically, CESA-LinUCB achieves sublinear regret of order ~O(√T·κ), where κ is the VC-dimension of the adversary's lying strategy. Empirically, in a hostile setting with 80% adversarial evaluators, CESA-LinUCB attains near-zero latent regret (~100) while baselines exceed 1900. The approach scales linearly with context dimension and requires only approximately 5% axiomatic supervision for optimal performance.

## Method Summary
CESA-LinUCB constructs per-evaluator trust boundaries by combining epistemic uncertainty forecasts with ridge regression that weights rewards by estimated trust. Sparse axiomatic checks—where ground-truth rewards are queried—calibrate the trust model. This allows the algorithm to discount biased evaluators in critical contexts while retaining their input in benign ones, thus avoiding contextual objective decoupling.

## Key Results
- Theoretical regret: ~O(√T·κ) where κ is the VC-dimension of the adversary's lying strategy.

- Efficiency: scales linearly with context dimension; requires only ~5% axiomatic supervision for peak performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CESA-LinUCB mitigates contextual sycophancy by dynamically separating evaluators into trusted and distrusted regimes based on context. Epistemic forecasting identifies when an evaluator's reward is likely biased, and trust-weighted regression reduces their influence on policy updates. Sparse axiomatic checks provide occasional ground truth to correct systematic errors in the trust boundary, preventing drift. This adaptive trust mechanism ensures that the learned policy remains aligned with true objectives even when most evaluators are adversarial in critical contexts.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Trust Boundary Learner → Epistemic Forecaster → Trust-Weighted Ridge Regression → Policy Update; Sparse Axiomatic Checks (feedback loop)
- **Critical path**: For each round: observe context, receive evaluator reward, update trust boundary using forecaster and regression, compute action via LinUCB with weighted rewards, occasionally trigger axiomatic check to update ground truth model.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Sparsity of axiomatic checks reduces supervision cost but risks trust boundary drift; high-dimensional trust boundaries capture complex bias patterns but increase computational load; linear scalability assumption may not hold for non-linear contexts.
- **Failure signatures**: Sudden regret spike indicates trust boundary failure; high variance in trust scores across similar contexts suggests overfitting; low usage of axiomatic checks may imply insufficient calibration.
- **First experiments**:
  1. Synthetic validation: Generate data with known sycophancy patterns (e.g., bias activated above context threshold) and verify trust boundaries recover the pattern.
  2. Adversarial scaling: Vary adversarial evaluator fraction (0%, 50%, 80%, 95%) and plot regret; expect CESA-LinUCB to degrade gracefully while baselines fail abruptly.
  3. Supervision ablation: Reduce axiomatic query rate (0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, 10%) and measure peak regret; identify minimal effective supervision level.

## Limitations
- Adversary model realism: The assumption of contextual sycophancy lacks empirical validation.
- Hidden implementation details: Core algorithms (boundary learning, trust-weighted regression, sparse checks) are not described, hindering reproducibility.
- Baseline comparison fairness: Baseline results are presented without tuning details, context distributions, or run statistics, questioning the robustness of improvements.
- Scalability claims: Linear scaling with context dimension is asserted without profiling or complexity analysis.
