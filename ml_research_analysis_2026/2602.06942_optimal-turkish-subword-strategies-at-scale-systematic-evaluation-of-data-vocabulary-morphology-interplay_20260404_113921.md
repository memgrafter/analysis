# Optimal Turkish Subword Strategies at Scale: Systematic Evaluation of Data, Vocabulary, Morphology Interplay

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06942  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06942  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Optimal WordPiece tokenizers for Turkish use vocabularies of 32k–52k with fertility 1.4–1.7 and continuation 0.30–0.45, balancing sequence efficiency with morphological fidelity.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a systematic evaluation of Turkish subword tokenization by jointly varying vocabulary size and training corpus scale across multiple tokenizer types, including WordPiece, morphology-aware, and character/word baselines. The study introduces morphology-aware diagnostics (boundary F1, lemma integrity) and evaluates on downstream tasks. Key findings indicate that for mixed-domain data, WordPiece tokenizers with vocabularies between 32k and 52k achieve a fertility of 1.4–1.7 and continuation rate of 0.30–0.45, which balances sequence length and morphological transparency, yielding strong performance on both syntax/morphology tasks and semantic benchmarks. Morphology-aware tokenizers improve interpretability and morph-sensitive tasks but depend on linguistic resources. Practical guidelines recommend using 32k–52k WordPiece by default, with smaller vocabularies for grammar-centric applications and larger ones for NER-heavy pipelines.

## Method Summary
The study used the BellaTurca corpus at three scales (Minimal ~5 GB, Medium ~20 GB, Alldata ~80 GB) to train tokenizers with vocabulary sizes ranging from 2k to 128k. Tokenizer types included WordPiece, a morphology-aware tokenizer based on Zeyrek/spaCy, and character/word baselines. Intrinsic evaluation used fertility, continuation rate, and morphology-aware metrics (boundary F1, lemma integrity). Pretrained BERT-base models (12 layers, hidden size 768) were trained on the Alldata corpus for 1M steps with sequence length 128. Downstream evaluation covered TrGLUE (NLI, STS, sentiment, acceptability), WikiNER, and BOUN Treebank (POS, dependency parsing, morphology). Results were analyzed to correlate intrinsic metrics with downstream performance, identifying optimal configurations.

## Key Results
- Optimal WordPiece tokenizers for mixed-domain Turkish data use 32k–52k vocabularies, achieving fertility 1.4–1.7 subwords/word and continuation rate 0.30–0.45.
- Morphology-aware tokenizers yield superior boundary alignment and performance on morph-sensitive tasks but depend on external analyzers; character-level models are robust but inefficient; word-level fails due to high OOV.
- Task-dependent tuning emerges: grammar-centric tasks (POS, DEP) favor slightly smaller vocabularies (20k–32k), while NER benefits from larger ones (32k–52k or up to 128k).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Vocabulary size and tokenizer training corpus scale interact non-linearly to determine the trade-off between sequence efficiency (fertility, continuation rate) and morphological fidelity (boundary alignment, lemma integrity) in WordPiece tokenizers for Turkish.
- **Mechanism:** With a small vocabulary on a large, diverse corpus, the tokenizer cannot assign whole morphemes as reusable units and defaults to short character fragments, causing high fertility (~6.5) and near-universal continuation (~0.98). Conversely, a very large vocabulary on the same corpus over-embeds frequent inflected forms, fusing morphemes into single tokens and lowering fertility (<1.2) and continuation (<0.15). A mid-size vocabulary (32k–52k on mixed-domain data) achieves equilibrium: fertility ~1.4–1.7 and continuation ~0.30–0.45, preserving visible morphological seams while keeping sequences compact.
- **Core assumption:** Morphological structure is recoverable and beneficial for syntax-sensitive tasks when subword boundaries align with morpheme boundaries; the optimal band is where sequences are short enough for efficient training but morphology remains legible.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports the optimal band and metrics; Section 6.3 discusses the non-linear interaction; Table 2 and Figure 12 quantify fertility/continuation across vocabularies and corpora.
- **Break condition:** If training corpus is very small (~5 GB) or extremely homogeneous, the optimal vocabulary may shift downward; if corpus is huge (~80 GB), a slightly larger vocabulary (up to 52k) may be needed to avoid over-fragmentation.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Morphology-aware subword tokenization (using FST/analyzer-driven splits) improves performance on syntax and morphology tasks (POS, dependency parsing, morphological tagging, acceptability) by explicitly preserving morpheme boundaries, which provides the model with direct access to inflectional cues.
- **Mechanism:** By splitting words into stems and suffixes, the representation disentangles lexical meaning from grammatical function. This allows the model to generalize across inflectional paradigms and leverage suffixes (case, tense, person) as discrete, reusable features. The resulting representations are more data-efficient for morph-heavy tasks and yield more interpretable attributions.
- **Core assumption:** Gold morphological segmentation is accurate and stable; the linguistic analyzer provides a consistent, correct decomposition for the majority of forms. The benefit comes from explicit boundary alignment, not merely from vocabulary size reduction.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes interpretability and morph-sensitive improvements; Section 5.3 shows morphology-aware tokenizers achieve near-perfect token coverage with a small morpheme inventory and consistently outperform baselines; Section 5.3.4 explainability heatmaps confirm model attention on morphemes.
- **Break condition:** If the linguistic analyzer is inaccurate for the target domain (e.g., slang, loanwords, orthographically noisy text), forced splits may introduce noise. For semantically-driven tasks (e.g., sentiment), the advantage diminishes.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The vocabulary-corpus-success triad dictates that the optimal tokenizer configuration is task-dependent: grammar-centric tasks (POS, DEP, CoLA) favor slightly smaller WordPiece vocabularies (20k–32k) to retain more morphological structure, while NER benefits from larger vocabularies (32k–52k) that preserve entity stems intact.
- **Mechanism:** Tasks differ in sensitivity to morphological fidelity vs. sequence compression. Syntax and morphology rely on explicit inflectional boundaries; over-merging (large vocabularies) obscures cues. NER depends on stable recognition of named entities; over-fragmentation (small vocabularies) breaks entity tokens across subwords. Corpus composition shifts the optimum: larger, more diverse corpora require larger vocabularies to avoid over-fragmentation.
- **Core assumption:** Different downstream tasks impose different constraints on tokenization quality; a single vocabulary size cannot be optimal for all.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract recommends task-specific adjustments; Section 6.5 (RQ1–RQ4) summarizes mid–large vocabularies (32k–52k) overall but NER peaks at higher ends (128k on Books) while POS/DEP peak around 52k; Table 11 and Table 12 show non-monotonic patterns.
- **Break condition:** If the application mixes tasks (e.g., parsing and NER), the 32k–52k band is a safe default; extreme specialization may require tuning beyond this band.

## Foundational Learning

**Fertility and Continuation Rate**  
Why needed: These are primary, model-agnostic metrics that quantify the fragmentation/compression trade-off. Fertility (subwords/word) governs sequence length and training cost; continuation rate (fraction of non-initial subwords) indicates how often morphemes are split across tokens. Together they predict whether morphology will be visible or obscured.  
Quick check: If a tokenizer has fertility=1.6 and continuation=0.38 on Turkish, is it likely over-fragmenting or over-merging? What range is considered balanced?  
Answer: These values lie within the balanced band (1.4–1.7 fertility, 0.30–0.45 continuation), indicating a good compromise between efficiency and morphological fidelity.

**Morphology-Aware Diagnostics (Boundary F1, Lemma Integrity)**  
Why needed: Standard tokenization metrics (fertility, OOV) do not measure alignment with linguistic structure. Boundary micro/macro-F1 and lemma single-token rate directly assess whether subword splits respect gold morpheme boundaries and preserve lemma forms. These metrics explain downstream performance on syntax/semantics tasks.  
Quick check: What does a high macro-F1 but low lemma single-token rate suggest about a tokenizer’s behavior on Turkish words with long suffix chains?  
Answer: It indicates that the tokenizer correctly identifies most individual morpheme boundaries (high macro-F1) but tends to split the lemma itself across multiple subwords (low lemma single-token rate), which may hurt tasks requiring root word identification.

**Vocabulary–Corpus Coupling**  
Why needed: Tokenizer quality depends on both vocabulary size and the scale/domain of the training corpus. A 32k vocabulary on 5 GB of data may over-fragment, while the same vocabulary on 80 GB may be well-calibrated. Understanding this interaction prevents misconfiguring tokenizers for available data.  
Quick check: If you have 80 GB of mixed-domain Turkish text, what vocabulary range should you try for a WordPiece tokenizer to avoid extreme fragmentation?  
Answer: Mid to mid-large vocabularies (32k–52k) are recommended; larger than 52k risks over-merging, smaller than 32k risks over-fragmentation.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Tokenizer training module → Tokenizer evaluation module → Pretraining module → Downstream fine-tuning module → Evaluation & diagnostics module.

**Critical path:**  
1. Select tokenizer type and define vocabulary size sweep (e.g., 2k, 5k, 10k, 20k, 32k, 52k, 128k).  
2. Choose tokenizer training corpus scale (Minimal/5 GB, Medium/20 GB, Alldata/80 GB) and domain mix.  
3. Train tokenizers for each (vocab size, corpus) pair.  
4. For each tokenizer, compute intrinsic metrics (fertility, continuation, boundary F1 on morphology test set).  
5. Preprocess pretraining corpus with each tokenizer and train a Transformer (controlled for architecture/hyperparameters).  
6. Fine-tune on each downstream task and record performance.  
7. Analyze correlations between intrinsic metrics and downstream scores to identify optimal configurations.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Vocabulary size vs. fragmentation: Small vocabularies inflate sequence length (costly training) but preserve morphemes; large vocabularies compress sequences but may fuse morphemes and harm morph-sensitive tasks.  
- Tokenizer type vs. resource dependence: Morphology-aware tokenizers require a high-quality analyzer/FST and may over-segment frequent forms if not frequency-weighted; WordPiece is data-driven but may not align with morphology without careful sizing.  
- Corpus scale vs. compute: Larger tokenizer training corpora improve vocabulary quality but increase preprocessing cost; diminishing returns beyond 20 GB for mid-size vocabularies.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Over-fragmentation: fertility > 3.0, continuation > 0.6, boundary recall near 1.0 but precision low, exact morph sequence match low, CoLA/POS scores poor despite long sequences.  
- Over-merging: fertility < 1.2, continuation < 0.15, lemma boundary hit low, CoLA/Morph accuracy drops while NER may be stable.  
- Corpus-vocabulary mismatch: training on 5 GB but using 128k vocabulary yields extreme fragmentation (fertility ~6); training on 80 GB with 2k vocabulary also fragments severely.  
- Task-specific failure: NER F1 low while POS high → tokenizer breaking entity spans; CoLA near zero while SST-2 reasonable → tokenizer losing acceptability cues.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Tokenizer intrinsic sweep:** Train WordPiece tokenizers at 2k, 32k, 128k on a fixed 20 GB mixed corpus. Compute fertility, continuation, and boundary micro-F1 on the Turkish morphology test set. Expect: 2k → high fertility/high continuation/low F1; 128k → low fertility/low continuation/medium F1; 32k → moderate values and higher F1.  
2. **Downstream correlation:** For each tokenizer from Exp. 1, pretrain a BERT-base model (1M steps, 128 seq len) on the same 20 GB corpus. Fine-tune on POS (BOUN) and NER (WikiNER). Plot downstream accuracy vs. fertility/continuation/F1. Expect POS to peak at moderate fertility (1.4–1.7) and high boundary F1; NER may tolerate slightly lower F1 if entity stems stay intact.  
3. **Morphology-aware vs. WordPiece:** Train a morphology-aware tokenizer (using Zeyrek/spaCy) and a WordPiece tokenizer (32k) on the same 20 GB corpus. Compare (a) token coverage with 10k most frequent subwords, (b) boundary F1, and (c) fine-tuned POS/NER scores. Expect morphology-aware to have higher boundary F1 and better POS, possibly similar NER if entity stems are preserved.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization to other agglutinative languages:** The study only examines Turkish; cross-lingual validation in related families (e.g., Finnish, Hungarian, Kazakh) remains future work. It is unknown whether the optimal 32k–52k vocabulary range and fertility/continuation band generalize due to possible differences in morpheme inventories and linguistic properties.
- **Decoder-only language models:** All experiments used BERT-style encoders. Tokenization trade-offs for autoregressive models (e.g., GPT) are unexplored; different objectives and longer contexts could shift the optimum.
- **Resource-free morphology-aware tokenization:** The morphology-aware approach depends on external linguistic resources (Zeyrek, spaCy). Developing unsupervised or weakly supervised methods that induce morpheme-like units without such analyzers is an open challenge for low-resource languages.
- **Adaptive tokenization:** Given task-specific optimal granularities (e.g., smaller vocab for POS, larger for NER), a single static tokenizer compromises. An adaptive mechanism that dynamically adjusts segmentation granularity per context or task could combine benefits but is not investigated.

## Limitations
- Corpus composition and preprocessing (BellaTurca) are underspecified, preventing exact reconstruction of training data splits.
- Pretraining hyperparameters (batch size, learning rate schedule, warmup) are not fully reported, potentially affecting convergence and final accuracy.
- Findings are restricted to BERT-base on Turkish; generalizability to other languages, domains, or larger model architectures remains unknown.

## Confidence
- Optimal WordPiece band (32k–52k): High  
- Morphology-aware tokenization benefits: Medium  
- Task-dependent vocabulary shifts: High  
- Practical prescription: High
