# How Controllable Are Large Language Models? A Unified Evaluation across Behavioral Granularities
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.02578
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02578
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A hierarchical benchmark (SteerEval) reveals prompt-based steering robustly outperforms activation-based methods across all granularities, with activation-based control degrading sharply at finer behavioral levels (L2/L3).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces SteerEval, a benchmark that organizes steering targets hierarchically—by domain (personality, sentiment, language features) and specification level (L1: what to express, L2: how to express, L3: how to instantiate)—to link high-level intent to concrete output. Using an automated synthesis pipeline, it provides paired preference data for evaluating steering methods. Experiments on three open-source models show that prompt-based steering (0/3-shot) maintains stable performance across all levels, while activation-based methods (PCA, DiffMean, RePS) degrade significantly at finer granularities, with domain-dependent success (personality > sentiment > language features). The findings establish a principled foundation for assessing and developing reliable LLM behavior control.

## Method Summary
SteerEval is constructed via an automated pipeline: a Concept Synthesizer generates hierarchical L1–L3 concept trees; a Question Pipeline creates and refines (via pivot) concept-related questions; an Answer Pair Generator produces minimally edited contrastive responses; a Validation Layer performs automated and manual checks; and an Evaluation Engine (GPT-4.1-mini) scores Concept, Instruction, and Fluency (0–4) on test outputs, computing a Harmonic Mean. Steering is tested on Gemma-2-9B, Qwen-2.5-7B, and Llama-3.1-8B using prompt-based (0/3-shot) and activation-based (PCA, DiffMean, RePS) methods. Activation interventions are applied at a single mid-to-late layer per model, with scaling factors optimized on a validation set per concept. Results are aggregated by domain and level.

## Key Results
- Prompt-based steering consistently maintains a harmonic mean around 3.0 from L1 to L3, while activation-based methods exhibit sharp degradation at L2/L3 (e.g., RePS on Gemma: 2.94 → 1.72 → 2.00).
- Activation-based steering performs best on personality, followed by sentiment, then language features.
- Few-shot prompting improves L1/L2 but can harm L3 performance due to interference from surface cues in demonstrations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
1. **Hierarchical specification aligns control with processing depth.** L1 targets high-level intent (computational), L2 constrains expression strategy (algorithmic), L3 enforces atomic surface markers (implementational). Finer levels require deeper, more distributed representations that exceed the capacity of fixed-direction activation interventions, which primarily affect shallower features. Evidence: The benchmark’s L1–L3 definitions and the observed degradation pattern for activation methods.
2. **Prompt-based steering leverages contextual flexibility.** Prompts dynamically activate pathways across the network to compose features for L2/L3 constraints. Activation-based steering applies a static vector, insufficient for encoding rare, specific markers needing coordinated multi-dimensional changes. Evidence: Stable prompt-based HM across levels vs. activation drop; few-shot gains for L2/L3.
3. **Domain differences reflect abstraction and data distribution.** Personality traits are high-level, stable priors easier to bias via coarse activations. Language features are low-level, context-specific surface patterns requiring precise token control that activation methods struggle with. Evidence: RePS HM ordering: personality > sentiment > language features.

## Foundational Learning
- **Marr’s Three Levels:** Distinguishes computational (L1: “what”), algorithmic (L2: “how”), and implementational (L3: “instantiate”) specifications. *Why needed:* Provides theoretical scaffold for probing different depths of behavioral control. *Quick check:* Map “express enthusiasm” (L1) → “use celebratory emphasis” (L2) → “must include ‘hooray’” (L3).
- **Prompt-based vs. Activation-based Steering:** Prompts use in-context learning; activations apply additive latent directions. *Why needed:* Fundamental distinction in intervention mechanisms; the paper’s core comparison hinges on this. *Quick check:* Why might 3-shot prompt succeed at L3 where a RePS vector fails?
- **Harmonic Mean (HM):** Aggregates Concept, Instruction, and Fluency scores; penalizes severe degradation in any dimension. *Why needed:* Reflects real-world deployment trade-offs where all aspects matter. *Quick check:* If CS=4, IS=1, FS=4, HM = 3 / (1/4 + 1 + 1/4) = 2.0; indicates usability limited by low instruction score.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Concept Synthesizer -> Question Pipeline -> Answer Pair Generator -> Validation Layer -> Evaluation Engine

Critical path: Domain input → Concept tree (L1/L2/L3) → Question set (train/test) → Answer pairs → Manual validation → Release. Evaluation runs separately using the benchmark.

Design tradeoffs: Automated synthesis scales but risks concept leakage (mitigated by question pivoting). Paired preference data isolates concept differences but may reduce ecological validity. LLM-as-judge is efficient but potentially biased toward prompt-based outputs.

Failure signatures: Activation-based HM drops precipitously at L2/L3, especially language features. Prompt-based few-shot overfitting: adding shots can harm L3 performance. Human review flags questions where pivot concept is too strong/weak. Model/checkpoint mismatch leads to baseline drift. Pivot failure: refined questions still cue target.

First 3 experiments:
1. Replicate main table: Run 0/3-shot prompt, PCA, DiffMean, RePS on Gemma-2-9B across all domains/L1–L3; verify HM patterns (Table 2).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What representational limitations cause activation-based steering to degrade at L2/L3 while prompt-based remains robust?
2. Why does activation-based steering perform best on personality, then sentiment, then language features? Is this due to representational differences across model layers?
3. How does the synthetic data pipeline (using LLMs) affect the hierarchy’s validity? Could L1–L3 separation be an artifact?
4. Can activation-based steering be improved for L2/L3 via advanced objectives or multi-layer interventions, and what are the trade-offs with capability retention?
5. Is the degradation at finer granularities a fundamental limit of inference-time intervention, or could better activation design (e.g., natural language prompts encoded into directions) close the gap?
6. How reliable is LLM-as-a-judge for L3’s exact token-level requirements? Could near-zero scores for PCA/DiffMean at L3 partly reflect judge insensitivity?
7. Does the hierarchical degradation generalize beyond the tested domains? Are there domains where activation-based steering remains effective at L3?
8. Why does few-shot prompting help L1/L2 but not L3, and sometimes hurt? Is this due to interference from surface forms in demonstrations?
9. Can the benchmark be extended to multi-turn dialogues or sequential steering, and would the same degradation patterns hold?
10. Is the observed boundary between activation and prompt steering inherent, or could better activation steering (e.g., using learned vectors from diverse descriptions) close the gap at L2/L3?

## Limitations
- Data/code access uncertain: SteerEval dataset and EASYEDIT2 framework lack direct download links.
- Evaluation bias: GPT-4.1-mini as judge may systematically favor prompt-based outputs, especially for L3 surface markers.
- Hyperparameter gaps: Activation method details (e.g., PCA training sample size, RePS loss specifics) are referenced but unspecified.
