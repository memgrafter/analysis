# LLMs as Cultural Archives: Cultural Commonsense Knowledge Graph Extraction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.17971  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17971  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: English Cultural Commonsense Knowledge Graph (CCKG) shows superior quality over native-language versions; CCKG augmentation improves smaller LLMs on cultural reasoning and story generation; chain-of-thought prompting harms performance.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the interpretability gap in LLMs’ cultural knowledge by extracting structured Cultural Commonsense Knowledge Graphs (CCKGs). An iterative prompt-based framework generates if‑then inference chains via initial assertion creation, multi‑step expansion using xNext and oNext relations, and semantic pruning. Human evaluation across five countries reveals that English CCKGs are more correct, culturally relevant, and logically coherent than those in native languages, indicating uneven cultural encoding in LLMs. Augmenting smaller models with CCKGs yields significant gains on cultural reasoning benchmarks (e.g., +10.3 on ArabCulture) and story generation (+1.4 in cultural relevance). Conversely, chain‑of‑thought prompting degrades performance, suggesting cultural commonsense relies on holistic intuition rather than explicit stepwise reasoning. The study highlights the potential of CCKGs to democratize cultural knowledge and raises important questions about language representational biases.

## Method Summary
The proposed framework (Algorithm 1) constructs a CCKG for a given culture and language. For each subtopic, an initial assertion is produced via Prompt 5. Stage 2 performs three expansion iterations: for every existing action, the system decomposes intermediate actions and generates forward actions using Prompt 6, yielding xNext and oNext relations. After each iteration, assertions are pruned: a maximum of six per action is retained, and semantically similar assertions (cosine similarity > 0.8 using sentence embeddings) are merged. Final duplicate removal yields the graph. For downstream tasks, the system retrieves the most relevant assertions and full paths from the CCKG via multilingual SBERT (stsb‑xlm‑r‑multilingual) and prepends them as few‑shot examples to the prompt of a smaller model.

## Key Results
- English CCKG consistently outperforms native‑language versions across correctness, cultural relevance, and logical coherence; cultural relevance ranges from 40.0–80.8 in English versus 13.4–59.1 in native languages.
- Smaller LLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5‑7B) augmented with CCKG show substantial gains on cultural reasoning benchmarks (+1.2 on IndoCulture, +10.3 on ArabCulture with native assertions) and story generation (+1.4 in cultural relevance).
- Chain‑of‑thought prompting harms performance across models and datasets, and story generation benefits more from English chains while reasoning tasks benefit more from native‑language examples.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Iterative expansion and semantic pruning build long, coherent chains without excessive noise, leveraging the LLM’s pretrained knowledge. The superior quality of English CCKGs likely stems from the predominance of English data in LLM pretraining, which yields more stable cross‑cultural representations; native‑language generations suffer from limited training data and linguistic variability. Augmentation with CCKG provides models with concrete, culturally relevant examples, improving their ability to reason about or generate content aligned with specific cultures. The failure of chain‑of‑thought prompting suggests that cultural commonsense is typically accessed holistically rather than through explicit stepwise reasoning; forcing decomposition introduces errors that outweigh benefits.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cultural Commonsense Knowledge Graph (CCKG)**  
  - *Why needed*: Structures implicit cultural knowledge into interpretable multi‑step if‑then chains, enabling reuse and analysis.  
  - *Quick check*: What are the two edge relation types in CCKG? (xNext, oNext)

- **Iterative Expansion (xNext/oNext)**  
  - *Why needed*: Generates intermediate reasoning steps to extend initial assertions into longer chains, capturing sequential cultural practices.  
  - *Quick check*: Given an initial assertion like “If you bowl, then you…”, suggest an xNext action and an oNext action. (Example: xNext → buy shoes; oNext → rent shoes)

- **Semantic Similarity Pruning (τ=0.8)**  
  - *Why needed*: Controls graph size by merging near‑duplicate actions, preserving diversity while avoiding redundancy.  
  - *Quick check*: If two candidate actions have cosine similarity 0.82 (threshold 0.8), should they be merged? (Yes)

- **Multilingual SBERT Retrieval**  
  - *Why needed*: Enables language‑agnostic lookup of relevant CCKG content for cross‑lingual transfer to smaller models.  
  - *Quick check*: Which multilingual model is used for retrieval? (stsb‑xlm‑r‑multilingual)

- **In‑context Learning Augmentation**  
  - *Why needed*: Allows small models to leverage CCKG without weight updates, simply by including retrieved examples in the prompt.  
  - *Quick check*: How many CCKG assertions are used as few‑shot examples? (5)

- **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) Prompting**  
  - *Why needed*: A common reasoning technique; its failure on cultural tasks highlights the intuitive nature of cultural knowledge.  
  - *Quick check*: According to the paper, does CoT improve or degrade performance on cultural benchmarks? (Degrades)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Subtopic → Initial Prompt (Prompt 5) → Seed Assertions → Iterative Expansion Loop (Prompt 6, xNext/oNext generation) → Pruning (similarity merge, limit 6) → Graph Assembly → CCKG Storage.  
  Downstream: Query → SBERT Retriever → Retrieved Assertions/Paths → In‑context Formatter → Small Model Augmented Prompt → Output.

- **Critical path**:  
  1. For a given subtopic and language, run Prompt 5 to obtain seed assertions.  
  2. Perform three expansion iterations: for each action, use Prompt 6 to produce intermediate and forward actions; prune after each iteration.  
  3. Assemble final directed graph with xNext/oNext edges.  
  4. For a downstream query, compute SBERT embedding, retrieve top‑5 similar assertions and top‑1 similar path, and prepend them to the small model’s prompt.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Expansion iterations (N): Longer chains vs. noise accumulation; paper uses N=3.  
  - Similarity threshold (τ=0.8): Higher avoids duplicates but may collapse distinct cultural actions; lower increases graph size.  
  - Max assertions per action (6): Balances coverage and noise.  
  - LLM choice: Stronger models (GPT‑4o) yield higher quality but reduce reproducibility and increase cost.  
  - Prompt language: English prompts exploit richer representations but may sacrifice native nuance.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Significant drop in human‑evaluated cultural relevance for native‑language CCKGs.  
  - Over‑merging (excessive graph density, many generic nodes) due to low τ.  
  - Under‑merging (many near‑duplicate assertions) from high τ or inadequate pruning.  
  - Downstream performance degrades when CCKG augmentation is added, indicating misaligned retrieval.  
  - Low inter‑annotator agreement in human evaluation (low Pearson correlation).

- **Exactly 3 first experiments**:  
  1. **Baseline quality replication**: Generate CCKGs for all five countries in English and native languages, sample 50 assertions/paths per language, and compute human‑rated COR, CR, LPC. Verify that English scores exceed native scores with statistical significance.  
  2. **Expansion iteration ablation**: Vary N (1,2,3,4) on a subset of subtopics; measure graph size (nodes, edges) and quality (human eval on fixed samples) to identify the optimal stopping point.  
  3. **Prompt language integrity check**: For Indonesian culture, produce CCKG using (a) native‑language prompts as described and (b) English prompts with post‑hoc translation; compare quality metrics to isolate the effect of prompt language.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Why do English CCKGs consistently outperform native‑language versions? The paper hypothesizes uneven LLM encoding due to limited language‑specific pretraining data, but this remains untested.  
- **Open Question 2**: Why does native‑language augmentation improve reasoning (MCQA/sentence completion) while English chains benefit story generation more? The interaction between language and task modality is not explained.  
- **Open Question 3**: How does the semantic similarity pruning (threshold 0.8) affect the diversity and cultural specificity of the CCKG? Over‑merging could erase meaningful cultural variations.  
- **Open Question 4**: Why does chain‑of‑thought (CoT) prompting harm performance on cultural commonsense tasks, contrary to its benefits in other reasoning domains? The intuitive nature of cultural knowledge is suggested but not validated.  
- **Open Question 5**: Do the observed patterns generalize to cultures with lower representation in LLM pretraining data or to subnational cultural groups? The study focuses on higher‑resource countries and may mask intra‑cultural diversity.

## Limitations
- **Prompt fidelity**: Exact prompt details (system messages, few‑shot examples) are unreported, risking divergence in reproduction.
- **Native‑language data integrity**: The authenticity of native prompts and annotations is unclear; possible reliance on English back‑translation undermines claims about native‑language benefits.
- **Retrieval design**: Downstream augmentation mechanics (query formulation, number/path selection) are underspecified, making it hard to isolate the effect of CCKG content from engineering choices.

## Confidence
- **High**: The iterative expansion and pruning algorithm is precisely defined and can be implemented faithfully.  
- **Medium**: Human evaluation metrics are clear, but sampling and annotator protocols are insufficiently described, limiting assessment of statistical reliability.  
- **Low**: Claims about language‑specific augmentation benefits rely on the assumption that native prompts were authentic, an uncontrolled and likely fragile factor.

## Next Checks
- **Exact prompt replication**: Re‑run the initial and expansion prompts (Figures 5 and 6) verbatim with GPT‑4o, compare output distributions (e.g., average assertion length, intermediate action count) to the paper’s reported statistics.
- **Translation integrity ablation**: For one native language (e.g., Indonesian), construct CCKG using (a) native prompts as described and (b) English prompts with post‑hoc translation; measure differences in COR/CR/LPC to isolate the effect of prompt language.
- **Retrieval parameter sweep**: On the IndoCulture/ArabCulture benchmarks, vary (a) query prefix (“In {country}, …”), (b) number of retrieved assertions (1,5,10), and (c) path retrieval policy (full chain vs. top assertions) to see if gains persist across settings.
