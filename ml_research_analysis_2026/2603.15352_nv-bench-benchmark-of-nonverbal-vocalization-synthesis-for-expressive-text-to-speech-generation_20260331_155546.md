# NV-Bench: Benchmark of Nonverbal Vocalization Synthesis for Expressive Text-to-Speech Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15352  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15352  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** NV-Bench introduces a dual-dimensional benchmark (instruction alignment via PCER, acoustic fidelity via Fréchet Distance/speaker similarity) with 1,651 multi‑lingual utterances; NV‑CV3 achieves PCER 27.69% (Mandarin single‑label) and NMOS 4.08, with strong objective‑human correlations (PCER vs IMOS ρ = –0.65).

## Executive Summary
Nonverbal vocalizations (NVs) are integral to expressive speech but have been evaluated superficially as acoustic artifacts or omitted entirely. NV‑Bench re‑frames NV synthesis as a communicative act, providing a functional taxonomy (Vegetative, Affect Bursts, Conversational Grunts) and a curated multi‑lingual corpus of 1,641 in‑the‑wild utterances with human references. Its dual‑dimensional protocol measures (1) instruction alignment via paralinguistic character error rate (PCER) and (2) acoustic fidelity via Fréchet Distance and speaker similarity. Evaluations show that NV‑CV3, a CosyVoice3 fine‑tune, attains the best PCER (27.69% on Mandarin single‑label) and highest naturalness (NMOS 4.08), and objective metrics correlate strongly with human perception, validating the benchmark as a standardized, interpretable framework.

## Method Summary
NV‑Bench supplies a test set of 1,651 utterances (650 Mandarin, 350 English single‑label; remainder multi‑label) balanced across 14 NV categories derived from a functional taxonomy. Ground‑truth is produced by a fine‑tuned multi‑lingual NV ASR (SenseVoice‑Small) and human verification. Evaluation quantifies (1) instruction alignment: the NV ASR transcribes TTS outputs, extracting NV symbols; edit distance on NV‑only sequences yields PCER (plus full‑transcript CER/OCER); (2) acoustic fidelity: Fréchet Distance (PAN‑N embeddings), speaker similarity (WavLM embeddings), and DNSMOS. Human ratings (IMOS for instruction accuracy, NMOS for naturalness) are collected for correlation analysis. Baselines include CosyVoice3 fine‑tuned with NV tokens (NV‑CV3) and FlexiVoice. The minimal reproduction requires acquiring the NV‑Bench test data and NV ASR, generating TTS audio for all prompts, running the metric pipeline, and comparing against paper results.

## Key Results
- NV‑CV3 reaches the lowest PCER (27.69% on Mandarin single‑label) and highest human‑rated naturalness (NMOS 4.08).
- Strong correlations confirm metric validity: PCER vs IMOS ρ = –0.65; Fréchet Distance aligns with NMOS.
- The dual‑dimensional protocol cleanly separates failure modes: some models suffer from poor controllability (high PCER) despite good acoustic quality, while others fail on sound realism (high FAD) even with correct NV placement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Viewing NVs as communicative acts, not acoustic textures, shifts evaluation from generic sound matching to pragmatic appropriateness. A functional taxonomy (Vegetative, Affect Bursts, Conversational Grunts) forces the task to map text prompts to discrete, learnable communicative functions, which are then validated against human references. This framing assumes NVs have clear roles that can be extracted from text and measured by alignment metrics. Evidence: the paper states NVs are “intrinsically communicative acts” (Sec. 1) and builds the corpus around this taxonomy. Break condition: if many in‑the‑wild NVs fall outside the 14‑category set – e.g., emerging hybrid functions or culture‑specific forms – the evaluation will misalign with actual communicative intent.

### Mechanism 2
Separating instruction alignment (PCER) from acoustic fidelity (Fréchet Distance, Speaker SIM) disentangles two orthogonal failure modes. PCER isolates errors in *which* and *where* NVs are generated, independent of audio quality; acoustic metrics then assess the realism of the produced sound. This prevents a system that generates clean speech but omits NVs from scoring well on alignment, and conversely prevents a system that produces noisy but correctly placed NVs from scoring well on fidelity. Evidence: Correlation analysis shows IMOS correlates negatively with PCER (ρ = –0.65) while NMOS aligns with Fréchet Distance, indicating the metrics tap distinct perceptual dimensions. Break condition: if PCER and acoustic metrics become highly correlated (e.g., a model that generates no NVs gets perfect acoustic scores), the disentanglement breaks down.

### Mechanism 3
The multi‑lingual NV ASR (SenseVoice‑Small fine‑tuned) acts as a scalable, automated backbone for computing alignment metrics. By transcribing both linguistic text and NV symbols in a unified sequence, it provides a fast, reproducible estimate of instruction following accuracy that correlates with human IMOS. This assumes the ASR is accurate enough on diverse NV speech to serve as a proxy for human judgment. Evidence: The ASR achieves low CER on NV‑specific testsets (e.g., 1.29% on SMIIP‑NV) and its derived PCER shows strong negative correlation with IMOS (ρ = –0.65). Break condition: if the ASR’s own PCER exceeds ~40% on a challenging, unfiltered corpus, its outputs become too noisy to trust.

## Foundational Learning

- **Functional Taxonomy of Nonverbal Vocalizations**  
  *Why needed:* NVs are not monolithic; their communicative function (physiological, emotional, or interactional) determines what the TTS system should generate. A taxonomy provides discrete classes for balanced data collection and unambiguous evaluation.  
  *Quick check:* How would you differentiate a vegetative "[Cough]" from a conversational "[Uhm]" in a prompt? The former conveys a bodily state, the latter manages turn‑taking.

- **Paralinguistic Character Error Rate (PCER)**  
  *Why needed:* Standard character error rate conflates linguistic and NV errors. PCER isolates edit distance on NV symbols only, giving a direct measure of *controllability*: did the model place the right NV in the right spot?  
  *Quick check:* For prompt "I want to [Cough]", if TTS output is transcribed as "I want to [Laughter]", PCER = 1 (substitution). If output is "I want to cough" (no brackets), PCER = 1 (deletion of the symbol and insertion of a word? Actually careful: PCER counts only NV symbols. The expected NV symbol sequence is "[Cough]"; the generated sequence is empty, so deletion of 1 symbol yields PCER 100%? We'll think: PCER is edit distance on NV-only sequences. Expected: [Cough]; generated: none → 1 deletion → PCER = 1 (100%). Alternatively if miswritten as "[Laugh]", that's one substitution → PCER = 1 (100%). So PCER measures exact symbol match.)

- **Fréchet Audio Distance (FAD) / Fréchet Distance (FD)**  
  *Why needed:* Captures distributional gap between generated NV audio and real human recordings in an embedding space. It reflects overall acoustic realism (timbre, temporal structure) better than sample‑wise metrics and aligns with human naturalness judgments.  
  *Quick check:* A model could have low PCER (symbols correctly placed) but high FAD – why might the NV sound unnatural? FAD captures distortions in the sound’s acoustic properties that PCER, which only cares about symbol presence/position, ignores.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Data Ingestion & Filtering Pipeline (Emilia‑Pipeline → MiMo‑Audio‑7B‑Instruct → Human Verification) → produces NV‑Bench; Evaluation Backbone: multi‑lingual NV ASR (SenseVoice‑Small fine‑tuned); Metric Computation Engine: computes PCER/OCER/CER and acoustic metrics (Fr\'echet Distance, Speaker SIM, DNSMOS); Human Evaluation Interface for IMOS/NMOS ratings.  

**Critical path:** User prompt (with NV tokens) → TTS model → generated audio → NV ASR transcription → PCER calculation; simultaneously, generated audio and reference audio → feature extraction (PANNs, WavLM) → FD/SIM. Both branches are required for a complete benchmark score.  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Realism vs. label purity*: In‑the‑wild audio provides ecological validity but often contains mixed NVs or speaker leakage; the filtering pipeline (MiMo‑Audio) sacrifices some data to ensure clean single‑label utterances.  
- *Single‑label vs. multi‑label*: Balanced single‑label subsets enable isolated category analysis but miss conversational complexity; multi‑label sets add realism but introduce label co‑occurrence imbalance.  
- *Automated vs. human evaluation*: NV ASR enables scalable PCER computation but introduces a single point of failure; human verification of a subset (5 %) is used to monitor this risk.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *PCER surges while SIM/FAD remain good:* Model likely omits or mislabels NVs but produces high‑quality speech – a *controllability* failure.  
- *PCER good but FAD/SIM poor:* Model correctly triggers the right NV type but the sound is acoustically unrealistic – a *fidelity* failure.  
- *Human‑verification Kappa drops below ~0.8:* The taxonomy or ASR transcripts are ambiguous, undermining ground‑truth reliability.  
- *Correlation between PCER and IMOS, or FAD and NMOS, weakens:* Either the automated metrics are misaligned with perception or the human protocol is flawed.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Validate the NV ASR backbone:** Run the provided ASR on the single‑label GT audio. Expect near‑zero PCER (perfect transcription), establishing that the ASR can reliably recognize target NVs in clean reference.  
2. **Establish single‑label baseline:** Compare NV‑CV3 and CosyVoice3 on the Mandarin single‑label subset. Replicate paper results: NV‑CV3 should lower PCER (27.69% vs. 57.69%) while maintaining speaker similarity.  
3. **Test multi‑label complexity & metric disentanglement:** Evaluate all models on both single‑ and multi‑label subsets. A robust model (NV‑CV3) will show smaller PCER degradation from single‑ to multi‑label than weaker models. Simultaneously, verify that FAD/SIM scores remain stable across subsets, confirming that acoustic fidelity is independent of label density.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Does the adopted functional taxonomy comprehensively capture the pragmatic diversity of nonverbal vocalizations across languages and contexts, particularly for nuanced conversational grunts or culturally specific affect bursts?**  
*Basis:* The paper grounds NV‑Bench in a three‑level taxonomy and standardizes 14 categories for Mandarin/English, but does not evaluate exhaustiveness or cultural portability.  
*Why unresolved:* NVs are highly context‑ and culture‑dependent; subtle functions (e.g., backchannels beyond “Uhm”) may be missing. Without cross‑linguistic validation, the benchmark may not generalize.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Expert elicitation or corpus analysis across diverse languages; extending NV‑Bench to more languages; measuring model performance on uncovered categories; user studies on perceived appropriateness in varied cultural contexts.

### Open Question 2
**To what extent does PCER fail to capture the acoustic or semantic appropriateness of the generated NV itself (e.g., a correctly placed but emotionally flat laugh)?**  
*Basis:* PCER measures NV symbol placement; acoustic fidelity is measured separately by Fréchet Distance and speaker similarity. The paper asserts disentanglement, but human IMOS may implicitly integrate both.  
*Why unresolved:* A model could achieve low PCER while generating acoustically unnatural or pragmatically mismatched NVs; PCER alone would overestimate controllability.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Correlate PCER with human ratings on NV‑specific quality dimensions (emotional authenticity, naturalness of the sound); conduct listening tests swapping NVs across contexts; develop hybrid metrics combining symbol accuracy with acoustic‑prosodic features.

### Open Question 3
**Can NV‑Bench’s metrics be reliably extended to languages beyond Mandarin and English, given potential differences in NV typology and phonotactics?**  
*Basis:* NV‑Bench includes only Mandarin and English (650/350 single‑label utterances). The unified taxonomy was normalized on English subsets; the multi‑lingual NV ASR was trained on several corpora but evaluated primarily on these two languages.  
*Why unresolved:* NV categories and their realizations vary across languages; the current taxonomy may not map cleanly to others, and ASR‑based PCER assumes consistent labeling. Cross‑lingual performance may degrade.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Expanding NV‑Bench to additional language families; evaluating cross‑lingual transfer of NV generation models; testing whether PCER‑IMOS and FAD‑NMOS correlations hold consistently across languages; studying language‑specific NV distributions.

### Open Question 4
**How can insights from NV‑Bench’s metrics be operationalized to improve NV generation during training, not just for diagnosis?**  
*Basis:* NV‑Bench is framed purely as an evaluation framework; models are fine‑tuned without explicit optimization for its metrics.  
*Why unresolved:* The gap between evaluation and training may limit progress; models are not directly encouraged to minimize PCER or FAD.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Ablations training with auxiliary losses derived from NV‑Bench metrics (e.g., PCER‑based reinforcement learning, adversarial FAD training); comparing performance when models are benchmark‑optimized versus standard likelihood; analyzing which training data compositions most improve metric correlates.

## Limitations
- **ASR bottleneck:** All alignment metrics depend on the fine‑tuned NV ASR; its accuracy on truly diverse, noisy “in‑the‑wild” speech is a risk, as errors directly inflate PCER.  
- **Taxonomy coverage & human agreement:** The 14‑category taxonomy may not capture the full pragmatic spectrum of NVs; its exhaustiveness, especially across cultures, is unproven despite high Kappa on a 5% subset.  
- **Ecological validity of the test set:** Heavy filtering for single‑label purity and speaker separation reduces the complexity and noise of real conversational NV usage, potentially favoring models tuned to the benchmark’s narrow distribution.

## Confidence
- **Dual‑dimensional protocol disentangles alignment and fidelity:** High confidence (empirical separation of metrics and distinct human correlations).  
- **NV ASR as a valid evaluation backbone:** Medium confidence (good performance on held‑out NV testsets and correlation with IMOS, but unvalidated on entirely new unfiltered corpora).  
- **Taxonomy as a complete communicative framework:** Low–Medium confidence (foundational but speculative; no proof of exhaustiveness across languages or contexts).

## Next Checks
1. **Hold‑out domain robustness:** Run the published NV ASR and full NV‑Bench pipeline on a completely external, high‑diversity NV corpus (e.g., raw podcasts or therapy sessions). A sharp PCER increase would signal benchmark overfitting.  
2. **Cross‑model correlation stability:** Replicate the human evaluation study (IMOS/NMOS) on TTS outputs from two architecturally distinct models (e.g., VALL‑E‑style or diffusion‑based). Verify the PCER‑IMOS and FAD‑NMOS correlations persist; a breakdown would indicate metrics are model‑family specific.  
3. **Taxonomy ambiguity stress test:** Annotate a subset of NV‑Bench prompts/audio where functional boundaries are theoretically blurry (e.g., “[Sniff]” as vegetative vs. conversational). Measure inter‑annotator agreement; a significant drop from the reported Kappa (>0.8) would pinpoint taxonomy weaknesses.
