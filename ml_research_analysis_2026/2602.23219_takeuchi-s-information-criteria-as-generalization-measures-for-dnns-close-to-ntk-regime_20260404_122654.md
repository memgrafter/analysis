# Takeuchi's Information Criteria as Generalization Measures for DNNs Close to NTK Regime
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23219
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23219
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Takeuchi’s Information Criterion (TIC) correlates strongly with the generalization gap for deep neural networks operating under the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime, and its efficient approximations enable effective trial pruning in hyperparameter optimization.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates Takeuchi’s Information Criterion (TIC) as a theoretically grounded measure for predicting the generalization gap of deep neural networks (DNNs). Standard statistical guarantees for TIC fail for DNNs because they are statistically singular models. The authors show that TIC’s validity is restored in the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime—a setting of significant overparameterization where the network’s kernel remains approximately constant during training. Under these conditions, the TIC bias term $\text{Tr}(H^{-1}C)$ becomes a consistent estimator of the generalization bias. The paper develops practical approximations (e.g., using the Fisher matrix instead of the Hessian, diagonal or lower-bound estimates) to make TIC computation feasible for large models. Experiments training over 5,000 models demonstrate that TIC estimates, particularly the lower bound $\text{Tr}(C)/\text{Tr}(F)$, correlate strongly with the generalization gap when models are close to the NTK regime (high $d/n$ ratio), but this correlation vanishes outside it. Furthermore, TIC-based trial pruning outperforms standard validation-loss methods in hyperparameter optimization tasks.

## Method Summary
The core method estimates the generalization bias using Takeuchi’s Information Criterion: $\text{TIC} = \text{training loss} + \text{Tr}(H^{-1}C)$, where $H$ is the Hessian of the training loss and $C$ is the covariance of the gradient noise. Exact computation of $H$ and $C$ is infeasible for large DNNs. The authors propose approximations justified under the NTK regime: (1) replace $H$ with the Fisher information matrix $F$ (valid for softmax cross-entropy with piecewise-linear activations), (2) use block-diagonal or diagonal matrix structures, and (3) employ the lower bound $\text{Tr}(C)/\text{Tr}(F)$ to avoid matrix inversion. For each trained model, they compute these quantities on the training data, then correlate the TIC estimate with the generalization gap (test loss minus training loss) across a hyperparameter grid. They also assess TIC’s utility for early trial pruning by comparing its ranking performance against validation loss in Successive Halving.

## Key Results
- TIC’s bias term $\text{Tr}(H^{-1}C)$ (or its approximations) correlates strongly with the generalization gap for models operating in the NTK regime (high $d/n$ ratio), with correlation coefficients (Pearson/Spearman/Kendall) often exceeding 0.9 for appropriate architectures.
- This correlation disappears for models outside the NTK regime (e.g., small networks, or those with batch normalization/data augmentation), confirming the regime’s necessity for TIC’s validity.
- Computationally efficient approximations, especially the lower bound $\text{Tr}(C)/\text{Tr}(F)$, retain predictive power. In hyperparameter optimization, TIC-based trial pruning selects better models than validation-loss pruning for a ResNet-8 on CIFAR-10.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
The NTK regime restores the regularity conditions required for TIC’s theoretical validity. For generic DNNs, statistical singularities (e.g., parameter symmetries, degenerate Fisher matrices) break the assumptions underlying TIC. However, under the NTK regime—characterized by large network width and a constant empirical Neural Tangent Kernel during training—the loss landscape becomes convex, the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is asymptotically normal, and the Fisher information is positive definite. These conditions (global convergence and asymptotic normality) allow the derivation that the generalization bias equals $\text{Tr}(H^{-1}C)$. The mechanism breaks when the NTK evolves substantially (feature learning), invalidating the regularity assumptions.

### Mechanism 2
TIC’s bias term $\text{Tr}(H^{-1}C)$ effectively captures an “effective number of parameters” by quantifying the mismatch between the curvature of the loss landscape ($H$) and the gradient noise covariance ($C$). Neither $H$ nor $C$ alone predicts generalization well. Their interaction measures how gradient noise propagates along curved directions, adjusting for overparameterization. In well-specified models $H \approx C$, reducing to AIC’s $d$. In misspecified DNNs, the ratio $\text{Tr}(H^{-1}C)$ accounts for the excess capacity and singularities.

### Mechanism 3
Computationally feasible approximations work because the NTK regime induces weak cross-layer correlations, making block-diagonal or diagonal approximations accurate. Additionally, under softmax cross-entropy and piecewise-linear activations, $H$ equals the Fisher $F$, allowing $C$ and $F$ to be estimated jointly via sampling, reducing cost. The lower bound $\text{Tr}(C)/\text{Tr}(F)$ avoids matrix inversion entirely and remains correlated with the exact TIC, as long as $H$ and $F$ are aligned (which they approximately are in the NTK regime).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** NTK Regime  
  *Why needed:* TIC’s theoretical justification hinges on the NTK regime, where the kernel is constant and training dynamics are linearized. Outside it, DNNs are singular and TIC may fail.  
  *Quick check:* “Is the network width large ($d \gg n$) and does the empirical NTK remain approximately constant during training (e.g., $\|K_t - K_0\|$ small)?”
- **Concept:** Information Matrices (Hessian $H$, Gradient Covariance $C$, Fisher $F$)  
  *Why needed:* TIC is defined via $\text{Tr}(H^{-1}C)$. Understanding their definitions, relationships (e.g., $H=F$ under certain losses/activations), and computational roles is essential.  
  *Quick check:* “For softmax cross-entropy and ReLU networks, is it valid to approximate $H$ with $F$?”
- **Concept:** Singular vs Regular Statistical Models  
  *Why needed:* DNNs are singular due to parameter redundancies (e.g., permutation symmetry), invalidating classical TIC derivations for regular models. The NTK regime mitigates this singularity.  
  *Quick check:* “Is the Fisher information matrix at the MLE full rank? If not, does the model have symmetries causing degeneracy?”
- **Concept:** Generalization Bias and Effective Degrees of Freedom  
  *Why needed:* TIC estimates the bias of the training error as an estimate of generalization risk. The term $\text{Tr}(H^{-1}C)$ generalizes AIC’s $d$ to singular settings.  
  *Quick check:* “Does the model’s complexity measure account for both curvature ($H$) and noise ($C$)?”
- **Concept:** Lazy Training (NTK)  
  *Why needed:* Lazy training (large width) ensures the network behaves linearly, making the NTK constant and restoring regularity conditions for TIC.  
  *Quick check:* “During training, does the network output change linearly with parameters, i.e., $f_t(\cdot) \approx f_0(\cdot) + J_0(\cdot)\Delta\theta$?”
- **Concept:** Monte Carlo Fisher Estimation  
  *Why needed:* Exact Fisher computation is costly; sampling labels from the model’s predictive distribution provides an unbiased estimate ($F_{1mc}$) that can be computed jointly with $C$.  
  *Quick check:* “Is the single-sample Fisher estimator $F_{1mc}$ computed as the average of $\|\nabla_\theta \ell(y'^{(i)}, f(x_i))\|^2$ over sampled $y'^{(i)}$?”

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Dataset → DNN (varied architectures) → SGD training → checkpointed model → compute $C$ (gradient covariance) and $F$ (Fisher) on training data → apply TIC estimator (lower-bound $\text{Tr}(C)/\text{Tr}(F)$) → compare with generalization gap (test loss − train loss) → correlation analysis.

**Critical path:**  
1. Define problem (dataset, architecture family).  
2. Train diverse models via hyperparameter optimization (e.g., Bayesian).  
3. For each trained model: compute TIC estimate using chosen approximation.  
4. Compute generalization gap.  
5. Aggregate over trials, compute Pearson/Spearman/Kendall correlations.  
6. Plot correlation vs. $d/n$ to identify NTK regime boundary.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Exact TIC (full $H$, $C$) vs. approximations: Exact is infeasible for large $d$ ($O(d^3)$). Diagonal/block-diagonal reduces memory to $O(d)$ and compute to $O(\sum d_l^3)$ but loses cross-unit interactions.  
- $F$ vs $H$: Using $F$ (when valid) halves compute since $C$ and $F$ can be estimated jointly.  
- Lower bound $\text{Tr}(C)/\text{Tr}(F)$ avoids matrix inversion and damping issues, but may be loose; still retains rank correlation in experiments.  
- Monte Carlo $F_{1mc}$ trades bias for speed; works well in practice.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Regime mismatch: Low $d/n$ (<~1) or presence of batch norm/data augmentation weakens/negates correlation.  
- Assumption violation: Non-softmax losses or non-piecewise-linear activations break $H=F$, requiring expensive $H$ computation or introducing error.  
- Numerical instability: $H$ near-singular causes inversion issues; lower bound avoids this but may underestimate bias.  
- Architecture-specific failures: Very deep or recurrent networks may have strong cross-layer correlations, degrading block-diagonal/diagonal accuracy.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Reproduce Figure 2 (correlation)**: Train 50+ models for a single workload (e.g., CIFAR-10 on 6-LNN with skip connections). Compute lower-bound TIC $\text{Tr}(C)/\text{Tr}(F)$. Plot TIC vs. generalization gap; expect strong positive correlation for high $d/n$. Validate correlation coefficient matches Table 7.  
2. **Approximation validation on small scale**: Use TinyMNIST models (2-NN, 3-LNN). Compute exact TIC (full $H$, $C$) and compare to block-diag, diagonal, and lower-bound estimates. Compute Spearman correlation between exact and approximate TIC across models; expect >0.9 for LNNs (as in Tables 8–10).  
3. **Regime boundary test**: Train same architecture (e.g., 3-LNN) on (a) TinyMNIST ($d/n \approx 0.02$), (b) SmallTinyMNIST with 5% data ($d/n \approx 0.36$), and (c) wide 3-NN ($d/n \approx 13.9$). Plot Pearson correlation vs. $d/n$ to show correlation emerges only at higher $d/n$.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can TIC be theoretically extended to DNNs operating in the feature learning regime where the NTK evolves during training? The derivation relies on constant NTK and asymptotic normality, which fail outside the lazy training regime.
- How do batch normalization and data augmentation—standard practices excluded from this study—affect the correlation between TIC estimates and the generalization gap? These techniques may move dynamics away from the NTK regime.
- Is the diagonal (or lower bound) approximation of TIC sufficiently accurate across a wide range of network architectures, especially those with high cross-layer correlation? The approximation error’s dependence on off-diagonal block magnitudes is not fully characterized.
- Does TIC consistently improve trial pruning efficiency across many model–dataset combinations and multi-fidelity HPO algorithms? The paper demonstrates advantage for one workload but calls for broader evaluation.

## Limitations
- Experiments restricted to small datasets (TinyMNIST, MNIST, CIFAR-10/100) and architectures without batch normalization or data augmentation, limiting generalizability to modern large-scale training.
- NTK regime proxied solely by high $d/n$ ratio; no direct measurement of NTK constancy during training, potentially conflating overparameterization with true NTK dynamics.
- Approximation quality for very deep or recurrent networks, or with non-standard activations, remains untested.
- Trial-pruning superiority shown only for one HPO method (Successive Halving) and one dataset/architecture combination.

## Confidence
- **NTK validity and correlation:** High – Strong theoretical foundation and consistent empirical results across 12 architectures and 4 datasets within the NTK regime.
- **Approximation effectiveness:** Medium – Diagonal and lower-bound estimates work well for tested architectures but may degrade for networks with strong cross-layer dependencies.
- **Trial pruning superiority:** Medium – Promising result on one workload but requires broader HPO benchmark validation.
- **Generalization beyond NTK regime:** Low – Correlation vanishes outside NTK; extension to feature learning is an open problem.

## Next Checks
1. Systematic width/depth sweeps including batch normalization and data augmentation, measuring both $d/n$ and NTK change ($\|K_t - K_0\|$) to precisely map the regime boundary where TIC correlation holds.
2. Apply exact and approximate TIC to large modern CNNs (e.g., ResNet-50) and vision transformers on ImageNet; verify whether correlations persist in realistic, large-scale settings.
3. Large-scale HPO benchmark: Compare TIC-guided Successive Halving/Hyperband against validation-loss baselines over 100+ random seeds and diverse architectures/datasets; evaluate sample efficiency and final test performance with statistical testing.
