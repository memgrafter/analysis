# A Family of LLMs Liberated from Static Vocabularies

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.15953
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15953
*   **Reference count:** 40
*   **Primary result:** Introduces Hierarchical Autoregressive Transformer (HAT) models that eliminate fixed tokenizers by operating directly on bytes, achieving comparable or superior performance on benchmarks while reducing parameter count and improving text compression.

## Executive Summary
Static tokenizers in large language models (LLMs) impose fixed, large vocabularies that hinder domain and language adaptability. This work presents Hierarchical Autoregressive Transformer (HAT) models, which remove the tokenizer entirely by processing raw UTF-8 bytes. The architecture introduces a three-stage hierarchy: an encoder compresses variable-length byte sequences into fixed-size word embeddings, a standard backbone transformer processes these word-level representations, and a decoder converts next-word predictions back into a byte stream. Models were built both by training a 7B parameter model from scratch and by "HATifying" existing Llama 3.1 8B/70B models. Results demonstrate that HAT models match or exceed tokenized counterparts on English and German benchmarks, offer better compression (more bytes per sequence position), and use fewer parameters. The approach promises enhanced robustness to spelling variations and out-of-vocabulary words without requiring vocabulary updates.

## Method Summary
The HAT architecture comprises three core components: (1) an encoder that processes input as UTF-8 bytes using local causal attention and aggregates them into word embeddings via a cross-attention mechanism with learned queries; (2) a standard autoregressive transformer backbone that operates on these word embeddings; and (3) a decoder that uses cross-attention over the backbone's next-word predictions to generate output bytes sequentially. Training is conducted in two ways: (a) from scratch, where all components are randomly initialized and trained on a curated multilingual corpus; and (b) via "HATification," where a pre-trained token-based model's embedding and output layers are replaced with randomly initialized encoder/decoder modules. The backbone is initially frozen while the encoder/decoder learn to interface with it, after which the entire model is fine-tuned. Data curation involves filtering for language quality and diversity, with specific proportions for English, German, math, and code.

## Key Results
*   HAT models achieve comparable or superior performance on standard English (MMLU, GSM8K) and German benchmarks versus their tokenized baselines.
*   The architecture improves text compression, representing more bytes per sequence position, and reduces total parameters (e.g., a 7B HAT vs. an 8B Llama 3.1).
*   The 70B HAT model outperforms Llama 3.3-70B-Instruct in MT-Bench evaluations for both English and German.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hierarchical Compression via Byte-to-Word Aggregation
**Claim:** The encoder's cross-attention aggregates variable-length byte sequences into fixed-size word embeddings, reducing the effective sequence length for the backbone's self-attention layers.
**Mechanism:** The encoder processes raw UTF-8 bytes with a local causal attention window. For each word chunk (defined by a splitter), a learned latent vector cross-attends to all corresponding byte embeddings, producing a single word embedding. The backbone then operates on these compressed representations.
**Core assumption:** Word boundaries align with semantic units; compressing bytes into words preserves essential information while discarding redundant details.
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract states improved text compression; Section 3.1 details the cross-attention aggregation.
**Break condition:** Poor word segmentation (e.g., on compound languages or non-standard orthography) would diminish compression benefits and potentially harm performance by losing byte-level information.

### Mechanism 2: Parameter Reduction via Tokenizer Elimination
**Claim:** Replacing the large, static embedding matrix and LM head with smaller, dynamically computed encoder/decoder modules reduces total parameters while maintaining representational capacity.
**Mechanism:** Standard LLMs have massive embedding tables (e.g., ~525M for Llama 3.1 8B's 128k vocab). HAT removes this, using a 6-layer encoder (~119M) to compute embeddings on-the-fly. The decoder similarly replaces the LM head. The pre-trained backbone weights remain unchanged.
**Core assumption:** Token embeddings are over-parametrized because token IDs are arbitrary; a smaller, learned function can achieve similar expressivity by exploiting byte-level similarities.
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes parameter reduction (7B vs 8B); Section 3.1 provides explicit parameter counts and percentages; Section 4.2 describes freezing the backbone during initial training.
**Break condition:** An encoder/decoder that is too shallow or poorly initialized may fail to extract sufficient information from bytes, causing degraded performance. The noted "bump" in training dynamics (Section 5.1) suggests a critical learning phase for the encoder/decoder-backbone interface.

### Mechanism 3: Open-Vocabulary Robustness via Direct Byte Processing
**Claim:** Operating directly on bytes eliminates out-of-vocabulary issues and captures intra-word variations (spelling, morphology) without vocabulary updates.
**Mechanism:** The encoder's input space is all possible UTF-8 byte sequences. While a word splitter defines chunks, the model can exploit low-level character/byte similarities (e.g., "color" vs. "colour") through shared patterns in the embedding computation, rather than arbitrary token ID collisions.
**Core assumption:** Linguistic and domain variations often manifest at the character/byte level; a model seeing these bytes directly generalizes better to unseen forms.
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions robustness to intra-word variations; Section 3.3 contrasts tokenization with splitting, noting natural similarity between byte chunks.
**Break condition:** An overly aggressive splitter (e.g., breaking meaningful camelCase tokens in code) could fragment information that should be preserved as a single unit, harming performance.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Autoregressive Transformer Causal Attention**
    *   **Why needed:** The encoder, backbone, and decoder all use masked self-attention. Understanding masking explains how the encoder's local window and backbone's global context cooperate, and why the decoder's cross-attention uses shifted predictions.
    *   **Quick check:** In the decoder block, why does the cross-attention use *shifted* next-word predictions from the backbone as keys/values, and what happens if they are not shifted?
*   **Concept: Cross-Attention for Information Routing**
    *   **Why needed:** Cross-attention is the connective tissue: encoder aggregates bytes into words (encoder-to-backbone), and decoder injects word context into byte generation (backbone-to-decoder). Misunderstanding this flow breaks the hierarchy.
    *   **Quick check:** What are the query, key, and value in the encoder's cross-attention layer that aggregates bytes into a word embedding? How do dimensions differ from standard self-attention?
*   **Concept: Mixed-Precision Training & Pipeline Parallelism**
    *   **Why needed:** Section 3.4 and 4.2 describe critical infrastructure (bfloat16, ZeRO-1, pipeline parallelism). These require adaptation for HAT's irregular compute/memory patterns (small encoder/decoder params but large activations on long byte sequences).
    *   **Quick check:** Why does HAT pose a challenge for standard flash attention kernels, and what specific adaptation was required (Section 3.4)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input (UTF-8 bytes) -> Splitter (word chunks) -> Encoder (6-layer, byte-to-word aggregation) -> Backbone Transformer (32/80-layer, word-level processing) -> Decoder (4-layer, word-to-byte generation) -> Output (byte logits)

**Critical path:**
1. Split byte sequence into word chunks.
2. Encode all bytes into byte embeddings.
3. For each word chunk, cross-attend its byte embeddings to a learned query -> word embedding.
4. Process word embeddings through backbone -> next-word contextual representations.
5. For each byte position in decoder, cross-attend to *preceding* backbone word predictions while keeping byte-level queries.
6. Generate next-byte probability from final decoder state.

**Design tradeoffs:**
*   **Memory vs. Compute:** Encoder/decoder are shallow but operate on long byte sequences (high activation memory). Backbone is deep but on short word sequences (high compute). Batching must balance both.
*   **KV Cache Complexity:** Dual caches (byte-level sliding window, word-level global). A sequence cannot proceed to the next word unless both caches have space, requiring custom scheduling (e.g., in vLLM).
*   **Training Stability:** Pre-trained backbone is initially frozen because a randomly initialized encoder/decoder would produce poor word embeddings, corrupting backbone gradients. A warmup-stable-decay LR schedule is critical.

**Failure signatures:**
*   **Training:** Absence of the characteristic "bump" in loss/performance upon unfreezing the backbone (Section 5.1) indicates the encoder/decoder failed to learn a usable interface.
*   **Inference:** Throughput collapse in batched serving when prompts have highly variable byte-generation lengths per word, causing scheduling inefficiencies and idle sequences.
*   **Memory:** Out-of-memory errors during long-context adaptation if attempting to use ring/striped attention with the encoder's sliding window layers (Section 3.4 notes avoidance of context parallelism for this reason).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Sanity-check pre-training:** Train a tiny HAT (e.g., 100M params) from scratch on a small, controlled corpus (e.g., 1B words). Verify next-byte prediction capability and analyze encoder's cross-attention to confirm proper byte-chunk aggregation.
2. **HATification conversion:** Take a Llama 3.1 8B checkpoint. Randomly initialize encoder/decoder. Freeze backbone. Train only encoder/decoder for ~2k steps on short sequences. Unfreeze backbone with reduced LR. Monitor loss curve for the "bump" and track bytes/position metric.
3. **vLLM integration stress test:** Implement the dual KV-cache and custom scheduler. Create a batch with prompts designed to force extreme variance in byte-generation lengths per word (e.g., short English words vs. long German compounds). Measure throughput vs. batch size and compare to a FLOP-matched token-based model.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** To what extent do the claimed benefits of HAT—improved robustness to prompt perturbations and adaptability to new domains/languages—hold across a broader range of tasks and linguistic contexts beyond English and German?
*Basis:* The conclusion states these benefits require further validation; evaluation covers standard NLP benchmarks but limited stress-testing on code or non-Latin scripts.

**Open Question 2:** Can the inference efficiency gap between hierarchical (HAT) and standard token-based transformers be closed through architectural or systems optimizations?
*Basis:* The inference section highlights lower throughput due to dual-sequence processing and KV cache challenges; the current vLLM implementation still lags behind FLOP-matched token-based models.

**Open Question 3:** What is the optimal strategy for "HATification"—converting a pre-trained token-based model to the HAT architecture—to maximize performance and training efficiency?
*Basis:* The paper describes a specific HATification process (random init, frozen backbone for 2k steps, then fine-tune) noting a "bump" in performance, but alternative strategies (e.g., different initialization, gradual unfreezing, distillation) are not explored.

**Open Question 4:** How does the HAT architecture perform on truly multilingual and low-resource language scenarios, particularly for languages with non-Latin scripts or rich morphology?
*Basis:* Evaluation is limited to English and German; the splitting rule (UAX#299) is designed for common languages but not evaluated for scalability to diverse scripts like Chinese or Arabic.

**Open Question 5:** Can HAT models achieve long-context capability without the trade-off of freezing most backbone parameters during context extension, and what are the limits of context scaling?
*Basis:* For long-context adaptation, they freeze all parameters except query/key projections, citing memory bottlenecks and avoidance of context parallelism. Maximum reported contexts (32K words for 8B, 16K for 70B) are given without analysis of degradation or scaling potential.

## Limitations
*   **Data & Training Secrecy:** Exact pre-training data sources and full hyperparameter settings (especially for the 70B model and post-training mixtures) are undisclosed, preventing precise replication.
*   **Splitter Dependency:** Performance hinges entirely on the word splitter's quality. Its behavior on morphologically rich languages, code-switching, or domain-specific jargon (e.g., biomedical compounds) is not deeply analyzed; a poor splitter could undermine the core compression assumption.
*   **Inference Efficiency Uncertainty:** While parameter count is lower, reported throughput advantages are qualified. The dual KV-cache design introduces scheduling complexity; real-world serving efficiency under heterogeneous request loads remains an open question.
