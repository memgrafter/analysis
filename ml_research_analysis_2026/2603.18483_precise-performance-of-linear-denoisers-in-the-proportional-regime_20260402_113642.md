# Precise Performance of Linear Denoisers in the Proportional Regime

## Quick Facts

- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.18483
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18483
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Asymptotic closed‑form expression for the generalization error of least‑squares trained linear denoisers with synthetic noise; optimal synthetic noise covariance often beats the empirical Wiener filter and converges to the oracle Wiener filter in the high‑sample limit.

## Executive Summary

This paper studies linear denoising when the clean data covariance is unknown but the noise covariance is known. The denoiser is trained by ordinary least squares on synthetic data obtained by adding Gaussian noise with a tunable covariance Σ₁. In the proportional regime (n/d → κ > 1) the authors use the Convex Gaussian Min‑Max Theorem to derive an exact asymptotic expression for the generalization error, which depends on Σ, Σ_z, Σ₁, and κ. Optimization of Σ₁ leads to a denoiser that generally outperforms the empirical Wiener filter and approaches the optimal Wiener filter as κ → ∞. For scalar covariances the optimal Σ₁ and the minimum error are obtained in closed form, revealing phase transitions. Numerical experiments confirm the theoretical predictions.

## Method Summary

The authors consider observations y = x + z with x∼N(0,Σ) unknown and z∼N(0,Σ_z) known. A training set of pairs (xᵢ+εᵢ, xᵢ) is generated, where εᵢ∼N(0,Σ₁) is synthetic noise controlled by a design matrix Σ₁. The linear map W is learned by solving the least‑squares problem W = argmin‖X − (X+Z)W‖_F², yielding W = ((X+Z)ᵀ(X+Z))⁻¹ (X+Z)ᵀ X. The operational denoiser is A = Wᵀ, and its generalization error is E = tr((A−I)Σ(A−I)ᵀ) + tr(A Σ_z Aᵀ). Under the proportional limit n,d→∞ with n/d→κ>1, the Convex Gaussian Min‑Max Theorem is applied to obtain a deterministic equivalent for E that depends only on the population covariances. In the scalar case (Σ = (c/d)I, Σ_z = (c_z/d)I) this simplifies to a scalar expression; minimizing it over Σ₁ gives a closed‑form optimal Σ₁ and a phase transition in the achievable error. For general Σ the true Σ is replaced by the sample covariance estimated from the training data, leading to a surrogate loss that can be optimized numerically (e.g., by projected gradient descent). The paper compares the optimized linear denoiser against the empirical Wiener filter A_emp = Σ̂(Σ̂+Σ_z)⁻¹, where Σ̂ is the centered sample covariance.

## Key Results

- Asymptotic closed‑form expression for the generalization error of the least‑squares denoiser, valid almost surely in the proportional regime.
- Optimizing the synthetic noise covariance Σ₁ produces a denoiser that typically outperforms the empirical Wiener filter and converges to the optimal Wiener filter as κ → ∞.
- For scalar covariances the optimal Σ₁ and the minimum error are derived explicitly (Corollary 3), exhibiting phase transitions determined by κ and the signal‑to‑noise ratios.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

The analysis leverages the Convex Gaussian Min‑Max Theorem (CGMT), a powerful tool for high‑dimensional random convex programs. By reformulating the least‑squares objective as a convex‑concave saddle‑point problem, the CGMT permits replacement of the random matrices with deterministic equivalents that converge almost surely. This yields an exact asymptotic error expression as a function of resolvent limits depending on Σ, Σ_z, Σ₁, and κ. The error is continuous in Σ₁, allowing analytic or numerical optimization. In the scalar case the high‑dimensional integrals reduce to scalar fixed‑point equations that can be solved explicitly, providing the optimal Σ₁ and revealing phase transitions—abrupt performance changes as parameters cross critical thresholds. For general covariances the same CGMT machinery provides a deterministic surrogate loss that depends on the unknown Σ; the authors approximate Σ by the sample covariance, making the optimization practically feasible.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Data generation (X, Z, ε) → Training set construction ((X+Z), X) → Least‑squares solve → Denoiser A = Wᵀ → Evaluation/Optimization of Σ₁.

**Critical path:**  
1. Generate matrices X, Z, ε with prescribed covariances.  
2. Form the training matrices and compute W = ((X+Z)ᵀ(X+Z))⁻¹ (X+Z)ᵀ X.  
3. Construct the denoiser A = Wᵀ and compute the generalization error (analytically or via simulation).  
4. (If optimizing) Evaluate surrogate loss and adjust Σ₁.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Synthetic noise covariance Σ₁*: Larger Σ₁ biases the solution toward the prior; smaller Σ₁ relies more on data. The optimal choice balances bias and variance.  
- *Stability*: When n is close to d, (X+Z)ᵀ(X+Z) may be near‑singular; ensuring κ>1 and moderate values avoids numerical issues.  
- *Surrogate vs true loss*: Replacing the unknown Σ with the sample covariance ˆΣ makes the optimization feasible but introduces estimation error.  
- *Scalar vs structured Σ₁*: The scalar case reduces to one parameter and yields closed‑form solutions; allowing full freedom for Σ₁ increases expressive power but also optimization difficulty.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Matrix inversion fails or yields extremely large condition numbers → inaccurate denoiser.  
- Simulation average deviates from theoretical prediction for moderate d → finite‑sample effects not captured by asymptotics.  
- Optimized Σ₁ does not improve over empirical Wiener → surrogate loss may be poor or optimization stuck in local minima.

**First experiments (3):**  
1. **Validate Theorem 1 (scalar case):** Simulate d=500, κ=2, c=1, c_z=1, Σ₁ = σ₁I with σ₁=c_z. Average empirical error over many trials; compare to the theoretical formula.  
2. **Optimal Σ₁ in scalar regime:** For fixed c, c_z, κ, perform a grid‑search over σ₁ to minimize theoretical error; verify the phase transition predicted by Corollary 3.  
3. **Non‑scalar Σ and plug‑in optimization:** For a diagonal Σ with decaying eigenvalues, use the surrogate loss (with ˆΣ) to optimize Σ₁ via projected gradient descent. Compare the resulting error against the empirical Wiener filter and the oracle Wiener filter.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Multiple batches:** Theorem 5 for N>1 yields a complex coupled fixed‑point system; simplification or closed‑form expressions for specific covariance structures are needed, as are monotonicity/convexity properties.  
- **Algorithmic optimization of Σ₁:** The current projected gradient descent on a non‑convex surrogate lacks convergence guarantees; an efficient method with provable optimality for arbitrary Σ and Σ_z is missing.  
- **Overparameterized regime:** Extending the CGMT analysis to n<d (underdetermined least squares) and to training via SGD or regularization remains an open challenge.  
- **Universality beyond Gaussian:** Proving that the asymptotic error holds for non‑Gaussian data with matching second moments would require new universality results for the specific loss; current proofs rely on Gaussianity.

## Limitations

- Finite‑sample deviations: The asymptotic theory may be inaccurate for small dimensions (d<200) or κ close to 1.  
- Distributional assumptions: Results are proved under Gaussian data and noise; extension to non‑Gaussian settings remains conjectural.  
- Heuristic optimization: The data‑driven choice of Σ₁ via a plug‑in sample covariance and gradient descent has no optimality guarantees for general covariance structures.

## Confidence

- **High:** Asymptotic error formulas (Theorems 1, 5); scalar optimal Σ₁ and phase transitions (Corollary 3).  
- **Medium:** Performance gains of the optimized denoiser over the empirical Wiener filter and its convergence to the oracle; empirical validation of the theory.

## Next Checks

1. **Finite‑size scaling:** Simulate dimensions d∈{50,100,200} and ratios κ∈{1.2,2,5} to quantify the accuracy of the asymptotic formula and its convergence rate.  
2. **Non‑Gaussian robustness:** Replace Gaussian data with heavy‑tailed (Student‑t with 3 df) or bounded (uniform) distributions; assess the empirical error against the theoretical prediction.  
3. **Heuristic optimality for general Σ:** For a small dimension (d=10) with non‑scalar Σ (e.g., diagonal with diverse eigenvalues), compare the plug‑in optimized Σ₁ (via projected gradient on the surrogate) against a brute‑force grid search over diagonal Σ₁ to evaluate the heuristic’s quality.
