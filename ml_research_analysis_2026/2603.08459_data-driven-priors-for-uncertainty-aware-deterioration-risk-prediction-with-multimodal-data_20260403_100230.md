# Data-Driven Priors for Uncertainty-Aware Deterioration Risk Prediction with Multimodal Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08459  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08459  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** MedCertAIn achieves AUROC 0.835 / AUPRC 0.498 and selective AUROC 0.857 / selective AUPRC 0.599, significantly outperforming baselines on in-hospital mortality prediction.

## Executive Summary
This work tackles the critical lack of reliable uncertainty estimation in multimodal clinical prediction models, which is essential for safe deployment in decision support systems. The authors propose MedCertAIn, a Bayesian framework that constructs data-driven priors for neural network parameters by combining cross-modal similarity in self-supervised latent spaces with modality-specific data corruptions, without requiring expert-annotated high-uncertainty samples. Evaluated on in-hospital mortality prediction using paired EHR time‑series and chest X‑ray images from MIMIC‑IV and MIMIC‑CXR, MedCertAIn shows substantial improvements in selective prediction metrics, enabling reliable deferral of uncertain cases for clinician review. Ablations confirm the hybrid prior strategy is most effective, offering a promising direction for uncertainty‑aware multimodal clinical AI.

## Method Summary
The task is binary in-hospital mortality prediction using paired EHR time–series and chest X‑ray images. The backbone is MedFuse: an LSTM (2‑layer) processes EHR sequences, a ResNet‑34 processes CXR images, and their features are concatenated and passed to a sigmoid classifier. To enable Bayesian uncertainty, the final‑layer weights are treated as random variables with a mean‑field Gaussian posterior $q(\Theta)$. The prior $p(\Theta)$ is *data‑driven*: it is derived from a curated context set $(X_c, Y_c)$ that approximates high‑uncertainty regions. This context set is built by (1) applying modality‑specific corruptions (e.g., noise, missing segments for EHR; flips, blur for CXR) and (2) selecting training pairs with low cross‑modal similarity from embeddings of a separately pre‑trained ConVIRT self‑supervised model. The variational objective maximizes the ELBO: (a) binary cross‑entropy on the labeled training batch $(X_D, Y_D)$, (b) KL divergence $D_{KL}(q(\Theta) \| p(\Theta| \check{z}))$ where $\check{z}$ summarizes the context set, and (c) an uncertainty regularization term $E_{q(\Theta)}[E_{p_{X_c}}[\log \tilde{p}(0|X_c,\Theta)]]$ that encourages high predictive entropy on context samples. Hyperparameters (prior variance, corruption types, similarity threshold) are tuned via random search (~250 configurations) using validation AUROC. At test time, multiple Monte‑Carlo samples from $q(\Theta)$ yield predictive means and entropies; selective prediction curves (AUROC/AUPRC vs. entropy‑based deferral thresholds) assess the model’s ability to identify errors.

## Key Results
- Standard metrics: AUROC 0.835, AUPRC 0.498, surpassing deterministic MedFuse (0.819 AUROC, 0.252 AUPRC) and other Bayesian baselines.
- Selective prediction: Selective AUROC 0.857, Selective AUPRC 0.599, representing large gains (e.g., MedFuse: 0.659 sel AUROC, 0.192 sel AUPRC).
- Ablation: The full hybrid prior (corruptions + inter‑modal similarity) yields the highest selective AUPRC (0.599) compared to components alone (Random Corruptions: 0.579; Inter‑Intra Similarity: 0.434) and an uninformative prior (0.357).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Uncertainty shaping via data‑driven prior regularization
**Claim:** Bayesian learning with a meaningful data‑driven prior yields a posterior whose predictive uncertainty is better calibrated for selective prediction than a standard Gaussian prior.  
**Mechanism:** The variational objective adds an uncertainty regularization term that forces the model to assign high entropy to a curated context set $(X_c, Y_c)$ representing high‑uncertainty regions; this shapes the approximate posterior $q(\Theta)$ away from overconfident solutions.  
**Core assumption:** Regularizing uncertainty on a predefined set of “difficult” examples improves overall uncertainty reliability.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 3.1 defines the ELBO with the entropy‑encouraging term; Section 5.2 shows the uninformative prior baseline’s selective AUPRC (0.357) is far below the hybrid (0.599).  
**Break condition:** If the context set does not contain clinically relevant uncertainty (e.g., only trivial corruptions or unrealistic OOD samples), the regularization may mis‑shape the posterior and harm selective performance.

### Mechanism 2: Hybrid context set construction
**Claim:** Combining modality‑specific corruptions and cross‑modal dissimilarity yields a more effective context set than either strategy alone.  
**Mechanism:** Corruptions simulate common data quality issues; low cross‑modal similarity identifies samples where the two modalities provide conflicting or poorly aligned signals. The combination covers different sources of predictive uncertainty.  
**Core assumption:** Uncertainty in multimodal clinical prediction arises from both modality‑specific degradation and cross‑modal semantic disagreement.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2 describes the two strategies; Table 3 demonstrates the combined “MedCertAIn I” (0.599 sel AUPRC) outperforms “Random Corruptions” (0.579) and “Inter‑Intra Similarity” (0.434).  
**Break condition:** If one source of uncertainty dominates in deployment, the hybrid prior may be less efficient than a singular, targeted prior; over‑reliance on easy corruptions could cause the model to ignore subtle, semantic uncertainty.

### Mechanism 3: Selective prediction as a clinically meaningful metric
**Claim:** Selective prediction performance, using entropy‑based deferral, is more clinically relevant than standard AUROC/AUPRC because it directly measures the model’s ability to identify its own errors.  
**Mechanism:** Predictions are ranked by Shannon entropy; metrics are computed on the non‑deferred subset across thresholds. Improvement indicates misclassifications are pushed into the high‑entropy tail, enabling clinicians to review those cases.  
**Core assumption:** In clinical decision support, it is safer to have a model that outputs “I don’t know” for cases it would get wrong than a slightly more accurate model that is confidently wrong.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 4.4 defines the selective prediction protocol; Section 5.1 shows the largest gains in selective metrics vs. standard ones; the abstract highlights “enabling reliable deferral.”  
**Break condition:** If entropy is poorly calibrated (e.g., high entropy on correct predictions, low entropy on errors), entropy‑based deferral will not effectively filter errors.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Data Inputs -> Modality Encoders (Φ_ehr: LSTM, Φ_cxr: ResNet34) -> Encoded Features; Self‑Supervised Module (ConVIRT) provides embeddings for similarity; Context Set Constructor applies corruptions to training data and selects low‑similarity pairs to form (Xc, Yc); Fusion Module concatenates encoded features from a training sample and passes through classifier; Variational Training Loop combines standard samples (XD, YD) and context samples (Xc) to optimize ELBO: (a) BCE loss on YD, (b) KL divergence between q(Θ) and data‑driven prior p(θ|ˇz), (c) entropy‑encouraging cost on Xc; Posterior q(Θ) used for predictive distribution via Monte‑Carlo sampling.

### Critical Path
1. Prepare paired EHR and CXR data from MIMIC, pre‑process to fixed length (48 timesteps) and ensure patient‑disjoint splits (80/20 train/test, 5‑fold CV on train).  
2. Pre‑train ConVIRT self‑supervised encoders on the training set to obtain latent embeddings for cross‑modal similarity.  
3. Construct the context set: apply modality‑specific corruptions to all training samples; select samples with low inter‑modal cosine similarity based on ConVIRT embeddings.  
4. Initialize variational posterior q(Θ) (e.g., from a deterministic MedFuse checkpoint).  
5. Train the Bayesian model with the combined objective using Adam, mixing standard and context batches, tuning hyperparameters via random search.  
6. At evaluation, draw multiple MC samples from q(Θ) for each test point to compute predictive mean and entropy; generate selective prediction curves by varying entropy‑based deferral threshold.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Prior strength** (τ, s1, s2): Too strong may restrict posterior flexibility; too weak reverts to uninformative prior. Requires careful tuning.  
- **Context set size and composition**: Larger, more diverse sets improve prior quality but increase computational cost; balance needed.  
- **Stochastic vs. deterministic backbone**: Initializing q(Θ) from a strong deterministic checkpoint improves convergence and performance; random initialization may yield poorer results.  
- **Corruption selection**: Generic corruptions may not cover all real‑world uncertainty sources; overly aggressive corruptions could bias the prior.

### Failure Signatures
- **Overfitting to known corruptions**: Model uncertain only on specific corruption types but not on other uncertainties. Diagnose by evaluating on held‑out corruption types.  
- **Underfitting due to excessive regularization**: High uncertainty term weight leads to poor task performance (low AUROC). Monitor training loss and validation AUROC.  
- **Unstable selective curves**: Volatile selective metrics across random seeds indicate unreliable uncertainty estimates.  
- **High standard AUROC but low selective AUROC**: Model is accurate but poorly calibrated; overconfident on errors. Indicates prior failed to shape uncertainty.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Ablation on Prior Components**: Train MedCertAIn with (a) only corruptions, (b) only inter‑modal similarity, (c) only inter+intra similarity, (d) full hybrid. Validate that hybrid yields highest selective AUPRC.  
2. **Context Set Sensitivity Analysis**: Vary the similarity threshold parameter `v` (e.g., 0.5–3.0) and evaluate selective performance to identify optimal range and check for overfitting.  
3. **Corruption Generalization Test**: After training with a set of corruption types, evaluate the model on unseen corruption types (e.g., EHR `TimeWarp`, CXR `ColorJitter`). A robust prior should still assign high uncertainty to these samples.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization across tasks:** Does MedCertAIn generalize to other clinical tasks beyond in‑hospital mortality prediction?  
- **Adding new modalities:** Can integrating additional modalities such as radiology reports improve uncertainty estimation?  
- **Clinically motivated perturbations:** Do data perturbations that reflect real clinical artefacts outperform generic corruptions?  
- **External validity:** Does MedCertAIn perform reliably across diverse hospitals and patient populations outside MIMIC?

## Limitations
- Exact corruption magnitudes and similarity threshold values are unspecified, hindering faithful reproduction and generalization assessment.  
- The clinical utility of entropy‑based deferral remains unvalidated with domain experts or in prospective clinical settings.  
- Evaluation is limited to a single dataset (MIMIC), raising concerns about external validity across institutions and patient groups.
