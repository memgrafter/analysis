# $μ$pscaling small models: Principled warm starts and hyperparameter transfer

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10545
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10545
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A theoretically grounded upscaling framework (µpscaling) that uses weight transformations and hyperparameter scalings to achieve exact dynamic equivalence between narrow and wide models, enabling hyperparameter transfer from cheap small-scale upscaling to large-scale training.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces µpscaling, a principled method for warm-starting large neural networks from smaller pretrained checkpoints. By extending the maximal update parametrization (µP), the authors derive precise weight transformations and coordinated hyperparameter scalings that ensure the upscaled model follows an identical training trajectory in function space as the original small model. Injection of appropriately scaled Gaussian noise breaks symmetry and unlocks the additional capacity of the wider architecture. Crucially, because the entire procedure adheres to µP, hyperparameters (learning rate, noise scale) tuned on a small system transfer directly to much larger models. The method applies broadly to standard architectures (MLP, ResNet, Transformer) and optimizers (SGD, Adam, AdamW). Experiments confirm faster convergence and stable optimal hyperparameters across widths, offering an efficient alternative to training large models from scratch.

## Method Summary
The method proceeds in three stages. First, a base model is trained under maximal update parametrization (µP) to ensure stable, feature-learning dynamics. Second, to upscale, the pretrained weights are duplicated and rescaled according to width multipliers (e.g., for a matrix-like layer, `W↑ = k_in⁻¹ W ⊗ 1_k_out 1_k_inᵀ`). Optimizer states (momentum, Adam moments) are similarly transformed. Third, Gaussian noise with variance scaled to µP's initialization prescription (e.g., `Var(ΔW) ∝ N_in⁻¹ σ_Δ²`) is injected. The learning rate and other µP hyperparameters (`γ↑`, weight decay) are set via µP's scaling laws. If the entire process respects µP, hyperparameters found optimal for upscaling from a small base width `n₀` to `k·n₀` transfer zero-shot to upscaling from a large width `n` to `k·n`.

## Key Results
- Upscaled models converge faster and reach comparable or lower training loss than training the same large model from scratch.
- Optimal hyperparameters (learning rate base constant, injected noise scale `σ_Δ`) remain stable across significantly different widths, validating transfer.
- The method yields exact dynamic equivalence between narrow and wide models when no noise is injected, confirming the weight and optimizer state transformations.
- Experiments span MLPs, ResNets, and Transformers with SGD, Adam, and AdamW, demonstrating broad applicability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Dynamic Equivalence via Coordinated Scaling
Weight duplication with rescaling and adjusted hyperparameters yields **dynamic equivalence**—the widened model's training trajectory in function space matches the base model's. For a base layer with weight matrix `W` and learning rate `γ`, construct the widened model `W↑ = k_in⁻¹ W ⊗ 1_k_out 1_k_inᵀ`. For SGD, set `γ↑ = k_out / k_in · γ`. More generally, for any optimizer with a homogeneous update function (degree `m`), set `γ↑ = k_out^m / k_in · γ`. This ensures that if `W↑_0` is derived from `W_0`, then `W↑_t` at any step `t` is the corresponding duplication of `W_t`. The core assumption is that the optimizer's update rule is homogeneous (e.g., SGD has `m=1`, Adam has `m=0`). Evidence appears in the formal proofs (Proposition 2.4, Theorem C.8) and is anchored in the µP literature. The equivalence breaks if the optimizer uses non-homogeneous operations (e.g., certain gradient clipping) or if the architecture includes operations outside the `Ne⊗or^⊤` class (e.g., weight tying not expressible as duplication).

### Noise Injection Unlocks Additional Capacity
Without noise, the duplicated model remains confined to a lower-dimensional subspace of the wider weight space, unable to utilize the extra parameters. Injecting Gaussian noise `Δ` with variance scaled according to µP's initialization (e.g., `Var(Δ_matrix) ∝ N_in⁻¹ σ_Δ²`) breaks this symmetry. The noise scale `σ_Δ` is a new hyperparameter. This perturbation has the same width-scaling as a fresh µP initialization, so its effect remains `Θ(1)` in the infinite-width limit, preventing signal corruption while allowing the model to explore functions only representable in the higher-dimensional space. The mechanism is supported by infinite-width analysis (Appendix E) where noise terms appear in the limiting recursion, and by empirical necessity: upscaling without noise often fails to improve over from-scratch training. Failure occurs if `σ_Δ` is too large (corrupts transferred knowledge) or too small (insufficient symmetry breaking).

### Hyperparameter Transfer via µP Regime Alignment
Because the upscaling procedure (weight transformation + noise injection) can itself be encoded as an `Ne⊗or^⊤` program, the µP theory from Yang et al. (2022) applies directly. This means the training dynamics of upscaled models at different widths are governed by the same asymptotic scaling laws. Consequently, hyperparameters (`γ↑`, `σ_Δ`) tuned on a cheap small-scale upscaling experiment (`n₀ → k·n₀`) remain optimal when applied to a much larger upscaling (`n → k·n`), provided both `n₀` and `n` are sufficiently large to be in the µP regime. Empirical verification across multiple architectures (Fig. 3) shows stable optimal hyperparameters across widths. Transfer likely fails for very narrow base widths (`n₀ < 50`) where µP's infinite-width approximations break down, or for architectures with strong violations of µP assumptions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Maximal Update Parametrization (µP)**
  - **Why needed:** µP prescribes width-dependent initialization and learning-rate scalings that keep gradients and activations at a constant scale during training, enabling "feature learning" and making dynamics width-aligned. Upscaling directly uses µP's scalings for weights, learning rates, and noise to preserve equivalence and enable transfer.
  - **Quick check:** For a matrix-like layer in an MLP under µP, how does the learning rate scale with width `n` compared to a base constant? (Answer: `γ ∝ n`.)
- **Concept: Dynamic Equivalence**
  - **Why needed:** The goal is not just to match the function at initialization but to ensure the entire training trajectory of the wide model matches the continuation of the small model. This requires coordinated scaling of weights, learning rates, and optimizer states.
  - **Quick check:** If you double all hidden-layer widths in an MLP trained with SGD, how must you adjust the learning rate for the first hidden layer to maintain dynamic equivalence? (Answer: Multiply by `k` if input width unchanged, i.e., `γ^{(1)↑} = k γ^{(1)}`.)
- **Concept: `Ne⊗or^⊤` Program**
  - **Why needed:** This formal language unifies the analysis of neural architectures (MLP, ResNet, Transformer) and backpropagation. The proofs that upscaling preserves µP dynamics rely on showing the upscaling operations can be expressed within this framework.
  - **Quick check:** Which operations are allowed in an `Ne⊗or^⊤` program? (a) elementwise nonlinearity, (b) matrix multiplication, (c) averaging over width, (d) all of the above. (Answer: d)
- **Concept: Symmetry Breaking in Parameter Space**
  - **Why needed:** Weight duplication creates a model whose parameters lie in a lower-dimensional subspace of the full wide parameter space. Injecting noise is necessary to allow the optimizer to escape this subspace and utilize the full capacity.
  - **Quick check:** If you duplicate every weight matrix `W` into `W ⊗ 1_k` without rescaling or noise, what is the rank of the new weight matrix relative to the original? (Answer: It has the same rank as `W`; the duplication creates block structure that does not increase rank.)
- **Concept: Homogeneous Optimizer Updates**
  - **Why needed:** The dynamic equivalence proof relies on the optimizer's update function being homogeneous in its inputs (weights, gradients). This property ensures the scaling transformation commutes with the update.
  - **Quick check:** For an update `θ_{t+1} = θ_t - γ ∇L(θ_t)`, what is the degree of homogeneity? (Answer: 1, because scaling `θ` by `c` scales `∇L` by `c` for linear losses, and the update scales by `c`.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Pretrained checkpoint -> Weight transformation (duplication & rescaling) -> Optimizer state duplication/rescaling -> Noise injection (scaled Gaussian) -> µP training with scaled hyperparameters (`γ↑`, `σ_Δ`).
- **Critical path:** 1) Train small model under µP. 2) At a chosen checkpoint, apply weight duplication rules (Table 1). 3) Duplicate and rescale optimizer buffers (momentum, Adam moments) per Appendix D. 4) Add noise `Δ` with variance proportional to µP init scale (controlled by `σ_Δ`). 5) Train the upscaled model under µP using the scaled base constants. 6) For hyperparameter transfer, tune `σ_Δ` and `γ↑` on a small-scale upscaling (e.g., 128→256) and reuse them for large-scale upscaling (e.g., 1024→2048).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - `σ_Δ` (noise scale): Low values risk trapping the model in the low-capacity duplicated subspace; high values may overwhelm the transferred signal. Must be tuned on a small system.
  - Base tuning width `n₀`: Must be large enough (>50) to be in the µP regime for transfer to hold, but small enough to keep sweep cost low. This tension is the main practical limitation.
  - Learning rate `γ↑`: Theory predicts `γ↑` should equal the base `γ` for zero noise, but with `σ_Δ>0` the optimal value may shift slightly; still, the same value should transfer across larger widths.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Exact training trajectory mismatch between zero-noise upscaled model and continued base model (check weight relation at each step).
  - No convergence speedup vs. from-scratch training for the wide model, especially when `σ_Δ=0` or `n₀` is too small.
  - Optimal `σ_Δ` or `γ↑` varies dramatically with target width, indicating µP regime has not been reached.
  - Training instability (loss spikes) if optimizer state or learning rates are not rescaled correctly.
  - Poor generalization relative to from-scratch training (as seen for ResNet on CIFAR-100), which the theory does not address.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Equivalence validation:** For an MLP with width `n`, train under µP. Construct zero-noise upscaled model with width `k·n` using the duplication rules. Train both from their respective initializations on identical data. Verify that at every step `t`, `W↑_t = k_in⁻¹ W_t ⊗ 1_k_out 1_k_inᵀ` holds exactly, and loss trajectories overlap perfectly.
  2. **Noise necessity sweep:** For a fixed well-trained base checkpoint (width `n`), upscale to `k·n` with varying `σ_Δ` (including 0). Train each upscaled model under µP with a fixed `γ↑`. Compare final training loss and convergence speed against (a) `σ_Δ=0` upscaling and (b) training the wide model from scratch under µP. Confirm `σ_Δ>0` yields superior results to both.
  3. **Hyperparameter transfer test:** Choose several increasing base widths (`n₀` = 128, 256, 512) and a large target width `N` (e.g., 2048). For each `n₀`, upscale to `k·n₀` (with `k = N / n₀` not necessarily integer) and sweep `σ_Δ` and `γ↑` to find the fastest converging pair. Then apply these same hyperparameters to upscale `N` to `k·N`. Verify that the training curve of the `N→k·N` upscaled model matches the curve from the optimal sweep at that large scale, and outperforms from-scratch training at `k·N`.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Can hyperparameter transfer be formally guaranteed for non-linear architectures under µP when upscaling mid-training? The only formal proof is for linear MLPs (Hayou, 2025). Extensions to ReLU, attention, etc., rely on empirical observation.
- **Open Question 2:** How can µpscaling be extended to simultaneously increase both width and depth of a pretrained model while preserving dynamic equivalence? The paper focuses on width scaling; depth scaling introduces new mean-field considerations.
- **Open Question 3:** Why does model upscaling sometimes harm generalization, as observed for ResNets on CIFAR-100? The theory only accounts for training dynamics, not generalization implicit biases.
- **Open Question 4:** How can one efficiently determine a minimal tuning width `n₀` that reliably supports hyperparameter transfer without prohibitive computational cost? Effective transfer requires `n₀` to be in the µP regime (often >100), creating a tension between cost and validity.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees for hyperparameter transfer are currently limited to linear MLPs; empirical success on non-linear models lacks rigorous proof.
- The analysis assumes optimizers with homogeneous update functions and architectures expressible as `Ne⊗or^⊤` programs; deviations (e.g., certain gradient clipping, weight tying) may break equivalence.
- Practical reproduction lacks fully explicit formulas for AdamW's second-moment buffer (`v`) and epsilon scaling, and precise handling of Batch/Layer Norm statistics is vaguely specified.
- The method's advantage hinges on the base width `n₀` being large enough to be in the µP regime, but determining this threshold systematically is an open problem.
- The theory does not address generalization; upscaling can sometimes harm test performance relative to from-scratch training.

## Confidence
- **Dynamic equivalence for homogeneous optimizers in `Ne⊗or^⊤` architectures:** High confidence. Core mechanism is rigorously derived from µP theory.
- **Hyperparameter transfer across widths:** Medium confidence. Strong empirical evidence across moderate widths, but no formal finite-width guarantees; likely fails for very narrow base models.
- **Noise injection as necessary and sufficient for capacity unlocking:** Medium-low confidence. Symmetry breaking motivation is sound and supported by infinite-width analysis, but the optimal `σ_Δ` scaling is heuristic and lacks a formal sufficiency proof.
- **Practical reproducibility with standard deep learning frameworks:** Low confidence. Critical details for optimizer state duplication (especially AdamW) and normalization layer handling are incompletely specified, risking subtle divergence.
