# Scalable Contrastive Causal Discovery under Unknown Soft Interventions
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03411  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03411  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: SCONE achieves scalable causal discovery under unknown soft interventions, significantly improving structural recovery (e.g., SHD 14.6±4.4 on 20‑node graphs) compared to state‑of‑the‑art baselines.

## Executive Summary
Causal discovery from paired observational and interventional data is challenging when the intervention targets are unknown and the shifts are soft (i.e., they alter mechanisms without completely breaking dependencies). Traditional methods struggle with Markov equivalence ambiguities and scaling to large graphs. SCONE introduces a three‑stage framework: first, estimate local subset‑level PDAGs using classical causal discovery; second, apply novel contrastive cross‑regime orientation rules (Single‑Sided Invariance, Contrastive V‑structure, Contrastive Discriminating Path) that leverage regime‑shift signals to resolve edge directions; third, aggregate constraints globally with axial attention. Theoretical guarantees prove soundness and asymptotic recovery of a restricted Ψ‑equivalence class. Empirical results on synthetic graphs up to 100 nodes show consistent improvements in F1 and SHD over baselines, and ablations confirm the critical role of the contrastive components.

## Method Summary
SCONE operates in three sequential stages to balance scalability and accuracy. (1) **Local PDAG estimation**: The graph is partitioned into overlapping subsets; on each subset a classical causal discovery algorithm (e.g., PC or GES) is run to obtain a partial DAG (PDAG) skeleton. This local approach avoids the combinatorial complexity of global search and scales linearly with graph size when subset size is bounded. (2) **Contrastive orientation**: For each undirected edge in the local PDAGs, the method applies three rules that compare distributions across regimes (observational vs. interventional). These rules exploit invariance properties caused by soft interventions to uniquely orient edges that are otherwise Markov‑equivalent. (3) **Global aggregation via axial attention**: The orientation constraints from all subsets are combined using an axial attention mechanism that propagates information across the graph efficiently, resolving conflicts and producing a final PDAG that belongs to a restricted Ψ‑equivalence class. Under standard assumptions (causal sufficiency, correct local skeletons, soft interventions), the method is shown to recover the true causal structure asymptotically.

## Key Results
- SCONE outperforms state‑of‑the‑art baselines (e.g., DCD‑FG, SEA) in structural accuracy: on 20‑node graphs, SHD 14.6±4.4, F1 0.655±0.094; on 100‑node graphs, SHD 126.7±25.0, F1 0.237±0.046.
- Ablation studies demonstrate that removing the contrastive orientation rules leads to drastic drops in performance, confirming their necessity.
- The approach scales to graphs with up to 100 nodes while maintaining reasonable accuracy, whereas many scalable baselines incur much higher SHD.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Soft interventions change the conditional distribution of the intervened variable in a way that often preserves certain invariance properties across regimes. SCONE’s contrastive rules are designed to detect these invariants. Single‑Sided Invariance identifies edges where only one endpoint’s distribution changes, pointing to the direction of intervention. Contrastive V‑structure uses regime‑specific conditional independence tests to determine colliders. Contrastive Discriminating Path distinguishes direct from indirect connections by checking whether intervening on a node breaks the dependence along a path. By applying these rules locally on subsets and then aggregating globally, edges that are ambiguous under purely observational data become uniquely orientable. The axial attention module efficiently combines many local constraints without exhaustive search, while respecting the partial order implied by the oriented edges. Theoretical analysis shows that under the stated assumptions, the set of rules uniquely orients every edge that can be distinguished by any contrastive method, and the aggregation yields a sound PDAG that converges to the true DAG as sample size grows.

## Foundational Learning
- **Causal Discovery**  
  Why needed: The core problem is inferring causal structure from data.  
  Quick check: Can you determine causality solely from observational correlations? (No, without assumptions such as interventions or temporal order.)
- **Soft Interventions**  
  Why needed: They model realistic regime shifts (e.g., drug perturbations that tweak rather than completely sever a gene’s regulation).  
  Quick check: Does a soft intervention change the conditional distribution of the target variable? Yes, but it does not necessarily remove all incoming edges.
- **Markov Equivalence**  
  Why needed: Explains why observational data alone cannot uniquely identify the DAG.  
  Quick check: Two DAGs are Markov equivalent if and only if they share the same skeleton and the same immoralities? (Yes, under causal sufficiency and faithfulness.)
- **Axial Attention**  
  Why needed: The global aggregation step uses this efficient transformer variant to fuse many orientation constraints.  
  Quick check: Axial attention processes data with separate row‑wise and column‑wise attention passes to reduce quadratic complexity? (True.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Paired Observational & Interventional Data -> Local PDAG Estimation (subset‑wise PC/GES) -> Contrastive Orientation Rules (Single‑Sided Invariance, Contrastive V‑structure, Contrastive Discriminating Path) -> Axial Attention Aggregation -> Output PDAG (Ψ‑equivalence class)

Critical path: The data flows sequentially; local PDAG skeletons provide the undirected edges that feed into the contrastive rules, which produce orientation constraints; axial attention then resolves any conflicts and merges constraints across subsets to yield the final graph.

Design tradeoffs:
- Local subset estimation trades off global accuracy for scalability; errors in local skeletons may propagate but are mitigated by later contrastive steps.
- Contrastive rules rely on detectable distributional changes; weak or noisy interventions reduce their effectiveness.
- Axial attention provides near‑linear global aggregation at the cost of additional hyperparameters and potential overfitting.

Failure signatures:
- High false‑positive/negative rates in local PDAGs lead to many unresolved edges or incorrect orientations.
- Presence of hidden confounders breaks causal sufficiency, creating spurious regime‑dependent dependencies that mislead rules.
- Hard interventions (e.g., do‑operations) violate the invariance assumptions of the Single‑Sided Invariance rule, causing it to fail.

First experiments:
1. **Baseline replication**: Run SCONE on 20‑node random graphs with known soft interventions and compare SHD/F1 to the reported numbers and to baselines (DCD‑FG, SEA). This validates core implementation.
2. **Ablation of contrastive rules**: Disable each contrastive rule individually and all together to quantify their individual contributions to orientation accuracy.
3. **Intervention strength sensitivity**: Vary the magnitude of soft interventions (e.g., noise level added) and observe performance trends to assess robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not include a dedicated open questions or future work section. However, based on its content and limitations, several open questions emerge:

- **Robustness to hidden confounders**: How does SCONE perform when causal sufficiency is violated?
- **Scalability beyond 100 nodes**: Can the method maintain accuracy and efficiency on larger, denser graphs?
- **Intervention sensitivity**: How does performance vary with different strengths of soft interventions?
- **Real‑world applicability**: How effective is SCONE on real interventional datasets with complex mechanisms and noise?
- **Theoretical guarantees under weaker conditions**: Can soundness be extended beyond the restricted Ψ‑equivalence class?

These questions align with the paper's own noted limitations and suggest directions for future research.

## Limitations
Assumes causal sufficiency (no hidden confounders) and that regime shifts are soft interventions; real‑world data often violate both. Theoretical recovery is limited to a restricted Ψ‑equivalence class, so some edge directions may remain ambiguous. Evaluation is solely on synthetic data; real‑world interventional datasets with noise and complex mechanisms remain untested. Scalability claims are supported only up to 100 nodes; larger or denser graphs may challenge runtime and accuracy. Errors from the initial local PDAG stage may propagate even after contrastive correction.

## Confidence
- **Soundness & asymptotic recovery**: High (theoretical guarantees under standard assumptions)
- **Empirical superiority over baselines**: Medium (improvements on synthetic data but lacking real‑world validation)
- **Necessity of contrastive components**: High (ablation studies show drastic performance drops when removed)
- **Scalability to large graphs**: Medium (tested up to 100 nodes; larger graphs untested)
