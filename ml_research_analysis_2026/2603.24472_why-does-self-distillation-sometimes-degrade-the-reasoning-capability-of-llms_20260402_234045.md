# Why Does Self-Distillation (Sometimes) Degrade the Reasoning Capability of LLMs?

## Quick Facts
- **arxiv**: 2603.24472  
- **source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24472  
- **references**: 40  
- **primary result**: Self-distillation (SDPO) suppresses epistemic verbalization (e.g., “wait”, “maybe”), leading to concise but overconfident reasoning that harms out-of-distribution (OOD) mathematical performance, while GRPO preserves uncertainty markers and generalizes better.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates why on-policy self-distillation (SDPO) for LLM post-training, while often improving in-domain performance and shortening responses, degrades mathematical reasoning on out-of-distribution benchmarks. The core mechanism identified is the suppression of epistemic verbalization—uncertainty expressions like “wait” or “maybe”—when the teacher model is conditioned on full, correct solutions during distillation. This suppression yields responses that are more confident and concise but less robust. In contrast, the GRPO objective, which does not involve a fixed teacher providing full solutions, maintains these uncertainty markers and achieves superior OOD generalization. The findings imply that training objectives for reasoning must explicitly preserve uncertainty-aware behavior, not just optimize for correct final answers.

## Method Summary
The work compares two post-training algorithms on mathematical reasoning: on-policy self-distillation (SDPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Both use the DAPO-Math-17k dataset (~14k problems). For SDPO, a fixed teacher model (initial weights, EMA=0) generates solutions conditioned on the full, correct trajectory (including ` The student is trained to match this teacher distribution via a Jensen–Shannon divergence loss (top-K logits K=100). GRPO uses on-policy rollouts without a fixed teacher providing full solutions. Training uses identical hyperparameters except learning rates (SDPO: 1e-5, GRPO: 1e-6). Evaluation on AIME24, AMC23, and MATH500 measures accuracy (Acc@16), average response length, and frequency of a predefined set of 10 epistemic tokens (e.g., “wait”, “maybe”, “check”).

## Key Results
- SDPO significantly reduces response length and suppresses epistemic token usage compared to the base model and GRPO.
- On OOD benchmarks (AIME24, AMC23), SDPO causes accuracy drops up to 40% relative to GRPO and the base model, while GRPO maintains or improves performance.
- The suppression of epistemic verbalization correlates strongly with degraded OOD generalization; preserving these markers is predictive of robust reasoning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core degradation mechanism is **teacher-induced confidence distortion**. In SDPO, the teacher provides a polished, correct trace that implicitly encodes a “perfect reasoning” prior. When the student is distilled toward this distribution, it learns to suppress intermediate expressions of doubt or reconsideration (epistemic markers) because the teacher’s demonstrations rarely contain them. This leads to a more direct, confident, but less calibrated reasoning style. GRPO avoids this by not conditioning the reference policy on full, correct solutions; its rollouts naturally retain uncertainty markers. The retained markers act as a form of **reasoning redundancy**—hesitations, checks, and alternative considerations—that provide robustness when faced with novel problems where the direct path fails.

## Foundational Learning
- **Self-Distillation (SDPO)**: A sequence-level distillation method where a student policy is trained to match a fixed teacher policy’s distribution on on-policy rollouts. *Why needed*: Enables knowledge transfer without a separate external teacher. *Quick check*: Verify student’s KL divergence to teacher decreases during training.
- **Epistemic Verbalization**: Linguistic markers (e.g., “maybe”, “wait”, “check”) that signal uncertainty or metacognitive evaluation during reasoning. *Why needed*: Serve as a proxy for model calibration and reasoning depth. *Quick check*: Count token frequency from a predefined list in generated traces.
- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**: A reinforcement learning algorithm that optimizes a policy relative to a group of rollouts without an external reference. *Why needed*: Avoids introducing bias from a fixed teacher. *Quick check*: Ensure group reward normalization is applied correctly in code.
- **On-Policy Sampling**: Generating responses using the current student policy for training. *Why needed*: Prevents distribution shift between training and inference. *Quick check*: Monitor that generation parameters (temperature, sampling) match evaluation conditions.
- **Mathematical Reward Verification**: Using programmatic or symbolic tools (e.g., `math-verify`) to extract and validate final answers from `\boxed{}`. *Why needed*: Provides dense, reliable supervision for math tasks. *Quick check*: Spot-check a sample of extracted answers against ground truth.
- **Response Length Compression**: The observed shortening of model outputs during distillation. *Why needed*: Often a side-effect of imitating confident teacher traces. *Quick check*: Track average token count per response across training steps.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Question -> Student Policy (current model) -> Rollout Responses -> Teacher Scoring (fixed model conditioned on full solution) -> JS Divergence Loss -> Student Update  
(For GRPO: remove teacher conditioning on full solution; use group-based advantage instead.)

**Critical Path**:  
1. Sample batch of questions from DAPO-Math-17k.  
2. Student generates multiple responses per question (rollouts).  
3. For SDPO: Teacher re-ranks each response with the full correct solution provided as context.  
4. Compute loss (JS div for SDPO, group-relative PPO for GRPO) using reward (answer correctness).  
5. Backpropagate and update student.  

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- *SDPO*: Fast in-domain gains via imitation of correct traces but risks overfitting to teacher’s confident style, suppressing useful uncertainty signals.  
- *GRPO*: Slower convergence, retains exploration and epistemic markers, better OOD robustness.  

**Failure Signatures**:  
- SDPO: Rapid decrease in epistemic token count and response length, followed by OOD accuracy drop.  
- GRPO: Stable or increasing epistemic tokens, maintained or improved OOD performance.  
- Training instability: loss spikes if teacher conditioning is mis-specified or if KL penalty is omitted.

**First 3 Experiments**:  
1. **Baseline Comparison**: Train SDPO and GRPO for 100 steps; evaluate length, epistemic tokens, and Acc@16 on AIME24/AMC23/MATH500. Expect SDPO to shorten outputs and degrade OOD accuracy.  
2. **Teacher Conditioning Ablation**: Vary teacher context: (a) full solution with `<think>`, (b) only final answer, (c) none. Measure impact on epistemic suppression and OOD performance.  
3. **Epistemic Token Ablation**: During training, penalize or mask specific epistemic tokens (e.g., remove “wait”) to test causality between their suppression and OOD degradation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can self-distillation objectives be modified to preserve epistemic verbalization while still achieving response length compression?** The paper diagnoses the problem but offers no algorithmic solution; it calls for objectives that optimize reasoning beyond answer correctness.
2. **What causes the initial performance improvement followed by degradation in hybrid teacher-student setups (e.g., OPSD with student thinking disabled but teacher enabled)?** The phenomenon is observed but not analyzed; possible causes include reasoning style mismatch or feedback loop dynamics.
3. **Do different epistemic markers (e.g., “wait” vs. “maybe”) have differential impacts on out-of-distribution generalization, and can selective preservation improve efficiency?** The paper measures aggregate counts uniformly; per-token functional importance remains unknown.
4. **How does task coverage interact with model scale in determining the severity of epistemic suppression and its impact on generalization?** Experiments varied training data size but only on 7B–8B models; scaling laws for this trade-off are unexplored.

## Limitations
- Exact training details (sampling strategy without replacement, number of steps beyond 100) are unspecified.
- Teacher conditioning format (concatenation, special tokens) is not precisely documented.
- Reward verification implementation (normalization, symbolic equivalence) may differ from original `verl` pipeline.
- Handling of Qwen3’s `<think>` tag generation and stopping conditions is ambiguous, affecting response length measurements.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1**: Self-distillation degrades OOD math reasoning and suppresses epistemic verbalization. **High** (consistent across three model families).
- **Claim 2**: GRPO preserves uncertainty markers and yields better OOD generalization. **High** (validated on multiple benchmarks).
- **Claim 3**: Preserving epistemic verbalization is crucial for robust reasoning; objectives should be uncertainty-aware. **Medium** (correlative evidence, but causal link not fully isolated).
