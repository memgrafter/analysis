# Holographic Invariant Storage: Design-Time Safety Contracts via Vector Symbolic Architectures

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.13558
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13558
- Reference count: 29
- Primary result: HIS provides design‑time safety contracts with theoretical recovery fidelity (0.707), robustness (2Φ(1/σ)−1), and multi‑signal capacity (≈√1/(K+1)), validated by Monte Carlo and a pilot behavioral experiment.

## Executive Summary
Holographic Invariant Storage (HIS) introduces a design‑time safety contract for large language models using bipolar Vector Symbolic Architectures. The method stores safety invariants as hypervectors and guarantees recovery fidelity under normalized noise: single‑signal recovery converges to 0.707 cosine similarity, continuous‑noise robustness follows 2Φ(1/σ)−1, and multi‑signal capacity scales as ≈√1/(K+1). Monte Carlo simulations (n=1000) validate these bounds (µ=0.7072, σ=0.0036). A pilot behavioral experiment (4 LLMs, 720 trials) indicates that HIS‑based re‑injection improves safety adherence at the 2B scale (p_Holm=0.065, d=0.71). These pre‑deployment bounds enable engineers to reason about trade‑offs independent of noise content or context length—capabilities that timers or embedding metrics lack.

## Method Summary
HIS encodes natural‑language safety instructions into bipolar hypervectors. Offline, each invariant is stored as H_inv = K_goal ⊗ V_safe (binding a random key with the safety vector). During inference, the current context is encoded into a real‑valued vector N_context, which is normalized to ∥N_hat∥=√D to match H_inv’s scale. The restoration algorithm then computes S = H_inv + N_hat, binarizes via S_clean = sign(S) (with abstention on zeros), and recovers V_recovered = S_clean ⊗ K_goal. For multiple invariants (K), a stored codebook enables exact retrieval by nearest‑neighbor search. In the pilot study, a drift monitor triggers re‑injection when the raw cosine similarity between H_inv and N_hat falls below τ=0.25, feeding the decoded safety instruction back into the LLM. The evaluation compares six conditions: no intervention, timers, HIS, matched timer, embedding monitor, and random timing.

## Key Results
- Theoretical bounds (single‑signal recovery 0.707, continuous‑noise robustness 2Φ(1/σ)−1, multi‑signal capacity ≈√1/(K+1)) validated by Monte Carlo (n=1000, µ=0.7072, σ=0.0036).
- Pilot behavioral improvement (4 LLMs, 720 trials) reports safety re‑injection at the 2B scale yields p_Holm=0.065, d=0.71.
- Pre‑deployment bounds enable engineering trade‑offs independent of noise content or context length—properties not offered by timers or embedding metrics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Single‑signal recovery converges to 0.707 under bipolar normalized noise
The restoration protocol sums H_inv with a normalized noise vector N_hat (∥N_hat∥=√D). Both vectors are bipolar (±1) and N_hat is independent and uniformly random. After addition, exactly half the dimensions cancel (producing zeros) and half agree, assuming sign(0)=0. The resulting sparse S_clean is geometrically closer to H_inv than the dense noisy vector. Under these assumptions the expected cosine similarity converges to 1/√2 ≈0.707, regardless of noise depth. Core assumptions: N_hat independent, uniformly random bipolar; sign(0) set to 0. Evidence: Theorem 1 proves E[CosSim]=1/√2; Monte Carlo yields µ=0.7072, σ=0.0036. Break if noise correlates with H_inv, normalization fails, or tie‑handling differs.

### Mechanism 2: Continuous‑noise robustness 2Φ(1/σ)−1
For i.i.d. continuous symmetric noise (e.g., Gaussian N(0,σ²)) after normalization, exact cancellation has probability zero, so S_clean remains dense (±1). Per‑dimension recovery succeeds when the sign of H_inv,i + noise_i matches H_inv,i, which occurs with probability Φ(1/σ). Overall cosine similarity becomes 2Φ(1/σ)−1. Core assumptions: noise i.i.d. from a symmetric continuous distribution; normalization holds; independence. Evidence: Proposition 1 derives the formula; Remark 4 explains why Monte Carlo with binarized noise converges to 0.707 (effective σ very small). Break if noise is asymmetric, heavy‑tailed, or normalization violated.

### Mechanism 3: Multi‑signal capacity scales as √1/(K+1)
Bundling K bound pairs (K_j ⊗ V_j) creates a composite vector. Unbinding for target V_k yields V_k plus (K−1) cross‑talk terms (random bipolar vectors) plus noise. This is equivalent to a (K+1)‑way superposition of random bipolar vectors. After sign cleanup, the surviving fraction of signal dimensions scales as 1/(K+1), giving CosSim ≈ √1/(K+1). Exact retrieval requires a codebook of all V_j and nearest‑neighbor search. Core assumptions: all keys and values independent random bipolar vectors; normalization holds; codebook uses exact nearest‑neighbor. Evidence: Proposition 2 heuristic; Monte Carlo (n=1000, D=10000) confirms scaling but actual values are slightly lower (e.g., K=3 → 0.474 vs. 0.500). For K>5, fidelity falls below 0.38 without codebook. Break if K too large without codebook, keys/values not independent, or normalization errors amplify cross‑talk.

## Foundational Learning
- **Vector Symbolic Architectures (VSA) / Hyperdimensional Computing**  
  *Why needed:* HIS relies on high‑dimensional bipolar spaces and algebraic operations (binding ⊗, bundling +) to store/retrieve invariants. Near‑orthogonality of random vectors and concentration of measure underpin the fidelity guarantees.  
  *Quick check:* Why does binding a⊗b produce a vector nearly orthogonal to both a and b in high dimensions?

- **Normalization constraint (∥N_hat∥=√D)**  
  *Why needed:* The theoretical bounds assume signal and noise have equal magnitude (0 dB SNR). Normalization is an active design choice that ensures fidelity is independent of noise magnitude.  
  *Quick check:* What happens to recovery fidelity if noise magnitude grows unbounded without normalization?

- **Codebook‑based decoding vs. direct recovery**  
  *Why needed:* For K>1, direct recovery from the superposition yields low fidelity (≈√1/(K+1)). A stored codebook enables exact retrieval by nearest‑neighbor search after restoration.  
  *Quick check:* When would you choose codebook cleanup over trusting raw cosine similarity from the superposition?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Semantic Encoder → Invariant Constructor (offline) → (store H_inv)  
Context Encoder & Normalizer → Restoration Engine → Drift Monitor & Trigger → [Codebook Decoder] → Re-injection Interface

**Critical path:** The normalization step (scaling N_context to ∥N_hat∥=√D) is the lynchpin for achieving the 0.707 recovery bound.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Normalization yields design‑time guarantees but discards noise magnitude information (no threat‑level sensing).  
- Codebook cleanup enables exact retrieval for K>1 but requires O(KD) external memory and nearest‑neighbor overhead.  
- Using sign(0)=0 improves single‑signal fidelity to 0.707 vs. 0.500 but introduces abstention (sparsity); other conventions break the bound.  
- Dimension D must be large (≫1000) for near‑orthogonality; D=10 000 is used in validation.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Codebook retrieval accuracy <100% in multi‑turn integration → check normalization, encoder orthogonality, codebook completeness.  
- Drift metric (cosine(H_inv, N_hat)) never drops below threshold → context noise may be too small or encoder not capturing corruption.  
- Restored fidelity <0.68 in simulation → violation of independence or normalization; inspect noise encoder distribution.  
- No behavioral improvement over timer → model‑scale ceiling (≥7B) or insufficiently challenging conversation script.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Monte Carlo validation: Run n=1000 trials with random bipolar H_inv and normalized noise; confirm mean recovery ≈0.707±0.004.  
2. Noise‑type robustness test: Encode three distinct noise sources (adversarial, flooding, semantic distraction); verify restored similarity ≈0.707 across all while pre‑restoration drift varies.  
3. Multi‑turn integration PoC: Simulate 50 turns with accumulating noise, use K=20 codebook; verify 100% retrieval accuracy and that raw integrity stays below τ=0.25 continuously.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
Open Question 1: Does HIS provide a statistically and practically significant improvement in safety adherence compared to simpler re‑injection methods (e.g., timers) in realistic deployments? The pilot is underpowered (n≈30), with ceiling effects and small effect size (d=0.25).

Open Question 2: How does HIS compare to RAG‑based safety retrieval in an end‑to‑end behavioral experiment? Only signal‑level comparison done; RAG may behave differently with natural language.

Open Question 3: Can learned semantic encoders improve HIS fidelity and robustness beyond the bag‑of‑words hash encoder? Current experiments used fixed encoders; learned may reduce variance and improve semantic mapping.

Open Question 4: Does HIS maintain performance over extended interactions (100+ turns) without cumulative degradation? Only 50‑turn simulation and 30‑turn trial; long‑term drift unknown.

## Limitations
- Encoder specification is incomplete (exact trigram hash parameters or projection matrices missing), risking correlated encoded vectors.
- Theory assumes independent, normalized noise; real LLM context drift may be structured and may not meet normalization, threatening fidelity bounds.
- Behavioral evidence is a pilot (4 LLMs, 720 trials) and underpowered; results are borderline (p_Holm=0.065) and lack human validation.

## Confidence
- Single‑signal recovery ≈0.707: **High**  
- Continuous‑noise robustness 2Φ(1/σ)−1: **Medium**  
- Multi‑signal capacity scaling ≈√1/(K+1): **Medium**  
- Behavioral improvement via re‑injection: **Low**
