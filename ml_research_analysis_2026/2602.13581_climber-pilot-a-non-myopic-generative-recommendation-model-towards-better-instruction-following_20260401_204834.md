# Climber-Pilot: A Non-Myopic Generative Recommendation Model Towards Better Instruction-Following

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.13581
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13581
- **Reference count:** 37
- **Primary result:** Achieves up to 4.24% Like Rate lift online and 10.95% HR@50 offline, with only 0.32 ms latency overhead.

## Executive Summary
Climber-Pilot tackles two key challenges in generative retrieval for recommendation: inherent myopia due to single-step inference and difficulty in following business constraints (instructions). It introduces innovations in pre‑training (Time‑Aware Multi‑Item Prediction, TAMIP) to distill long‑horizon user intent and in fine‑tuning (Condition‑Guided Sparse Attention, CGSA) to embed constraints directly into the generative process. The two‑stage paradigm maintains inference efficiency while enabling both general retrieval and instruction‑specific adaptation. Extensive offline and online evaluations on industrial and Amazon datasets demonstrate substantial gains, including a 4.24% increase in Like Rate on NetEase Cloud Music with negligible latency overhead.

## Method Summary
Climber‑Pilot reformulates sequential recommendation as a generative retrieval task. Its pre‑training stage employs a shared Transformer encoder (L layers) augmented with K parallel TAMIP branches. Each branch predicts a future item (i_{n+1}, …, i_{n+K}) from the encoded context. Crucially, TAMIP applies a time‑aware mask that blocks attention to historical items whose timestamps lie within a window [τ_{n+1} – Δτ, τ_{n+1}] around the target, thereby filtering spurious correlations caused by consumption lag in batch‑exposed logs. The fine‑tuning (SFT) stage reuses the pre‑trained backbone, but applies CGSA only to the final branches: a sparse binary mask (M_sparse) denoises the context by allowing attention only to items matching the given condition c (e.g., genre). During SFT each branch also processes a truncated history (S_{n−k+1}) to vary context lengths. At inference, the system pre‑computes top‑P interest conditions per user; batched multi‑condition serving reuses the shared L‑layer output across conditions, computes branch‑specific scores for each condition, and unions top‑K candidates for ANN retrieval. This design shifts complexity to training while preserving single‑step efficiency.

## Key Results
- HR@50 reaches 10.95% on industrial data, outperforming SASRec (7.62%).
- Online A/B test at NetEase Cloud Music shows 4.24% lift in Like Rate for general recommendation and 4.10% for genre‑specific.
- Inference latency increase is minimal (7.26 ms vs. 6.94 ms baseline, i.e., 0.32 ms overhead).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: TAMIP Alleviates Myopia
TAMIP mitigates generative retrieval’s inherent myopia by distilling long‑horizon, multi‑item user intent into model parameters during training, enabling effective single‑step inference. During pre‑training, K parallel branches each predict a future item. The time‑aware mask blocks attention to historically proximal items (within Δτ of the target), forcing the model to rely on longer‑term signals rather than spurious batch‑level correlations. Core assumption: consumption lag in batch‑exposed logs introduces non‑causal patterns that myopic models overfit to. Evidence: The abstract states TAMIP distills “long‑horizon, multi‑item foresight” via “time‑aware masking”; Section 3.2.2 formalizes the mask and its role in eliminating within‑batch contamination.

### Mechanism 2: CGSA Enables Constraint‑Aware Generation
Condition‑Guided Sparse Attention (CGSA) embeds business constraints directly into the generative process without additional inference steps. During SFT, a sparse binary mask (M_sparse) is applied to the final TAMIP branches, blocking attention to historical items whose category ≠ c. This denoises the user sequence, focusing generation on condition‑relevant context and aligning predictions with constraints. Core assumption: standard multi‑head attention aggregates noise from irrelevant categories, diluting condition‑specific intent; sparse attention on matching categories preserves personalization while enforcing constraints. Evidence: Abstract notes CGSA “incorporates business constraints directly into the generative process via sparse attention”; Section 3.3.2 details mask construction and application only to final branches.

### Mechanism 3: Two‑Stage Paradigm Shifts Complexity to Training
The pre‑training (TAMIP) + SFT (CGSA) stages shift complexity from inference to training, preserving single‑step efficiency while acquiring both general long‑horizon intent and instruction‑following capabilities. Stage 1 internalizes multi‑step foresight into a shared encoder. Stage 2 fine‑tunes with condition data, reusing early encoder layers and activating CGSA only in late branches, thereby retaining pre‑trained knowledge while adapting to constraints. Core assumption: catastrophic forgetting can be mitigated by layer reuse, branch‑localized CGSA, and a reduced learning rate. Evidence: Abstract describes “training‑time distillation” and “attention‑level control” to avoid multi‑step inference; Sections 3.3.1/3.3.3 outline SFT data construction, branch reuse, and reduced learning rate.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Layer (S_n, c) -> Shared Sequence Encoder (L layers) -> TAMIP Branches (K parallel; each: branch layer + optional CGSA) -> Projection Layer -> Item Scores
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Encode S_n through first L shared layers (with time‑aware mask in pre‑training).  
  2. For each branch k: process context S_{n−k+1} through branch layer (apply CGSA mask if in SFT).  
  3. Compute inner‑product scores between branch output and candidate item embeddings.  
  4. In deployment, union top‑P candidates from all branches for the given instruction, then perform ANN search.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - TAMIP adds K× computational cost during training/forward but preserves single‑step inference; larger K improves foresight distillation but increases memory; Δτ must align with serving intervals (15 min in paper).  
  - CGSA adds O(n) mask computation per branch but no extra layers; strict sparsity may hurt recall if condition matches few items; applied only to final layers to retain pre‑trained context.  
  - Two‑stage training: pre‑training cost high (45B interactions) but enables reuse; SFT uses reduced LR to prevent forgetting; if too low adaptation suffers, if too high forgetting occurs.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Pre‑training failure: HR@K curves drop steeply beyond step 1 → check Δτ or data temporal signal quality.  
  - SFT failure: Low CC@K despite high HR@K (or vice versa) → verify CGSA mask construction and condition label consistency.  
  - Online failure: Latency exceeds budget → profile shared L‑layer reuse and P (number of pre‑computed conditions).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Ablate time‑aware masking: pre‑train TAMIP without M_temp on Amazon Toys; expect HR@50 drop >30% if Mechanism 1 holds.  
  2. Compare CGSA against baselines (PinRec, LUM, AdaLN‑Zero) on industrial SFT dataset; measure HR@50 and CC@50.  
  3. Online A/B test: 5% traffic for (a) Baseline Transformer, (b) Climber‑Pilot w/o SFT, (c) Climber‑Pilot full model; verify Like Rate lifts and latency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Robustness of TAMIP’s temporal margin Δτ:** How sensitive is performance to Δτ, and does optimal Δτ depend on dataset characteristics or user behavior patterns? *(Basis: Authors fix Δτ=15 min without sensitivity analysis.)*
2. **Extension of CGSA to complex instructions:** Can CGSA handle non‑categorical, multi‑faceted, or fuzzy retrieval instructions (e.g., mood‑based queries)? *(Basis: CGSA defined for discrete category matching only; evaluated on genre/language attributes.)*
3. **Trade‑offs of TAMIP branch count (K):** What are the practical trade‑offs between K and inference latency/scalability, and is K=2 optimal? *(Basis: Authors set K=2 to balance efficiency but do not explore larger K or quantify marginal benefits.)*
4. **Sensitivity to recall instruction quality:** How sensitive is the overall system to the quality of pre‑computed recall instructions used in batched multi‑condition inference? *(Basis: Deployment caches top‑P interest categories per user but no evaluation of error propagation.)*

## Limitations
- Exact industrial data preprocessing (negative sampling, sequence truncation) is underspecified, limiting replication fidelity.
- SFT instruction generation (e.g., sliding window size/threshold for condition assignment) is not detailed, risking misalignment of CGSA.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity (K, Δτ) lacks systematic exploration; chosen values may not generalize to other domains.

## Confidence
- Offline result replication on industrial data: **Medium** confidence  
- Generalizability of CC@K to new conditions: **Low** confidence  
- Transfer of hyperparameter choices (Δτ, K) to other datasets: **Medium** confidence
