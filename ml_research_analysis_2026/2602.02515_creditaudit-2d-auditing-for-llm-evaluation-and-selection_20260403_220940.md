# CreditAudit: 2D Auditing for LLM Evaluation and Selection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02515  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02515  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Introduces a 2D audit outcome (mean ability μ and fluctuation σ) to quantify LLM stability under prompt variations, enabling risk‑aware deployment via credit grades (AAA–BBB).

## Executive Summary
Leaderboard scores often misrepresent real‑world LLM reliability because small prompt shifts can cause disproportionate performance drops. CreditAudit addresses this gap by evaluating models on fixed subsets of three multiple‑choice benchmarks (GPQA, TruthfulQA, MMLU‑Pro) across a family of semantically aligned system‑prompt templates. For each model it computes mean ability μ (average accuracy) and scenario‑induced fluctuation σ (standard deviation across templates). Mapping σ to credit grades via cross‑model quantiles highlights stability risks. Experiments reveal that models with similar μ can have vastly different σ (0.63–2.63), and in agentic or high‑failure‑cost settings, robustness may outweigh average performance. The resulting score‑fluctuation map and grades support a two‑tier selection strategy: prioritize models with low σ first, then optimize μ within the same grade tier.

## Method Summary
The method begins by defining a fixed evaluation subset D_b for each benchmark b (controlling item sampling variance) and constructing a set of T system‑prompt templates that are semantically aligned and non‑adversarial. For each model m, template t, and benchmark b, the model generates responses to the items in D_b; a deterministic parser g(·) extracts the chosen option ˆy and computes correctness. This yields a full evaluation cube of scores S_{m,t,b}. Scores are first averaged across benchmarks to S_{m,t}, then across templates to mean ability μ_m = E_t[S_{m,t}], while the standard deviation over templates gives fluctuation σ_m. Quantiles of σ across all models map σ to credit grades (AAA–BBB). A model’s deployment risk is thus represented by the pair (μ_m, σ_m) and its grade G(m). The minimum viable reproduction involves: (1) downloading the benchmarks and sampling fixed subsets; (2) implementing the template set and parser; (3) running the cube evaluation, aggregating, and assigning grades.

## Key Results
- Models with similar mean accuracy μ can exhibit widely different fluctuation σ (range 0.63–2.63), exposing hidden stability differences.  
- In agentic or high‑failure‑cost pipelines, low σ can outweigh higher μ, overturning standard leaderboard rankings.  
- The (μ, σ) map and corresponding credit grades enable tiered deployment: filter by robustness grade first, then optimize ability within the tier.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Systematically varying semantically aligned system prompts across fixed evaluation subsets isolates protocol‑sensitivity.  
**Mechanism:** Holding test items D_b constant and only changing the system prompt π_t forces any measured score variation S_{m,t,b} across templates to primarily reflect the model’s behavioral response to protocol shifts, not item sampling or benchmark content drift.  
**Core assumption:** The template set represents benign, routine deployment variations and does not globally alter task difficulty (scenario neutrality).  
**Evidence anchors:** abstract – “evaluates models under a family of semantically aligned and non‑adversarial system prompt templates”; section 4.1 – “all models and all prompt templates are evaluated on the same D_b, ensuring strict horizontal comparability.”  
**Break condition:** If templates introduce global difficulty drift (some π_t are universally easier/harder), σ_m conflates model sensitivity with artifact.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** The standard deviation of scores across templates σ_m serves as a proxy for expected volatility under normal protocol shifts.  
**Mechanism:** Under a continuous representation φ(π) and local differentiability, Var_π[S_m(π)] ≈ ∇_φ S_m(π_0)^⊤ Cov(φ(Π)) ∇_φ S_m(π_0). The empirical σ_m estimates this variance from the discrete sample {π_t}.  
**Core assumption:** Performance is differentiable in a continuous protocol representation and the sampled templates adequately cover the space of “reasonable” perturbations.  
**Evidence anchors:** section 4.3 – variance approximation; abstract – “scenario‑induced fluctuation σ (standard deviation across templates) as a stability risk signal.”  
**Break condition:** Performance gradients are non‑linear or the template set misses critical perturbation dimensions relevant to target deployment.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Mapping σ_m to credit grades (AAA–BBB) via cross‑model quantiles creates an interpretable risk tier that complements mean ability μ_m for regime‑specific selection.  
**Mechanism:** Quantile‑based discretization (G(m)) transforms the continuous σ into a coarse label; better (lower) σ yields a better grade. This supports the selection heuristic: prioritize low‑σ tier first, then optimize μ within the tier.  
**Core assumption:** Operational cost (integration, monitoring, failure) correlates more strongly with σ than with small μ differences, especially in agentic/high‑cost settings.  
**Evidence anchors:** abstract – “mapping volatility into interpretable credit grades from AAA to BBB via cross‑model quantiles”; section 5.4 – “prioritize low σ first, then consider score within the acceptable grade tier.”  
**Break condition:** If deployment cost is insensitive to σ or the quantile thresholds are not representative of the target regime’s risk tolerance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Protocol‑Conditional Capability**  
  *Why needed:* Redefines “capability” as conditional on interaction protocol (Cap(m)=E_π[P(ˆy=y|x,π)]). This reframes the notion that a single‑score evaluation is incomplete.  
  *Quick check:* If a model’s accuracy changes when you add “be concise” to the system prompt, what does that say about its “capability” as a single number?

- **Concept: Stability as a First‑Class Metric**  
  *Why needed:* Core innovation is treating variance across benign prompts (σ) not as noise but as a meaningful risk signal for deployment. Requires shifting from a “score‑only” to a “score‑and‑robustness” mindset.  
  *Quick check:* Why might a model with a lower average score be preferable to a higher‑scoring but high‑σ model in a multi‑step agent pipeline?

- **Concept: Credit‑Rating Analogy for Model Selection**  
  *Why needed:* The AAA–BBB grade maps a technical metric (σ) to a business/operational decision frame (risk tier), bridging evaluation output to deployment policy.  
  *Quick check:* What operational costs does a “BBB” (high‑σ) model likely incur compared to an “AAA” model, even if their average scores are similar?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Template Engine -> Fixed‑Subset Sampler -> Evaluation Runner -> Aggregator -> Grade Assigner -> Diagnostics

**Critical path:**  
Template design -> fixed subset creation -> full cube evaluation (model × template × benchmark) -> (μ, σ) computation -> quantile grading -> report generation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Fixed subsets vs. generalizability: Fixed D_b eliminates sampling noise but limits findings to that item set; a larger D_b reduces variance but increases cost.  
- Transparent scoring vs. task coverage: Multiple‑choice ensures auditability; open‑ended tasks require potentially subjective rubrics, introducing evaluator noise.  
- Template set size (T): More templates better approximate P(π) but linearly increase evaluation cost (|M| × T × K × N_b).

**Failure signatures:**  
- Scenario drift: Figure 4 shows strong slope in ¯S_t → templates not neutral; σ confounds sensitivity with global difficulty shift.  
- Parsing failures: Inconsistent option extraction (e.g., “A” vs. “Answer: A”) inflates score variance; parser g(·) must be robust.  
- Grade compression: All σ_m very similar → quantile grades meaningless; template set may be too homogeneous or models too alike.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Run scenario neutrality check: compute ¯S_t for all templates; if std(¯S_t) > small threshold (e.g., 0.5), revise templates.  
2. Inspect per‑model distribution: for each model, plot {S_m,t}; identify heavy tails vs. tight clusters – tail‑heavy models are scenario‑fragile.  
3. Generate benchmark‑level score‑fluctuation maps: compute (μ_m,b, σ_m,b) per benchmark; check if high σ is driven by one benchmark (e.g., GPQA) and consider workload alignment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to open‑ended generation?**  
   *Basis:* Limitations state future work should extend to open‑ended tasks with verifiable rubrics.  
   *Why unresolved:* Current method relies on transparent multiple‑choice accuracy; open‑ended tasks require subjective rubrics that may confound fluctuations.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* Automated rubrics validated by human‑AI agreement; demonstration that σ from such rubrics still isolates protocol sensitivity.

2. **Principled construction of scenario families?**  
   *Basis:* Paper notes that the fixed set of 10 templates is a practical approximation; broader and domain‑specific families could improve coverage.  
   *Why unresolved:* Ad‑hoc set may miss important variation dimensions; without systematic construction, σ may not be generalizable.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* Taxonomy of protocol variation from production logs; ablation studies on how σ stabilizes with larger scenario sets; cross‑domain transfer tests.

3. **Integration with other deployment factors?**  
   *Basis:* Limitations mention robustness is only one axis; cost, latency, safety, refusal patterns should be integrated.  
   *Why unresolved:* Paper suggests prioritizing low σ first but offers no guidance on trade‑offs; operational constraints may justify higher σ models.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* Quantification of robustness failure costs; utility function combining σ, μ, cost, latency, safety; case studies selecting under different risk budgets.

4. **Does σ predict real‑world stability and cost?**  
   *Basis:* Inferred premise that σ captures operational risk; not empirically validated.  
   *Why unresolved:* Correlation between controlled σ and production outcomes assumed; could be confounded by model size, training data, etc.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* Longitudinal studies tracking production performance as protocols evolve; correlation between pre‑deployment σ and incident rates, testing frequency, or engineer effort; A/B tests using CreditAudit grades to select models and measuring real‑world outcomes.

## Limitations
- Exact wording of the 10 system‑prompt templates is unreported, making direct replication impossible and risking scorer drift.
- Deterministic parsing function g(·) is unspecified; minor implementation variations can introduce noise in μ and σ.
- Evaluation restricted to multiple‑choice benchmarks and fixed subsets; generalizability to open‑ended generation or diverse item pools is unproven.

## Confidence
- σ isolates model protocol‑sensitivity: **Medium** (relies on untested scenario neutrality; aggregate evidence in Figure 4).  
- σ predicts deployment volatility: **Low** (theoretical gradient argument lacks empirical production validation).  
- Credit grades enable risk‑aware selection: **Medium** (quantile mapping is transparent but thresholds lack grounding in actual failure‑cost curves).

## Next Checks
1. **Template neutrality stress test:** Compute mean scores per template (¯S_t) across all models; apply a trend test (e.g., Bar’s test). Significant slope indicates templates are not neutral; σ then conflates sensitivity with global drift.
2. **Parser robustness audit:** For a sample of models, run the parser on responses with minor formatting variations (e.g., “A” vs. “Answer: A”). Measure parsing agreement; high disagreement invalidates σ comparability.
3. **Workload alignment correlation:** In a downstream multi‑step agent task (e.g., web navigation), measure failure rates per model. Correlate these with σ from the audit. Low or zero correlation would falsify the deployment‑risk premise.
