# Positional Cognitive Specialization: Where Do LLMs Learn To Comprehend and Speak Your Language?

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2604.00923
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00923
*   **Reference Count:** 6
*   **Primary Result:** Decoder-only LLMs exhibit positional specialization where early layers handle perception (input comprehension) and late layers handle production (output generation). Fine-tuning only the outermost 25% of layers (CogSym) achieves performance within 2-3% of full fine-tuning for low-resource language adaptation.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a functional division of labor within the transformer architecture of decoder-only LLMs during language adaptation. Through systematic layer ablation studies on low-resource Javanese and Sundanese, the authors demonstrate that early layers near the input specialize in perceptual processing (comprehending the new language), while late layers near the output specialize in productive processing (generating in the new language). The middle layers form a relatively stable, language-agnostic reasoning core. Based on this, they propose CogSym, a simple heuristic that fine-tunes only the outermost 25% of layers (both ends symmetrically). This method matches the performance of full fine-tuning across translation, sentiment analysis, and MMLU benchmarks while offering significant computational savings. It is compatible with parameter-efficient methods like LoRA and outperforms other layer-selection strategies.

## Method Summary
The core experimental method is a layer ablation sweep on a Llama 2 7B model. The model is fine-tuned on a translated Alpaca instruction dataset (~52k examples) for Javanese and Sundanese. For a given parameter budget *k*, three training configurations are evaluated: **front** (unfreezing the first *k* layers and the input embedding), **rear** (unfreezing the last *k* layers and the LM head), and **CogSym** (unfreezing the first *k*/2 and last *k*/2 layers plus both heads). Performance is then evaluated on held-out tasks: word translation, machine translation (NusaX), sentiment analysis (NusaX), and multiple-choice reasoning (IndoMMLU). The optimal CogSym heuristic (training the outermost 25% of layers) is derived from these sweeps and validated against competing heuristics and with the LoRA parameter-efficient fine-tuning method.

## Key Results
*   Layer ablation reveals a clear functional split: rear layers are critical for translation *into* the new language (generation), while front layers are critical for translation *from* the new language (comprehension).
*   The CogSym heuristic (symmetric outermost 25% layer tuning) achieves downstream task performance within 2-3% of full model fine-tuning.
*   CogSym is effective with both full fine-tuning and LoRA (rank 128), outperforms other layer-selection strategies (e.g., training only rear layers), and works without additional data or computation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Positional Perceptual-Productive Specialization
During low-resource language adaptation, decoder-only LLMs develop a functional division where early layers (near input) specialize in perceptual processing (language comprehension) and late layers (near output) specialize in productive processing (language generation). This split emerges from the transformer's sequential processing: early layers encode the input token sequence into representations, while late layers decode these into output tokens. The middle layers operate as a language-agnostic or high-resource-dominated "thinking" space. This relies on a base model pre-trained multilingually.
*   **Evidence:** Translation performance is overwhelmingly dependent on rear layers for `known -> unknown` direction (generation) and on initial layers for `unknown -> known` direction (comprehension).

### Mechanism 2: Outer-Layer Sufficiency for Interface Retrofitting
Efficient adaptation requires updating only the outermost perceptual and productive layers (the "linguistic interfaces"). The middle layers' shared representations are sufficient for core reasoning. Adapting the front-end teaches the model to parse the new language's tokens into the established shared space; adapting the back-end teaches it to generate the new language's tokens from that space. This retrofits the I/O systems without destabilizing the stable, general-purpose middle-layer computations.
*   **Evidence:** CogSym (outer 25%) matches full fine-tuning. Performance degrades monotonically as the trained region moves inward from the extremities.

### Mechanism 3: Architectural Invariance to Parameter Update Method
The observed specialization and CogSym's efficacy are properties of the model's architecture and pretrained state, not the specific fine-tuning algorithm. Whether using full gradient descent or low-rank adapters (LoRA), modifying the same positional regions yields similar outcomes because layer importance is structurally determined.
*   **Evidence:** CogSym performance patterns with LoRA (r=128) are "remarkably consistent" with full fine-tuning results.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Multilingual Processing Stages**
    *   **Why needed:** Frames the novel claim about the *location* (early/mid/late) of comprehension/shared/generation stages during adaptation.
    *   **Quick check:** In a trained multilingual LLM, which layer region typically operates in a language-agnostic or dominant-language mode? (Answer: Middle layers).
*   **Concept: Layer Ablation Sweeps**
    *   **Why needed:** Core methodology for isolating the functional contribution of different architectural positions.
    *   **Quick check:** In a front-layer ablation sweep with budget *k*, which layers are trained? (Answer: The first *k* consecutive layers and the input embedding).
*   **Concept: Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT), specifically LoRA**
    *   **Why needed:** Demonstrates CogSym's practicality and robustness across different update mechanisms.
    *   **Quick check:** What does LoRA approximate by decomposing weight updates into low-rank matrices? (Answer: The low-rank update matrix ΔW ≈ BA, where B is `out x r` and A is `r x in`).

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Input Token IDs -> Input Embedding Layer -> Front Interface (Early Transformer Blocks) -> Shared Core (Middle Transformer Blocks) -> Rear Interface (Late Transformer Blocks) -> LM Head -> Output Token Logits
*   **Critical path:** Input -> [Front Interface: Emb + Early Layers] -> [Shared Core: Mid Layers] -> [Rear Interface: Late Layers + LM Head] -> Output Logits. Efficient adaptation targets the two interfaces.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Compute vs. Generality:** CogSym saves ~75% parameters vs. full FT but may underperform on tasks requiring middle-layer restructuring (e.g., formal logic in new language).
    *   **Symmetry vs. Budget:** Heuristic uses symmetric allocation (e.g., 12.5% front, 12.5% rear). Asymmetric splits (e.g., 20% rear) are unexplored.
    *   **Sequential vs. Joint Training:** Sequential training (front-then-rear) halves peak memory but causes asymmetric catastrophic forgetting (Fig 9).
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   Poor comprehension task performance (e.g., sentiment) when *only* rear layers are tuned.
    *   Poor generation task performance (e.g., translation *to* target) when *only* front layers are tuned.
    *   Performance degrades as trained region moves inward from extremities.
    *   Methods like LSN/AlphaLoRA fail because they skew toward rear layers, missing the perceptual interface need.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  Replicate ablation sweeps on Llama 3 8B with Basque, validating the U-shaped CogSym curve and front/rear asymmetry on translation.
    2.  With a fixed layer budget (e.g., 8), compare outermost (0-3, L-4..L-1) vs. next-inner (4-7, L-8..L-5) selections, confirming a monotonic performance drop inward.
    3.  Apply CogSym (25% outer) to a reasoning-heavy task (e.g., math QA) in the target language, quantifying potential degradation vs. full FT.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question 1:** Do the middle layers primarily support non-linguistic cognitive functions like reasoning, and how does tuning them compare to tuning the extremities for such tasks? (Basis: Future Directions; unresolved due to lack of reasoning-task experiments).
*   **Question 2:** Does the perceptual-productive specialization generalize to encoder-decoder architectures (e.g., T5, mBART) and languages beyond Austronesian? (Basis: Limitations; experiments only on decoder-only LLM with two Austronesian languages).
*   **Question 3:** What is the precise interplay between perceptual and productive regions during adaptation, and why does sequential tuning cause asymmetric degradation? (Basis: Inferred from sequential training results; mechanism unclear without internal dynamics analysis).
*   **Question 4:** Is the optimal layer-tuning budget (e.g., 25%) fixed, or does it vary with model size, pretraining data, or linguistic distance? (Basis: Inferred; heuristic derived from Llama 2 7B with two languages, no systematic variation test).
*   **Question 5:** Does CogSym's efficiency advantage extend to high-resource language adaptation? (Basis: Inferred; study exclusively uses low-resource languages).

## Limitations
*   Evidence is limited to two low-resource languages (Javanese, Sundanese) from a single family; generalizability is unknown.
*   Critical hyperparameters (learning rate, exact model checkpoint type) are unreported, hindering precise replication.
*   The 25% symmetric heuristic may not transfer to non-transformer architectures or models without extensive multilingual pretraining.

## Confidence
*   **Positional Specialization:** Medium (Strong internal ablation evidence but lacks direct external corroboration; assumes multilingual pretraining foundation).
*   **Outer-Layer Sufficiency:** Medium (Validated on their tasks and with LoRA; untested on tasks requiring fundamental reasoning restructuring).
*   **Architectural Invariance:** Medium (Confirmed for LoRA r=128; other PEFT methods could alter rankings).
