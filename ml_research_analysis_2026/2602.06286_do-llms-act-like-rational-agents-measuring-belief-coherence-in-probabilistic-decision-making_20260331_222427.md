# Do LLMs Act Like Rational Agents? Measuring Belief Coherence in Probabilistic Decision Making

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.06286  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06286  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: No model fully meets rational-agent criteria; all reject conditional independence, some show monotonicity violations, and cross-prompt stability varies across models.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies whether large language models (LLMs) can be treated as rational agents whose verbalized probabilities serve as coherent subjective beliefs guiding decisions under uncertainty. The authors develop a decision-theoretic testing framework grounded in Bayesian utility maximization. Three necessary conditions for rationality are derived: (1) actions must be conditionally independent of true outcomes given elicited beliefs; (2) pairwise choice probabilities should be monotonic in those beliefs; (3) beliefs should be consistent across different decision framings.  

Applying these tests to four medical diagnosis datasets, the authors evaluate GPT-5, DeepSeek-R1, and Llama-4 models. All models violate conditional independence, some exhibit monotonicity failures (particularly with defer actions), and cross-prompt stability differs markedly across models. Internal consistency checks also reveal errors beyond a random forest baseline. Although some approaches (e.g., GPT-High) approach rationality, no model passes all criteria, suggesting that LLMs' elicited probabilities are at best partially mechanistically meaningful for decision support.

## Method Summary
The evaluation collects tuples \((x, p_E(\theta|x), a, \theta)\) from LLMs across four medical diagnosis tasks with binary outcomes. For each dataset, 200 patient cases spanning the probability spectrum are sampled. Each case is queried five times under a standard diagnostic prompt to elicit a probability and an action (Yes/No/Defer). Additional prompts (MSE-loss, absolute-loss, Bayesian-reasoning) are used to test consistency. Ground-truth posteriors are computed using Bayesian networks. The core tests are: (i) Conditional Independence: assessed via conditional mutual information (kNN-CMI) and out-of-sample predictive improvement of adding \(\theta\) to a CatBoost model of \(A \sim p\); (ii) Monotonicity: binning elicited probabilities and checking that the relative choice share for any action pair is monotone across bins (one-sided binomial test, \(\alpha=0.05\)); (iii) Cross-prompt Consistency: comparing mean elicited probabilities across prompts with RMSE and within-prompt standard deviation. An additional Law of Iterated Expectation (LIE) test evaluates internal coherence. All tests are black-box, comparing models without any training.

## Key Results
- **Conditional Independence**: All model–task pairs show significant CMI > 0 and positive predictive improvement when including \(\theta\), rejecting the hypothesis that actions depend only on elicited beliefs.
- **Monotonicity**: Violations occur for DeepSeek and Llama in several tasks (up to 30% in Heart–DeepSeek for Defer/No), while GPT-High shows no violations; violations are concentrated in decisions involving the "Defer" action.
- **Consistency**: Cross-prompt RMSE is low for GPT-High, moderate for DeepSeek, and high for Llama (0.24–0.32 vs. within-prompt std 0.08–0.16). LIE errors exceed those of a random forest baseline on true probabilities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The framework tests three necessary conditions for a rational Bayesian agent:

**1. Conditional Independence (CI)**  
*Claim*: If elicited probabilities \(p_E\) are the true subjective beliefs, then the chosen action \(a\) must be conditionally independent of the true state \(\theta\) given \(p_E\).  
*Mechanism*: Under expected utility maximization, the optimal action depends solely on the belief \(P_S(\theta|x)\). Once \(p_E\) is known, \(\theta\) provides no additional information about \(a\). The authors model the decision process as a random utility maximizer (RUM) with independent noise, ensuring that conditional on \(p_E\), \(a\) and \(\theta\) are independent.  
*Core assumption*: The RUM's noise is independent of \((x, \theta, p_E)\).  
*Evidence*: Proposition 3.3 and Section 3.2 formalize the test; empirical results (Tables 1, 5) show CMI significantly > 0 for all models, indicating residual dependence. The break condition is a statistically significant CMI or CatBoost predictive improvement when adding \(\theta\) to a model of \(A\) based on \(p_E\).

**2. Monotonicity of Pairwise Choice Probabilities**  
*Claim*: Under expected utility maximization with Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA), the relative probability of choosing one action over another is a monotonic function of the belief \(p_S\).  
*Mechanism*: For actions \(a_i, a_j\), the expected utility difference \(\Delta(p_S)\) is (weakly) monotone in \(p_S\) under RUM (or prospect theory). The choice share \(s(p_S) = \Pr(a_i \mid a \in \{a_i,a_j\}, p_S)\) equals \(F(\Delta(p_S))\) where \(F\) is a CDF of noise, which is monotone. Hence \(s(p_S)\) must be monotone in \(p_S\).  
*Core assumption*: IIA holds, i.e., the relative odds of \(a_i\) vs. \(a_j\) are unaffected by other available actions.  
*Evidence*: Proposition 3.6 and Section 3.2 derive the condition; Figure 1 and Table 2 report violation rates. A violation is a statistically significant reversal in \(s(p)\) between adjacent probability bins (one-sided test, \(\alpha=0.05\)). GPT-High shows no violations; DeepSeek and Llama exhibit violations up to 30%, often involving the "Defer" action.

**3. Cross-Prompt Consistency**  
*Claim*: True subjective beliefs should be invariant to changes in the decision problem's framing; thus elicited probabilities for the same state should be similar across different action sets or loss functions.  
*Mechanism*: The belief \(P_S(\theta|x)\) is determined by the evidence \(x\), not by the subsequent decision context. The test compares probabilities from the standard diagnostic prompt against alternative prompts (MSE-loss, absolute-loss, Bayesian reasoning).  
*Core assumption*: The LLM's belief state is robust to prompt variations that do not alter the evidence.  
*Evidence*: Section 3.2 defines within-prompt standard deviation and cross-prompt RMSE; Section 5.3 shows GPT-High has low RMSE, Llama high. The break condition is cross-prompt RMSE substantially larger than the within-prompt standard deviation, indicating sensitivity to framing.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bayesian Decision Theory (Expected Utility Maximization)**  
  *Why needed*: Forms the theoretical foundation; understanding how beliefs and utilities combine to choose actions is essential to see why CI and monotonicity should hold.  
  *Quick check*: Given a posterior belief \(P_S(\theta|x)\) and known utilities \(u(a,\theta)\), how does an agent choose the optimal action? (Answer: Choose \(a\) that maximizes \(\sum_\theta P_S(\theta|x) u(a,\theta)\).)

- **Random Utility Model (RUM) and Stochastic Choice**  
  *Why needed*: Real agents (and LLMs) seldom choose deterministically; RUM adds independent noise to utilities, yielding softmax-like probabilities. This justifies testing monotonicity on choice *shares* rather than on single decisions.  
  *Quick check*: Why does adding independent extreme-value noise to utilities produce the logit model? (Answer: The Gumbel distribution’s properties make \(\Pr(a) \propto \exp(V(a))\).)

- **Calibration vs. Decision-Sufficiency**  
  *Why needed*: The paper distinguishes between marginal calibration (probabilities matching frequencies) and decision-sufficiency (whether beliefs alone explain actions). A model can be well-calibrated yet fail CI because actions depend on additional factors.  
  *Quick check*: Can a model that outputs perfectly calibrated disease prevalence probabilities still fail the conditional independence test? (Answer: Yes, if its actions depend on \(\theta\) beyond the effect through the elicited probability.)

## Architecture Onboarding
The evaluation pipeline consists of the following components:

**Component Map**  
Elicitation Module → Data Generator → Statistical Testing Module  
(Validation Harness supports ground-truth computation throughout)

**Critical Path**  
1. Elicitation: Generate probability and action responses for each patient case.  
2. Ground-truth annotation: Compute true posteriors \(\theta|x\) via Bayesian network inference.  
3. Test execution: Apply CI (CMI, CatBoost), monotonicity, consistency (RMSE), and LIE tests.  
4. Bootstrap/statistical validation: Estimate confidence intervals and test significance.  
5. Aggregate: Summarize results across models and tasks.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Black-box vs. mechanistic*: The framework treats LLMs as opaque input-output systems, sacrificing insight into internal representations for general applicability.  
- *Test stringency*: Conditional independence is a minimal falsification; allowing prospect theory or non-IIA utilities relaxes conditions but complicates interpretation.  
- *Prompt engineering*: Standardizing prompts enables comparison, yet variations reveal sensitivity; tradeoff between control and ecological validity.

**Failure Signatures**  
- CMI significantly > 0 (fail CI)  
- Non-monotone choice shares in any probability bin comparison  
- Cross-prompt RMSE > within-prompt standard deviation  
- LIE error exceeding random forest baseline on true probabilities

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. **CI replication**: On the heart disease dataset with GPT-High, compute CMI and CatBoost predictive improvement for \(A \sim p\) vs. \(A \sim (p, \theta)\). Expect a small but positive CMI (e.g., 0.075) and around 4% improvement (Table 1).  
2. **Monotonicity diagnostic**: For the same model/task, bin elicited probabilities into deciles, plot choice shares for (Yes, No) and (Yes, Defer), and compute violation % using one-sided proportion tests. Expect near-zero violations for Yes/No but possible violations for pairs involving Defer.  
3. **Prompt consistency check**: Elicit probabilities for the same patient cases under the standard prompt and an MSE-loss prompt for Llama. Compute RMSE between the two sets. Expect RMSE > within-prompt std, illustrating Llama’s prompt sensitivity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Characterizing deviations**: *How can we systematically characterize the specific types and causes of deviations from Bayesian rationality in LLM decision-making to inform model development?* The paper identifies violations but does not analyze their underlying sources. Needed: detailed cross-model/task error analysis correlating violation patterns with model architecture, scale, or training data; controlled ablation studies.  
2. **Optimizing elicitation**: *What elicitation protocols or prompt designs maximize the decision-theoretic coherence of LLM-reported probabilities?* The paper compares a few prompts but does not test their effect on belief-action alignment. Needed: systematic evaluation of diverse elicitation techniques using the paper’s tests to identify prompts that minimize violations.  
3. **Deployment guidelines**: *Given the observed irrationalities, what concrete guidelines should govern the deployment of LLMs as decision-support agents in high-stakes domains like healthcare?* The paper shows LLMs often fail rational-agent criteria but does not translate findings into actionable policies. Needed: simulation or observational studies where decisions based on LLM outputs are evaluated against clinical outcomes; development of policies like automatic deferral when violations exceed thresholds; pilot testing.  
4. **Internal representations**: *Do LLMs maintain internal probabilistic representations that are more coherent than their verbalized outputs, and can these be leveraged to improve decision-making?* The black-box focus leaves open whether internal states better satisfy decision-theoretic constraints. Needed: probing or representation analysis to extract latent belief signals and test if they better predict actions or satisfy rationality conditions.

## Limitations
- Ground-truth Bayesian networks for two medical tasks (pediatric fever, infant crying) are not publicly available, preventing exact replication of true posteriors.  
- The natural language conversion from covariate vectors to patient descriptions is underspecified, potentially introducing variance in how cases are presented to the model.  
- Access to the exact model variants used (GPT-5 Thinking High, DeepSeek-R1 671B, Llama-4 Scout) is limited, hindering direct reproduction and comparison.

## Confidence
- **Conditional independence failure**: High  
- **Monotonicity violations**: Medium  
- **Cross-prompt instability**: Medium  
- **LIE inconsistency**: Medium
