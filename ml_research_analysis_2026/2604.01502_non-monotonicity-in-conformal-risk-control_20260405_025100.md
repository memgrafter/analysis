# Non-monotonicity in Conformal Risk Control

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01502  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01502  
- **Reference count:** 7  
- **Primary result:** For bounded non-monotone losses over a finite grid of size *m* with *n* calibration samples, conformal risk control (CRC) with a finite-sample correction yields an excess risk of *O*(√(log *m* / *n*), which is minimax optimal; under Lipschitz smoothness and a margin condition, threshold disagreement decays exponentially.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses conformal risk control (CRC) when the loss function is non-monotonic in the prediction parameter—a common situation when tuning thresholds for prediction sets. The core challenge is that selecting a parameter from a finite grid of size *m* using *n* calibration samples introduces a uniform concentration problem. The authors prove that for bounded losses, the excess risk above the target level α is at most *O*(√(log *m* / *n*)) and show this rate is minimax optimal. Under additional smoothness (Lipschitz) and a margin condition, the probability that the selected threshold differs from the oracle decays exponentially. The analysis extends to distribution shift via importance weighting, with an extra factor of the likelihood-ratio bound. Empirical results on multilabel classification and object detection demonstrate that the proposed method (CRC-NM), which directly applies a finite-sample correction, achieves more stable risk control than monotonization techniques while producing less conservative prediction sets.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes a correction to standard conformal risk control for non-monotone losses. Given a bounded loss ℓ(·; λ) and a finite grid Λ of size *m*, compute empirical risks ˆ*R*_n(λ) for all λ ∈ Λ on calibration data. Instead of choosing the smallest λ with ˆ*R*_n(λ) ≤ α, adjust the target level to α' = α − *D*(*m*,*n*), where *D*(*m*,*n*) = *B*√(log(2*m*)/(2*n*)) + (*B*/2)√(2*n*log(2*m*)) (with *B* a known bound on ℓ). Then select λ̂ = inf{λ ∈ Λ: ˆ*R*_n(λ) ≤ α'}. This correction accounts for worst-case uniform deviations across the grid. For distribution shift, replace ˆ*R*_n with an importance-weighted version and scale *D* by the likelihood-ratio bound *W*. The method is compared against monotonization techniques (redefining loss or risk to be monotone) and a stability-based bootstrap correction (CRC-C).

## Key Results
- For any bounded non-monotone loss over a grid of size *m*, CRC-NM guarantees excess risk ≤ *O*(√(log *m* / *n*), and this rate is minimax optimal (Theorem 1, Proposition 1).
- If the loss is *K*-Lipschitz in λ and there exists λ⋆ with true risk *R*(λ⋆) ≤ α − ε for some margin ε ≥ (*B*−α)/*n*, then the probability that the selected λ̂ differs from the oracle selection decays as exp(−2*n*ε²/*B*²) (Proposition 2).
- Under distribution shift with likelihood ratio bounded by *W*, the guarantee extends by replacing *B* with *W B* in the excess term (Proposition 4).
- Experiments show CRC-NM achieves target risk control with stable set sizes, while monotonization methods either over-control (excessive conservatism) or fail to control risk in non-monotone settings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** For bounded non-monotonic losses over a finite grid Λ of size *m*, the CRC selection rule with an appropriately adjusted level yields an excess risk of order √(log *m* / *n*).
- **Mechanism:** Treat the parameter selection as a uniform concentration problem over the finite class {*L*(·,λ):λ∈Λ}. Using Hoeffding’s inequality and a union bound, the supremum of the empirical risk deviations is bounded in expectation by *D*(*m*,*n*) = *B*√(log(2*m*)/(2*n*)) + (*B*/2)√(2*n*log(2*m*)). This bound accounts for the worst-case deviation across all *m* candidates, and the selected λ̂ inherits this deviation.
- **Core assumption:** Losses are bounded (0 ≤ *L* ≤ *B*), i.i.d. across samples for each λ, and there exists at least one λ⋆ ∈ Λ with *R*(λ⋆) ≤ α (feasibility). The grid is finite and discrete.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "For bounded losses… excess risk above target α is at most O(√(log *m* / *n*))… and shows this rate is minimax optimal."  
  - [section 3.2] Theorem 1 gives the explicit *D*(*m*,*n*) bound and the adjusted selection rule.  
  - [section 3.1] Counterexample shows risk control depends on the relationship between *n* and log *m*.  
- **Break condition:** If the grid size *m* grows exponentially with *n* (e.g., *m* > exp(*c n*)), the √(log *m* / *n*) term no longer vanishes, and the guarantee becomes vacuous. Also fails if the loss is unbounded, violating the Hoeffding mgf condition.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Under additional Lipschitz smoothness and a margin condition, the probability that the selected threshold disagrees with the oracle decays exponentially in *n*, leading to a tighter excess risk bound.
- **Mechanism:** Decompose the excess risk into the oracle risk plus a term proportional to *K*·diam(Λ)·P(λ̂ ≠ λ̂′), where λ̂′ is the infeasible oracle selection. The Lipschitz property bounds the loss difference by *K* times the threshold difference. The disagreement probability is controlled via Hoeffding applied to the margin ϵ = α − *R*(λ⋆), yielding an exponential bound exp(−2*n*ϵ²/*B*²) when ϵ ≥ (*B*−α)/*n*.
- **Core assumption:** Losses are *K*-Lipschitz in λ, and there exists λ⋆ with *R*(λ⋆) ≤ α−ϵ for some ϵ > 0 large enough (margin condition).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "Under additional Lipschitz smoothness and a margin condition, the excess probability of threshold disagreement decays exponentially."  
  - [section 4.1] Proposition 2 states the exponential bound on P(λ̂ ≠ λ̂′) and the resulting risk bound.  
- **Break condition:** If the loss is not Lipschitz (e.g., discontinuities), the threshold disagreement probability may not decay exponentially. Also breaks if the margin ϵ is too small, making the exponential bound trivial or if ϵ < (*B*−α)/*n*, in which case the bound reverts to the √(log *m* / *n*) rate.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** In settings with distribution shift (P_test ≠ P_train) and bounded likelihood ratio *W*, the CRC guarantee extends by replacing empirical risks with importance-weighted versions, with an excess term scaling linearly in *W*.
- **Mechanism:** Use importance weighting to express the test risk as an expectation under P_train: *R*_test(λ) = E_P_train[*w*(*X,Y*)*L*(*X,Y*;λ)], where *w* = dP_test/dP_train ≤ *W*. Apply Theorem 1 to the weighted losses *L*^w, which lie in [0, *WB*]. The uniform concentration bound now has range *WB* instead of *B*, directly propagating the *W* factor.
- **Core assumption:** P_test ≪ P_train with likelihood ratio bounded by *W* < ∞, and weighted losses remain bounded (0 ≤ *wL* ≤ *WB*).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "Extend the analysis to settings with distribution shift via importance weighting, with an extra factor of the likelihood-ratio bound."  
  - [section 7] Proposition 4 gives the bound with *WB* in place of *B*.  
- **Break condition:** If the likelihood ratio *W* is very large (e.g., due to severe distribution shift or non-overlapping supports), the excess term *WB*√(log *m* / *n*) becomes large, requiring impractically large *n*. Also fails if *w* is unbounded or if P_test is not absolutely continuous with respect to P_train.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Uniform convergence over finite classes via Hoeffding.**  
  - *Why needed here:* The selection rule searches over *m* candidate parameters; to control the excess risk, we need a uniform bound on empirical risk deviations across all λ ∈ Λ simultaneously.  
  - *Quick check question:* What is the expected supremum deviation for *m* bounded i.i.d. losses, and how does it scale with *n* and *m*?  

- **Concept: Minimax lower bounds via Fano’s inequality.**  
  - *Why needed here:* To show the √(log *m* / *n*) rate is unavoidable, not an artifact of the CRC procedure.  
  - *Quick check question:* How does constructing a packing of distributions with small KL divergence lead to a lower bound on excess risk?  

- **Concept: Importance weighting for distribution shift.**  
  - *Why needed here:* To extend the i.i.d. guarantee to cases where calibration and test data come from different distributions.  
  - *Quick check question:* If *w*(*X,Y*) is the likelihood ratio, how does one express E_P_test[*L*] in terms of P_train?  

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Calibration dataset D_{1:n} -> Compute empirical risks ˆR_n(λ) for all λ in grid Λ -> Apply uniform bound D(m,n) to compute adjusted α' -> Select λ̂ = inf{λ: ˆR_n(λ) ≤ α'} -> Output prediction set C(X_{n+1}; λ̂)

**Critical path:**  
1. Evaluate loss for all grid points on calibration data.  
2. Compute the correction term D(m,n).  
3. Set adjusted risk level α' = α - D(m,n).  
4. Pick the smallest grid parameter whose empirical risk does not exceed α'.  
5. Use that parameter for the test prediction set.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Finer grid (*m*↑) reduces discretization error but increases D(m,n) logarithmically.  
- Using Bernstein refinements (variance-sensitive) reduces D when losses are low-variance, but requires estimating σ² (oracle vs empirical).  
- Monotonization (Loss/Risk Mono) avoids the D term but introduces structural conservatism; CRC-NM pays a small statistical cost instead.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Empirical risk curve highly non-monotonic with bumps causing the feasible region {λ: ˆR_n(λ) ≤ α} to be empty or disconnected -> then λ̂ defaults to λ_m (most conservative), violating risk control if D(m,n) under-estimates.  
- For distribution shift, if *W* is large and *n* moderate, the weighted bound may be too loose.  
- Margin condition ϵ too small in Proposition 2 -> disagreement probability not exponentially small.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic multilabel (Section 6.1):** Precision-based loss with sinusoidal modulation inducing strong non-monotonicity; compare CRC, CRC-C, Loss Mono, Risk Mono, CRC-NM. CRC-NM achieves target risk while others either over-control or fail.  
2. **COCO object detection (Section 6.2):** Real non-monotone loss combining recall and oversize penalty; CRC-NM gives stable risk control with moderate set sizes, Loss Mono is overly conservative.  
3. **Distribution shift extension (Section 7):** Importance-weighted CRC on shifted data; excess term scales with *W*, demonstrated via synthetic shift scenarios (not shown in experiments but theoretically validated).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the finite-sample excess risk bound of order √(log *m* / *n*) for non-monotone losses be extended to continuous (or arbitrarily fine) parameter grids, and what is the minimax optimal rate in that setting?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 8 ("extending the analysis to continuous parameter spaces") and Proposition 1 proves the lower bound only for a discrete grid of size *m*, leaving the continuous case open.
- **Why unresolved:** The proof of Theorem 1 heavily relies on a union bound over a finite set Λ. For a continuous parameter space, uniform concentration over an infinite class would require covering arguments or entropy-based bounds, and the existing minimax lower bound (Proposition 1) does not rule out a different, possibly slower, rate.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A matching lower bound for continuous Λ (e.g., via smoothing arguments or adaptation of the binary testing construction to a continuous parameter) or a new upper bound proving √(log *m* / *n*) is achievable with a carefully discretized procedure and a refined analysis.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Under what structural conditions on a non-monotone loss (e.g., moderate non-monotonicity, Lipschitz stability of the risk curve) can the stability-based correction (CRC-C) provably match or improve upon the minimax-optimal correction D(m, n) used by CRC-NM?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 5.2 compares CRC-C (stability-based) and CRC-NM (minimax correction) empirically, noting that CRC-C can be less conservative when the stability constant β is small, but provides no theoretical comparison between the two approaches under non-monotonicity.
- **Why unresolved:** The stability framework (Equation 4) guarantees risk control if an oracle with risk ≤ α – β exists, but the relationship between β and the excess term D(m, n) is unclear. D(m, n) is worst-case over all bounded losses, while β depends on the specific algorithm and loss geometry. No result identifies when β < D(m, n) or vice versa.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A theorem bounding the stability deviation β in terms of the grid size *m* and sample size *n* (e.g., β = O(√(log m / n)) or smaller under additional smoothness), followed by a comparison showing regimes where the stability correction is asymptotically tighter than the uniform bound.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** For non-monotone losses that are unbounded or heavy-tailed, can one derive finite-sample risk control guarantees that scale sub-Gaussianly (or with weaker moment assumptions) while still accounting for grid selection?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 8 notes that extending to "heavy-tailed or unbounded loss functions" is an open direction. Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 assume bounded losses (Li(λ) ∈ [0, B]).
- **Why unresolved:** The proof of Theorem 1 uses Hoeffding’s inequality, which requires boundedness. For unbounded losses, one would need sub-Gaussian or sub-exponential tails, or truncation arguments. The minimax lower bound of Proposition 1 is for binary {0,1}-valued losses; it does not address whether the √(log *m* / *n*) rate is achievable or optimal under lighter tails.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive an upper bound for unbounded losses under a sub-Gaussian or sub-exponential assumption, and construct a corresponding lower bound in that setting (e.g., using Gaussian or heavy-tailed distributions) to identify the optimal rate.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** In the presence of distribution shift with a large likelihood ratio bound *W*, does the inflated excess term *W*·D(m, n) in Proposition 4 remain tight, or can one obtain adaptive corrections that depend on the effective sample size from importance weighting?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Proposition 4 extends the guarantee to distribution shift with excess term scaling linearly in *W* (the bound on w(x,y)). The paper notes this reflects "increased variability" but does not investigate whether this linear dependence is fundamental.
- **Why unresolved:** Importance-weighted estimation often suffers from high variance when *W* is large. The linear dependence on *W* in Proposition 4 comes from applying Theorem 1 to *WB*-bounded weighted losses. It is plausible that the worst-case dependence could be worse (e.g., *W*²) or that a more refined analysis (e.g., using weights with truncation or median-of-means) could reduce the inflation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A minimax lower bound for risk control under distribution shift showing that at least Ω(*W*·√(log *m* / *n*) or a larger term is unavoidable, or an upper bound that adapts to the effective sample size (e.g., via Truncated Importance Weighting) yielding a smaller dependence on *W*.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** For Lipschitz non-monotone losses, when does the disagreement probability P(ˆλ ≠ ˆλ′) decay faster than exponentially in *n* (e.g., as exp(−*c n*²)) under a margin condition, and how does this interact with the grid resolution *m*?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Proposition 2 shows that if there exists λ⋆ with *R*(λ⋆) ≤ α – ε and ε ≥ (*B*−α)/*n*, then P(ˆλ ≠ ˆλ′) ≤ exp(−2*n*ε²/*B*²). The paper does not explore whether the margin condition can be relaxed or whether the exponent can be improved under additional smoothness.
- **Why unresolved:** The proof uses Hoeffding’s inequality on the event ˆ*R*_n(λ⋆) > α – ε, leading to an exponential rate in *n*. For Lipschitz losses, one might exploit the fact that if the empirical risk is close to the true risk uniformly over a neighborhood, then disagreement might require a larger deviation, potentially yielding a faster rate. The current analysis does not leverage Lipschitz continuity in the probability bound itself, only in the excess risk decomposition.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A refined analysis (e.g., using a chaining argument over the grid combined with the Lipschitz property) that yields a bound like exp(−*c n*² / (log *m*) or similar, and a matching lower bound showing this is the best possible under the Lipschitz assumption.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees require bounded losses and a finite grid; unbounded or continuous grids are not covered.
- Grid size *m* must satisfy *m* = exp(o(*n*)); otherwise the excess risk bound becomes vacuous.
- Exponential disagreement rate under Lipschitz + margin relies on strong smoothness and margin conditions that may not hold widely.
- Distribution shift extension requires a finite, often unknown, likelihood-ratio bound *W*; large *W* leads to loose bounds.
- Computational cost O(*m n*) from evaluating all grid points may be prohibitive for very fine grids.
- Theoretical bounds are worst-case and may be conservative in practice.

## Confidence
- **Excess risk O(√(log *m*/*n*), minimax optimal:** High (rigorous theorem and matching lower bound).  
- **Exponential disagreement under Lipschitz + margin:** Medium (theoretically sound but relies on strong smoothness/margin assumptions; limited empirical evidence).  
- **Importance-weighted extension (factor *W*):** Medium (straightforward theory extension; practical utility constrained by *W*’s magnitude and availability).
