# Evaluating Causal Discovery Algorithms for Path-Specific Fairness and Utility in Healthcare

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15926
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15926
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** Algorithm choice significantly impacts structural recovery and path-specific fairness analysis; PC best on synthetic Alzheimer's (F1 0.50), FCI best on real heart failure (F1 0.38), and graph errors distort fairness decomposition and utility ratios.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of evaluating causal discovery algorithms for fairness analysis in healthcare, where ground truth causal graphs are typically unknown. The authors create expert-defined benchmark graphs for a synthetic Alzheimer's dataset and a real heart failure dataset. They compare constraint-based (PC, FCI) and score-based (GES) algorithms on structural accuracy and how recovered graphs affect path-specific fairness decomposition (direct, indirect, spurious effects). Results show that structural recovery is data-dependent—PC performs best on synthetic data, while FCI excels on real data—and that inaccuracies in the discovered graph can collapse or distort fairness components, altering the perceived fairness-utility trade-off measured by the Causal Fairness Utility Ratio (CFUR). The study concludes that algorithm selection must be graph-aware to ensure reliable fairness insights in healthcare applications.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses two datasets: a synthetic Alzheimer's dataset (1,000 samples) with a literature-derived causal graph, and a real heart failure clinical records (HFCR) dataset (299 samples) with an expert-validated causal graph. Multiple causal discovery algorithms—PC, GES, FCI (for HFCR), and for Alzheimer's also NOTEARS, DAGMA, DAG-GNN—are run on each dataset to learn graphs. Learned graphs are compared to ground truth using structural metrics (F1, SHD, TPR, FDR). Fairness analysis applies the Standard Fairness Model to decompose total outcome variation into direct (Ctf-DE), indirect (Ctf-IE), and spurious (Ctf-SE) effects mediated by the protected attribute (sex/gender). Variable-level contributions to these effects are estimated via bootstrapping (200 samples). The Causal Fairness Utility Ratio (CFUR) quantifies the fairness gain per unit predictive accuracy loss when intervening on each causal path, with accuracy estimated using a predictive model (details assumed from cited work). Discovery metrics are averaged over 30 bootstrap samples.

## Key Results
- On synthetic Alzheimer's data, PC achieves the highest structural recovery F1-score (0.50), while score-based methods lag.
- On real heart failure data, FCI attains the best structural F1 (0.38) and correctly recovers non-zero indirect (2.49%) and spurious (-6.97%) fairness effects; PC and GES collapse these components to zero.
- Path-specific analysis of the heart failure ground truth reveals that ejection fraction contributes 3.37 percentage points to the indirect effect, and CFUR profiles differ drastically across algorithms, showing that graph errors change the assessed fairness-utility trade-offs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The choice of causal discovery algorithm significantly impacts the accuracy of the recovered graph structure, which in turn affects downstream fairness and utility analyses.
- **Mechanism:** Different algorithm paradigms (constraint-based, score-based, continuous optimization) make different assumptions about data (e.g., faithfulness, distribution type, presence of latent confounders). These assumptions lead to variations in the edges they recover from the same observational data. The resulting graph determines which variables are identified as mediators or confounders, directly shaping the decomposition of path-specific fairness effects.
- **Core assumption:** The expert-defined ground truth graph is a valid, causally correct representation of the data-generating process for the domain.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] States PC best on synthetic Alzheimer's data (F1 0.50) while FCI best on real heart failure data (F1 0.38), demonstrating algorithm performance is data-dependent.  
  [section 3.1 & 3.2] Presents Tables 1 and 5 showing quantitative differences in structural metrics (F1, SHD, TPR, FDR) across algorithms and datasets.  
  [corpus] Neighbor papers like "A pipeline for enabling path-specific causal fairness..." confirm the field recognizes algorithm selection as a critical step for reliable fairness analysis in health data.
- **Break condition:** If the ground truth graph contains complex cyclic relationships (noted as a limitation) or violations of standard DAG assumptions (faithfulness, Markov equivalence), the structural recovery evaluation and its downstream validity become questionable.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Fairness decomposition results (direct, indirect, spurious effects) are highly graph-dependent; a structurally inaccurate discovered graph can collapse or distort these components, leading to incorrect conclusions about disparity sources.
- **Mechanism:** The definition of mediators (variables on directed paths from protected attribute to outcome) and confounders (ancestors of the outcome) is derived solely from the directed acyclic graph (DAG) structure. An algorithm that misses key edges may fail to identify true mediators/confounders, causing path-specific effects (Ctf-DE, Ctf-IE, Ctf-SE) to be miscalculated or aggregated incorrectly (e.g., collapsing to only direct effect).
- **Core assumption:** The causal fairness decomposition (Ctf-DE, Ctf-IE, Ctf-SE) [14] is the correct and complete model for quantifying disparity in this context.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] Notes "discovered graphs can collapse indirect and spurious components" and that "graph choice drives fairness decomposition."  
  [section 3.2] Table 6 shows PC recovers only direct effect (Ctf-DE=-0.42%) while FCI recovers indirect (2.49%) and spurious (-6.97%) components on heart failure data, illustrating the collapse.  
  [section 4] Discussion explicitly states: "Composite fairness decomposition varied by graph... Discovered graphs often collapsed or distorted indirect and spurious components."
- **Break condition:** If the true causal graph contains unmeasured latent confounders not handled by the discovery algorithm (e.g., using PC instead of FCI), the mediator/confounder roles will be misassigned, invalidating the decomposition. The paper notes FCI was included for this reason.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The Causal Fairness Utility Ratio (CFUR) provides a graph-dependent, fine-grained quantification of the fairness-accuracy trade-off per causal pathway, and its value (positive/negative, magnitude) is sensitive to structural discovery errors.
- **Mechanism:** CFUR is computed for each path (direct, indirect, spurious) using the graph structure to define interventions (blocking paths). It requires correctly identifying the set of variables mediating each path. Variations in the discovered graph alter which paths exist and their estimated effect sizes, thereby changing the ratio of fairness gain to utility loss for each intervention.
- **Core assumption:** The utility metric (predictive performance loss upon path blocking) is correctly operationalized within the given causal graph and that the trade-off is linear and interpretable as defined in [16].
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] States "CFUR quantified the trade-off between fairness gain and accuracy loss per path."  
  [section 2.5] Defines CFUR and its use to evaluate "fine-grained analysis of which pathways offer the best fairness gain per unit accuracy cost."  
  [section 3.2] Table 8 shows CFUR profiles differ drastically: ground truth shows positive IE CFUR (+10.0±27.5) while FCI shows near-zero (+0.9±1.2), indicating graph errors change intervention viability assessments.
- **Break condition:** If the graph discovery is sufficiently inaccurate that it omits a major causal pathway, the CFUR for that path will be undefined or zero, potentially masking a high-impact intervention target. The paper notes "variations in these graph-dependent effects drive differences in the fairness-utility ratio."

## Foundational Learning
- **Structural Recovery Metrics (F1, SHD, TPR, FDR)**  
  Why needed here: These are the primary objective measures to judge if a discovery algorithm has learned a graph close to the expert-defined ground truth. They quantify edge-level correctness (TP/FP/FN).  
  Quick check: If an algorithm has high TPR but also high FDR (like PC on HFCR in Table 5), what does that imply about its precision in edge recovery? (It adds many false positive edges, leading to a dense, incorrect graph.)

- **Causal Fairness Decomposition (Ctf-DE, Ctf-IE, Ctf-SE)**  
  Why needed here: This is the core analytical framework used to move beyond aggregate fairness scores and attribute total outcome disparity to specific causal pathways involving the protected attribute.  
  Quick check: In the heart failure ground truth (Table 6), why is the total variation (TV) nearly zero (-0.42%) while the decomposed effects are larger in magnitude (-5.11%, +0.06%, -4.75%)? (Because effects offset each other; direct and spurious effects are negative while indirect is slightly positive, summing to near zero.)

- **Causal Fairness Utility Ratio (CFUR)**  
  Why needed here: CFUR translates graph-based fairness effects into a clinically actionable trade-off metric: the fairness improvement gained per unit of predictive accuracy lost when intervening on a specific path.  
  Quick check: A positive CFUR for the indirect effect path suggests what about the clinical or policy value of intervening on mediators? (That intervening on the mediators yields net fairness gain relative to accuracy cost, making it a potentially beneficial target.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Input Data → Discovery Engine → Learned Graph → Graph-to-Fairness Mapper → Fairness/Utility Calculators → Evaluation Harness.  
(Note: Ground Truth Graph is used only for evaluation, not in the pipeline.)

**Critical Path:**  
The graph is the pivotal artifact; its quality determines the validity of all downstream fairness and utility conclusions.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Algorithm choice:* Constraint-based (PC, FCI) are robust to latent confounders (FCI) and mixed data (PC), while score-based (GES) may be more efficient but sensitive to distributional assumptions.  
- *Evaluation scope:* Composite fairness scores (TV) vs. path-specific decomposition; the latter reveals distortions masked by aggregates.  
- *Benchmark ground truth:* Synthetic data with known graph vs. expert-defined real-world graph; the latter is more relevant but inherently uncertain.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- *Structural:* Very low F1 (<0.3) and high SHD indicate the learned graph is largely incorrect.  
- *Fairness decomposition:* Collapse of Ctf-IE and Ctf-SE to zero signals mediator/confounder misidentification.  
- *CFUR:* Wildly different or counterintuitive CFUR profiles across algorithms for the same dataset, especially if one shows negative CFUR for paths that are positive in ground truth.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. Replicate Alzheimer’s results: Run PC, GES, NOTEARS, DAGMA, DAG-GNN on the synthetic data. Verify Table 1 metrics, specifically that PC achieves highest F1 (0.50) and that PC/GES show collapsed fairness (Table 2).  
2. Replicate Heart Failure results: Run PC, GES, FCI on the HFCR data. Verify Table 5: FCI has best F1 (0.38) vs. PC/GES (~0.16-0.18). Then compute fairness decomposition and confirm FCI recovers non-zero Ctf-IE (2.49%) and Ctf-SE (-6.97%) while PC does not (Table 6).  
3. Path-specific analysis: Using the ground truth graphs, manually compute variable contributions to Ctf-SE and Ctf-IE. Verify ejection fraction's contribution to Ctf-IE is 3.37% (Table 7). Then, for each discovered graph, compute CFUR for DE, IE, SE paths and compare profiles to ground truth in Table 8, noting FCI's negative DE CFUR (-16.9) vs. ground truth (-3.8).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**1. Evaluating reliability of path-specific fairness analysis from discovered graphs**  
The paper notes that evaluating whether discovered graphs support reliable path-specific fairness analysis in clinical settings remains an open question because current evaluations rely on structural metrics that do not measure how graph errors propagate to fairness decomposition. Evidence from benchmarks with known ground-truth graphs and metrics correlating structural distance with fairness error would help.

**2. Validation of expert-defined ground-truth causal graphs**  
The paper highlights the need to validate ground truth graphs, as expert consensus may be subjective and associations may not be causal. Without validation, benchmarks may reflect biases. Cross-validation among experts, comparison with interventional data, or objective plausibility scoring could resolve this.

**3. Generalizing path-specific fairness to larger, diverse cohorts**  
The paper points out that current studies use small datasets and assume effect homogeneity, so path-specific effects may not generalize. Scalable methods with subgroup effect estimation validated on multi-center diverse datasets are needed.

## Limitations
- Ground truth uncertainty: Expert-defined benchmark graphs may contain errors, affecting all comparisons.
- Data limitations: Small heart failure sample (n=299) yields high variance; latent confounders may be present but not handled by all algorithms.
- Metric robustness: CFUR's dependence on predictive model choice and unstable estimates with small samples.

## Confidence
- **Algorithm choice impacts structural recovery and fairness analysis:** Medium confidence.
- **Fairness decomposition is highly graph-dependent:** Medium confidence.
- **CFUR quantifies trade-off and is graph-sensitive:** Low confidence.

## Next Checks
1. Independent ground truth audit: Recruit additional clinical experts to review and possibly revise the benchmark graphs, or employ a semi-synthetic simulator with a known causal structure for cross-validation.
2. Full replication on raw HFCR data: Preprocess the original heart failure dataset (or follow the authors’ pipeline exactly), rerun all discovery algorithms, and conduct hyperparameter sensitivity analyses.
3. External generalizability test: Apply the same evaluation framework to another healthcare dataset (e.g., MIMIC-III) with a pre-specified causal model to confirm whether the observed algorithm-specific patterns hold beyond the two studied cases.
