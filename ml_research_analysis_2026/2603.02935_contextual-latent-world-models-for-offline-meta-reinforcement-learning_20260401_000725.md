# Contextual Latent World Models for Offline Meta Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.02935  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02935  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: SPC jointly trains a context encoder and a discrete latent world model with self-predictive and contrastive losses, achieving state‑of‑the‑art offline meta‑RL performance and more disentangled task representations across multiple benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Offline meta‑reinforcement learning (OMRL) equips agents to adapt to new tasks using only pre‑collected datasets, but learning effective task representations without supervision remains difficult. This paper introduces **Self‑Predictive Contextual OMRL (SPC)**, which couples a task‑inferring context encoder with a latent world model operating in a discrete space via finite scalar quantization (FSQ). The world model is trained with a multi‑step temporal consistency loss that predicts future latent codes, forcing the task representation to capture predictive dynamics. A contrastive loss further regularizes the context encoder to sharpen task discrimination. Evaluated on MuJoCo, Contextual DeepMind Control, and Meta‑World, SPC significantly outperforms prior work in few‑shot and zero‑shot returns (IQM, optimality gap) and task success rates. It also learns more disentangled representations, as shown by higher DCI and InfoMEC scores, greater feature rank, and lower dormant neuron ratios. Ablations confirm the importance of discrete classification‑based temporal consistency and joint training.

## Method Summary
SPC follows the context‑based offline meta‑RL (COMRL) framework. A context encoder \( g_\theta \) ingests a history of transitions and outputs a task representation \( z \). The latent world model consists of an observation encoder \( F_\phi \), an FSQ quantization module \( f \) that discretizes continuous embeddings into codes \( c \), latent dynamics \( D_\phi \) (predicting next‑code logits given \((c, a, z)\)), and a reward model \( R_\phi \). The core loss is a multi‑step temporal consistency loss \( \mathcal{L}_{TC} \), a cross‑entropy between predicted and target next‑code distributions; gradients flow through the dynamics into the context encoder, encouraging \( z \) to encode task‑specific dynamics. An InfoNCE contrastive loss \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{Contrastive}} \) is added to enforce separation between task representations. The policy and value networks are trained on the latent MDP using implicit Q‑learning (IQL). At test time, the context buffer accumulates recent transitions, \( z \) is recomputed online, and actions are selected based on the current latent state and task representation.

## Key Results
- SPC achieves state‑of‑the‑art performance on MuJoCo, Contextual DeepMind Control, and Meta‑World benchmarks, with large gains in IQM and optimality gap for few‑shot and zero‑shot evaluation and higher success rates.
- It learns significantly more disentangled task representations, reflected in improved DCI and InfoMEC scores, higher feature rank, and lower dormant neuron ratios.
- Ablations confirm that discrete latent space with cross‑entropy temporal consistency and joint training of the context encoder with the world model are essential contributions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Joint Training of Context Encoder and World Model
- **Claim**: Jointly training the context encoder with a latent world model improves task representations by making them predictive of future latent states and rewards, rather than merely discriminative.
- **Mechanism**: The context encoder \( g_\theta \) maps a history \( \mathcal{H} \) to \( z \). The world model (observation encoder \( F_\phi \), quantization \( f \), dynamics \( D_\phi \), reward model \( R_\phi \)) is conditioned on \( z \). Training uses a multi‑step temporal consistency loss \( \mathcal{L}_{TC} \) that predicts future discrete latent codes \( \hat{c}_{t+h} \) from \( (c_t, a_t, z) \). Gradients flow through \( D_\phi \) into \( g_\theta \), encouraging \( z \) to encode information necessary for predicting the latent MDP.
- **Core assumption**: Task variation primarily affects transition dynamics and reward functions, which can be captured in a latent space where temporal consistency provides a sufficient learning signal.
- **Evidence**: [abstract] and [section 3.1] state that conditioning latent world models on inferred task representations enforces task‑conditioned temporal consistency, yielding representations that capture task‑dependent dynamics. Ablations (Table 11, 12) show significant performance drops when the world‑model loss is removed.
- **Break condition**: If tasks differ only in reward functions without dynamic changes, the temporal consistency signal may be weak, limiting the informativeness of \( z \).

### Mechanism 2: Discrete Latent Space with Classification Loss
- **Claim**: Using discrete latent states via finite scalar quantization (FSQ) and formulating temporal consistency as a classification (cross‑entropy) loss prevents representation collapse and improves expressiveness.
- **Mechanism**: FSQ discretizes continuous embeddings \( x_t = F_\phi(s_t) \) into codes \( c_t \) across multiple channels. The dynamics \( D_\phi \) predicts logits over the next code distribution. Cross‑entropy loss between predicted and target codes forces the model to learn a structured, non‑collapsing latent space where each dimension carries predictive meaning.
- **Core assumption**: A discrete latent space with classification loss provides a more stable and expressive optimization landscape than regression (MSE) on continuous latents, especially for multimodal or stochastic transitions.
- **Evidence**: [section 3.1] notes that discrete transition dynamics are modeled as categorical distributions, enabling learning of stochastic and multimodal transitions. Figure 4 shows "Dis+CE" significantly outperforms continuous regression variants (MSE, cosine).
- **Break condition**: Excessive quantization (too few bits/channels) may discard subtle but task‑relevant state information; deterministic continuous dynamics may not benefit from classification.

### Mechanism 3: Contrastive Regularization of Task Representations
- **Claim**: Adding a contrastive (InfoNCE) loss to the context encoder’s objective improves task discrimination and prevents task representations from collapsing into a few modes.
- **Mechanism**: The contrastive loss encourages representations from the same task to be similar (using a moving average \( \bar{z}_i \)) and from different tasks to be dissimilar. This explicitly regularizes \( z \) to occupy distinct regions in representation space, complementing the predictive pressure from \( \mathcal{L}_{TC} \).
- **Core assumption**: Task representations benefit from an explicit separation signal, especially when dynamics alone may not fully discriminate tasks with similar transition structures.
- **Evidence**: [section 3.1] states that contrastive loss provides a lower bound on mutual information between tasks and representations. Figure 3 shows increases in DCI "Informativeness" and InfoMEC "Explicitness" when contrastive is added to world modeling. Table 12 ablations demonstrate robust in‑distribution and OOD performance with the combined losses.
- **Break condition**: Over‑emphasis on separation may harm generalization between semantically similar tasks (e.g., forward vs. backward locomotion).

## Foundational Learning
- **Context‑based Offline Meta‑RL (COMRL)**  
  *Why needed*: SPC is a COMRL method; understanding that a context encoder infers a task representation \( z \) from a history \( \mathcal{H} \) without explicit task IDs is fundamental.  
  *Quick check*: How does a context encoder differ from a standard state encoder in RL?

- **Latent World Models & Self‑Predictive RL**  
  *Why needed*: SPC’s world model operates in a discrete latent space trained via multi‑step temporal consistency, not observation reconstruction. Grasping that self‑predictive losses (predicting future latent states) are the primary representation learning signal is key.  
  *Quick check*: Why is predicting \( \hat{c}_{t+1} \) from \( (c_t, a_t, z) \) a stronger signal than reconstructing \( s_{t+1} \)?

- **Disentanglement Metrics (DCI, InfoMEC)**  
  *Why needed*: The paper uses these to evaluate whether \( z \) captures true task variation factors (e.g., speed, length) rather than spurious correlations.  
  *Quick check*: What does a high "Completeness" score in DCI indicate about the task representation?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  1. **Context Encoder** \( E_\theta \): MLP, input = transition tuple \( (s,a,r,s') \), output = \( z \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z} \).
  2. **Observation Encoder** \( F_\phi \): MLP, maps state \( s \) to continuous \( x \in \mathbb{R}^d \).
  3. **Quantization** \( f \) (FSQ): discretizes \( x \) to code \( c \in \mathbb{R}^d \) (grid‑based).
  4. **Latent Dynamics** \( D_\phi \): MLP, takes \( (c, a, z) \), outputs logits over next code indices.
  5. **Reward Model** \( R_\phi \): MLP, takes \( (c, a, z) \), outputs scalar \( \hat{r} \).
  6. **Policy/Value Networks**: MLPs, conditioned on concatenated \( (c, z) \).

- **Critical path**:
  - *Training*: Sample meta‑batch → for each task: compute \( z_i \) from context → sample trajectory → encode states to \( c_t \) → predict \( \hat{c}_{t+h} \) and \( \hat{r}_{t+h} \) via \( D_\phi, R_\phi \) → compute \( \mathcal{L}_{TC} + \beta \mathcal{L}_{\text{Contrastive}} \) → update \( (\theta, \phi) \) → update policy via IQL.
  - *Testing*: Reset \( z \gets 0 \), interact: at each step, add new transition to context buffer, recompute \( z \), encode \( s \to c \), action \( \pi(a|c,z) \).

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Discrete vs. continuous latent space*: Discrete (FSQ + classification) beats continuous regression (MSE/cosine) in experiments; trade‑off is quantization error vs. prevention of collapse and richer classification loss.
  - *World model size*: Larger models scale better but increase memory/computation.
  - *Contrastive weighting \( \beta \)*: Default \( \beta=1 \) works; too high may override predictive signal, too low may not enforce task separation.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - High dormant neuron ratio (>0.3) in context encoder (representations under‑utilized).
  - Low feature/matrix rank (redundancy or collapse).
  - Poor OOD generalization (task representation fails to capture dynamics‑relevant factors; check InfoNCE loss magnitude).
  - Policy collapses to a subset of actions (latent MDP not expressive enough or world model error high).

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Ablation on latent space formulation**: Compare "Discrete+CE" (default) vs. continuous+MSE. Expect significant drop in IQM for continuous regression.
  2. **Disentanglement metrics on Cheetah‑LS**: Compute DCI/InfoMEC for world model alone vs. world model + InfoNCE. Expect informativeness/explicitness to increase with contrastive loss.
  3. **Zero‑shot OOD performance**: Test on Ant‑dir OOD tasks. Expect SPC to significantly outperform FOCAL/DORA if temporal consistency captures direction dynamics.

## Limitations
- The core assumption that task differences manifest primarily in *dynamics* may be invalid if tasks vary only in reward functions, weakening the temporal consistency signal.
- FSQ discretization resolution is task‑dependent; overly aggressive quantization may discard subtle but task‑relevant state information.
- Contrastive loss may over‑separate semantically similar tasks, potentially harming generalization between them.

## Next Checks
1. **Reward‑only task variation test**: Construct tasks with identical dynamics but different reward functions (e.g., same MuJoCo robot, varying goal positions/velocities). Measure SPC’s zero‑shot performance and representation informativeness (DCI). *Expected*: Significant drop if temporal consistency is the sole driver.
2. **FSQ resolution sensitivity analysis**: Vary number of quantization channels and codebook size on a complex benchmark (e.g., Meta‑World). Evaluate trade‑off between latent expressiveness (feature rank, dormant neurons) and policy IQM. *Expected*: Identify a “sweet spot” where discretization aids without excessive information loss.
3. **Contrastive loss on similar tasks**: Evaluate SPC on a suite of tasks with highly correlated dynamics (e.g., quadruped locomotion with minor gait variations) and measure generalization *between* these tasks vs. to dissimilar ones. Compare performance with/without \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{Contrastive}} \). *Expected*: Contrastive may impair within‑cluster generalization, indicating over‑separation.
