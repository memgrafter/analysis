# DeepStock: Reinforcement Learning with Policy Regularizations for Inventory Management

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.19621
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19621
- **Reference count**: 20
- **Primary result**: Policy regularizations embedding classical base stock concepts enable deep reinforcement learning to achieve significant inventory cost savings (∼350 M RMB) in large-scale real-world deployment while reducing hyperparameter tuning effort.

## Executive Summary
Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) holds promise for inventory control but suffers from high hyperparameter sensitivity and deployment difficulty. This paper introduces **policy regularizations** that embed classical “Base Stock” concepts into the action space via simple remappings, without restricting policy expressiveness. Three variants—Base, Coeff, and Both—are proposed and evaluated with DDPG, PPO, and Differentiable Simulation (DS). Synthetic experiments show that regularizations accelerate convergence, improve performance across all DRL methods, reduce tuning effort, and shift the balance in favor of traditional DRL over DS. On Alibaba’s offline data, DDPG with Both regularization achieved the best stockout rate and turnover time after only 10 hyperparameter trials. Real deployment across 1 M+ SKU‑warehouse pairs on Tmall delivered pilot reductions of 0.83% in stockout rate and 9.53 days in turnover, while a major rollout cut turnover by 1–2 days (20% in July–August 2025) without worsening stockouts, yielding ∼350 M RMB inventory reduction and ∼13.3 M RMB annual capital cost savings.

## Method Summary
The task is multi‑period inventory replenishment for individual SKUs. The state consists of the endogenous inventory vector \(I_t\) (upcoming shipments) and exogenous features \(x_t\) (e.g., product category, demand forecasts, promotions). The action is the order quantity. The core innovation is **policy regularization**, which modifies the raw neural network output \(\mu(I_t, x_t)\) as follows:
- **Base**: \(\text{order} = \max\{ \mu_{\text{Base}}(I_t, x_t) - \text{total\_inventory}(I_t), 0\}\)
- **Coeff**: \(\text{order} = \mu_{\text{Coeff}}(I_t, x_t)^\top \cdot \text{feat}(x_t)\), where \(\text{feat}(x_t)\) is a hand‑crafted 5‑dimensional feature extractor (historical/forecasted demand plus bias).
- **Both**: combination of both forms.

These regularizations encode classical inventory logic while preserving the policy’s ability to learn beyond the base‑stock heuristic. The method is agnostic to the DRL algorithm; the paper evaluates DDPG (off‑policy actor‑critic), PPO (on‑policy clipped objective), and DS (trajectory optimization via differentiable simulation). Training proceeds with extensive hyperparameter tuning (Bayesian optimization with multi‑seed validation) and early stopping on a validation set. For real data, the reward combines stockout rate \(\ell_{SR}\) and turnover time \(\ell_{TT}\) with business‑defined weights; synthetic experiments use underage/overage loss \(\ell_{bh}\). Features and inventory states are normalized per SKU to enable meta‑learning a single policy across heterogeneous SKUs.

## Key Results
- **Synthetic validation**: Regularizations accelerate convergence and improve final performance for DDPG, PPO, and DS; they also narrow the gap between DRL and DS, especially when hyperparameter tuning is limited.
- **Alibaba offline experiment**: With 50 k SKU trajectories, DDPG equipped with Both regularization achieved the lowest stockout rate and turnover time after only 10 hyperparameter trials (∼24 h each), demonstrating robustness to tuning effort.
- **Real‑world impact**: Deploying DDPG‑Both on Tmall (1 M+ SKU‑warehouse pairs) yielded a 0.83% stockout reduction and 9.53‑day turnover decrease in the international pilot; the full rollout reduced turnover by 1–2 days (∼20% in July–August 2025) without increasing stockouts, translating to ∼350 M RMB inventory reduction and ∼13.3 M RMB annual capital cost savings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Base Regularization Stabilizes Learning
Base regularization embeds a base‑stock structure, forcing the order quantity to depend on the difference between a learned target level and the current total inventory. This separation of target‑level learning from inventory‑state handling stabilizes Q‑function estimation by narrowing the distribution of visited states, leading to faster convergence and reduced hyperparameter sensitivity. The core assumption is that base stock policies are effective heuristics; the regularization guides exploration without prohibiting the discovery of superior policies. Evidence includes lower critic loss and tighter state distributions under Base regularization (Fig. 3) and the abstract’s claim of accelerated tuning. A break condition arises if the environment violates base‑stock assumptions (e.g., highly stochastic lead times), where the regularization may force suboptimal, unlearnable actions.

### Mechanism 2: Coeff Regularization Enables Cross‑SKU Meta‑Learning
Coeff regularization multiplies a learned coefficient vector by a fixed feature extractor \(\text{feat}(x_t)\) (e.g., demand forecasts). Combined with per‑SKU normalization of \(I_t\) and \(x_t\), this yields actions that scale proportionally with demand magnitude, allowing a single policy network to serve heterogeneous SKUs without explicit clustering. The assumption is that normalized feature patterns and linear combination with \(\text{feat}(x_t)\) capture common ordering logic across diverse sales scales. The paper’s abstract and Section 6.2 support this meta‑learning capability, though no external corpus directly corroborates it. The method may fail if feature normalization discards SKU‑specific signals or \(\text{feat}(x_t)\) is poorly designed.

### Mechanism 3: Regularizations Tilt Scales Toward Traditional DRL Over Differentiable Simulation
Policy regularizations constrain policy outputs, creating more stable and frequently visited state spaces. This accelerates value‑based learning (DDPG/PPO) by improving sample efficiency and mitigating the overfitting tendency of DS, which directly optimizes a trajectory loss without cross‑ temporal bootstrapping. Consequently, regularized DRL matches or exceeds DS performance even with limited hyperparameter trials, whereas DS suffers from overfitting on small sample sizes (Fig. 4). The core assumption is that traditional DRL benefits more from structural inductive biases in data‑limited regimes. Evidence: Figure 2 shows DDPG/PPO surpass DS with fewer trials when regularized; Figure 4 shows DS’s larger validation‑testing loss gap shrinks under regularization. Under abundant IID trajectories, DS may catch up (Setting 2 in synthetic experiments).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
State preprocessing (normalize \(I_t\), \(x_t\) per SKU; extract \(\text{feat}(x_t)\) for Coeff/Both) → Policy network (\(\mu(I_t, x_t)\)) → Action remapping (Base/Coeff/Both) → DRL trainer (DDPG/PPO/DS) → Reward calculator (\(\ell_{bh}\) for synthetic; weighted \(\ell_{SR}+\ell_{TT}\) for real) → Hyperparameter tuner (Bayesian optimization, multi‑seed validation).

**Critical path**:
1. Data ingestion: Load historical trajectories \((x_t, d_t)\), infer demands during stockouts, split into train/validate/test.
2. Normalize features per SKU using training statistics.
3. Initialize policy network with chosen regularization; select DRL algorithm.
4. Train with validation‑based early stopping; tune hyperparameters sequentially via Bayesian optimization.
5. Deploy best policy; monitor \(\ell_{SR}\) and \(\ell_{TT}\); retrain every 1.5 months.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Regularization strength: Both may converge fastest but could limit adaptation to irregular demand patterns.
- Model simplicity: Using simplified deterministic lead‑time and unit‑processing assumptions eases training; paper argues robustness outweighs modeling complexity.
- Computational cost: DDPG/PPO require many hyperparameter trials (∼24 h each at scale); DS is faster but prone to overfitting with few trajectories.

**Failure signatures**:
- Training: Validation loss stagnates or rises; large gap between validation and test loss; critic loss in DDPG oscillates.
- Deployment: Stockout rate increases post‑deployment; turnover time unchanged or rises; metrics degrade after retraining.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Synthetic baseline: Train DDPG with None vs. Base on INDEP distribution (20 trajectories); compare testing loss gap after 10 hyperparameter trials—expect Base to converge faster and with lower gap.
2. Offline data stress test: Run DDPG with Both regularization on Alibaba offline data (50k SKUs) with limited trials (10); compare \(\ell_{SR}\) and \(\ell_{TT}\) to no regularization—expect Pareto improvement.
3. Pilot deployment: Deploy DDPG‑Both on 10% of international SKUs for one month; use difference‑in‑differences against control—expect stockout reduction and turnover decrease without trade‑off.

## Limitations
- Exact feature engineering and the 5‑dimensional \(\text{feat}(x_t)\) for Coeff regularization on Alibaba data are unspecified, hindering full replication.
- Hyperparameter search ranges and network architectures for the large‑scale Alibaba offline experiment (10 trials, ∼24 h each) are omitted.
- Business‑level confounders (e.g., procurement team interventions) may partially explain observed real‑world gains, limiting causal attribution.

## Confidence
- Synthetic acceleration & robustness: **High** (well‑controlled experiments, consistent across DRL methods).
- Real‑world deployment gains (1 M+ SKUs): **Medium** (large‑scale A/B test but lacks isolated causal proof; seasonality controls are imperfect).
- Mechanism explanations (stabilization, meta‑learning): **Low to Medium** (supported by loss curves and intuition but no ablation isolating why regularization aids convergence).

## Next Checks
