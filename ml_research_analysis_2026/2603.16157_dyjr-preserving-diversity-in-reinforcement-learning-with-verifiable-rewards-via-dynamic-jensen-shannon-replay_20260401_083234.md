# DyJR: Preserving Diversity in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards via Dynamic Jensen-Shannon Replay

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.16157
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16157
- Reference count: 19
- Primary result: +4.3% average accuracy over GRPO on math reasoning and state-of-the-art on SQL with comparable computational efficiency.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for LLM reasoning suffers from sample inefficiency and mode collapse when past rollouts are discarded. DyJR introduces two complementary mechanisms: a time‑sensitive dynamic buffer that captures diverse early trajectories and contracts later, and Jensen‑Shannon (JS) divergence regularization that constrains policy updates relative to a mixture of historical successful distributions. Combined with the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) backbone, DyJR achieves 34.1% average accuracy on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (+4.3% over GRPO) and attains state‑of‑the‑art results on SQL tasks. It maintains token‑level diversity, reduces Rank‑1 token probability dominance, and exhibits superior Pass@k scalability while matching GRPO’s computational footprint.

## Method Summary
DyJR augments the GRPO algorithm with a dynamic experience replay buffer and a JS divergence regularization term. The buffer stores only perfect (reward = 1) rollouts, with a time‑sensitive capacity: during the first 20 training steps, the admission fill rate η is set high (e.g., 20%) to capture a broad spectrum of exploratory trajectories; thereafter it is reduced (e.g., 5%). A FIFO eviction with maximum age M ensures recency. For each new batch, samples are admitted in descending order of group confidence (the number of correct rollouts per query) until the fill target is met. The JS regularization loss is computed on uniformly sampled replay batches: for each token, the importance ratio u = exp(log π_θ – stored_log π_old) is transformed by f(u) = u log u – (u+1) log (u+1) + ½, then averaged across tokens and batches. The total loss combines the GRPO loss on the online batch and α_JS times the JS loss. The replay batch size (e.g., 512) is smaller than the online batch (e.g., 4096), keeping overhead marginal.

## Key Results
- 34.1% average accuracy on math reasoning benchmarks (+4.3% over GRPO).
- State‑of‑the‑art Pass@1 on SQL tasks.
- Sustained token distribution diversity, mitigated Rank‑1 token probability dominance, and superior Pass@k scaling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
DyJR’s gains arise from three synergistic mechanisms.

### Mechanism 1: Time‑sensitive dynamic buffer preserves diverse early trajectories.
During initial training steps (first 20), the buffer capacity expands (higher fill rate η) to capture a broad spectrum of exploratory trajectories. After this warm‑up, the buffer contracts (lower η) and a FIFO eviction enforces a maximum age M, retaining only temporally proximal samples. This exploits the empirical rapid early entropy drop: high‑entropy samples from this volatile period retain diversity that later peaked distributions lack. *Core assumption*: The value of historical data decays with temporal distance; early samples are more valuable for diversity than later high‑accuracy ones. *Evidence anchors*: The abstract mentions the buffer; Section 3.1 details adaptive capacity, FIFO, and confidence‑stratified admission. *Break condition*: If M is too large, stale samples introduce distribution mismatch and degrade performance (Table 1 ablation‑M). If η during warm‑up is too low, early diversity is not captured (Appendix C.2).

### Mechanism 2: Jensen‑Shannon regularization prevents mode collapse.
Instead of directly reusing replay samples for policy gradient updates (which overfits specific paths), DyJR minimizes the Jensen‑Shannon (JS) divergence between the current policy π_θ and a reference distribution Q_B (empirical mixture of stored trajectories). The symmetric, bounded nature of JS provides a stable constraint that mitigates the mode‑covering tendency of forward KL and avoids the instability of reverse KL in non‑stationary settings. *Core assumption*: Direct gradient updates from replay cause overfitting to narrow modes; a distributional constraint better preserves diversity. *Evidence anchors*: Abstract states it replaces direct gradient updates; Section 3.2 derives the generative estimator f(u) and loss; Section 4.3 shows JS outperforms forward KL (34.1% vs. 32.5%) and that performance follows an inverted U‑shape with α_JS (optimal at 0.05). *Break condition*: α_JS too low → under‑regularization, drift away from reference; α_JS too high → over‑constraint, stifles exploration (Table 1 ablation‑α). The effect reverses if the buffer becomes irrelevant.

### Mechanism 3: Synergy sustains diversity and Pass@k scalability.
The dynamic buffer supplies a continuously updated set of high‑entropy samples, while JS regularization gently pulls the policy toward the mixture of these samples rather than forcing exact imitation. This combined effect prevents the Rank‑1 token probability from dominating, maintains higher entropy, and enables discovery of better reasoning paths as training progresses. This manifests in superior Pass@k curves and robustness across tasks. *Core assumption*: Diversity maintenance is prerequisite for long‑term reasoning improvement and scalability with larger sampling budgets. *Evidence anchors*: Abstract highlights superior Pass@k scalability and reduced Rank‑1 token probability dominance; Section 5.1 visualizes entropy and Rank‑k evolution showing GRPO collapse while DyJR maintains diversity; Section 5.3 Pass@k plot on Beyond AIME shows DyJR (JS) consistently above others; neighboring works also link diversity to better exploration. *Break condition*: Disabling either component (e.g., static buffer or no JS) degrades diversity and Pass@k performance (Figure 2, RLEP→DyJR transition). If buffer recency is lost (large M), the JS anchor becomes stale and ineffective.

## Foundational Learning
Understanding DyJR draws on three key concepts.

- **Experience Replay (Off‑Policy RL)**  
  *Why needed*: DyJR reinterprets replay as a diversity‑preserving regularizer rather than a direct policy update source. This departure from traditional replay (which improves sample efficiency but can degrade on‑policy stability) is central to its design.  
  *Quick check*: What are the typical risks of naive experience replay in on‑policy algorithms (e.g., PPO), and why does DyJR avoid them?

- **Divergence Measures (KL vs. Jensen‑Shannon)**  
  *Why needed*: The core regularization uses JS divergence; knowing its properties (symmetry, boundedness) versus KL (asymmetric, unbounded) explains why it is more suitable for anchoring to a mixture of non‑stationary policies.

- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**  
  *Why needed*: GRPO is the on‑policy backbone; DyJR adds replay and JS regularization to it. Grasping group‑based advantage estimation (without a value function) is essential to see how the base objective interacts with new terms and why it is computationally efficient for LLM reasoning.

## Architecture Onboarding
DyJR extends GRPO with two new components and a modified training loop.

### Component Map
Queries X → πθ_old → Responses Y → Rewards R & Group confidence C_id → {GRPO module, Buffer S_t}. Buffer S_t (FIFO, age M, fill η) → Replay batch → JS regularization (with current πθ) → L_JS. GRPO module → L_GRPO. L_GRPO + α_JS·L_JS → Optimizer → πθ update.

### Critical Path (per training step)
1. **Rollout**: Sample queries X, generate group Y ~ π_θold (G = 8); compute binary rewards R and group confidence C_id.
2. **Buffer update**: Evict samples with age > M. Admit new perfect samples from current batch in descending order of C_id until admitted count reaches η·batch_size. Store (x, y, logπ_old) with current step.
3. **Optimization**:  
   - Compute L_GRPO on online batch (X, Y).  
   - Sample replay batch B_replay uniformly from S_t (size 512).  
   - Compute L_JS on B_replay using stored logπ_old and current logπ_θ.  
   - Minimize L_total = L_GRPO + α_JS·L_JS and step optimizer (AdamW).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Buffer recency (M) vs. coverage**: Smaller M ensures freshness but may lose rare modes; larger M risks stale reference. Optimal M = 8 (Table 1 ablation‑M).
- **Regularization strength (α_JS)**: Too low → under‑regularization; too high → over‑constraint. Requires tuning; optimal ~0.05 on math.
- **Warm‑up fill rate (η_init)**: Higher captures early diversity; must balance memory overhead.
- **Replay batch size**: Larger improves gradient estimate but adds compute; DyJR uses 512 vs. online 4096 to keep overhead marginal (Section 5.4).

### Failure Signatures
- **Mode collapse**: Entropy drops sharply and Rank‑1 token probability approaches 1.0 (as in GRPO, Figure 1).
- **Stagnant Pass@k**: High Pass@1 but minimal gain as k increases, indicating lack of diverse solutions.
- **Divergence spikes**: L_JS becomes unstable or extremely high, suggesting buffer content misalignment (e.g., M too large).
- **Memory bloat**: If buffer capacity grows unchecked (e.g., η=20% with large M), GPU memory overhead increases.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Main performance comparison**: Run DyJR vs. GRPO and other baselines on math benchmarks; expect ~34% average accuracy (+4.3% over GRPO) and SOTA on SQL (Table 1, Table 2).
2. **Pass@k scalability**: Sample k from 4 to 1024; plot Pass@k curves. DyJR (JS) should show superior scaling compared to GRPO and Forward‑KL variant (Figure 3a).
3. **Ablation on buffer recency (M)**: Train with M ∈ {8, 16, 32, 64}. Observe performance peak at M=8; monotonic decline as M increases (Table 1 ablation‑M). This confirms importance of temporal proximity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Optimal adaptive schedule for η(t):** The paper uses a heuristic (20% initial, 5% later) but lacks a theoretical principle to determine η(t) from training dynamics like entropy decay. A framework linking η(t) to real‑time entropy measurements could improve performance across tasks.
- **Is Jensen‑Shannon the best divergence?** The paper compares JS with forward KL but does not test other symmetric divergences (e.g., Hellinger). It is unclear if JS is optimal for non‑stationary policies.
- **Applicability beyond verifiable rewards:** DyJR is evaluated only on tasks with binary rewards (math, SQL). Its adaptation to dense or shaped rewards would require redefining sample selection and reference distribution; evidence is lacking.

## Limitations
- Buffer fill rate semantics (batch fraction vs. buffer capacity) are ambiguous, affecting early diversity capture.
- Replay gradient integration (joint vs. separate steps) and α_JS scaling are unspecified, potentially distorting regularization.
- Token‑level reduction for L_JS may not align with L_GRPO averaging across differing batch/token counts.
- Benchmarks limited to math and SQL; generalizability to other reasoning tasks unknown.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity (M, η, α_JS) is high; optimal values appear dataset‑specific.

## Confidence
- Performance gain: **High** (consistent +4.3% over GRPO across math, SOTA on SQL).
- Diversity preservation: **Medium** (entropy/Rank‑k trends and Pass@k curves supportive but lack statistical rigor).
- Computational parity: **Medium** (overhead claimed minimal but not directly measured).
