# MINAR: Mechanistic Interpretability for Neural Algorithmic Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.21442
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21442
- **Reference Count**: 40
- **Primary Result**: MINAR automatically discovers sparse, high-fidelity circuits in GNNs performing algorithmic reasoning, revealing shared subgraphs across related tasks and delayed pruning under regularization.

## Executive Summary
MINAR is an automated toolbox for neuron-level circuit discovery in graph neural networks (GNNs) adapting attribution patching methods like EAP and EAP-IG to graph-structured data. It averages node-wise attribution scores to identify critical edges and uses a longest-path algorithm to extract connected end-to-end circuits. Case studies on algorithmic tasks demonstrate MINAR's ability to recover minimal, high-performance circuits (e.g., a 10-edge Bellman-Ford circuit) and uncover nuanced behaviors like delayed pruning under L1 regularization. Benchmarking across seven CLRS tasks shows circuits occupy less than 1% of edges and exhibit significant overlap among related algorithms, offering insights into transfer learning for neural algorithmic reasoning.

## Method Summary
MINAR extends attribution-based circuit discovery to GNNs by first computing node-level attribution scores (via patching or integrated gradients), then averaging these scores across nodes to obtain edge importance. A threshold filters edges, and a longest-path heuristic connects salient edges into end-to-end circuits. This approach adapts techniques originally designed for transformers to the message-passing paradigm of GNNs, enabling automated, fine-grained analysis of graph models without manual annotation.

## Key Results
- Recovered a minimal Bellman-Ford circuit (10 edges) that replicates full model test loss (0.0545 vs. 0.0578) and is necessary (ablation loss >20000).
- Discovered delayed circuit pruning: the optimal sparse circuit only emerges after generalization despite L1 regularization during training.
- On SALSA-CLRS (7 tasks), found high-characterization circuits using <1% of edges, with substantial overlap among related tasks (e.g., Dijkstra, Prim, Bellman-Ford), suggesting shared subroutines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MINAR works by translating neuron-level importance scores from attribution patching into graph-edge salience via node-wise averaging, which respects the GNN's message-passing structure. The longest-path algorithm then enforces connectivity, ensuring circuits represent executable subgraphs. This combination effectively isolates minimal working subunits because algorithmic GNNs often rely on sparse, distributed computations; thresholding captures critical edges while the connectivity constraint preserves functional coherence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Attribution Patching**: Intervenes on neurons/edges to measure causal impact on loss. *Why needed*: Isolates components responsible for correct predictions. *Quick check*: Apply to an MLP on a simple parity task to verify recovered neurons match known logic.
- **Circuit Discovery**: Identifying minimal subgraphs that replicate model behavior. *Why needed*: Enables mechanistic explanation without full model analysis. *Quick check*: Extract circuits from a small transformer on modular arithmetic and test for completeness via ablation.
- **GNN Message Passing**: Iterative node updates via neighbor aggregation. *Why needed*: Defines how information flows in graph models; adaptations must respect this. *Quick check*: Visualize node embeddings before/after a layer to confirm aggregation effects.
- **Algorithmic Reasoning**: Training neural networks to execute classical algorithms (e.g., shortest path). *Why needed*: Provides ground-truth circuits for validation. *Quick check*: Train a GNN on a line-graph shortest path and check if loss approaches zero.
- **Sparsity Regularization (L1)**: Induces sparse connectivity during training. *Why needed*: May simplify circuit extraction but can delay emergence of optimal circuits. *Quick check*: Compare circuits from models trained with/without L1 on the same task.
- **Cross-Task Overlap Analysis**: Comparing circuits across tasks to infer shared structure. *Why needed*: Sheds light on transfer and modularity. *Quick check*: Compute Jaccard similarity of edge sets from Dijkstra and Bellman-Ford circuits.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map**: Input Graph -> GNN Layers (message passing) -> Node Embeddings -> Attribution Patching (node-wise) -> Aggregated Edge Scores -> Thresholding -> Longest-Path Algorithm -> Extracted Circuit.
- **Critical Path**: Attribution patching → score aggregation → thresholding → longest-path connection. Failures often originate in threshold choice or score aggregation.
- **Design Tradeoffs**: Threshold stringency (low: large, possibly noisy circuits; high: fragmented, incomplete circuits); longest-path heuristic (ensures connectivity but may not yield minimal circuits computationally).
- **Failure Signatures**: Ablation of extracted circuit does not increase loss (circuit non-essential); circuit not fully connected (path breaks); poor generalization of circuit to new graphs (overfitting to training distribution).
- **First Three Experiments**:
  1. Apply MINAR to a GNN solving Bellman-Ford on small graphs; manually verify the recovered circuit matches the known algorithm's steps.
  2. Perform a threshold sweep on a single task (e.g., BFS) to measure stability of circuit size and performance; identify a robust threshold range.
  3. Extract circuits from Dijkstra and Bellman-Ford models; compute edge overlap and interpret shared subgraphs (e.g., distance updates).

## Limitations
- Evaluated only on small synthetic algorithmic tasks and specific GNN architectures; untested on real-world graphs or graph transformers.
- Node-wise averaging of attribution scores may discard important cross-node interactions inherent in GNNs.
- Longest-path heuristic for circuit extraction does not guarantee minimality and may include redundant edges.
- Scalability of attribution patching to larger graphs or deeper networks not assessed.
- Observed task overlap could reflect shared low-level operations rather than high-level algorithmic similarity.

## Confidence
- **Circuit recovery fidelity** (Bellman-Ford case study): High
- **Circuit sparsity** (<1% edges on SALSA-CLRS): Medium
- **Cross-task overlap** (e.g., Dijkstra/Bellman-Ford): High
- **Delayed pruning under L1** observation: Low–Medium
- **MINAR as a general method** for diverse GNNs: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Test MINAR on a broader set of GNN architectures (GCN, GAT, Graph Transformer) and larger synthetic graphs to evaluate generalizability and computational scaling.
2. Use tasks with provably known ground-truth circuits (e.g., shortest path on a line graph) to quantitatively measure precision and recall of extracted subgraphs.
3. Conduct a systematic sensitivity analysis of attribution thresholds; measure stability of circuit size, performance, and overlap across thresholds to derive adaptive selection criteria.
