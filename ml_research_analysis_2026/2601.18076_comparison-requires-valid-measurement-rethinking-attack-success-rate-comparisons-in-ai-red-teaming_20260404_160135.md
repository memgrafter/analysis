# Comparison requires valid measurement: Rethinking attack success rate comparisons in AI red teaming
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.18076
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18076
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: ASR comparisons often conflate different estimands (conceptual failures) or are biased by invalid prompts and differential judge error, leading to unsupported claims about system safety or attack efficacy.

## Executive Summary
Attack success rate (ASR) is a central metric in AI red teaming, but comparisons of ASR across systems or attacks frequently yield unsupported conclusions. This paper shows that such comparisons are often invalid due to two fundamental problems: conceptual incoherence (comparing different population parameters) and measurement bias (systematic errors in how ASR is estimated). Using social science measurement theory, the authors formalize ASR as an estimate of a threat-model-defined success probability and derive necessary conditions for valid comparison. They demonstrate empirically that common practices like repeated sampling can inflate Top-1 ASR without changing one-shot success, and that many sophisticated jailbreaks do not outperform simple baselines when evaluated properly. The work calls for standardized threat models and rigorous validation of prompts and judges to ensure meaningful red teaming evaluations.

## Method Summary
The study applies measurement theory to AI red teaming by defining a probabilistic threat model \(M = (s, D, C)\) where \(s\) is an oracle success criterion (e.g., policy violation), \(D\) is the goal distribution of attack prompts, and \(C\) are constraints (e.g., sampling budget). The target estimand is \(\alpha = \mathbb{P}_{P \sim D}[s(L(P)) = 1]\), the true attack success probability for a system \(L\). An operational ASR uses a finite sample \(\hat{D} \subseteq D\), a judge \(J\) to approximate \(s\), and an aggregation rule (e.g., Top-1 over \(K\) samples). Valid comparison requires: (1) conceptual coherence—estimands must be mathematically identical; (2) measurement validity—operationalization must yield unbiased estimates. The authors analyze two failure modes: mismatched aggregation schemes that change the estimand (e.g., Top-1 vs. one-shot), and biases from non-representative prompts or differential judge error across systems/jailbreaks. Theoretical arguments are supported by case studies and empirical examinations of prior work.

## Key Results
- Conceptual incoherence: Mismatched aggregation schemes (Top-1 vs. one-shot) change the estimand, making comparisons invalid.
- Measurement bias: Misaligned prompt sets and differential judge error (varying TPR/FPR across systems/jailbreaks) bias ASR estimates.
- Empirical findings: Repeated sampling under high-entropy decoding inflates Top-1 ASR without improving per-attempt success; many advanced jailbreaks do not outperform simple baselines when measured correctly.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Conceptual Incoherence
- **Claim:** Mismatched estimands invalidate ASR comparisons because aggregation schemes (e.g., Top-1 vs. one-shot) alter the population parameter being estimated.
- **Mechanism:** ASR estimates an attack success probability defined by a threat model. The aggregation rule changes the mathematical form of the estimand; comparing ASRs with different aggregations compares different population parameters.
- **Core assumption:** The comparison aims to infer relative safety/efficacy based on a consistent definiendum.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] conceptual failures from mismatched aggregations; [section 4] case study comparing Generation Exploitation's Top-1 (of 392) vs GCG's one-shot; corpus notes on neighbors.
- **When valid:** Both ASRs use the same aggregation rule, same set of attack trials, same target system and prompts.

### Mechanism 2: Misaligned Prompt Distributions
- **Claim:** Measurement validity fails when the operational prompt set \(\hat{D}\) does not represent the threat model's goal distribution \(D\), biasing ASR.
- **Mechanism:** True attribute \(\alpha = \mathbb{P}_{P \sim D}[s(L(P)) = 1]\). Observed ASR uses \(\hat{D} \subset D\). If \(\hat{D}\) lacks content/face validity (prompts not actually harmful), \(\mathbb{E}[\text{ASR} \mid \hat{D}]\) diverges from \(\alpha\).
- **Core assumption:** The threat model's \(D\) correctly captures real-world attack goals.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] misaligned prompt distributions; [section 5.1] inspection of Chu et al. "Political Activities" prompts, many do not violate policy; corpus weak support.
- **When valid:** \(\hat{D}\) is a representative sample from \(D\) preserving the prevalence of \(s(P)=1\) across relevant subgroups.

### Mechanism 3: Differential Judge Error
- **Claim:** Differential judge error (varying TPR/FPR across systems/jailbreaks) biases ASR comparisons, creating apparent safety differences where none exist.
- **Mechanism:** Observed ASR = TPR \(\cdot\) \(\alpha\) + FPR \(\cdot\) \((1-\alpha)\). If TPR or FPR differ across compared systems, ASR can differ even if \(\alpha\) is equal.
- **Core assumption:** Judge \(J\) is intended to proxy oracle \(s\); conditional success probabilities \(P(J=1 \mid s=1)\) and \(P(J=1 \mid s=0)\) must be invariant across systems/jailbreaks.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] differential judge error; [section 5.2] mathematical derivation; empirical notes from Andriushchenko et al. (Claude false positives) and Mazeika et al. (length dependence); corpus weak.
- **When valid:** For all compared systems/jailbreaks, \(P(J=1 \mid s=1)\) and \(P(J=1 \mid s=0)\) are constant.

## Foundational Learning
- **Probabilistic Threat Model:** Defines the target estimand \(\alpha\) as a population parameter via \((s, D, C)\). Forces explicit articulation of the gold standard for comparison. *Quick check:* Given \(M = (s, D, C)\), write the precise expression for \(\alpha\).
- **Conceptual Coherence:** Ensures ASR comparisons are "apples-to-apples" by requiring that estimands represent the same underlying attribute under identical conditions. *Quick check:* If Study A reports a Top-1 (of 50) ASR and Study B reports a one-shot ASR for the same model on the same prompts, are these estimands comparable? No—they define different population parameters.
- **Measurement Validity & Differential Misclassification:** Requires that the operational judge approximates the oracle with consistent error rates (TPR/FPR) across compared entities. *Quick check:* If a judge has 90% overall accuracy on outputs from System X and System Y, can you conclude ASR comparisons are valid? No—need equal TPR and FPR separately, not just overall accuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Threat Model (M=(s,D,C)) → Operationalization (translates s→Judge J, D→Prompt set D; defines jailbreak T and aggregation rule) → Execution (collect data: apply T to D, query system, adjudicate with J) → Computation (aggregate J outputs to compute ASR).
Critical path: Background Concept (e.g., safety) → Threat Model M (systematization) → Operational Protocol (operationalization) → Data Collection (execution) → ASR Computation.
Design tradeoffs:
- Standardization vs. Validity: Standardizing components (prompts, judge, sampling) is necessary but not sufficient; must also ensure \(\hat{D}\) matches \(D\) and judge error is constant.
- Aggregation K: Larger \(K\) (more samples/configurations) increases Top-1 ASR even if per-sample success probability is unchanged, altering the estimand.
Failure signatures:
- Very high baseline ASR (>0.5) on "harmful" prompts → suspect prompt validity.
- ASR varies dramatically with output length or judge model → suspect differential judge error.
- ASR reported without explicit aggregation definition → suspect conceptual coherence.
- Cross-study/judge ASR ordering flips → likely differential error or aggregation mismatch.
First 3 experiments:
1. Aggregation Audit: Reverse-engineer the estimand expression for a reported ASR; check if it matches the comparative claim's implied estimand.
2. Prompt Set Validation: Sample 20–50 base harmful prompts from \(\hat{D}\); have domain experts label whether compliance violates policy; compute validity rate; reject if <80%.
3. Judge Error Profiling: For each compared system/jailbreak, run judge \(J\) on a held-out set of system responses gold-labeled by experts; estimate TPR and FPR separately; if confidence intervals do not overlap, ASR comparisons are biased.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the AI red teaming community standardize the definition and computation of attack success rate (ASR) estimands to ensure conceptual coherence in comparisons across studies?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper identifies mismatched aggregations (e.g., Top-1 vs. one-shot) as a key cause of apples-to-oranges comparisons. Section 4 deconstructs how different aggregation schemes (repeated sampling, multiple configurations) alter the underlying estimand.
- **Why unresolved:** Current literature lacks consistent terminology and explicit definitions for ASR calculations, making it unclear what population parameter (e.g., one-shot success probability vs. Top-1 over many samples) is being estimated in each study. Without comparable estimands, comparative claims about system safety or attack efficacy are unsupported.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A consensus framework that precisely specifies threat model components (goal distribution, success criteria, constraints) and aggregation functions, adopted by major red-teaming benchmarks. Empirical validation showing that standardized estimands reliably reflect intended concepts of safety or efficacy across diverse scenarios.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What methods can be developed to assess and correct for differential judge error (e.g., varying true positive/false positive rates) across target AI systems and jailbreak methods to improve measurement validity of ASRs?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 5.2 demonstrates how judge errors biased ASR comparisons, noting that judges (human or LLM-based) often have system- and method-specific error patterns. The paper calls for reporting judge performance disaggregated by system and method.
- **Why unresolved:** Most red-teaming studies rely on a single judge without validating its accuracy on outputs from different systems or attacks, risking biased comparisons. Statistical techniques for adjusting for differential misclassification are underutilized in this context, and no standard practice exists for calibrating judges.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic benchmark evaluation of judge systems across a diverse set of target models and jailbreaks, measuring TPR/FPR per system-method pair. Development and application of correction methods (e.g., calibration, Bayesian adjustments) in case studies, showing reduced bias in ASR comparisons and alignment with human expert judgments.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can we ensure the content and face validity of harmful prompt datasets used in jailbreaking studies, so that base prompts truly reflect undesirable behaviors as defined by system usage policies?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 5.1 critiques the Chu et al. [11] prompts for the "Political Activities" category, showing many do not clearly violate OpenAI's policy. The paper links this to failures of content and face validity, where test items (prompts) do not accurately reflect the target domain.
- **Why unresolved:** Prompt datasets are often constructed through convenience sampling or without rigorous mapping to policy definitions, leading to ASRs that measure something other than intended safety violations. This undermines the interpretive meaningfulness of ASR as a safety metric and may mislead about relative risk areas.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A validated methodology for generating or curating prompt datasets, involving expert policy mapping and iterative refinement. Studies correlating prompt compliance rates with human expert judgments and real-world incident reports to establish criterion and predictive validity.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Under what conditions do sophisticated jailbreak methods genuinely improve attack success probability beyond simple repeated-sampling baselines, and how should efficacy be benchmarked?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 4.III shows that repeated sampling under high-entropy decoding can achieve ASRs comparable to advanced jailbreaks like LAA. The paper argues that new jailbreaks should be compared against such baselines to avoid overestimating efficacy.
- **Why unresolved:** Many jailbreak studies report Top-1 ASRs without controlling for the effect of repeated sampling, potentially attributing to the jailbreak what is merely a statistical artifact of multiple attempts. There is no standard baseline for fair comparison, and the field lacks understanding of when jailbreaks add value over brute-force sampling.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments where jailbreak methods are evaluated both with and without repeated sampling, using one-shot metrics or matched sampling budgets. Analysis of prompt-level success probabilities to distinguish methods that increase per-attempt success from those that merely increase attempt count, across diverse models and prompts.

## Limitations
- External validation: Findings rely on limited empirical examples and need broader replication across diverse red teaming studies.
- Threat model assumptions: Formal model requires explicit \(D\) and \(C\), which are rarely provided, and oversimplifies real-world harm diversity.
- Oracle reliance: Obtaining gold-standard success criteria and judge error profiles is challenging for complex policies.

## Confidence
- Conceptual coherence (aggregation mismatch): High
- Content validity (prompt misalignment): Medium
- Differential judge error: High
