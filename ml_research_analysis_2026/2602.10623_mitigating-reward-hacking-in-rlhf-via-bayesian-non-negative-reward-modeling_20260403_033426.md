# Mitigating Reward Hacking in RLHF via Bayesian Non-negative Reward Modeling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10623
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10623
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** BNRM mitigates reward hacking with 5.4–13.3 percentage point gains across ID/OOD benchmarks and sets a new state-of-the-art on RewardBench.

## Executive Summary
Reward hacking in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) stems from noisy preferences and spurious biases like response length. The Bayesian Non-Negative Reward Model (BNRM) addresses this by combining non-negative factor analysis with the Bradley-Terry model, using sparse latent factors to disentangle instance-level features and remove global biases. Scalable amortized variational inference with Weibull posteriors enables efficient training. BNRM substantially reduces reward over-optimization, achieving significant improvements on multiple alignment benchmarks and demonstrating robustness in low-resource and high-noise settings.

## Method Summary
BNRM integrates non-negative factor analysis into the Bradley-Terry framework for pairwise preference modeling. It introduces sparse latent factors to separate instance-specific signals from universal biases (e.g., length). The Bayesian approach treats reward parameters as random variables, with amortized variational inference sharing an encoder network across data points to enable scalability. Weibull distributions model the posteriors, capturing heavy-tailed uncertainty. The non-negativity constraint ensures interpretable reward values while the sparsity prior promotes disentanglement and debiasing.

## Key Results
- Achieves gains of 5.4–13.3 percentage points on in-distribution and out-of-distribution benchmarks (Unified Feedback, HHH Alignment, MT-Bench, RewardBench) with 40K training samples.
- Full fine-tuning on SP data yields 93.6% on RewardBench, a new state-of-the-art; reduces length bias correlation from 0.488 to 0.123 and improves RM-Bench Hard subset accuracy by 2.7 points.
- Demonstrates superior robustness in low-resource (1K samples) and high-noise (40% label noise) settings, outperforming baselines by up to 16.7%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Sparse latent factors disentangle reward-relevant features from spurious correlations (e.g., length), while the non-negative constraint prevents unrealistic negative rewards. The Bayesian framework accounts for uncertainty in human preferences, reducing overfitting to noise. Amortized inference with flexible Weibull posteriors maintains computational efficiency and captures heavy-tailed variations in rewards. Together, these elements prevent the model from exploiting superficial patterns and focus on genuine preference signals.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Preference Data -> Encoder Network -> Sparse Non-negative Latent Factors -> Reward Scores -> Bradley-Terry Loss -> Parameter Updates  
Critical path: Input pairwise preferences are encoded into latent factors under sparsity and non-negativity constraints; rewards are derived and compared via Bradley-Terry likelihood; Bayesian parameters updated via amortized VI with Weibull posteriors.  
Design tradeoffs: Non-negativity improves interpretability but may limit expressiveness; sparsity promotes debiasing but risks underfitting; amortization boosts efficiency but may reduce posterior accuracy.  
Failure signatures: High correlation between predicted rewards and response length; degraded performance on out-of-distribution prompts; unstable latent factor activations under high label noise.  
First experiments: 1) Verify SOTA on RewardBench with 40K samples. 2) Ablate non-negativity and sparsity to measure impact on length bias correlation. 3) Test on a non-LLM preference dataset (e.g., robotic trajectories) to assess generalizability.

## Limitations
- No analysis of failure modes or sensitivity to latent factor dimensionality choices.
- Unclear computational overhead of amortized Bayesian inference at larger scales; no runtime comparisons provided.
- Evaluation restricted to LLM alignment tasks; generalizability to other domains (e.g., robotics, games) is unknown.
