# On the Role of Reasoning Patterns in the Generalization Discrepancy of Long Chain-of-Thought Supervised Fine-Tuning

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2604.01702
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01702
*   **Reference count:** 40
*   **Primary result:** Filtering branch-heavy trajectories from DeepSeek-R1 SFT data improves performance on reasoning benchmarks by up to 5.1% (AIME25) and 3.6% average across five benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with long chain-of-thought (CoT) trajectories from different large reasoning models (LRMs) yields a generalization paradox: training on DeepSeek-R1-0528 data achieves lower training loss but significantly worse performance on mathematical reasoning benchmarks than training on gpt-oss-120b data, despite both providing correct solutions for the same problems. Analysis reveals that DeepSeek-R1 trajectories exhibit a divergent, branch-heavy exploration pattern (frequent "Propose" steps), while gpt-oss-120b trajectories are convergent and deductive. SFT transfers these patterns to student models, causing them to get trapped in redundant exploratory branches. Filtering out the most branching trajectories from the DeepSeek-R1 dataset using simple proxy metrics mitigates this issue and substantially improves generalization.

## Method Summary
The study uses a dataset of ~500k math problems, each with two verified correct CoT trajectories generated by DeepSeek-R1-0528 and gpt-oss-120b. A base model (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B) is fine-tuned via SFT on trajectories from each teacher separately. To diagnose the generalization discrepancy, the authors conduct token-level loss analysis and annotate reasoning steps into behavioral categories (Propose, Deduce, etc.). They identify two proxy metrics to filter DeepSeek-R1's data: (1) step-count difference ratio relative to gpt-oss, and (2) frequency of branching keywords. Student models are retrained on these filtered subsets and evaluated on five math benchmarks (MATH500, AIME24/25, BeyondAIME, HMMT25).

## Key Results
*   DeepSeek-R1 trajectories show a long-tail token loss distribution with many near-zero-loss "routine" tokens, diluting the average loss signal and obscuring under-optimization on sparse, critical "key reasoning" tokens.
*   DeepSeek-R1 has a higher proportion of `Propose` steps and `Propose→Propose` transitions; student models trained on its data inherit this divergent pattern.
*   Filtering out the top 10-20% most-branching DeepSeek-R1 trajectories (by proxy metrics) improves student performance across all five benchmarks, with gains of up to 5.1% on AIME25 and an average of 3.6%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Loss Distribution Dilution
*   **Claim:** Lower average SFT training loss on DeepSeek-R1 data is a misleading metric because the loss distribution is diluted by a high proportion of low-loss "routine" tokens, obscuring under-optimization on sparse, high-loss "key reasoning" tokens that signal logical transitions.
*   **Mechanism:** The token-level cross-entropy loss follows a long-tail distribution. DeepSeek-R1 trajectories contain more tokens with near-zero loss (e.g., simple calculations) compared to gpt-oss-120b. This dilutes the average loss, creating a false signal of better fit while the critical high-loss tokens—indicating moments of branching or hypothesis generation—remain under-optimized.
*   **Core assumption:** Student generalization depends primarily on learning to predict the high-loss key reasoning tokens, not the routine tokens. Saturation with easy tokens wastes model capacity and causes the optimizer to neglect harder, more informative transitions.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] DeepSeek-R1's divergent pattern leads to structural redundancy. [section 4.1] Figures 2 and 6 show the long-tail loss and frequent high-loss tokens (e.g., "Perhaps", "Another"). Table 2 shows similar average loss for key tokens but more near-zero tokens for DeepSeek. [corpus] Neighbors discuss token-level importance and negative samples.
*   **Break condition:** If filtering improves performance despite the dilution, it supports the hypothesis. If key token loss does not correlate with benchmark performance across filtered subsets, the mechanism is weakened.

### Mechanism 2: Transfer of Inefficient Exploration Pattern
*   **Claim:** DeepSeek-R1's inherent "divergent, branch-heavy" reasoning pattern (frequent `Propose` steps) is transferred to student models via SFT, trapping them in shallow, repetitive exploratory branches that waste context and hinder convergence.
*   **Mechanism:** Behavioral analysis shows DeepSeek-R1 has more `Propose` steps and higher `Propose→Propose` transition probability (0.53 vs. 0.34). SFT shapes the student's policy to mimic this inefficient tree-like search with many dead-end branches, as reflected in its generated solutions.
*   **Core assumption:** A reasoning trajectory's effectiveness for SFT is determined by its macro-level structure. Convergent, deductive chains (`Deduce`-heavy) are more learnable and generalizable than divergent, exploratory ones with shallow branches.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] gpt-oss-120b is convergent and deductive; DeepSeek-R1 is divergent and branch-heavy. [section 4.2] Figure 3 shows behavior distributions and transition matrices; students inherit the high `Propose→Propose` rate. [section 4.3] Random step deletion harms gpt-oss-trained models more, indicating their trajectories are dense and dependent. [corpus] Neighbors explore efficiency and redundancy in long CoT.
*   **Break condition:** If filtering based on branch metrics does not improve performance, the transferred pattern is not the primary cause. If students trained on DeepSeek data show low `Propose→Propose` rates despite high teacher rates, transfer is not occurring.

### Mechanism 3: Redundancy as Harmful Noise
*   **Claim:** Structural redundancy in DeepSeek-R1 trajectories means many steps are non-essential. Removing the most frequently branching trajectories acts as implicit regularization, forcing the student to focus on the core deductive path, thereby improving generalization.
*   **Mechanism:** Proxy filtering metrics identify trajectories with the most shallow exploration. Training on this curated subset reduces the student's exposure to the divergent pattern, resulting in a higher proportion of `Deduce` steps in its own reasoning and better benchmark performance.
*   **Core assumption:** Redundant branches are not neutral but actively harmful—they teach an inefficient policy. Removing them denoises the dataset, improving the signal-to-noise ratio of the reasoning structure.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Filtering improves performance by up to 5.1%. [section 5] Tables 3 and 4 show consistent gains; control groups (top branching subsets) show drops. [section 4.3] Figure 4 shows random deletion sometimes improves DeepSeek-trained models, supporting redundancy. [corpus] Neighbors discuss overthinking and redundancy.
*   **Break condition:** If optimal filtering threshold varies wildly across tasks or if filtering harms some benchmarks, redundancy is not uniformly detrimental. If proxy metrics fail to correlate with student `Propose` rates, the filtering mechanism is flawed.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Token-level loss distribution analysis (long-tail phenomenon)**
    *   **Why needed:** To diagnose why lower average loss (DeepSeek) correlates with worse generalization. It reveals that average loss is misleading when token contributions are heterogeneous.
    *   **Quick check:** Given two datasets with identical final answers but different token-level loss profiles, what would a long-tail distribution imply about the "learnability" of each?
*   **Concept: Reasoning behavior taxonomy and Markov transition matrices**
    *   **Why needed:** To move beyond tokens to macro-structural patterns (e.g., `Propose` vs. `Deduce`). This quantifies the "divergent vs. convergent" difference and tracks its transfer.
    *   **Quick check:** How would you distinguish a `Propose` step from a `Deduce` step based on logical role, not just keywords?
*   **Concept: Data curation via proxy metrics for structural properties**
    *   **Why needed:** Full behavioral annotation is costly. Proxies (step count difference, keyword density) offer scalable ways to filter datasets based on inferred reasoning patterns.
    *   **Quick check:** What are the risks of using keyword-based proxies (e.g., "Perhaps") to infer branching propensity?

## Architecture Onboarding
Teacher Data Generator (DeepSeek-R1, gpt-oss-120b) -> Behavior Annotator (DeepSeek-V3.2) -> Proxy Calculator -> SFT Trainer (on base model) -> Evaluator (on benchmarks)

**Critical path:** Generate paired trajectories -> Annotate behaviors -> Compute proxy metrics -> Filter dataset -> Train student -> Evaluate -> Compare student behavior distributions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
*Annotation quality vs. cost:* Using a powerful LLM for annotation is expensive but necessary for reliable labels. Proxies trade accuracy for scalability.  
*Context length:* Training capped at 32k tokens; DeepSeek's are longer on average. Inference context must be scaled, but truncation does not explain performance gaps.  
*Proxy design:* Metric 1 (step-count diff) requires paired teacher data. Metric 2 (keyword density) is standalone but may miss nuanced branching without keywords.

**Failure signatures:**  
*Token-level:* No long-tail in loss distribution; key tokens have similar loss distributions across teachers.  
*Behavioral:* Student trained on DeepSeek data shows high `Propose→Propose` probability (>0.4) versus low (<0.3) for gpt-oss-trained.  
*Performance:* Filtering does not improve benchmarks; or gain is inconsistent across models/benchmarks.  
*Deletion experiment:* Random step deletion harms DeepSeek-trained models as much as gpt-oss-trained, indicating no redundancy.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1.  **Token loss distribution sanity check:** For a base model (e.g., Qwen3-8B), compute pre/post-SFT token loss distributions for both teacher datasets. Verify the long-tail and dilution effect (more low-loss tokens for DeepSeek).
2.  **Behavior annotation validation:** Randomly sample 100 trajectories, annotate steps with DeepSeek-V3.2, compute behavior distributions and transition matrices. Confirm DeepSeek has higher `Propose` % and `Propose→Propose` probability.
3.  **Proxy filtering pilot:** For Qwen2.5-7B, filter using Proxy 2 (bottom/top 80% by `Propose` keyword density). Train and evaluate on AIME24/25. Expect bottom group to outperform top and baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   Does the observed generalization discrepancy persist in non-mathematical reasoning domains (e.g., code generation, agentic tasks)?
*   Are the divergent (branch-heavy) versus convergent (deductive) reasoning patterns inherent to teacher models, or can they be modified by changing generation strategies?
*   Can more advanced data curation (beyond simple filtering of branching trajectories) further improve SFT generalization?
*   Do other LRMs exhibit similar reasoning pattern spectra, and does teacher effectiveness correlate with these patterns?

## Limitations
*   Access to raw teacher trajectories is restricted; independent verification may require expensive generation.
*   Behavioral annotation relies on an LLM prompt without human validation, risking label noise.
*   Branching proxies (keyword frequency, step-count difference) are indirect and might miss non-keyword branching.
*   Evaluation is limited to mathematical reasoning; transfer to other domains is unknown.

## Confidence
*   **Token-level dilution mechanism:** Medium. Evidence shows a long-tail loss distribution, but the causal link to under-optimized key tokens driving generalization remains inferential.
*   **Behavioral pattern transfer:** High. Direct comparison of teacher and student transition matrices confirms inheritance of the divergent `Propose` pattern.
*   **Filtering effectiveness:** High. Consistent improvements across multiple models and five benchmarks, using two independent filtering criteria.

## Next Checks
1.  **Token loss vs. key reasoning correlation:** Annotate tokens that represent logical transitions (e.g., "therefore", "however") and test if their average loss strongly predicts downstream benchmark performance.
2.  **Human-annotation validation:** Randomly sample 100 trajectories, manually label `Propose` vs. `Deduce` steps, and evaluate agreement with the keyword-based proxy to quantify misclassification rate.
3.  **Cross-domain generalization:** Apply the same filtering pipeline to a non-math dataset (e.g., commonsense QA) and assess whether performance gains persist.
