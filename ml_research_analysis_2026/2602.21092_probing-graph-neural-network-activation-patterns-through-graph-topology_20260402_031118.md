# Probing Graph Neural Network Activation Patterns Through Graph Topology

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21092  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21092  
- **Reference count:** 18  
- **Primary result:** Graph Transformer attention does not generally align with topological bottlenecks (negative Balanced Forman curvature) except on long-range benchmarks where it induces a curvature collapse and becomes pathologically dependent on the resulting edges.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how Graph Transformer attention mechanisms interact with graph topology, using Balanced Forman curvature (BFc) to identify topological bottlenecks and Massive Activations (MAs) as a probe for extreme attention values. The authors test whether learned attention preferentially targets negative‑curvature edges. On synthetic barbell graphs and molecular benchmarks (ZINC, Tox21), MAs do not consistently enrich for negative BFc edges, indicating a disconnect between geometric theory and learned attention. In contrast, on the Long Range Graph Benchmark (peptides‑func, peptides‑struct), global attention causes a systemic “Curvature Collapse”: the effective graph geometry shifts toward significantly more negative curvature, raising the fraction of negative edges from ~57% to ~84%. Causal pruning confirms the model’s reliance on these exacerbated bottlenecks; removing them leads to large performance drops (e.g., validation loss increase of +0.1124 on peptides‑func). This reveals a pathological dependency where global attention fails to create shortcuts and instead reinforces inefficient, locally‑biased pathways.

## Method Summary
The study trains Graph Transformers (e.g., GraphGPS, GraphIT, SAN) that combine local MPNN layers with global self‑attention. For each validation graph, Balanced Forman curvature is computed for every edge based on triangles and 4‑cycles; negative values indicate bottlenecks. Attention weights are extracted per layer and head, then normalized by the median activation within each layer to obtain activation ratios. Edges whose maximum normalized activation across layers/heads exceeds the 95th percentile are flagged as Massive Activations (MAs). Alignment is assessed via the enrichment ratio \(E(c)\), comparing the probability that an MA has curvature \(c\) to the overall probability of \(c\). Curvature collapse is the difference between the static fraction of negative edges and the attention‑weighted fraction (sum of MA ratios on negative edges divided by total sum). Causal pruning removes three edge subsets: (A) MAs with negative BFc, (B) MAs with non‑negative BFc, and (C) non‑MAs with negative BFc; the resulting validation loss increase is measured. Experiments use synthetic barbell graphs, molecular datasets (ZINC, Tox21), and LRGB (peptides‑func, peptides‑struct).

## Key Results
- **Misalignment on standard benchmarks:** On barbell and molecular datasets, MAs do not show systematic enrichment for negative BFc edges; enrichment ratios hover around 1, indicating no preferential attention to bottlenecks.
- **Curvature collapse on LRGB:** Global attention amplifies negative curvature; the attention‑weighted fraction of negative edges rises from ~57% (static) to ~84% (weighted by MAs).
- **Pruning confirms dependency:** Removing MA edges with negative BFc causes a large validation loss increase (e.g., +0.1124 on peptides‑func), while pruning other edge sets yields smaller effects, demonstrating a strong reliance on the exacerbated bottlenecks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper’s hypothesis is that attention should focus on topological bottlenecks because they facilitate information flow. However, the results show that on most datasets, learned attention is driven by other factors such as node features or task objectives rather than pure curvature. On LRGB, the collapse suggests that softmax global attention, when applied to graphs with long-range dependencies, tends to reinforce existing narrow pathways. The competitive normalization of softmax may limit the diversity of connections, effectively “collapsing” the effective graph geometry toward more negatively curved edges. Causal pruning then reveals that the model becomes dependent on these edges, making the collapse not a feature but a maladaptive behavior that hampers the creation of efficient shortcuts.

## Foundational Learning
- **Graph Transformer**  
  *Why needed:* Combines local message‑passing (to capture immediate structure) with global self‑attention (to capture long‑range dependencies).  
  *Quick check:* Does the architecture contain both MPNN layers and a global attention module? Are residual connections and layer norms present?

- **Self‑attention (softmax)**  
  *Why needed:* Allows each node to attend to every other node; the softmax normalization induces competition that may affect how attention distributes over edges.  
  *Quick check:* Are attention weights computed as softmax of pairwise affinities? Is attention applied globally (no masking by distance)?

- **Balanced Forman curvature (BFc)**  
  *Why needed:* A discrete curvature measure that identifies topological bottlenecks; negative BFc signals edges that connect densely and sparsely connected regions, hypothesized to be important for information flow.  
  *Quick check:* For an edge \(e={u,v}\), BFc ≈ \(2 - \frac{\text{triangles through } e}{\sqrt{d_u d_v}} - \frac{\text{4‑cycles through } e}{2}\) (exact formula varies). Can you compute triangles via \(A^3\) and 4‑cycles via \(\text{tr}(A^4)/8\)?

- **Massive Activation (MA)**  
  *Why needed:* A data‑driven probe to locate edges that receive unusually high attention, indicating where the model focuses.  
  *Quick check:* For every edge, compute the maximum median‑normalized activation ratio across layers/heads; flag edges above the 95th percentile.

- **Curvature Collapse**  
  *Why needed:* The observed phenomenon where global attention skews the effective graph geometry toward higher negative curvature, indicating reinforcement of bottlenecks.  
  *Quick check:* Compare the fraction of static edges with negative BFc to the fraction of total attention mass (sum of MA ratios) on negative edges; a significant increase indicates collapse.

- **Causal Pruning**  
  *Why needed:* Determines whether the model truly relies on specific edge subsets by removing them and measuring performance change.  
  *Quick check:* Zero out selected adjacency entries, run the trained model on the modified graph, and record the validation loss increase relative to the unpruned baseline.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Features -> Embedding -> (Local MPNN + Global Attention) × L -> Readout -> Prediction  

**Critical path:** Node features are first projected into a hidden space. Each of the L layers then applies a local MPNN (e.g., GINE) to incorporate neighborhood information, followed by a global self‑attention block (e.g., TransformerEncoder layer) that mixes information across the entire graph. Residual connections and layer normalisation stabilize training. After the final layer, a readout (sum/mean pooling) produces a graph‑level representation, which passes through an MLP head for the task.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Depth vs. over‑smoothing:* Deeper local MPNN stacks can cause node embeddings to become indistinguishable; combining with global attention mitigates this.  
- *Local vs. global:* Pure global attention has quadratic complexity; inserting local MPNN reduces computational cost and biases the model toward local structure, but may hide long‑range signals.  
- *Attention heads:* More heads capture diverse interaction patterns but increase parameters and risk overfitting on small datasets.  
- *Normalization:* The median activation scaling used to define MAs is a design choice that affects the sensitivity of the probe.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Training divergence:* Loss becomes NaN or plateaus – check learning rate, gradient clipping, correct masking, and data loading.  
- *No curvature collapse:* Could indicate insufficient global attention, mis‑computed BFc, or inappropriate MA percentile; verify each step.  
- *Pruning has no effect:* The model might not use the adjacency matrix (e.g., if attention is purely pairwise without structural bias) or edges may have been removed after positional encodings were baked in.  
- *Baseline performance below chance:* Architecture or hyperparameters may be unsuitable for the dataset.

**First experiments:**  
1. Train a baseline Graph Transformer on LRGB with standard settings (≈5 layers, 64 hidden dim, 8 heads, Adam lr=1e‑3, warm‑up) and confirm validation ROC‑AUC >0.6 on peptides‑func and reasonable MSE on peptides‑struct.  
2. Compute curvature collapse: for the validation set, obtain BFc for all edges, identify MAs (top 5% median‑normalized activation), and compare static vs. attention‑weighted negative‑edge fractions.  
3. Perform causal pruning: for each validation graph, remove edges that are (MA ∧ negative BFc), re‑run the model, and average the validation loss increase across the set to verify dependence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Attention mechanism universality:** Is the curvature collapse phenomenon universal across different attention mechanisms (e.g., linear or sparse attention) or specific to the softmax‑based formulation?  
- **Determinants of Massive Activations:** What factors beyond pure graph topology influence where Massive Activations appear? Node features, task objectives, and training dynamics may all play a role.  
- **Mitigation via regularization:** Can curvature‑aware regularization (e.g., penalizing negative curvature in the attention‑induced graph) mitigate curvature collapse and improve long‑range dependency modeling?

## Limitations
- **Missing hyperparameter details:** Exact training settings for LRGB (number of layers, hidden width, attention heads, learning rate schedule, early stopping) are not reported, which may affect attention patterns and collapse magnitude.  
- **Metric ambiguity:** The paper does not specify how activation ratios are aggregated across layers/heads (sum vs. max), the precise polarity threshold for BFc (strictly <0 vs ≤0), or whether continuous weights or binary MA masks are used for weighted curvature calculations.  
- **Architectural uncertainty:** It is unclear which Graph Transformer variant (SAN, GraphIT, etc.) produced the LRGB results and whether the adjacency matrix is incorporated as an attention bias; this matters for interpreting causal pruning outcomes.

## Confidence
- MAs do not consistently target negative‑curvature edges on synthetic and molecular benchmarks: **Medium** confidence.  
- Curvature collapse phenomenon on LRGB datasets: **Low** confidence.  
- Model dependence on exacerbated bottleneck edges (pruning effect): **Low** confidence.

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter sweep:** Train a standard Graph Transformer (e.g., GraphGPS) on LRGB with varied depth, width, heads, and learning rate schedules; recompute curvature collapse and compare to reported values.  
2. **Adjacency bias inspection:** Examine the model code to determine whether the adjacency matrix is used as an attention bias; repeat pruning experiments with the bias removed to test the causality of edge removals.  
3. **Robustness analysis:** Vary the MA percentile threshold (90th, 95th, 99th), BFc sign threshold (<0 vs ≤0), and attention aggregation method (sum vs. max across layers/heads) to assess whether curvature collapse persists under different definitions.
