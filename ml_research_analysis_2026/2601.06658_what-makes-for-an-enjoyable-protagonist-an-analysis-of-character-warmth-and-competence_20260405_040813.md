# What makes for an enjoyable protagonist? An analysis of character warmth and competence

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.06658
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06658
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Warmth and competence each positively predict IMDb ratings (β ≈ 0.04-0.06), while male protagonists have a larger positive effect (β = 0.36).

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether the warmth and competence of movie protagonists influence audience ratings, drawing on psychological theories like the Stereotype Content Model. Analyzing 2,858 films from the Movie Scripts Corpus, the authors used AI (gpt-4.1-mini) to identify protagonists and annotate their traits, then applied Bayesian regressions to predict IMDb ratings. Results show small but positive associations for both warmth and competence, while protagonist gender (male) exhibits a substantially stronger effect. Genre interactions were not meaningfully present. The findings suggest that while character personality traits matter, structural factors like gender may play a larger role in audience evaluations.

## Method Summary
The study utilized the Movie Scripts Corpus, comprising 2,858 films with available scripts and IMDb ratings. Protagonists were identified using gpt-4.1-mini with a custom prompt, as the heuristic of "most lines" was often inaccurate. Trait annotations for warmth and competence were generated via the LLM_annotate package, which parses scripts to extract actions and statements from the identified protagonist and rates them on six-point scales. Human validation on a random sample of 100 films showed strong agreement with LLM scores (r = .83). Bayesian linear regressions (using brms in R) were fitted with standardized warmth, competence, and genre memberships (limited to ≤3 genres per film to ensure convergence) as predictors of standardized IMDb ratings. Models were compared using Bayes Factors, and gender was included as a control variable.

## Key Results
- A one standard deviation increase in protagonist warmth predicts a 0.042-point higher IMDb rating (β = 0.04, 95% CI [0, 0.09]).
- A one standard deviation increase in competence predicts a 0.055-point increase (β = 0.06, 95% CI [0.01, 0.10]).
- Movies with male protagonists receive higher ratings (β = 0.36, 95% CI [0.26, 0.47]), an effect several times larger than warmth or competence.
- Genre interactions with warmth or competence do not meaningfully improve model fit (Bayes Factors < 1), indicating these effects are robust across genres.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Warmer protagonists lead to higher IMDb ratings because warmth facilitates audience social bonding and positive affect toward the character.
- **Mechanism:** Perceiving a character as warm (good-natured, trustworthy) triggers innate social approach motivations, making the viewing experience feel companionable and satisfying, especially for narratives with positive outcomes.
- **Core assumption:** Audience enjoyment is proximally driven by affective responses to characters, and warmth is a primary driver of such positive affect.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Drawing on psychological and literary theory... Warmth Hypothesis: Movies with warmer protagonists receive more favorable audience evaluations."
  - [section] Page 3: "Warmth and agreeableness are crucial traits for social bonding... People are more likely to approach someone—physically and emotionally—if they appear agreeable, warm, and pro-social, which could lay the foundation for enjoying a story about this person."
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; corpus neighbors discuss warmth in avatars/books but not movie protagonist rating effects. Assumption: mechanism generalizes from interpersonal psychology to media characters.
- **Break condition:** If the warmth effect vanishes when controlling for other character dimensions (e.g., moral complexity, arc) or genre-specific norms, the bonding mechanism may be indirect or confounded.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** More competent protagonists lead to higher IMDb ratings because competence enables wishful identification and feelings of vicarious empowerment.
- **Mechanism:** Observing a character's skill, intelligence, or success allows audiences to imagine themselves possessing those attributes, generating pleasurable feelings of agency and control.
- **Core assumption:** audiences seek not just social connection but also vicarious mastery from entertainment, and competence directly fuels this.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Competence Hypothesis: Movies with more competent protagonists receive more favorable audience evaluations."
  - [section] Page 4: "Rather than liking competent characters, audiences might admire them for their success, intelligence, or other desirable attributes, and engage in wishful identification. Inhabiting a super competent protagonist allows observers to feel powerful, making for a pleasurable viewing experience."
  - [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., "Who Is the Story About?") focus on entity recognition, not rating outcomes. Limited corpus support.
- **Break condition:** If the competence effect is entirely mediated by warmth (e.g., competence only helps if it makes characters seem warmer), the primary driver is still social evaluation, not vicarious mastery.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The stronger association of protagonist gender with ratings (male > female) reflects systemic industry biases or audience preferences that overwhelm personality effects.
- **Mechanism:** Gender may proxy for a bundle of co-occurring factors (budget, marketing, genre conventions, societal biases) that more directly influence audience reach and pre-release expectations.
- **Core assumption:** The gender coefficient captures confounding structural factors rather than an intrinsic preference for male characters per se.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Male protagonists were slightly less warm... movies with male leads received higher ratings on average (an association that was multiple times stronger than the relationships between movie ratings and warmth/competence)."
  - [section] Page 9: "Having a male (as opposed to female) protagonist had a positive association with IMDb ratings (β = 0.36)."
  - [section] Page 10: Discussion notes gender effect includes "sizeable confounding factors like systematically smaller budgets for women-led movies."
  - [corpus] Neighbor "Explaining the Reputational Risks of AI-Mediated Communication" touches on perceived character, not gender ratings gaps. No direct corpus anchor.
- **Break condition:** If gender effects disappear within subgenres or after rigorously controlling for production budget, director gender, and marketing spend, the mechanism may be more about character portrayal than structural bias.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Stereotype Content Model (SCM)
- **Why needed here:** The study's core traits (warmth, competence) are directly from SCM. Understanding SCM's evolutionary and social functional bases (e.g., threat vs. competition) explains why these two dimensions are theorized to be primary.
- **Quick check question:** According to SCM, how would you predict perceptions of a protagonist who is high in competence but low in warmth (e.g., a skilled but arrogant detective)?

### Concept: Bayesian Regression Interpretation
- **Why needed here:** The paper uses Bayesian models with Bayes Factors. Interpreting "small but positive" effects, and comparing model fits (BF > 10^7 vs. BF < 0.1) is central to the conclusions.
- **Quick check question:** What does a Bayes Factor of 2.2 × 10^7 for the warmth model, compared to an intercept-only model, tell you about the evidence for the Warmth Hypothesis?

### Concept: LLM-Based Annotation Reliability
- **Why needed here:** The entire analysis depends on AI-generated warmth/competence scores. Knowing that human-LLM agreement was r = .83 (Page 8) and the limitations (e.g., no "end-of-movie reinterpretation") is crucial for assessing construct validity.
- **Quick check question:** If the human-LLM correlation were only r = .50 instead of .83, how would that change your confidence in the reported β coefficients?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Movie Scripts Corpus → Protagonist Identification (gpt-4.1-mini) → Trait Annotation (LLM_annotate) → Human Validation → Analysis (brms)

**Critical path:** raw script → Protagonist ID → Trait Annotation → aggregated score → regression model → coefficients & BF.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Cost vs. Accuracy: Used `gpt-4.1-mini` instead of more capable models to manage cost for ~2.8k films, accepting minor accuracy loss.
- Genre Complexity: Initial model with all genre memberships didn't converge. Tradeoff: restrict each movie to ≤3 genres (random selection), losing some genre purity for model stability.
- Static vs. Dynamic Traits: Annotation averages all actions. Tradeoff: loses character arc information for feasibility.

**Failure signatures:**
- Protagonist ID failure: "James Bond" not identified. Symptom: top character by lines is not the actual lead. Fix: manual spot-checks; consider fine-tuning a custom NER model.
- Model non-convergence: R̂ > 1.01, divergent transitions. Symptom: too many interaction terms or complex random effects. Fix: simplify model (e.g., reduce genres), increase iterations, re-parameterize.
- Annotation drift: LLM ratings systematically deviate from human review (e.g., sarcasm misclassified). Symptom: human-LLM correlation drops in validation sample. Fix: refine trait definitions/examples in `LLM_annotate` prompt.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Replication Core: Run the exact preregistered main-effect models for warmth and competence. Confirm β ≈ 0.04-0.06 and BF >> 10^7. Check trace plots for convergence.
2. Genre Interaction Stress Test: Re-test genre hypotheses using the restricted 3-genre dataset. Confirm BF < 1 (e.g., 0.058, 0.125), indicating no meaningful variation. Try an alternative: analyze top 3 genres separately with simpler models.
3. Gender Effect Decomposition: Replicate the gender coefficient (β ≈ 0.36). Then, add a control for `log(budget)` (if scrapable) or `number of votes` as a proxy for popularity. Assess if gender effect attenuates, probing the "confounding factors" claim.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do dynamic changes in a protagonist's warmth and competence over the course of a film influence audience ratings?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note that protagonists can appeal through trait changes, which were averaged out in their analysis and "likely warrant further exploration."
- **Why unresolved:** The study used a single average warmth/competence score per film, ignoring potential non-linear or temporal effects of character arcs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal annotation of trait scores across narrative beats (e.g., beginning, midpoint, climax) and modeling their trajectories as predictors of ratings, possibly supplemented by experimental manipulation of arc types.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Do amateur or low-budget films show stronger or more genre-specific effects of protagonist warmth and competence on ratings compared to mainstream films?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors suggest rerunning analyses for amateur productions, which might violate genre expectations more, leading to "larger and more genre-dependent effects."
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis used a corpus of mainstream films/series; production scale and its interaction with genre conformity were not examined.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Collect a comparable dataset of amateur/indie films, annotate protagonist traits, and test whether the warmth/competence × genre interactions are stronger in that subset.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent can LLM-based trait annotations reliably capture nuanced character dynamics (e.g., redemption arcs, unreliable narration) that human raters interpret differently?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors acknowledge that AI annotations "occasionally fell short" of human ability to reinterpret behaviors after late-revealed information.
- **Why unresolved:** The study assessed overall LLM-human agreement (r = .83) but did not systematically compare performance on films with complex narrative structures.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A targeted study where both LLMs and human experts annotate characters from films with/without plot twists, measuring discrepancies in trait ratings and their correlation with audience evaluations.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How much of the observed advantage for male-led films in IMDb ratings is explained by confounding factors like production budget, marketing, or systemic industry biases?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors report a large positive effect for male protagonists (β = 0.36) and note this includes "sizeable confounding factors like systematically smaller budgets for women-led movies."
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis included gender as a predictor without adjusting for budgetary or distributional covariates that correlate with gender.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-analyze the existing dataset with added variables on film budget, studio size, and marketing spend; or collect new data with these controls to test if the gender effect attenuates.

## Limitations
- Annotation validity: The AI-generated warmth/competence scores may not capture narrative context, sarcasm, or character arcs, threatening construct validity despite high human-LLM agreement.
- Protagonist identification: The LLM-based identification process is a black box with limited validation (only n=30), risking errors in ensemble casts or misidentified leads.
- Confounding in gender effect: The strong positive coefficient for male protagonists likely reflects structural biases (budget, marketing) but these were not controlled, leaving the causal interpretation uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Warmth effect:** Small positive association with ratings (Confidence: Medium).
- **Competence effect:** Small positive association with ratings (Confidence: Medium).
- **Gender effect:** Stronger association for male protagonists (Confidence: Medium-High).
- **Genre moderation:** No meaningful moderation (Confidence: Low-Medium).
