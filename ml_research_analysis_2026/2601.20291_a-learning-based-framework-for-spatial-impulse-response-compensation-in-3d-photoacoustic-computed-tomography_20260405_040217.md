# A Learning-based Framework for Spatial Impulse Response Compensation in 3D Photoacoustic Computed Tomography

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.20291
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20291
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A learned data-domain framework compensates for spatial impulse response (SIR) degradation in 3D photoacoustic computed tomography, enabling high-resolution reconstructions with fast analytic methods.

## Executive Summary
Photoacoustic computed tomography (PACT) suffers from spatial resolution degradation when using large-area ultrasound transducers due to uncorrected spatial impulse responses (SIRs). This paper proposes a learning-based framework that operates in the data domain to map distorted pressure measurements to equivalents from point-like transducers, allowing the use of fast analytic reconstruction. Two neural network architectures—a 3D U-Net and a physics-inspired Deconv-Net—are trained on analytically generated synthetic data from stochastic sphere objects. Virtual studies with numerical breast phantoms and in vivo data demonstrate significant improvements in resolution (FWHM restored to ~0.5 mm) and image quality metrics, with Deconv-Net consistently outperforming U-Net. The approach shows robustness to noise, object complexity, and sound speed heterogeneity.

## Method Summary
The framework addresses the SIR problem by learning an inverse mapping from raw pressure data (corrupted by finite-aperture transducers) to ideal point-source data. Training uses a continuous-to-discrete analytic forward model that efficiently generates pressure signals from stochastic sphere objects, avoiding costly discretized simulations. Two models are proposed: a full-array 3D U-Net that directly transforms the entire data tensor, and a Deconv-Net that processes patches via learned Wiener deconvolution with multiple spatially localized kernels, followed by a synthesis U-Net. After compensation, the data is reconstructed using Universal Backprojection (UBP), a fast analytic algorithm assuming point-like detectors. This separation enables high-quality reconstructions without iterative optimization.

## Key Results
- Both models significantly improved spatial resolution (FWHM restored to ~0.5 mm) and image quality metrics (RSE, NCC, DICE) compared to uncompensated reconstructions.
- Deconv-Net consistently outperformed U-Net across all test phantoms.
- The framework demonstrated robustness to noise, object complexity, and sound speed heterogeneity, with in vivo breast data revealing fine vascular structures previously obscured.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Operating in the data (pressure) domain, rather than the image domain, for SIR compensation prevents the introduction of anatomically implausible structures during reconstruction.
- **Mechanism:** The network transforms distorted raw pressure time traces (`P_rect`) into compensated traces (`P_comp`). These are then fed into a standard, physics-based reconstruction algorithm (UBP). Errors made by the network in the data domain propagate through a physically accurate forward model, reducing the risk of generating false structural details in the final image.
- **Core assumption:** The forward model (UBP) is sufficiently accurate such that errors in the input data primarily cause localized blurring or noise, not the creation of coherent, anatomically incorrect features.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "...learning in the data domain reduces the risk of introducing false structures that are visually plausible but physically inaccurate." [section VII]: "...errors introduced in the data domain are less likely to propagate into plausible but incorrect structures after reconstruction." [corpus]: Context from neighboring papers supports domain-specific learned restoration, but direct evidence is provided by the authors' rationale and validation results.
- **Break condition:** If the subsequent reconstruction algorithm is itself highly non-linear or contains heuristics that amplify input errors, data-domain learning may not prevent artifact generation.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Training on analytically generated "Stochastic Spheres" data provides sufficient diversity and signal-level realism to enable the learned model to generalize to complex, anatomically realistic objects and *in vivo* data.
- **Mechanism:** The training objects are composed of 200 randomly placed, sized, and valued uniform spheres. This simple basis set, when convolved with the analytic C-D imaging model, produces a vast distribution of pressure waveforms that captures the superpositional nature of the photoacoustic signal from heterogeneous tissues. The statistical properties (e.g., range of amplitudes, spatial frequency content) of these synthetic objects are designed to match expected biological signal ranges.
- **Core assumption:** The key information for learning the SIR mapping lies in the statistical distribution of signal waveforms and their relation to source positions, not in high-fidelity anatomical structure. A simple parametric model can cover the relevant manifold of measurement data corrupted by SIR.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section III-A]: "The superposition of stochastic spheres enables the construction of complex and heterogeneous objects... the spatial distribution and pressure amplitudes... were intentionally designed to fall within the typical range observed in actual breast imaging data." [section V, Study 2 & 3]: Models trained on Stochastic Spheres generalized successfully to NBP and *in vivo* data, showing significant improvements in RSE, NCC, and DICE. [corpus]: Weak direct comparison; related work uses synthetic datasets but the specific claim of "signal-level" alignment needing structural simplicity is an inference from the paper's discussion.
- **Break condition:** If the real-world data contains specific structural patterns (e.g., highly periodic vasculature) or contrast mechanisms not represented in the sphere statistics, generalization may fail.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The physics-inspired Deconv-Net architecture, by incorporating a learned deconvolution step constrained by the known parametric form of the SIR, achieves better performance and robustness than a purely data-driven U-Net.
- **Mechanism:** Deconv-Net explicitly factors the SIR compensation problem into (1) a set of `K` learned, spatially-localized SIR kernels followed by deconvolution, and (2) an adaptive synthesis step that weights these locally compensated signals. This structure embeds the prior knowledge that the degradation is a non-stationary convolution. The learnable parameters (`r_local_k`, `λ_k`) are a small set representing plausible SIR variations, constraining the solution space.
- **Core assumption:** The spatial variation of the SIR can be effectively modeled by a discrete set of representative kernels corresponding to a sparse sampling of source-detector relative positions.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section III-C]: "Deconv-Net integrates learnable deconvolution filters that embed the physical SIR model... the SIR is dependent on the relative position... this process can be computationally undone by solving the corresponding inverse problem." [section V, Tables I & II]: Deconv-Net consistently and significantly outperformed U-Net across all metrics (RSE, NCC, DICE) and both homogeneous (NBP-H) and heterogeneous (NBP-Het) test sets. [corpus]: Corpus indicates a trend for "physics-informed" models to outperform pure data-driven ones in inverse problems, supporting the architectural rationale.
- **Break condition:** If the true SIR varies in ways not captured by the parameterized kernel family (e.g., beyond far-field approximation, unusual transducer shaping), the deconvolution step may become a poor model, hurting performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Continuous-to-Discrete (C-D) Imaging Model with SIR**
  - **Why needed here:** It mathematically defines how finite-aperture transducers blur the ideal pressure measurements via a spatially variant convolution with the SIR. This is the inverse problem the learning framework aims to solve approximately in the data domain.
  - **Quick check question:** How does equation (4) represent the SIR effect as a temporal convolution that varies with source location `r` relative to the transducer?
- **Concept: Universal Backprojection (UBP) as a FBP analog**
  - **Why needed here:** UBP is the fast, analytic reconstruction method assumed for the compensated data. Understanding its simplicity and speed (vs. iterative methods) highlights the computational advantage of the proposed two-step approach (learned compensation + fast UBP).
  - **Quick check question:** What key assumption about transducers does UBP make that is violated by large-area detectors?
- **Concept: Supervised learning with synthetic paired data**
  - **Why needed here:** The core paradigm requires input-target pairs (`P_rect`, `P_ideal`). Since real point-like transducer data is unavailable, this concept covers the necessity and design of an analytical forward model for synthetic data generation.
  - **Quick check question:** Why is using a discrete-voxel-based forward model (D-D) for training data generation considered computationally prohibitive compared to the sphere-based analytic approach?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Analytic Data Generator -> SIR Compensation Model (U-Net or Deconv-Net) -> (Sliding-Window Inference for Deconv-Net) -> Reconstruction Engine (UBP) -> Reconstructed Image
- **Critical path:** Trained Compensation Model → Compensated Pressure Data → UBP → Reconstructed Image
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **U-Net vs. Deconv-Net:** U-Net is simpler (full-array, no explicit physics) but may require more data/parameters. Deconv-Net is more parameter-efficient (local kernels) and physically motivated but requires patch-based processing and more complex training.
  - **Patch size in Deconv-Net:** Smaller patches reduce GPU memory but may miss larger-scale context; larger patches may exceed memory. `K` kernels trade spatial resolution of SIR modeling for memory.
  - **Training data realism:** Stochastic spheres are fast but simple. Increasingly complex phantoms (NBPs) improve realism but require expensive D-D forward models, defeating the efficiency goal.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Model failure:** High MAE on validation set; no visual improvement in simple resolution tests; artifacts in reconstructed images that look like "checkerboarding" or over-smoothing.
  - **Generalization failure:** Good performance on Stochastic Spheres test but poor on NBP or *in vivo* data (high RSE, low NCC/DICE). Indicates training data distribution mismatch.
  - **Deconv-Net specific:** Stitching artifacts from sliding-window inference if patches are too small or overlap insufficient; poor performance if learned kernels do not approximate true SIR shapes.
  - **Incorrect FWHM measurement:** Fitting a Gaussian-blurred rectangular model requires careful initialization and constraint (positive width). *Diagnostic:* Visually inspect fitted profiles against data.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Reproduce Resolution Test (Study 1):** Use the Deterministic Spheres dataset. Verify that both networks restore FWHM to ~0.5 mm across sphere positions and under noise/SOS variations (Fig. 3).
  2. **Generalize to Anatomical Complexity (Study 2):** Apply trained models to NBP-Homogeneous dataset. Confirm significant improvement (p<0.05) in RSE, NCC, and DICE compared to uncompensated baseline (Table I). Visually inspect for recovered vessel structures in surface regions (Fig. 4a-d).
  3. **Test Robustness to Heterogeneity (Study 3):** Apply to NBP-Heterogeneous dataset. Confirm performance gains persist despite unseen SOS variations (Table II). Ensure Deconv-Net's advantage over U-Net holds (p<0.05).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How does the robustness of the proposed SIR compensation models vary across a wider and more extreme range of physical transducer sizes (e.g., significantly larger than the emulated 5.5 mm × 1.1 mm), and what are the practical limits of the framework for very large apertures?  
  *Basis:* The authors explicitly state in the Conclusion that as transducer sizes increase, SIR-induced information loss becomes more pronounced, highlighting the need for further investigation. They also note that further experimental validation using systems equipped with physically large transducers is needed.  
  *Why unresolved:* The current validation used data emulated by binning from a small-pixel transducer array, not from a physically manufactured large transducer. Theoretical studies used a fixed, moderate transducer size; performance on significantly larger transducers has not been tested.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic virtual or experimental studies with transducer sizes ranging from very small to very large (e.g., 1 mm² to >50 mm² apertures) would quantify performance degradation. Metrics would include resolution restoration, artifact suppression, and sensitivity to noise as a function of aperture area. Physical experiments using custom-built large-element arrays would provide definitive validation.
- **Open Question 2:** What is the fundamental trade-off between improved sensitivity from larger transducer apertures and the concomitant increase in SIR-induced blurring that remains even after learned compensation, and how does this trade-off affect optimal imaging depth?  
  *Basis:* The authors call for future work to examine the trade-offs between transducer size, resolution, and imaging depth in the context of SIR compensation. The Introduction frames larger transducers as improving sensitivity but degrading resolution via SIR.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper demonstrates compensation for a fixed system configuration but does not perform a systematic parametric study varying transducer size and measuring the ultimate limit on spatial resolution and SNR at different depths after compensation. The interaction between aperture size, SIR correction accuracy, and depth-dependent light fluence is not quantified.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Virtual imaging studies where transducer aperture dimensions are systematically varied would measure the point-spread function (PSF) and CNR as a function of depth in a standardized phantom. This would reveal if compensation efficacy plateaus or degrades with extreme aperture sizes and identify an optimal size range for a given depth target.
- **Open Question 3:** To what extent does the choice of training data distribution (stochastic uniform spheres) limit the generalization performance of the models to object structures with highly complex, spatially correlated textures or specific pathological features not represented in the spheres?  
  *Basis:* While the authors argue that signal-level alignment enabled generalization despite simple sphere-based objects, they do not test objects with extreme structural statistics (e.g., dense microcalcifications, highly fractal tumor vasculature). The Conclusion mentions that the spatial distribution and pressure amplitudes were intentionally designed to fall within the typical range observed in actual breast imaging data, but this is a heuristic assumption about coverage.  
  *Why unresolved:* The Stochastic Spheres dataset uses a specific generative model (random uniform spheres with log-normal radii). It is an open question whether this distribution adequately covers the manifold of realistic photoacoustic initial pressure maps. Performance on objects with very high spatial frequency content, anisotropic textures, or rare high-amplitude point sources could reveal limitations.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Curated test sets of objects with known, challenging features (e.g., sub-voxel point sources, dense wire networks, highly clustered absorbers) would be used to probe failure modes. Ablation studies could train models on alternative synthetic datasets (e.g., based on real vessel segmentations or generative adversarial networks) and compare performance on these extreme cases.
- **Open Question 4:** How physically interpretable are the learned SIR kernel parameters (locations and Wiener filter regularization) in Deconv-Net, and do they correspond to meaningful spatial sampling of the true, position-dependent SIR?  
  *Basis:* Deconv-Net introduces `K` learnable kernel parameters (`r_local_k`, `λ_k`) intended to approximate the spatially variant SIR. The paper presents Deconv-Net's superior performance but does not analyze whether the learned kernel parameters and their associated weights correlate with the known analytic SIR model or with geometric quantities like source-to-detector distance or angle.  
  *Why unresolved:* The network is a black-box mapping, even with a physics-inspired structure. The learned `r_local_k` values are not constrained to be on the object support or to follow the expected SIR variation pattern. The adaptive weights could be overfitting to training artifacts rather than correctly estimating local SIR.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Visualizing and quantifying the learned `r_local_k` parameters and the weight tensor `W` across various input data would show if they partition the measurement space in a physically meaningful way (e.g., smooth variation with view angle and distance). Comparing the effective deconvolution kernels to analytically computed SIRs at corresponding positions would test fidelity. Sensitivity analysis perturbing `r_local_k` could show stability.
- **Open Question 5:** How sensitive is the framework's performance to violations of the homogeneous speed-of-sound (SOS) assumption used in the analytic training data generation (C-D model), and can the learned compensation inadvertently absorb or amplify errors from SOS mismatch?  
  *Basis:* Training data used a homogeneous SOS of 1.5 mm/µs. The framework showed robustness in the NBP-Heterogeneous test set, which used a two-region SOS model for reconstruction but still generated training data with homogeneous SOS. This suggests some tolerance, but the mechanism is unclear. The paper does not analyze if the learned operator is implicitly learning an SOS correction or if its performance degrades with more severe, spatially continuous SOS variations.  
  *Why unresolved:* The NBP-Heterogeneous dataset used piecewise-constant SOS (breast/water). The impact of continuous, high-contrast SOS heterogeneities (e.g., near bone or cysts with SOS >1.6 mm/µs) on the SIR correction operator is unknown. The model may compensate for SOS errors in an unpredictable way, leading to new artifacts.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Virtual studies with increasingly complex SOS maps (e.g., from real patient CT scans) applied to the wave propagation simulation would test limits. Diagnostics could involve comparing the compensated data to the true point-like data (not just reconstructed images) to isolate SIR correction errors from SOS-induced wavefront distortion errors. Training on data with synthesized SOS variations could be compared to the current homogeneous training.

## Limitations
- Training relies on synthetic stochastic sphere data; its fidelity to diverse real tissues is uncertain.
- The analytic forward model may not capture all real-world acoustic effects (e.g., reflections, heterogeneity).
- In vivo validation is limited to a single breast dataset; generalizability to other organs or scanners is not demonstrated.
- Deconv‑Net's sliding‑window inference may cause stitching artifacts; overlap strategy is not fully specified.

## Confidence
- **Data-domain learning reduces false structure risk:** Low (rationale only; no direct comparison to image-domain baselines).
- **Stochastic sphere training generalizes to real data:** Medium (supported by NBP and single in vivo dataset, but diversity limited).
- **Deconv-Net outperforms U-Net:** High (consistent significant gains across multiple metrics and phantoms).
- **Robustness to noise and SOS heterogeneity:** High (verified in virtual studies with controlled perturbations).

## Next Checks
1. Conduct a controlled experiment comparing data-domain vs. image-domain compensation using identical network architectures and training data; measure artifact incidence in reconstructions.
2. Test the framework on at least two additional in vivo datasets from different PACT systems (e.g., brain, musculoskeletal) to establish broader generalizability.
3. Perform an ablation of Deconv-Net's physics-informed components—remove or randomize the learned kernels and quantify performance drop relative to the full model.
