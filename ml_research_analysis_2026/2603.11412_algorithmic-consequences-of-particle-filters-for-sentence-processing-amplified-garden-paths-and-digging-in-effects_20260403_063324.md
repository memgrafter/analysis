# Algorithmic Consequences of Particle Filters for Sentence Processing: Amplified Garden-Paths and Digging-In Effects

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11412
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11412
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** Particle filter resampling inherently causes digging-in effects (difficulty increases with ambiguous region length) and limited parallelism amplifies garden-path effects compared to fully parallel processing.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates why large language model (LLM) surprisal underpredicts human processing difficulty for syntactic violations, hypothesizing that LLMs lack explicit representation of structural ambiguity. It analyzes particle filter models as a cognitively plausible alternative with limited parallelism. Theoretical results prove that stochastic particle loss during resampling leads to real-time digging-in effects: as an ambiguous region lengthens, disambiguation difficulty increases because correct structural hypotheses are probabilistically eliminated. This effect's magnitude scales inversely with particle count. Furthermore, limited-parallelism models show amplified garden-path effects relative to fully parallel models, quantified via higher expected surprisal and KL divergence between prior and posterior structural distributions.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct a formal theoretical analysis of particle filter instantiation for sentence processing. Particles represent competing syntactic structures. At each time step (word), a proposal distribution updates particles based on local evidence, followed by resampling that stochastically duplicates or removes particles proportional to their weights. This mimics cognitive resource limits by maintaining only a finite sample of hypotheses. The analysis derives closed-form expressions for expected surprisal and KL divergence under this dynamics, comparing a fully parallel baseline (all hypotheses retained) to the limited-parallelism particle filter. No empirical data is fitted; results are mathematical proofs within the model's assumptions.

## Key Results
- Resampling induces digging-in: expected surprisal at disambiguation increases with the length of the ambiguous region due to stochastic particle loss.
- The digging-in magnitude scales inversely with the number of particles (higher limit → weaker effect).
- Limited-parallelism particle filters amplify garden-path effects versus fully parallel models, shown by increased expected surprisal and KL divergence at the disambiguation point.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
In a particle filter, resampling randomly discards lower-weight particles. During a temporarily ambiguous region, particles supporting incorrect structures may survive early resampling steps by chance. As ambiguity persists, repeated resampling raises the probability that particles representing the correct (but initially weak) structure are lost. This stochastic particle depletion means the posterior distribution at disambiguation is more skewed toward the wrong hypothesis than in a fully parallel system, increasing expected surprisal—the digging-in effect. With fewer particles (limited parallelism), particle loss is more frequent and drastic, further amplifying the garden-path difficulty. The model thus ties real-time processing difficulty directly to the stochastic instability of maintaining multiple structural alternatives under resourcing constraints.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component Map: Input Sentence -> Proposal Distribution (updates particle weights) -> Resampling (stochastic selection) -> Particle Set (represents structural hypotheses) -> Expected Surprisal/KL Divergence Computation -> Difficulty Prediction

Critical Path: For each incoming word: (1) Proposal step computes local likelihood for each particle's structure and updates weights; (2) Resampling step selects a new generation of particles based on weights, potentially discarding some hypotheses; (3) Particle set now represents the posterior distribution over structures up to that point. Over an ambiguous region, step 2 repeatedly introduces noise that can eliminate correct-but-weak particles.

Design Tradeoffs: Particle count (N): larger N reduces stochastic noise (weaker digging-in) but increases computational cost; resampling frequency: more frequent resampling amplifies particle loss but maintains weight normalization.

Failure Signatures: (1) Particle collapse: all particles converge to a single (wrong) structure early, making recovery impossible; (2) High variance: repeated runs yield diverging difficulty predictions for the same sentence; (3) Inability to model temporary ambiguity: particle set becomes deterministic before disambiguating information arrives.

First Experiments:
1. **Ambiguity Length Scaling:** Process sentences with identical disambiguation but varying lengths of ambiguous prelude. Measure expected surprisal at the disambiguating word as a function of length for fixed N. Expect monotonic increase.
2. **Particle Count Inverse Scaling:** For a fixed garden-path sentence, vary N (e.g., 5, 20, 100). Quantify digging-in magnitude (slope of surprisal vs. ambiguity length). Expect negative correlation with N.
3. **Parallelism Comparison:** Simulate a fully parallel model (all possible structures retained) and a particle filter (N=20) on classic NP/Z garden-path stimuli. Compare expected surprisal and KL divergence at the disambiguation region. Expect higher values for the particle filter.

## Limitations
- **Empirical Validation Gap:** Theoretical predictions (e.g., digging-in scaling with ambiguity length and particle count) lack direct testing against human reading time or eyetracking data.
- **LLM Comparison Speculative:** The claim that LLMs underpredict due to missing explicit ambiguity representation is inferred, not tested by manipulating structural ambiguity in LLMs.
- **Conflicting Corpus Evidence:** A邻近 corpus study finds no robust digging-in effect in human data, contradicting the paper's foundational premise.
