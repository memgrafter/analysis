# Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14080
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14080
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LLMs encode nearly all facts (95–98%) but fail to recall 25–33% directly; thinking recovers 40–65% of those, highlighting recall as the key bottleneck.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a knowledge profiling framework to separate factual errors in LLMs into missing knowledge (encoding failures) and inaccessible encoded knowledge (recall failures). Using the automatically constructed WikiProfile benchmark—2,150 Wikipedia facts evaluated across 10 task types—the authors assess 13 LLMs. They find that frontier models encode almost all facts, yet direct recall fails for a substantial portion, especially for long-tail facts and reverse questions. Inference-time thinking (e.g., chain-of-thought) partially recovers these facts but leaves a persistent recall bottleneck. Scaling model size mainly reduces encoding failures, suggesting future gains require improved mechanisms for accessing stored knowledge, such as post-training or inference-time techniques.

## Method Summary
WikiProfile is built via an automated pipeline: Wikipedia pages are sampled, entities extracted with NER, and suitable object entities selected using prompts. Direct and reverse questions are generated, refined for specificity, and filtered with Google Search grounding. Natural/contextual variants and multiple-choice distractors are added. Each fact is evaluated on 10 tasks (completion, direct/reverse questions in two phrasings, multiple-choice) with and without thinking. For 13 LLMs, 8 responses per task are generated (temperature=1) and graded by an LLM autorater (Gemini-2.5-Pro). Encoding is defined as passing either completion task (grade > τ=0.5); knowledge requires all four QA tasks to exceed τ. Five knowledge profiles are assigned based on combinations of encoding and recall (direct/with thinking). This behavioral operationalization isolates encoding from recall without relying on model internals.

## Key Results
- Encoding saturation: Frontier models encode 95–98% of facts.
- Recall bottleneck: Direct recall fails for 25–33% of encoded facts; even with thinking, 11–12% remain inaccessible.
- Systematic gaps: Recall disparities exceed 20 points for long-tail facts and reverse questions; multiple-choice verification shows no such gap.
- Thinking recovers 40–65% of encoded-but-not-directly-known facts and narrows popularity and directionality gaps.
- Scaling reduces encoding failures but leaves recall as the dominant limitation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Separating Encoding and Recall
- Claim: Factual errors split cleanly into missing knowledge (encoding failures) vs. inaccessible encoded knowledge (recall failures).
- Mechanism: Knowledge profiling uses five profiles based on performance on encoding tasks (existential quantification) and knowledge tasks (universal quantification) with a threshold (τ=0.5). Encoding is measured via pre-training-like completion tasks, excluding thinking to avoid inference contamination.
- Core assumption: Behavioral performance on completion tasks reliably indicates whether a fact is stored in parameters.
- Evidence: Abstract and Section 2.1 define encoding/knowledge via ∃/∀ over task grades; neighbor "Thinking to Recall" supports decomposition.

### Mechanism 2: Thinking Enhances Recall
- Claim: Inference-time thinking (e.g., chain-of-thought) recovers many encoded-but-not-directly-accessible facts.
- Mechanism: Models with thinking show higher recall; 40–65% of such facts become known. Gains are larger for rare facts and reverse questions, narrowing systematic gaps.
- Core assumption: Additional computational steps help retrieve stored information not activated by direct queries.
- Evidence: Abstract and Section 5.3 quantify recovery and gap narrowing; neighbor "Thinking to Recall" provides independent support.

### Mechanism 3: Systematic Recall Failures
- Claim: Recall failures are distribution-dependent, disproportionately affecting long-tail facts and reverse directions.
- Mechanism: Comparing encoding vs. recall across popularity tiers shows small encoding gaps (<5 points) but large recall gaps (>20 points). Reverse questions have similar encoding to direct but much lower generation recall, yet no gap in multiple-choice verification.
- Core assumption: Facts are stored with surface forms and directions from pre-training; recall degrades when queries diverge.
- Evidence: Abstract, Section 5.2 (Figures 5,6); neighbor "Why Does ChatGPT Fall Short..." supports patterned failures.

## Foundational Learning
- **Knowledge profiling**: Five-profile classification based on encoding and recall (direct/with thinking). *Why needed*: It isolates the bottleneck between missing vs. inaccessible knowledge. *Quick check*: What are the five profiles and which combinations of encoded/known (with/without thinking) define each?
- **Behavioral operationalization**: Encoding measured via ∃ of completion tasks > τ; knowledge via ∀ of four QA tasks > τ, with thinking separate. *Why needed*: Enables black-box evaluation without internal states. *Quick check*: Why is thinking excluded from encoding measurement, and how is knowledge assessed with thinking?
- **Systematic recall errors**: Recall gaps tied to fact popularity and query directionality. *Why needed*: Points to specific improvement strategies (e.g., diverse post-training). *Quick check*: How do they demonstrate recall, not encoding, is the bottleneck for rare facts and reverse questions?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Wikipedia sampling -> NER -> entity selection -> direct question generation -> refinement/filtering -> reverse question generation -> pair validation -> variant creation (natural/contextual) -> multiple-choice distractor generation -> model inference (with/without thinking) -> LLM grading -> profile assignment.

**Critical path**:  
1. Sample Wikipedia pages using page-view stats.  
2. Run NER to extract candidate entities.  
3. Filter entities via prompts to ensure unique, non-trivial completions.  
4. Generate direct questions; refine for specificity and minimalism; filter with Google Search grounding.  
5. Generate reverse questions; verify directional pair validity; filter.  
6. Create natural and contextual phrasing variants.  
7. Generate multiple-choice options with plausible distractors via search.  
8. Run model inference on all 10 tasks per fact (8 samples each, temperature=1).  
9. Grade responses with LLM autorater (Gemini-2.5-Pro with thinking).  
10. Compute profile distributions based on grades and thresholds.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Automation vs. manual review: Pipeline scales but prompt engineering is critical; manual review only on flagged cases.  
- Threshold (τ=0.5): Robustness checks show stability but alternative thresholds shift counts.  
- Response count (n=8): Stabilizes estimates at ±0.5% but increases compute.  
- External search grounding: Ensures plausible distractors but adds dependency and cost.

**Failure signatures**:  
- High reverse-question pair rejection (31.5%) indicates difficulty creating valid directional pairs.  
- Elevated PARTIAL/OTHER grades may signal grader inconsistency; agreement is high (98.2%) but not perfect.  
- Near-chance encoding on some models suggests capacity/data limits.  
- Large generation-verification gaps for reverse questions highlight recall-specific issues.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Profile distribution across 13 LLMs: Shows encoding saturation and recall bottleneck.  
2. Popularity gap analysis: Compares encoding and recall rates for high vs. low popularity facts.  
3. Reverse direction analysis: Compares generation vs. verification for direct/reverse questions and evaluates thinking's impact.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Why does thinking improve recall for some encoded facts but not others? (Correlation with popularity/directionality observed, but no mechanistic explanation.)
2. How can pre-training or post-training interventions reduce the recall bottleneck? (Specific methods not studied; need controlled experiments varying training data and objectives.)
3. Does the recall bottleneck extend to long-form generation and reasoning tasks? (Profiling framework proposed for extension but not tested.)
4. What explains the dissociation between generation and multiple-choice performance on reverse questions? (Representational bias, decoding constraints, or other factors not investigated.)

## Limitations
- WikiProfile dataset not publicly released; reproduction requires rebuilding pipeline with Google Search and exact Gemini version.
- Automated pipeline (LLM + search) may introduce selection biases; manual validation only on flagged cases.
- Encoding measurement via completion tasks risks inferential contamination (model may infer rather than recall directly).
- LLM-based grading, despite 98.2% agreement, could misclassify edge cases; threshold choices affect profile assignments.
- Frontier models (GPT-5, Gemini-3) are proprietary, limiting reproducibility and generalizability.

## Confidence
- Error separation (encoding vs. recall): **Medium** – framework sound but operational validity uncertain due to possible task contamination.
- Near-saturated encoding (95–98%): **Medium-High** – consistent across models but depends on completion task design.
- Recall as dominant bottleneck: **High** – converging evidence from gaps, MC results, and thinking recovery.
- Thinking recovery (40–65%): **Medium** – substantial but sensitive to thinking setup and grading.

## Next Checks
1. Release WikiProfile and evaluate diverse open-source LLMs (e.g., Llama 3.1) to verify profile distributions and systematic gaps.
2. Conduct human evaluation on boundary facts (e.g., encoded but not known) to validate that completion tasks truly measure stored knowledge.
3. Ablate task design: vary completion formats, question phrasings, and grading thresholds to test robustness of encoding/recall separation.
