# Epileptic Seizure Detection in Separate Frequency Bands Using Feature Analysis and Graph Convolutional Neural Network (GCN) from Electroencephalogram (EEG) Signals

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.00163
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00163
- Reference count: 3
- Primary result: Band-specific GCN analysis of EEG yields up to 99.7% accuracy (lower beta) and overall 99.01% accuracy, with a marked drop to 51.4% in the higher beta band.

## Executive Summary
This study proposes a novel framework for epileptic seizure detection by decomposing EEG signals into distinct frequency bands and leveraging Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) to model spatial dependencies among electrodes. Using the CHB-MIT dataset, the authors extract eleven time‑domain and statistical features per band and train separate GCN classifiers. Results demonstrate exceptional accuracy in mid‑frequency bands (alpha: 99.5%, lower beta: 99.7%) while higher beta performs near chance (51.4%). The broadband model achieves 99.01% accuracy, 98.06% sensitivity, and 99.03% specificity. The approach highlights the importance of frequency‑specific analysis and provides enhanced spatial interpretability compared to conventional broadband methods.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with segmentation of raw EEG into 6‑second non‑overlapping windows, labeled as ictal if they overlap annotated seizure intervals. Each window is filtered into five frequency bands (delta, theta, alpha, lower beta, higher beta) using a 4th‑order Butterworth band‑pass filter. Within each 6‑second window, six 1‑second sub‑windows are extracted; for every channel, eleven features (including power spectral density entropy, Hjorth parameters, kurtosis, skewness, standard deviation, maximum amplitude, variance, median, and mean) are computed per sub‑window and concatenated, yielding a 66‑dimensional feature vector per channel. These vectors form node features for a graph where nodes represent the 23 EEG electrodes and edges encode spatial adjacency based on the 10‑20 montage (self‑loops included). The adjacency matrix is normalized, and a GCN with two to three graph convolution layers (ReLU activation) processes the graph. A SoftMax classifier produces a binary seizure/non‑seizure prediction. Class imbalance is addressed with SMOTE applied only on training data. Hyperparameters include Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.01) for up to 500 epochs, using cross‑entropy loss. Performance is evaluated via 5‑fold cross‑validation, reporting accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1‑score, and ROC‑AUC.

## Key Results
- Mid‑frequency bands achieve extremely high detection accuracy: alpha 99.5%, lower beta 99.7%.
- Higher beta band performs poorly with 51.4% accuracy, indicating minimal discriminative information.
- The broadband GCN model reaches 99.01% accuracy, 98.06% sensitivity, 99.03% specificity, F1‑score 96.67%, and AUC 97.67%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Decomposing EEG into separate frequency bands isolates frequency‑specific seizure biomarkers, improving detection accuracy for mid‑frequencies (alpha, lower beta) while revealing uninformative bands (higher beta).
- **Mechanism:** Band‑pass filtering (Section 3.1) isolates oscillatory activity within predefined delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), lower beta (13–22 Hz), and higher beta (22–30 Hz) ranges. The model learns discriminative patterns per band, as evidenced by alpha (99.5%) and lower beta (99.7%) accuracy versus higher beta (51.4%), suggesting seizures predominantly alter mid‑frequency dynamics.
- **Core assumption:** Seizure‑related neural discharges manifest as distinct spectral power shifts that are band‑specific and can be captured by fixed‑frequency filters.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Results showed highest accuracy in mid‑frequency bands: alpha (99.5%), lower beta (99.7%)… Higher beta performed poorly (51.4%)."
  - [Section 3.1] "The raw EEG signal was separated into five frequency bands… using band‑pass filtering."
  - [corpus] Neighbor abstracts broadly support frequency‑band analysis but do not directly evidence this specific band‑wise performance split; weak external corroboration.
- **Break condition:** If seizure dynamics span broad spectra without band‑specific concentration (e.g., in rare seizure subtypes), decomposition may fragment informative signals, reducing accuracy; higher beta’s failure (51.4%) may indicate this band carries minimal seizure‑related energy for this cohort.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Training exclusively on ictal‑phase features focuses the model on seizure‑active dynamics, enhancing sensitivity and reducing false positives from non‑ictal variability.
- **Mechanism:** Only segments overlapping annotated ictal intervals [𝑡𝑠, 𝑡𝑒] are used for feature extraction and training (Section 3.1, Eq. 5). This avoids contaminating the learning signal with pre‑ictal/post‑ictal transitions or inter‑ictal noise, as evidenced by high sensitivity (98.06% broadband) and specificity (99.03%).
- **Core assumption:** Ictal‑phase EEG contains the most salient, consistent biomarkers for seizure detection, whereas other phases exhibit higher inter‑patient or artifact variability.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "The proposed method… is based on active duration of the seizure as opposed to broad per‑seizure windows… The active seizure onset period, known as the ictal phase, provides more precise information."
  - [Section 3.1] "We only separated the ictal phase seizure signals according to the timings… A segmented window 𝑘 is considered ictal if it overlaps the annotated interval."
  - [corpus] Multiple neighbors (e.g., Jibon et al. 2024) use ictal‑phase labeling, but this paper’s explicit band‑wise ictal focus is novel; corpus does not refute the assumption.
- **Break condition:** If significant predictive biomarkers exist in pre‑ictal phases (e.g., for seizure forecasting), ictal‑only training would miss them, limiting clinical utility for prediction. Artifact contamination within ictal annotations could also mislead the model.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) model spatial dependencies among EEG electrodes as a brain network, capturing propagation patterns that improve discriminability over methods treating channels independently or as grid data.
- **Mechanism:** Electrodes are graph nodes; edges reflect physical adjacency or functional connectivity (Section 3.3). GCN layers aggregate neighbor features (Eq. 22–24), learning spatial patterns of seizure spread. This yields interpretable graph‑based representations compared to CNNs, with high accuracy (99.01% broadband) supporting effective spatial modeling.
- **Core assumption:** Electrode placements on the scalp reflect underlying neural connectivity, and seizure propagation generates coherent spatial patterns detectable via graph‑structured learning.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "A graph convolutional neural network (GCN) is then employed to model spatial dependencies among EEG electrodes… enhances spatial interpretability."
  - [Section 3.3] "EEG electrodes are represented as graph nodes… to capture spatial dependencies across brain regions."
  - [Section 6] Confusion matrices (Fig. 13) show consistent performance across bands, suggesting stable spatial pattern capture.
  - [corpus] Neighbors (Grattarola 2022, Wang 2023) use GCNs for spatial modeling, supporting the approach’s validity, though not confirming this exact architecture’s superiority.
- **Break condition:** If the predefined adjacency matrix (based on electrode proximity) poorly approximates true functional connectivity (e.g., due to volume conduction or patient‑specific anatomy), the GCN may propagate misleading signals. Performance collapse in higher beta could also indicate spatial patterns are frequency‑dependent.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  1. **Preprocessing:** Band‑pass filters (Butterworth) per band; 6s windowing; ictal‑phase labeling via CHB‑MIT annotations.  
  2. **Feature extraction:** 11 time‑domain/statistical features per channel per band → 253 features/s (23×11).  
  3. **Graph construction:** Nodes = 23 channels; edges = physical adjacency (10‑20 montage); adjacency matrix 𝐴 ∈ {0,1}²³⁺²³.  
  4. **GCN backbone:** 2–3 graph convolution layers (ReLU, Eq. 25); node features updated via neighborhood aggregation.  
  5. **Classifier:** SoftMax layer (Eq. 26) for binary seizure/non‑seizure prediction per graph.  
- **Critical path:** Raw EDF → band‑filter → segment → extract 11 features × 23 channels → reshape to (253×6) per segment → construct graph → GCN forward pass → loss (cross‑entropy, Eq. 29) → Adam update (η=0.01, Eq. 30).  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Window duration (6s):* Balances temporal resolution vs. computational load; longer windows may capture more dynamics but increase graph size.  
  - *Fixed adjacency:* Simple but ignores patient‑specific connectivity; dynamic graphs (neighbors: Yang 2024) could adapt but add complexity.  
  - *SMOTE:* Mitigates class imbalance but may synthesize unrealistic feature vectors, risking overfitting to synthetic minority patterns.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *Higher beta band (22–30 Hz) collapse:* Accuracy 51.4%, specificity 3% → model predicts all positive; indicates higher beta features lack discriminative power or are noise‑dominated.  
  - *Per‑band accuracy variance:* If delta/theta accuracy drops significantly vs. alpha/beta, suggests band‑specific feature instability or inadequate modeling of low‑frequency dynamics.  
  - *Over‑smoothing in deep GCNs:* Beyond ~3 layers, node representations may converge, losing discriminability; monitor node feature variance per layer.  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. *Validate band‑pass filtering:* Apply filters to a sample EEG segment; plot power spectral density (PSD) pre/post‑filter to confirm isolation (e.g., alpha band should show 8–13 Hz dominance).  
  2. *Feature separability test:* For ictal vs. non‑ictal segments in alpha band, compute t‑tests/effect sizes for all 11 features; expect features like PSD, kurtosis to show significant shifts (𝑝 < 0.01).  
  3. *Graph sanity check:* Visualize adjacency matrix (23×23) as heatmap; confirm expected connectivity pattern (e.g., contiguous temporal‑frontal lobes linked). Then, pass a random batch through GCN to verify output dimensions and absence of NaN/inf values.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What neurophysiological mechanisms cause mid‑frequency bands (alpha, lower beta) to exhibit markedly superior discriminative power for ictal EEG compared to delta/theta and especially higher beta?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The results consistently show alpha (99.5%) and lower beta (99.7%) accuracy, while higher beta performs poorly (51.4%). The discussion notes this reveals "frequency‑specific seizure characteristics" but does not explain the underlying neural or signal processing reasons for this sharp band‑dependent performance divergence.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates the *phenomenon* but does not investigate the causal signal properties or brain dynamics (e.g., specific seizure‑related oscillations, synchronization patterns, or artifact susceptibility) that would explain why certain bands are more informative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analysis of band‑specific signal‑to‑noise ratios, spatial coherence patterns, and spectral power changes during ictal onset across a larger, more diverse patient cohort. Correlating band performance with known seizure foci types (focal vs. generalized) and underlying etiologies.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Is the consistently poor detection performance in the higher beta band (22‑30 Hz) an intrinsic limitation of this frequency range for seizure detection, or an artifact of the specific feature set, GCN graph construction, or preprocessing (e.g., filtering) used?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The higher beta band yields 51.4% accuracy and an AUC of 0.52, with confusion matrices showing a severe specificity collapse (3%). The authors suggest this band "may contain minimal discriminative information" but do not rule out methodological factors.
- **Why unresolved:** The framework applies the identical 11‑feature pipeline and static electrode graph to all bands. It is unknown if different features (e.g., phase‑locking value, cross‑frequency coupling) or a different graph topology (reflecting higher‑frequency functional connectivity) could salvage information from this band.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled ablation study varying feature extraction methods specifically for higher beta. Testing alternative graph construction strategies (e.g., based on high‑frequency coherence) or different window durations for this band. Analyzing the raw higher‑beta filtered signals to check for prevalent non‑neural artifacts (e.g., muscle activity) that might dominate the band.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does the proposed band‑specific GCN model's performance generalize beyond the pediatric CHB‑MIT dataset to adult populations, different EEG montages, and recordings with significant artifact contamination?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study uses exclusively the CHB‑MIT dataset (pediatric patients, 23 consistent channels). The discussion acknowledges "performance on datasets with higher noise levels or artifact contamination remains to be explored" and "inter‑patient variability is still a major impediment." The methodology assumes a static, consistent electrode montage.
- **Why unresolved:** Claims of robustness and clinical applicability cannot be sustained without validation on external, more heterogeneous datasets. The model's reliance on a fixed 23‑channel graph topology may fail with missing or additional channels common in real‑world clinical settings.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication studies on adult seizure datasets (e.g., TUSZ, EPILEPSIAE) with varying channel counts and montages. Stress‑testing the model with synthetic and real artifact injections (eye blinks, muscle noise) to quantify robustness. Developing a flexible graph construction protocol that adapts to variable channel sets.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the use of a static, anatomically‑derived adjacency matrix for the GCN fully capture the seizure‑related, dynamic functional connectivity changes between brain regions during the ictal phase?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The graph is built on fixed electrode proximity (Appendix: "adjacency in a typical EEG montage"). The method's innovation is modeling spatial dependencies, but it assumes a stationary network structure. Seizures involve evolving, abnormal synchronization that may not respect the static anatomical graph.
- **Why unresolved:** A static graph cannot model time‑varying functional connectivity, a key hallmark of seizure propagation. The paper's GCN propagation steps treat node relationships as fixed, potentially missing critical dynamic patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implementing a dynamic GCN where the adjacency matrix is learned from the signal data or updated per time window, and comparing performance against the static graph. Analyzing the learned GCN filters/attention weights to see if they align with known seizure pathways versus fixed anatomy.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can the proposed band‑separated framework be extended to provide real‑time, patient‑adaptive seizure detection and onset prediction (pre‑ictal) in a continuous streaming EEG scenario?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The impact statement mentions "real‑time and resource‑constrained systems," and the conclusion notes the model identifies "preictal, ictal, and postictal phases." However, the experiments use fixed 6‑second segments from pre‑labeled files without streaming simulation or prospective prediction.
- **Why unresolved:** Real‑world deployment requires handling continuous data, potential concept drift, and minimizing latency. The current 6‑second window and offline training may not optimize for earliest possible detection or patient‑specific adaptation without retraining.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluation on continuous, multi‑hour EEG recordings with simulated online processing (sliding windows). Developing an online learning or fine‑tuning mechanism for patient‑specific adaptation. Measuring detection latency from electrographical seizure onset and computational throughput on edge hardware.

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details (exact adjacency matrix, GCN hyperparameters, SMOTE configuration, cross‑validation strategy) hinder faithful replication.
- Poor performance in the higher beta band (51.4% accuracy) remains unexplained and may indicate band‑specific limitations or methodological issues.
- Evaluation is restricted to the pediatric CHB‑MIT dataset; generalizability to adult populations, varied montages, and artifact‑rich recordings is unknown.
