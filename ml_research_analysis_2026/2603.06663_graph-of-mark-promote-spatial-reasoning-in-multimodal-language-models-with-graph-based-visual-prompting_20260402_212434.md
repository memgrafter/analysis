# Graph-of-Mark: Promote Spatial Reasoning in Multimodal Language Models with Graph-Based Visual Prompting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06663
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06663
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces a pixel-level visual prompting method that overlays scene graphs onto images, boosting spatial reasoning in multimodal language models by up to 11 percentage points on VQA and localization tasks.

## Executive Summary
Graph-of-Mark (GoM) addresses the limited spatial reasoning in multimodal language models (MLMs) by introducing the first pixel-level visual prompting technique that overlays scene graphs—encoding object nodes and their spatial relations—directly onto input images. Unlike prior methods that treat objects as isolated entities, GoM's graph-based prompting steers the model's attention toward inter-object spatial configurations. In zero-shot evaluations across three open-source MLMs and four datasets (GQA, VQAv1, VQAv2, RefCOCOg), GoM consistently boosts accuracy, with the best configuration achieving 83.6% VQA accuracy and 57.6% REC accuracy, while adding only about 1.13 seconds of preprocessing overhead per image.

## Method Summary
GoM is a training-free pipeline that augments an input image with a scene graph overlay. It begins with an ensemble of object detectors (OWL-V2, YOLOv8, Mask R-CNN) and a merger to produce object masks via SAM-HQ. A monocular depth estimator (MiDaS) provides relative depth values. From these, a spatial relation engine computes directional, depth, and proximity relations between objects. A query-driven filter then prunes the graph to retain only objects and relations relevant to the user's text query, using lexical/semantic matching and spatial proximity. Finally, a renderer draws colored masks, numeric or textual ID marks, directed arrows, and optional relation labels on the image, employing a collision-resolution algorithm to avoid overlapping marks and maintain interpretability. The augmented image is fed to an off-the-shelf MLM for zero-shot inference.

## Key Results
- Up to 11 percentage point improvement in VQA and localization accuracy over baselines (including Set-of-Mark).
- Best configuration: 83.6% VQA accuracy (LlamaV-o1) and 57.6% REC accuracy.
- Consistent gains across three MLMs (LlamaV-o1, Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2) and four datasets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Visual graph prompting shifts MLM perception from a "bag of objects" to a relational network.
- **Claim:** Overlaying a scene graph (with object nodes and spatial relation edges) directly onto the image changes how the MLM processes visual tokens, encouraging it to reason about object interactions rather than isolated detections.
- **Mechanism:** The overlaid graph (ISG) is merged into the pixel stream, influencing the attention of the MLM. The graph structure is implicit in the spatial arrangement of marks and arrows.
- **Core assumption:** MLMs can implicitly parse and reason over relational structures present in the visual input without explicit training.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper states this is the first pixel-level visual prompting technique that encodes both objects and relations. Neighbor work suggests token-level modifications aid perception, but direct evidence for this mechanism is limited.
- **Break condition:** Excessive graph density (too many edges) introduces visual noise, degrading performance.

### Mechanism 2: Query-driven graph filtering focuses the scene graph on task-relevant spatial relations.
- **Claim:** Pruning the initial dense object-relation graph using lexical/semantic matching with the user query retains only the spatial context necessary for answering the question.
- **Mechanism:** The pipeline matches query terms to object labels and relation types, keeping relevant objects and then selecting the top-k relations per object by query relevance and spatial proximity.
- **Core assumption:** Not all objects and relations are pertinent; relevance can be approximated via text alignment.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper describes a two-tier sorting (query relevancy primary, spatial distance secondary) and retains relations for which an object is the head.
- **Break condition:** Vague or underspecified queries may lead to fallback on all objects/relations, adding noise; overly strict matching may drop necessary context.

### Mechanism 3: Collision-free mark allocation preserves visual interpretability of the overlaid graph.
- **Claim:** A placement algorithm that resolves overlaps between marks (IDs, labels, arrows) ensures the graph remains legible to the MLM.
- **Mechanism:** The renderer first positions edge labels at object midpoints, then object ID marks at centroids, and then iteratively displaces conflicting marks along axis-aligned directions, connecting displaced labels with dashed guide lines.
- **Core assumption:** Visual clutter from overlapping marks confuses spatial grounding; clear annotations are necessary.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper proposes a novel allocation algorithm and uses a resolver to eliminate collisions incrementally.
- **Break condition:** Failure to resolve all collisions within displacement limits leads to ambiguous cues.

   - *Why needed:* GoM injects scene graphs (nodes=objects, edges=spatial relations) into the visual modality. Understanding this structure is prerequisite.

   - *Why needed:* GoM modifies input images at inference time without retraining the model. This paradigm explains the "no retraining" claim and efficiency trade-offs.
   - *Quick check:* How does Set-of-Mark (SoM) differ from GoM in terms of relational encoding?

3. **Object detection/segmentation and monocular depth estimation.**
   - *Why needed:* GoM's pipeline depends on these modules to obtain object regions and relative depth. Their limitations impact graph quality.
   - *Quick check:* Why does GoM use an ensemble of detectors (OWL-V2, YOLOv8, Mask R-CNN) instead of a single model?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Input (image + query) -> Object Detector Ensemble (OWL-V2, YOLOv8-X, Mask R-CNN) -> Merger & Segmenter (WBF + SAM-HQ) -> Depth Estimator (MiDaS) -> Spatial Relation Engine -> Query Filter -> Renderer -> Augmented image -> MLM

- **Critical path:** Object detection -> segmentation -> relation estimation -> query filtering -> rendering. Failure at any stage propagates.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Accuracy vs. Efficiency: More detectors improve recall but increase preprocessing time (~1.13s vs SoM's 0.92s).
  - Mark Type: Numeric IDs for REC (precise reference); textual IDs for VQA (interpretable). Relation labels add clarity but increase clutter.
  - Filtering Aggressiveness: High k risks noise; low k may miss edges. Thresholds require tuning.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Accuracy drop on images with >10 objects or >16 edges.
  - REC tasks failing with textual IDs (returns text like "couch_3").
  - Depth relations failing in scenes with ambiguous depth cues.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Baseline replication: Run raw image, segmentation-only, and SoM on GQA with Qwen2.5-VL.
  2. Ablation - Graph density: Vary max relations per object (k) on VQAv2; expect peak at moderate density.
  3. Mark type comparison: Test numeric vs textual IDs ± relation labels on VQA and REC.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can scene hypergraphs be defined, rendered, and utilized to represent complex multi-object relations beyond pairwise edges, and what is their impact on spatial reasoning in densely populated scenes?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states "Future directions include scene hypergraphs for complex scenes."
- **Why unresolved:** Current GoM uses pairwise edges only; hypergraphs could capture n-ary relations but require novel rendering strategies and graph construction algorithms.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a hypergraph-based extension of GoM that renders hyperedges (e.g., polygons or shaded regions) connecting >2 objects, then evaluate on datasets with complex spatial queries involving multiple simultaneous relations (e.g., "A is between B and C") and measure accuracy gains over pairwise graphs in dense scenes.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent does incorporating stereo vision or 3D scene graph data improve the accuracy and robustness of spatial relation estimation in GoM, particularly for depth-based relations like "in front of" and "behind"?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion mentions "stereo vision for improved depth reasoning" as a future direction.
- **Why unresolved:** GoM relies on monocular depth estimation (MiDaS), which may be noisy and scale-invariant; stereo or 3D graphs could provide metric depth but require multiple views or external 3D annotations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replace the monocular depth module with a stereo matching pipeline or integrate precomputed 3D scene graphs, then compare performance on depth-sensitive tasks (e.g., VQA questions about occlusion) using datasets with ground-truth depth or 3D annotations.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can GoM be extended to video understanding by incorporating temporal modeling of object trajectories and relations over time, and does this enhance spatial reasoning in dynamic scenes?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion lists "temporal modeling for video understanding" as a future direction.
- **Why unresolved:** GoM processes static frames; video requires tracking objects across frames and modeling temporal relations (e.g., "moving toward"), which introduces new challenges in graph consistency and rendering.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design a video-GoM variant that links object nodes across frames via temporal edges, then evaluate on video-based spatial reasoning benchmarks (e.g., action recognition with spatial preconditions) and compare against frame-wise GoM.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How can GoM be specialized and optimized for domain-specific applications such as medical image analysis, and what adaptations are necessary to handle domain-specific visual features and spatial queries?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion projects "significant potential in the clinical domain" and the introduction mentions biomedical research and clinical practice as application areas.
- **Why unresolved:** Medical images (e.g., CT, MRI) have different modalities, fewer object classes, and fine-grained spatial queries (e.g., organ boundaries); GoM's general-purpose detectors and relation estimators may not transfer effectively.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Adapt GoM's pipeline with medical-specific detectors (e.g., for anatomical structures) and depth estimators suitable for volumetric data, then test on medical VQA or localization tasks (e.g., "Is the tumor adjacent to the artery?") using curated medical datasets.

## Limitations
- Core assumption gap: No direct evidence that MLMs parse the graph structure vs. treating marks as novel tokens.
- Query filtering brittleness: Degrades with vague or complex questions.
- Pipeline dependency: Errors in upstream modules (detectors, depth estimator) directly corrupt the graph.

## Confidence
- **GoM boosts spatial reasoning accuracy:** High
- **Mechanism 1 (graph shifts perception) is the primary driver:** Low
- **Query-driven filtering effectively focuses on relevant relations:** Medium
- **Collision-free allocation is necessary for interpretability:** Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Structure Isolation Test:** Corrupt graph structure (randomized arrows, shuffled IDs, removed labels) vs intact graphs on spatial QA subset to isolate the role of relational encoding.
2. **Query Generalization Stress Test:** Construct a benchmark of ambiguous/complex questions to measure filtering recall/precision versus a no-filter ablation.
3. **Module Error Propagation Audit:** Introduce calibrated noise/errors into upstream components (drop objects, blur depth, degrade masks) and measure resultant accuracy to quantify robustness ceiling.
