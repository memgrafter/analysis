# Do Reasoning Models Ask Better Questions? A Formal Information-Theoretic Analysis on Multi-Turn LLM Games

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17716
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17716
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: LLMs with explicit Chain‑of‑Thought reasoning achieve significantly higher Information Gain per turn and win rates, especially in partially observable settings (e.g., Qwen3‑8B with CoT: 0.41 IG/turn, 94% win rate; without CoT: 0.17 IG/turn, 8% win rate).

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether LLMs that employ explicit reasoning ask more informative questions in ambiguous multi‑turn interactions. Using a hierarchical “Guess My City” game with Seeker, Oracle, and Pruner agents, the authors measure question effectiveness via Information Gain (IG) computed from Shannon entropy. Results show that models equipped with Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) reasoning achieve higher per‑turn IG and better win rates, particularly under partial observability where the Seeker only sees dialogue history. Larger models tend to generate fewer but higher‑potential‑IG candidate questions, while smaller models explore more candidates, revealing a trade‑off between breadth and depth of reasoning.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a geographical guess‑my‑city task featuring the 40 most populous cities organized in a five‑level hierarchical graph (region → subregion → country → state → city). Three LLM agents interact: the Seeker asks yes/no questions; the Oracle (truthfully) answers and detects when the target city is identified; the Pruner updates the active hypothesis space by logically eliminating cities inconsistent with the Q&A. The primary evaluation metric is per‑turn Information Gain (IG), defined as the reduction in Shannon entropy of the active city set: IG = log₂(N_before) – log₂(N_after). Games are run under two conditions: fully observable (Seeker sees the full graph) and partially observable (Seeker only sees the dialogue history). For Qwen‑based models, the Seeker is evaluated with and without explicit Chain‑of‑Thought prompting. Each model is tested across 120 game instances (40 cities × 3 runs).

## Key Results
- CoT reasoning dramatically improves performance in the partial observability setting; for Qwen3‑8B, CoT yields a 0.41 IG/turn and 94% win rate versus 0.17 IG/turn and 8% without CoT.
- Smaller models (e.g., Qwen3‑8B) generate and consider more candidate questions per turn, while larger models (Qwen3‑30B) produce candidates with higher average optimal IG and select the optimal candidate more frequently.
- Across both FO and PO conditions, models with explicit reasoning reach the target in fewer turns, demonstrating more efficient information‑seeking strategies.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Quantifying Information Gain via Entropy
- **Claim:** Quantifying Information Gain (IG) via Shannon entropy reduction provides a fine‑grained, turn‑level metric for evaluating the informativeness of a Seeker’s yes/no questions.
- **Mechanism:** Before each turn, the active hypothesis space (set of candidate target cities) is assigned a uniform prior; entropy `H = log₂(N)` bits is computed. After the Oracle’s answer and Pruner’s update, the active set shrinks, and IG = `H_before – H_after` directly measures uncertainty reduction achieved by that query.
- **Core assumption:** The hypothesis space is fully enumerable and the Pruner correctly implements logical constraints to eliminate impossible nodes.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.4 define the entropy and IG formulas; neighboring work uses expected IG, but the turn‑level calculation here is distinct.
- **Break condition:** If the Pruner mis‑applies logical rules (e.g., pruning wrong cities for a given question), the IG metric becomes misaligned with actual hypothesis reduction.

### Mechanism 2: CoT Enables Strategic Question Selection
- **Claim:** Explicit Chain‑of‑Thought reasoning in the Seeker leads to higher per‑turn IG and better overall efficiency, especially under partial observability.
- **Mechanism:** CoT prompts the model to generate intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., evaluating candidate questions) before finalizing a query. This structured deliberation helps the model simulate the IG outcome of different queries, enabling more strategic choices that compensate for the limited context in PO.
- **Core assumption:** The model’s explicit reasoning trace correlates with an accurate mental model of the hypothesis space.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5 report strong improvements for CoT in PO; Wei et al. (2023) show CoT elicits reasoning for symbolic tasks.
- **Break condition:** If CoT becomes unfocused or hallucinates graph attributes, it may add latency without improving question quality, possibly degrading performance.

### Mechanism 3: Model Scale Influences Exploration–Assertiveness Trade‑off
- **Claim:** Larger models exhibit “assertiveness” by generating fewer but higher‑potential‑IG candidate questions during CoT, while smaller models compensate via “aggressive exploration” of more candidates.
- **Mechanism:** The paper analyzes the CoT traces and finds that larger models (Qwen3‑30B) produce a smaller set of candidates with higher average optimal IG and a higher frequency of selecting the optimal question. Smaller models (Qwen3‑8B) consider more candidates on average but select the optimal one less often.
- **Core assumption:** The number and quality of candidate questions generation are functions of model capacity, not prompt or external factors.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.1, Table 2 quantify the differences in average questions per turn, optimal IG, and chosen IG between the two model sizes.
- **Break condition:** If the candidate‑generation breadth‑depth pattern collapses across scales (e.g., due to similar fine‑tuning), the scaling relationship breaks down.

## Foundational Learning

- **Shannon Entropy & Information Gain**  
  *Why needed:* The entire evaluation metric is built on quantifying uncertainty reduction. `H = -Σ p log p` simplifies to `log₂(N)` for uniform hypothesis distributions; IG is the entropy difference before vs. after a question‑answer‑prune cycle.  
  *Quick check:* Starting with 40 candidate cities (entropy ≈5.32 bits) and reducing to 10 candidates yields IG = `log₂(40) - log₂(10) = 2.0 bits`.

- **Hypothesis Space Pruning via Logical Constraints**  
  *Why needed:* The Pruner agent translates a yes/no answer into a set of eliminated nodes, requiring set operations (subset, complement) within the hierarchical graph.  
  *Quick check:* For “Is target in Asia?” with answer “No”, prune all cities where `subregion == Asia`.

- **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) Prompting**  
  *Why needed:* The core independent variable is explicit reasoning; CoT elicits intermediate steps (candidate generation, evaluation) that help the model select questions with higher expected IG, especially under partial observability.  
  *Quick check:* In PO, the Seeker cannot see the current graph. How does CoT help? (It allows the model to simulate the effect of questions on its internal memory‑based representation of the narrowing hypothesis space).

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Seeker Agent → generates yes/no questions → Oracle Agent → answers and checks for target guess → (if not game over) Pruner Agent → updates active hypothesis → Hypothesis Space Manager → recalculates entropy and IG → Evaluator/Controller → logs metrics and next turn. The Manager holds the graph and processes Pruner’s output.

- **Critical path:**  
  Seeker question → Oracle answer (`answer`, `game_over`) → Pruner output (`pruned_ids`) → Graph update → Entropy recomputation → Metrics logged → Next turn if needed.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  1. **FO vs. PO:** FO provides the full graph to the Seeker (easier, less realistic), while PO restricts the Seeker to dialogue history, better simulating real‑world ambiguity.  
  2. **Oracle/Pruner homogeneity:** Using the same model (Qwen3‑8B) for Oracle and Pruner introduces correlated errors (e.g., lack of city knowledge affects both answering and pruning). A stronger, separate Oracle could isolate Seeker performance.  
  3. **IG as reward:** The metric is computed after the fact by the Pruner; it is objective but cannot directly guide the Seeker during training. For reinforcement learning, a predicted IG would be needed.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  1. Low IG/turn and high turn count: Seeker asks vague or redundant questions.  
  2. Pruner inconsistency: Same Q&A pair in different states produces different pruned sets, indicating flawed logic.  
  3. CoT degeneration: In PO, CoT‑enabled models perform worse than non‑CoT, suggesting misaligned reasoning traces.  
  4. Oracle error: Incorrect answers (e.g., “No” for a city that belongs to Asia) or failure to detect an explicit target guess.

- **First three experiments for validation:**  
  1. **Baseline replication:** Reproduce the exact setup (Qwen3‑8B Seeker, PO, with/without CoT) to confirm the reported performance gap (e.g., 0.94 vs. 0.08 win rate) and verify Pruner logic.  
  2. **Component isolation test:** Replace Oracle and Pruner with deterministic, rule‑based implementations (ground‑truth attributes and exact logical pruning) to eliminate component quality as a confound; check if the CoT advantage persists.  
  3. **Optimal choice rate stress test:** For CoT runs, extract all candidate questions and compute their potential IG; analyze the distribution of `(Optimal IG − Chosen IG)` across game states and model scales to identify when models fail to pick the optimal question.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Domain generalization:** The framework is evaluated only on a geographical “Guess My City” game. It is unknown whether the IG metrics and agent dynamics transfer to other domains (e.g., medical diagnosis, troubleshooting) with different hypothesis space structures.  
2. **IG as reinforcement learning reward:** Could turn‑level and cumulative IG serve as effective reward signals to train LLM agents to improve question‑asking? The stability of IG as a gradient signal and its alignment with human judgment remain untested.  
3. **Hypothesis space assumptions:** The framework requires a complete, pre‑enumerated, and structured hypothesis space. This limits applicability to real‑world ambiguous requests where the set of interpretations is not known in advance or is open‑ended.  
4. **Trade‑off explanation:** Why smaller models explore more candidate questions while larger models are more “assertive”? The paper observes the pattern but does not dissect whether it stems from reasoning depth, token budget, search algorithm differences, or other factors.

## Limitations
- The study uses the same model (Qwen3‑8B) for Oracle and Pruner, risking correlated errors that confound Seeker performance.  
- Critical experimental details are missing: exact LLM inference hyperparameters (temperature, top_p, max_new_tokens), graph serialization format for the Seeker, and the mechanism for toggling CoT.  
- Results are limited to the top 40 most populous cities; generalization to larger hypothesis spaces or different domains is uncertain.

## Confidence
- IG metric validity: Medium (computation is clear but depends on flawless Pruner and an enumerable hypothesis space).  
- CoT improves information‑seeking: High (consistent empirical gap across models, supported by extensive CoT literature).  
- Model scale shapes candidate strategy: Medium (observed in Qwen3 series; may not generalize to other architectures or without fine‑tuning).

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Systematically vary temperature, top_p, and max_new_tokens for all agents; evaluate stability of per‑turn IG, win rate, and the CoT vs. non‑CoT gap.  
2. **Oracle/Pruner decoupling:** Replace Oracle and Pruner with deterministic rule‑based equivalents; rerun CoT ablations to confirm the Seeker reasoning advantage persists independently of component quality.  
3. **Candidate optimality correlation:** For CoT runs, compute IG of every generated candidate; correlate the gap between optimal and chosen IG with win rate across model scales to test the assertion–exploration trade‑off hypothesis.
