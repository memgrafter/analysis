# Evaluating Counterfactual Strategic Reasoning in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.19167
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19167
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: LLMs succeed in default game setups but become brittle under payoff‑based counterfactuals; stronger models adapt better; advanced prompting often increases token cost without proportional strategic gains.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) exhibit genuine strategic reasoning or merely rely on memorized patterns in repeated game‑theoretic settings (Prisoner’s Dilemma and Rock‑Paper‑Scissors). Using counterfactual variants that alter payoff structures or merely relabel actions, the authors probe models’ ability to recompute incentives. Results show that while LLMs perform well in default configurations, they degrade significantly—showing lower total points, delayed opponent comprehension, and reduced cooperation—when payoffs change. Stronger models (e.g., Claude 3.7, Llama 3.3) adapt more successfully than weaker ones (e.g., Mistral Large). Label‑only changes have minor impact, highlighting that sensitivity to incentives is the key limitation. Moreover, advanced prompting strategies (CoT, SC) often raise token consumption without commensurate improvements, revealing an efficiency–capability trade‑off.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates LLMs on repeated two‑player games: 16‑round Prisoner’s Dilemma (PD) and 24‑round Rock‑Paper‑Scissors (RPS). Counterfactual variants are created by (a) altering payoff matrices (e.g., converting PD to Stag Hunt) and (b) permuting action labels (e.g., Cooperate/Defect to Stag/Hare). Models face algorithmic opponents (SREP, PP, MF, TFT) and other LLMs, using prompting strategies including zero‑shot, chain‑of‑thought, solo‑performance, and self‑consistency. Key metrics are total points, opponent comprehension (*m*—the earliest round after which the model’s payoff is no worse than the opponent’s in ≥90% of remaining rounds), cooperation rate, and efficiency (points per thousand tokens). Experiments are repeated (5 runs for non‑SC, 2 for SC) with sampling temperature 0.7, top‑p 1.0, max_tokens 50; self‑consistency uses 3 (PD) or 5 (RPS) trials per round. Models tested include Claude Sonnet 3.5 v2, Claude Sonnet 3.7 (with/without thinking), Claude Sonnet 4 (with/without thinking), DeepSeek R1, Llama 3.3 70B, and Mistral Large (24.07). Action extraction uses the last valid token; invalid outputs are rare. An orchestrator cycles through all combinations of model, prompt, opponent, and counterfactual, logging actions, payoffs, and token usage for metric calculation.

## Key Results
- LLMs handle default games well but degrade under payoff‑based counterfactuals, indicating reliance on memorized patterns rather than true incentive recomputation.
- Stronger models (Claude 3.7, Llama 3.3) adapt more successfully to payoff changes, while weaker ones (Mistral Large) show high instability; label‑only changes have little effect.
- Advanced prompting (CoT, SC) often increases token consumption without proportional improvements in points or comprehension, revealing an efficiency trade‑off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Payoff recomputation vs. pattern matching**  
Payoff‑based counterfactuals break familiar dominance relations and symmetries, forcing models to derive new optimal strategies from first principles. Models that rely on cached strategies from pre‑training fail even if action labels remain familiar. *Evidence*: The abstract and section 6.1.1 show that payoff changes expose reasoning differences, while label changes do not.

**Mechanism 2: Opponent comprehension as adaptation speed**  
The metric *m* identifies the earliest round after which the LLM’s payoff is no worse than the opponent’s in ≥90% of subsequent rounds. A low *m* indicates rapid inference of opponent policy and stable response. *Evidence*: Section 5.3 defines *m*; section 6.1 shows delayed *m* under counterfactuals for most LLMs, especially in RPS.

**Mechanism 3: Prompting efficiency trade‑off**  
Extra reasoning steps (CoT, SC) should improve decision quality. Efficiency = points / (tokens × scaling factor). If token cost rises without proportional point gains, the model exhibits inefficient reasoning. *Evidence*: The abstract and section D.4 tables show efficiency declines from zero‑shot to CoT/SC for many models, notably Claude 4 and DeepSeek R1.

## Foundational Learning
- **Game‑theoretic solution concepts** (Nash equilibrium, dominant strategies, repeated‑game strategies like tit‑for‑tat). Needed to interpret whether model behavior aligns with rational play. *Quick check*: “What is the unique Nash equilibrium of a one‑shot Prisoner’s Dilemma, and how does repeated interaction enable cooperation?”
- **Counterfactual interventions** (distinguishing label changes from payoff changes). Understanding this difference is essential to interpret the observed brittleness patterns. *Quick check*: “In PD, how does switching to Stag Hunt payoffs (removing strict dominance) alter the strategic challenge compared to merely renaming actions?”
- **Evaluation metrics for interactive agents** (total points, opponent comprehension, cooperation rate, efficiency). No single metric suffices; each captures distinct aspects of strategic competence. *Quick check*: “Why is ‘opponent comprehension’ more informative than total points alone when assessing strategic reasoning?”

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Orchestrator -> Game engine (with Counterfactual generator) -> LLM player interface & Opponent pool; Game engine -> Metric calculator -> Orchestrator.

**Critical path:**  
1. Load the LLM and apply the system prompt.  
2. For each round: provide history, request action, extract valid action, apply game rules to determine payoffs.  
3. After N rounds, compute raw metrics.  
4. Repeat for required runs, then aggregate means and standard deviations.  
5. Compare results across default and counterfactual conditions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Rounds vs. tokens: More rounds allow deeper adaptation but increase token usage.  
- Opponent diversity: Algorithmic opponents offer clean patterns; LLM‑LLM captures emergent dynamics but with higher variance.  
- Prompting strategies: Zero‑shot is cheapest; self‑consistency improves robustness but multiplies token cost.  
- Metrics vs. complexity: Total points summarize performance; comprehension adds temporal dimension; efficiency balances cost.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Invalid outputs (no recognizable action) – reported near‑100% validity, so rare.  
- High variance in total points or comprehension across runs – indicates unstable behavior.  
- Delayed comprehension (*m* > 10) – failure to adapt within game horizon.  
- Efficiency drop under SC/CoT without performance gain – overthinking.  
- Cooperation collapse in PD‑LLM‑LLM – model defaults to defection (e.g., Claude 4, DeepSeek R1).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline PD evaluation:** Run all models with zero‑shot prompt against each opponent (SREP, PP, MF, TFT, and same‑model LLM). Record total points, cooperation rate, and *m*.  
2. **Label‑based PD counterfactual:** Replace C/D with S/H while keeping PD payoffs. Compare to baseline to assess impact of relabeling.  
3. **Payoff‑based PD counterfactual (Stag Hunt):** Change payoffs to Stag Hunt structure (R > T ≥ P > S). Measure cooperation rate and *m* in LLM‑LLM and adjustments against algorithmic opponents. Expect strong models to increase cooperation while weak ones remain defection‑heavy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**: What is the primary cognitive mechanism causing LLM brittleness under payoff‑based counterfactuals—failure to recompute incentives, over‑reliance on memorized game templates, or inability to maintain consistent multi‑round strategies?  
*Basis*: The paper shows strong models like Claude 3.7 adapt to payoff changes while weaker ones like Mistral Large fail, suggesting sensitivity to incentive restructuring.  
*Why unresolved*: Without probing model internals or controlled ablations, we cannot determine whether deficits arise from training distribution limitations, reasoning depth constraints, or fundamental representational gaps.  
*What evidence would resolve*: Comparative analyses of model attention patterns, policy gradients, and success rates across systematically varied payoff matrices, coupled with interventions that decouple memorization from reasoning (e.g., novel game variants with unseen action labels but computable incentives).

**Open Question 2**: Does counterfactual strategic robustness generalize across game structures beyond Prisoner’s Dilemma and Rock‑Paper‑Scissors, particularly for games with multiple equilibria, incomplete information, or anti‑coordination incentives?  
*Basis*: The study focuses on PD and RPS, noting these probe distinct strategic dimensions, but does not test other classes.  
*Why unresolved*: The two games represent only a narrow slice of game‑theoretic mechanics; generalization to games like Stag Hunt, Chicken, or Bayesian games remains unknown.  
*What evidence would resolve*: Replication of the counterfactual evaluation framework on a diverse battery of canonical games with well‑defined equilibrium concepts and varying solution properties.

**Open Question 3**: Which model‑scale, architecture, or training‑objective factors most strongly predict counterfactual strategic performance, and can targeted improvements mitigate brittleness?  
*Basis*: The paper compares models from different families and notes larger, more advanced models generally show better adaptation, but does not isolate drivers.  
*Why unresolved*: The observed differences could stem from scale, tuning objectives, or data diversity; without controlled ablations or meta‑analyses across many models, key drivers remain unclear.  
*What evidence would resolve*: A systematic study correlating model specifications (parameter count, pretraining corpus diversity, fine‑tuning strategies) with counterfactual metrics across a large model suite, ideally with fine‑tuned variants isolating each factor.

**Open Question 4**: How do LLMs perform in multi‑agent strategic environments with heterogeneous, dynamically changing opponents, as opposed to fixed two‑player matchups?  
*Basis*: The evaluation uses fixed opponents and predefined payoff structures; the limitations section suggests results may vary in more diverse or multi‑agent settings.  
*Why unresolved*: Real‑world strategic interactions often involve populations, learning opponents, and shifting coalitions; two‑player static settings may underestimate or misrepresent LLM strategic competence.  
*What evidence would resolve*: Extending the counterfactual framework to repeated games with opponent pools (e.g., rotating algorithmic strategies, mixtures of LLMs with different prompting) and measuring adaptation speed, stability, and exploitability over longer horizons.

## Limitations
- History formatting in prompts is unspecified; different layouts could affect early‑round decisions and overall performance.
- Efficiency metric lacks clarity on whether input tokens are included and which tokenizer to use, hindering cross‑model comparisons.
- Opponent first‑move policies (e.g., TFT default action) are not defined, introducing variability in early interactions.

## Confidence
- Payoff counterfactuals reveal genuine strategic reasoning: **High**
- Opponent comprehension metric validity: **Medium**
- Advanced prompting often inefficient: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt format test**: Implement multiple history layouts (e.g., line‑per‑round vs. tabular) and run default PD on a subset of models; measure effects on cooperation rate and opponent comprehension.
2. **Efficiency re‑calculation**: Use each model’s correct tokenizer, include both input and output tokens, recompute efficiency; verify whether the token‑point trade‑off pattern persists.
3. **Claude “thinking” resolution**: Compare zero‑shot, explicit chain‑of‑thought, and any available Claude “thinking” API flag to determine which condition matches the reported results.
