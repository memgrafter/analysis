# Evaluating and Calibrating LLM Confidence on Questions with Multiple Correct Answers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07842
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07842
- **Reference count:** 18
- **Primary result:** Semantic Confidence Aggregation (SCA) achieves state-of-the-art calibration on multi‑answer QA with AUROC 81.2 (LLaMA‑3.1‑70B‑Instruct) without clustering or threshold tuning.

## Executive Summary
Existing LLM confidence calibration methods fail on questions with multiple correct answers because natural disagreement among equally valid responses is misinterpreted as uncertainty, leading to systematic underestimation of confidence. The authors introduce MACE, a benchmark of 12 000 factual QA pairs with 1, 2, 4, or 6 ground‑truth answers across six domains. Evaluations of 15 calibration methods across four LLM families (7B–72B) reveal a counterintuitive trend: accuracy rises with more correct answers, while confidence estimates drop, causing severe miscalibration in mixed‑answer settings. To address this, they propose Semantic Confidence Aggregation (SCA), which sums token‑level probabilities across all sampled answers instead of relying on a single response or consistency. SCA attains SOTA calibration (AUROC 81.2 on mixed [1,2] for LLaMA‑3.1‑70B‑Instruct) while matching best performance on single‑answer questions, and it operates effectively without clustering or threshold tuning, simplifying deployment.

## Method Summary
The paper constructs MACE, a high‑quality benchmark of 12 000 factual QA pairs. Each question is annotated with 1, 2, 4, or 6 correct answers, selected from Wikidata triplets and verified manually. Models generate answers; correctness and semantic equivalence are judged by GPT‑4o. Fifteen existing calibration methods are evaluated, spanning token‑probability, verbalized, consistency‑based, and double‑turn approaches. The proposed SCA samples multiple responses per question, computes each response’s sequence probability (product of token probabilities), and sums these probabilities over all semantic clusters (or all responses when τ = 0). This aggregation avoids the pitfall of consistency‑based methods by capturing the model’s overall confidence in the set of plausible answers. Performance is measured using AUROC and Expected Calibration Error (ECE).

## Key Results
- Accuracy increases with the number of correct answers, but estimated confidence consistently decreases, evidencing systematic miscalibration in mixed‑answer settings.
- SCA achieves AUROC 81.2 on mixed [1,2] and 79.2 on mixed [1,2,4] for LLaMA‑3.1‑70B‑Instruct, while matching the best performance on single‑answer questions.
- SCA works effectively without clustering or threshold tuning (τ = 0), offering a simple, robust solution.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Consistency‑based calibration systematically underestimates confidence on multi‑answer questions because it interprets natural disagreement among equally correct responses as uncertainty.
- **Mechanism:** These methods estimate confidence by measuring agreement among multiple sampled outputs. When a question has multiple valid answers, the model may generate different correct answers across samples, reducing consistency scores even though all samples are factually accurate. Lower consistency is misinterpreted as higher uncertainty.
- **Core assumption:** High agreement among sampled responses correlates with correctness; this assumption breaks when multiple correct answers exist and are sampled.
- **Evidence anchors:** [Abstract] “disagreement among equally correct responses can lead to low confidence estimates”; [Section 5.1] “as the number of ground‑truth answers increases, LLMs achieve higher QA accuracy but exhibit lower confidence”; [corpus] Neighboring papers (e.g., SelfCheckGPT) focus on single‑answer settings; multi‑answer failure is not addressed.
- **Break condition:** When all sampled responses converge to the same correct answer (e.g., in single‑answer questions or when the model’s answer space is narrow), consistency‑based calibration works well.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Larger models experience a sharper confidence decline on multi‑answer questions because they possess broader knowledge coverage, leading to greater diversity in sampled correct answers.
- **Mechanism:** As model scale increases, the set of facts the model knows expands. When multiple correct answers exist, larger models are more likely to generate a wider variety of correct answers across samples, increasing semantic diversity and thus lowering consistency‑based confidence. Smaller models have limited knowledge, often sampling from a narrower subset of correct answers, so consistency degrades less.
- **Core assumption:** Confidence from consistency is inversely related to the effective size of the model’s answer set; answer‑set size grows with model scale and question answer cardinality.
- **Evidence anchors:** [Section 5.3] LLaMA‑70B’s confidence drops 0.154 from 1a to 6a, while 8B drops only 0.056; [Figure 5] Knowledge coverage (number of high‑probability semantic clusters) increases with model size and answer count; [Figure 6] Larger models distribute probability mass over more clusters, indicating broader answer diversity; [corpus] Prior work (e.g., scaling laws) shows larger models have broader knowledge, but the link to calibration in multi‑answer settings is novel.
- **Break condition:** If a large model’s sampling is forced to a single answer (e.g., greedy decoding), the diversity disappears; or if the multi‑answer questions have answers that are semantically identical (e.g., synonyms), clustering may collapse them.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** SCA achieves robust calibration by aggregating token‑level probabilities across all sampled responses, effectively summing the model’s confidence in all plausible answers without requiring clustering or threshold tuning.
- **Mechanism:** Each sampled response’s generation probability (product of token probabilities) reflects how strongly the model favors that answer. SCA sums these probabilities over all samples. Because low‑probability, likely‑incorrect responses contribute little, the total approximates the probability mass assigned to the set of correct answers. This sum remains high whenever the model knows at least one correct answer with high confidence, regardless of whether multiple distinct correct answers are generated.
- **Core assumption:** Token‑level probabilities are meaningful: correct answers receive higher sequence probabilities than incorrect ones. Also, spurious responses have negligible probability, so summing all samples is safe.
- **Evidence anchors:** [Abstract] SCA “performs effectively without clustering or threshold tuning (τ=0)”; [Section 6] Equation (1)–(2) and Table 4 show τ=0 yields AUROC 81.2 on mixed [1,2] for LLaMA‑70B, matching or beating tuned baselines; [Figure 7] Illustration shows SCA confidence dominated by high‑probability clusters; low‑proba clusters add little; [corpus] Unlike SNCA/SFCA (which normalize or use frequency), SCA uses absolute probabilities; this design is justified by the empirical distribution of cluster probabilities (most clusters near zero).
- **Break condition:** If the model is miscalibrated at the token level (e.g., overconfident on wrong answers), SCA may overestimate confidence. Also, if many correct answers exist but each has very low individual probability, the sum could be low even though the model’s knowledge is broad.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:**  
Wikidata triplet collection -> Heuristic filtering -> Manual verification -> QA generation (templates for 1a/2a/4a/6a) -> MACE benchmark -> Model sampling (20 responses per question) -> Calibration method (SCA: sum token probabilities; Baselines: token‑prob, verbalized, consistency, double‑turn) -> Evaluation (GPT‑4o correctness labeling, semantic clustering, compute AUROC/ECE)

**Critical Path:**
1. Construct MACE: collect knowledge triplets, apply heuristic filters, manual verification, generate QA pairs with controlled answer cardinality, sample 500 per setting/domain.
2. Evaluate baselines: run 15 calibration methods on MACE across model families; collect confidence scores and correctness using GPT‑4o as judge.
3. Identify problem: analyze correlation between answer cardinality and confidence; observe systematic drop in confidence despite rising accuracy.
4. Design SCA: replace consistency with summation of sequence probabilities; hypothesize that absolute probabilities capture confidence in the answer set.
5. Validate: compare SCA (τ=0 and tuned) against baselines on mixed‑answer test sets; ablate threshold and clustering alternatives (SNCA, SFCA).

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- MACE construction: manual verification ensures quality but is costly; heuristic filtering reduces burden but may discard rare facts. Domain selection balances verifiability and diversity.
- SCA simplicity: avoiding clustering and threshold tuning eases deployment but may sacrifice peak performance; tuned τ can help in some cases.
- Evaluation: using GPT‑4o as an automatic judge scales but may embody its own biases; human validation (300 instances) shows high agreement (κ=0.92).

**Failure Signatures:**
- Consistency‑based methods: AUROC decreases monotonically as mixed‑answer ratio increases.
- Verbalized methods: tend to be overconfident on single‑answer questions but become under‑confident on multi‑answer; poor discrimination (low AUROC).
- SCA edge cases: if the model assigns high probability to many incorrect answers (e.g., on out‑of‑distribution questions), SCA may produce over‑confident scores. Also, on extremely long responses, token‑level probabilities might be numerically unstable.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. Reproduce confidence drop: For LLaMA‑70B, run Consistency on 1a vs. 6a subsets of MACE; expect confidence ~0.51 → ~0.36 while accuracy rises.
2. Knowledge coverage analysis: Sample 20 responses per question, cluster semantically (e.g., using sentence‑BERT), compute cluster probabilities; count clusters with P(Cₘ) > 0.1. Verify coverage increases with model size and answer cardinality.
3. SCA validation: Implement SCA (τ=0) and baselines on mixed [1,2,4,6] test set; report AUROC. Also run sensitivity analysis varying τ (Figure 8) to confirm flat optimum at 0.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Multilingual extension:** How do confidence calibration methods generalize to multilingual or cross‑lingual multi‑answer QA settings? The paper explicitly notes its evaluation is restricted to English, leaving unknown whether the observed miscalibration pattern holds across languages.
2. **Training‑based methods:** Can training‑based calibration methods be effectively adapted to maintain calibration in mixed‑answer settings, and how do they compare to training‑free approaches like SCA? The paper focuses exclusively on training‑free methods and does not evaluate any training‑based approach on MACE.
3. **Sampling budget trade‑off:** What is the optimal trade‑off between sampling budget (number of responses) and calibration performance for SCA, especially under varying answer cardinalities? The paper uses a fixed 20 samples per question and does not systematically vary the count.
4. **Generalization to open‑ended tasks:** Will the confidence‑underestimation phenomenon persist in open‑ended, long‑form, or opinion‑based generation tasks beyond short factual QA? The paper acknowledges its scope is limited to short factual responses.
5. **Internal model representations:** What are the internal model representations (e.g., hidden states, attention patterns) that explain why larger models exhibit sharper confidence declines with increasing answer cardinality? The analysis remains at the output distribution level, without probing internal mechanics.

## Limitations
- Semantic clustering implementation details (embedding model, similarity threshold) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication of baselines and SCA variants.
- GPT‑4o evaluation prompts and settings are missing; despite reported high human agreement, automatic evaluation may introduce bias.
- SCA assumes token‑level probabilities are well‑calibrated; if not, confidence sums could be over‑estimated, particularly on out‑of‑distribution questions.
