# Layer-wise Positional Bias in Short-Context Language Modeling
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.04098
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04098
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Positional importance profiles are stable across diverse texts and invariant to lexical scrambling (r > 0.99); recency bias increases monotonically with layer depth while primacy bias is weak and diminishes; early layers preferentially weight content words over function words, but this differentiation fades in deeper layers; and early layers dominate contributions for most words, whereas later layers concentrate on the most recent token.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how positional bias—systematic preferences for specific input positions—manifests across layers in autoregressive transformer language models operating on short contexts. The authors introduce an attribution framework based on layer conductance combined with a sliding-window approach to measure each layer's causal contribution to next-token prediction for every relative position. Analysis across multiple models and diverse textual corpora reveals that positional importance profiles are highly stable and invariant to lexical scrambling. Recency bias (weight on immediately preceding tokens) grows monotonically with layer depth, while primacy bias (preference for initial tokens) is weak and fades. Moreover, early layers show a marked preference for content words (nouns, verbs) over function words, which diminishes in deeper layers that become increasingly driven by positional recency. These findings suggest a functional specialization: early layers encode lexical-semantic information, whereas later layers consolidate and rely heavily on recent context for prediction.

## Method Summary
The authors compute token-level conductance for each transformer layer using Integrated Gradients (via Captum) with a zero-embedding baseline, attributing the model’s next-token logit for a given window back to input tokens. Conductance values are normalized within each sliding window (length P=10 tokens, stride 1) to enable fair comparison across positions. Subword tokens are summed to obtain word-level conductance, yielding a three-dimensional tensor C of shape (layers × words × positions). Averaging C over words produces layer-wise positional importance profiles (¯Cℓ,p). Additional analyses compute recency/primacy fractions (proportion of a layer’s conductance assigned to the last and first 20% of positions), average conductance by part-of-speech categories, and identify which layer contributes most to each word-position pair. The same procedure is applied to both natural texts and a scrambled control to test invariance to lexical content.

## Key Results
- Positional importance profiles exhibit remarkable stability across diverse natural texts and remain nearly identical after lexical scrambling (mean pairwise Pearson correlation r > 0.99).
- Recency bias increases monotonically with layer depth, while primacy bias is weak and diminishes in most models.
- Early layers assign higher conductance to content words (nouns, verbs) than to function words regardless of position; this differentiation fades in deeper layers that become more uniformly positional.
- Early layers dominate contributions for most words at all positions, whereas later layers concentrate increasingly on the most recent token.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The observed patterns arise from three interconnected mechanisms:

1. **Recency consolidation through depth**  
   *Claim:* Deeper layers exhibit stronger recency bias, focusing conductance on immediately preceding tokens.  
   *Mechanism:* Successive attention layers progressively enrich recent token representations with contextual information. Early layers distribute importance broadly across positions; later layers rely on these enriched representations, needing less input from earlier positions.  
   *Core assumption:* Layer conductance accurately isolates each layer’s causal contribution to next-token prediction, and within-window normalization enables fair positional comparison.  
   *Evidence anchors:* Abstract (recency increases monotonically); Section 4.1 (Figure 2 shows concentration on last position strengthens with depth; Figure 4a quantifies monotonic RecFracℓ).  
   *Break condition:* If longer contexts or encoder-decoder architectures break monotonicity, the mechanism may be limited to short-context decoders.

2. **Weak, decaying primacy bias**  
   *Claim:* Primacy bias is weak, diminishes with depth, and varies by architecture.  
   *Mechanism:* Early layers may anchor on initial tokens (e.g., attention sink), but this signal attenuates as representations deepen; later layers prioritize recency for prediction.  
   *Core assumption:* The sliding-window design controls lexical confounds, so residual primacy peaks reflect intrinsic positional handling.  
   *Evidence anchors:* Abstract (primacy weak and diminishes); Section 4.2 (Figure 4b shows decreasing PrimFracℓ for most models; GPT-Neo oscillates).  
   *Break condition:* If scrambling or diverse texts induce stronger primacy, the pattern would be input-dependent rather than architectural.

3. **Fading content-function differentiation**  
   *Claim:* Early layers preferentially weight content words over function words across all positions; this fades in deeper layers.  
   *Mechanism:* Early layers perform lexical/semantic role identification, assigning higher conductance to content-bearing tokens. Deeper layers integrate this information and shift toward position-driven processing (recency), reducing sensitivity to word type.  
   *Core assumption:* POS grouping and position-averaging capture word-type sensitivity fairly.  
   *Evidence anchors:* Abstract (early layers weight content words; differentiation fades); Section 4.3 (Figure 5 shows early layers (blue) have higher conductance for content vs. function POS; later layers (red) converge toward uniform 1/P).  
   *Break condition:* If different tokenization or POS tagging reverses the pattern, the effect may be an artifact.

## Foundational Learning
### Conductance Attribution
- **Why needed:** Quantifies each layer’s causal contribution to next-token prediction, unlike attention scores which are correlational.
- **Quick check:** How does conductance differ from attention weights? (Path-integrated vs. routing; attribution vs. correlation.)

### Sliding-Window Isolation
- **Why needed:** Ensures each relative position contains the same set of words across inputs, disentangling positional bias from lexical/semantic confounds.
- **Quick check:** Why is lexical scrambling necessary? (To show profiles persist when syntax/semantics are destroyed, isolating position as template.)

### Positional Importance Profiles
- **Why needed:** Summarizes a layer’s distribution of importance across input positions (¯Cℓ,p), enabling comparison of depth-dependent bias.
- **Quick check:** What do primacy/recency fractions measure, and why compute them per layer? (Proportion of conductance assigned to early/late positions; reveals layer specialization.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Pretrained autoregressive decoder-only transformers (GPT-2, GPT-Neo, Phi-2, Llama-3) with causal masking -> token embedding layer -> sequence of transformer blocks (each: layer norm, attention, residual, feed-forward, residual) -> final layer norm -> output logits; external tools: Captum (conductance), spaCy (POS tagging), sliding-window segmenter.

### Critical path
1. **Preprocess:** Tokenize natural texts; split into overlapping windows of 10 tokens (stride 1); remove boundary words so each relative position sees the same word set; also generate scrambled version via random word shuffling.
2. **Compute conductance:** For each window, run model through Captum’s LayerIntegratedGradients to attribute the next-token logit to each input token’s embedding. Sum conductance over subwords to word level. Store in tensor C.
3. **Aggregate:** Average C over words to obtain layer-position profiles ¯Cℓ,p; compute primacy/recency fractions; average conductance by POS categories; compute layer dominance maps.
4. **Validate:** Correlate profiles across texts and against scrambled control; check monotonic recency trend; test POS differentiation.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Window size (P):** Smaller P (10) offers fine position resolution but limited context; larger P (50) tests robustness but increases compute. Patterns hold across P.
- **Token vs. word level:** Word-level aggregation reduces tokenizer artifacts but loses subword nuance; position-averaging for POS may obscure position-word interactions.
- **Model selection:** Diverse decoder-only architectures reveal invariance; however, findings may not transfer to encoder-decoder or novel architectures.

### Failure signatures
- **Low cross-text correlation (r < 0.99):** Indicates content confounds or implementation error.
- **Non-monotonic recency:** Recency fraction does not increase with depth; suggests flawed conductance or atypical model behavior.
- **POS pattern reversal:** Function words show higher early-layer conductance; may be due to tagging inconsistencies.
- **Memory overflow:** With large models (e.g., 8B), sliding windows exhaust GPU memory; need to process sequentially or reduce batch size.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate Figure 2:** Run sliding-window conductance on “Pie Man” for GPT-2; verify that recency peak strengthens with depth and primacy peak appears in early layers.
2. **Scrambling invariance:** Compute mean pairwise correlation between natural and scrambled Pie Man profiles; expect r > 0.99 across layers.
3. **POS differentiation:** For each layer, compute average conductance for content vs. function POS (position-averaged); confirm early layers show higher content conductance and that later layers converge toward uniform weighting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the layer-wise positional bias generalize to long-context scenarios and tasks beyond next-word prediction (e.g., QA, summarization)?
2. How do specific architectural choices (e.g., RoPE vs. ALiBi, attention patterns) causally influence the evolution of positional bias across layers?
3. Do alternative attribution methods (e.g., attention flow, gradient-based techniques) produce consistent layer-wise positional importance profiles, or is the method choice decisive?
4. What is the causal role of specific subcomponents (e.g., attention heads, feed-forward network layers) within each transformer layer in driving the monotonic increase in recency bias?
5. How do finer-grained linguistic features (e.g., argument structure, semantic classes) interact with positional bias across layers, beyond coarse POS categories?

## Limitations
- Unspecified baseline construction (zero embedding vs. padding) and precise layer output (residual stream vs. block output) may affect conductance magnitudes.
- Ambiguity in normalization timing (before vs. after subword aggregation) influences word-level scores.
- Analysis limited to short contexts (P=10) and decoder-only models; generalizability to longer contexts or encoder-decoder architectures is uncertain.
- POS categorization into “content” vs. “function” lacks a standard mapping and may vary with tagger or tokenization.

## Confidence
- **Recency monotonicity:** High confidence (consistent across four architectures, multiple texts, and scrambling).
- **Primacy weakness/diminishing:** Medium confidence (holds for most models but GPT-Neo oscillates; limited text corpus).
- **Early-layer content preference:** Medium confidence (pattern aligns with lexical processing but depends on spaCy tags and aggregation).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate conductance with a zero-embedding baseline and verify that normalization is applied before subword aggregation; confirm recency fraction still increases monotonically.
2. Extend analysis to an encoder-decoder model (e.g., T5) and test longer window sizes (e.g., P=20, 50) to assess depth-recency relationship under varied conditions.
3. Rerun POS analysis using alternative linguistic tagsets (e.g., Universal Dependencies) or a character-level tokenizer; check robustness of early-layer content preference.
