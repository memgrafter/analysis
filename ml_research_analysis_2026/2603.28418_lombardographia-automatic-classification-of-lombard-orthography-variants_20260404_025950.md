# LombardoGraphia: Automatic Classification of Lombard Orthography Variants

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.28418
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28418
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Best model achieves 96.06% overall accuracy (SVM with combined n‑grams), but average per‑class accuracy is 85.78% (Logistic Regression with byte features) due to severe class imbalance.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces LombardoGraphia, a curated corpus of 11,186 Lombard Wikipedia samples labeled across 9 orthographic variants, to address the lack of a unified orthography that hinders Lombard NLP development. Training 24 traditional and neural classifiers, the authors achieve a peak overall accuracy of 96.06% with an SVM using combined byte, character, and word n‑gram features. However, extreme class imbalance (major classes ~94%) causes poor performance on minority orthographies; the best average per‑class accuracy—85.78%—comes from Logistic Regression with byte features, yet some rare classes still see near‑zero accuracy.

## Method Summary
The LombardoGraphia corpus was built by extracting Lombard text from a Wikipedia dump (July 2025) via `wikiextractor`, followed by manual filtering to remove non‑Lombard content, boilerplate, short lines, and bot‑generated articles. The resulting 11,186 samples (lines) with orthography tags were split into 80% train, 10% validation, and 10% test. For traditional models, TF‑IDF vectorization produced features from byte (1‑4‑grams), character (1‑4‑grams), word (1‑4‑grams), or their concatenation (max 10 000 features). Neural models processed sequences of length 200 using either a character vocabulary or raw bytes. Four traditional classifiers (Logistic Regression, SVM/LinearSVC, MultinomialNB, Random Forest) and four neural architectures (CNN‑wide, Deep CNN, Bi‑LSTM, Transformer) were trained with `class_weight='balanced'` to mitigate imbalance. Model selection used validation accuracy; final evaluation reported overall and per‑class accuracy.

## Key Results
- Best overall accuracy: 96.06% (SVM with combined byte, character, and word n‑gram features).
- Best average per‑class accuracy: 85.78% (Logistic Regression with byte features), indicating stronger minority‑class performance.
- Severe disparity: Minority classes with <100 samples (e.g., BERGDUC, LSI) sometimes achieve 0% accuracy in several models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: N‑gram feature combination
Orthographic variants differ in consistent spelling conventions (diacritics, vowel representations, etc.). Byte, character, and word n‑grams capture these patterns at different granularities without explicit linguistic knowledge; their combination synthesizes multiple evidence sources. Evidence: The SVM with combined features achieves the highest overall accuracy (96.06%). Break condition: For minority classes with very few examples, n‑gram statistics become sparse, limiting pattern capture.

### Mechanism 2: Balanced class weighting
All classifiers used `class_weight='balanced'` to penalize errors on rare classes more heavily, forcing the model to allocate decision boundary capacity to minorities. Evidence: Logistic Regression with byte features achieves the best average per‑class accuracy (85.78%), outperforming others on minority classes. Break condition: Extremely scarce samples (e.g., 3 for LSI) still yield insufficient training signal.

### Mechanism 3: Orthographic similarity between variants
Variants sharing geographic/historical roots (e.g., Western vs. Eastern Lombard) exhibit similar graphemic rules and lexical stock, causing inherent confusion. Misclassifications reflect true typological proximity. Evidence: The most frequent error is LOCC misclassified as MILCLASS (56 errors), and similar patterns appear among related orthographies. Break condition: Texts mixing conventions or using highly idiosyncratic spelling not seen in training fail the model’s similarity‑based heuristics.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Ingestion & Filtering -> Feature Extraction -> Model Training -> Evaluation

**Critical path:** Raw Wikipedia XML dump -> filtered corpus (orthography‑tagged lines) -> feature vectors (TF‑IDF or sequences) -> trained model -> test evaluation. The filtering step (removing non‑Lombard, boilerplate, short lines) is most time‑intensive and quality‑critical.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Traditional vs. neural: Traditional models (Logistic Regression, SVM) are data‑efficient and better for minority classes; neural models (CNN, LSTM, Transformer) scale poorly with small data.  
- Feature combination: Combining byte, character, and word n‑grams boosts majority‑class accuracy but may hurt per‑class balance.  
- Encoding level: Byte‑level avoids tokenization issues but increases feature dimensionality; character‑level is slightly more interpretable.

**Failure signatures:**  
- 0% accuracy on any minority class indicates complete failure to learn patterns (common in Naive Bayes, neural models on SL/NOL).  
- High confidence but wrong predictions on untagged data suggest overfitting to majority‑class priors.  
- Confusion between LOCC/MILCLASS or BREMOD/LORUNIF is expected; if absent, model may be over‑regularized.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline check: Train Logistic Regression with byte n‑grams only. Expected: ~93% overall, ~85% avg class accuracy. Validates feature pipeline.  
2. Feature combination test: Train SVM with byte+char+word n‑grams. Expected: highest overall accuracy (~96%), but avg class accuracy may drop vs. single‑feature baselines.  
3. Minority‑class stress test: Evaluate best traditional model (Logistic Regression with byte features) on minority classes only (SL, NOL, CRES, BREMOD, BERGDUC). Expected: significant accuracy drop (>15% from majority), confirming imbalance impact.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the LombardoGraphia corpus be expanded to better represent minority orthographies, and what data collection strategies (e.g., targeted scraping, crowdsourcing) would most effectively increase their coverage and balance?
2. Would shifting from line‑level to document‑level classification improve overall and minority‑class accuracy, and what model architectures or feature aggregation methods are most effective for longer Lombard texts?
3. To what extent do the trained models generalize to non‑Wikipedia text (e.g., digitized books, social media, user‑generated content) that may contain informal, mixed, or subjective orthographies not seen in the training corpus?
4. Can unsupervised clustering or multi‑label classification methods better capture the continuum of Lombard orthographies, especially for texts that mix features from multiple systems or use non‑standard spellings?
5. How effective are current models at detecting non‑Lombard text (e.g., Italian, English, other languages), and what architectural or data‑augmentation modifications are needed to integrate robust non‑Lombard filtering as a preliminary step?

## Limitations
- Generalization beyond Wikipedia: The corpus is exclusively Wikipedia text; performance on other Lombard domains (e.g., social media, legacy literature) is unknown.
- Minority class viability: Classes with <100 samples show near‑zero accuracy in some models, questioning practical utility for comprehensive NLP resource development.
- Annotation reliability: Tags derive from Wikipedia contributor choices, not expert linguistic annotation; potential errors may inflate confusion between historically similar variants.

## Confidence
- N‑gram feature effectiveness: High (supported by ablation results and established practice).
- Balanced weighting mitigates imbalance: Medium (evidence mixed; best average class accuracy achieved but many models still fail catastrophically on minorities).
- Orthographic similarity causing confusion: High (confusion patterns match known dialectological relationships).
