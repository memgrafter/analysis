# Video-based Music Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07063
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07063
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: EMSYNC generates multi-instrument symbolic music from video with emotional and temporal synchronization, outperforming state-of-the-art methods in user studies.

## Executive Summary
EMSYNC is a system that generates symbolic music (MIDI) from arbitrary videos, ensuring both emotional and temporal alignment. It comprises VEMOCLAP, a video emotion classifier that fuses frozen pretrained multimodal features to predict six discrete emotions, and a transformer-based music generator conditioned on continuous valence-arousal values (obtained via a Gaussian mixture mapping) and boundary offset encodings derived from scene cuts. Trained on a new large-scale emotion-labeled MIDI dataset (34,791 samples), EMSYNC produces multi-instrument music that matches the video’s emotional tone and rhythmically follows its structure. In user studies (n=153), it outperforms Video2Music and CMT across all evaluated metrics (music richness, quality, emotion match, timing match, overall preference). Component models also achieve state-of-the-art results on Ekman-6 (65.28% accuracy) and MovieNet genre classification (66.02% mAP).

## Method Summary
EMSYNC’s pipeline begins with video preprocessing: frames are extracted at 1 fps and audio resampled to 16 kHz mono. VEMOCLAP then processes the multimodal signals: CLIP features from frames, BEATs features from audio chunks, facial expression predictions, and OCR/ASR text with sentiment analysis. These frozen features are fused via cross-attention and classified into six Ekman emotions. Meanwhile, scene cuts are detected using FFmpeg with a pixel-difference threshold (27%) and a minimum separation of 4 seconds. The discrete emotion probabilities are mapped to continuous valence-arousal by constructing a Gaussian mixture using predefined means and variances from psychological literature. For each token during music generation, a boundary offset is computed as the time until the next scene cut (clamped to a maximum, e.g., 1 s). The transformer decoder (11 layers, 8 heads, 512-D) processes event-based tokens (TIME-SHIFT, NOTE-ON, NOTE-OFF, CHORD, etc.) and is conditioned on the emotion vector and the offset encoding (via an MLP and concatenation with positional encodings). Training uses the Lakh-MIDI dataset annotated with Spotify-derived valence/arousal (34,791 samples) with data augmentation (pitch transposition). The loss weights CHORD tokens 10× to encourage alignment. During inference, nucleus sampling (p=0.7, temperature 1.2) generates tokens until the summed TIME-SHIFT durations reach the video length plus a 3-second fade-out. The token stream is converted to MIDI, synthesized to audio with Fluidsynth (FluidR3 soundfont), and a fade-out is applied.

## Key Results
- VEMOCLAP achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on Ekman-6 (65.28%) and MovieNet genre classification (66.02% mAP).
- In a user study with 153 participants, EMSYNC significantly outperforms Video2Music and CMT across all metrics: music richness, quality, emotion match, timing match, and overall preference.
- The system introduces a new large-scale emotion-labeled MIDI dataset (34,791 samples) enabling robust training.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Emotion mapping via Gaussian mixtures
EMSYNC converts discrete emotion probabilities from VEMOCLAP into continuous valence-arousal values using a Gaussian mixture model. For each of the six Ekman emotions, a Gaussian distribution (with means and variances from Russell & Mehrabian, 1977) is weighted by the predicted probability; sampling from the mixture yields a continuous emotional state. This provides fine-grained emotional conditioning beyond categorical labels. Evidence includes the abstract and Section 6.1, and ablations show continuous conditioning outperforms discrete alternatives. Potential failure: inaccurate VEMOCLAP predictions or inappropriate Gaussian parameters can misalign emotions.

### Mechanism 2: Boundary offset encodings for temporal alignment
To synchronize music with video scene changes, EMSYNC computes for each token the time remaining until the next scene cut (boundary offset), projected via an MLP and combined with positional encodings. This soft guidance encourages the transformer to place strong chords (CHORD tokens) near scene boundaries. Scene cuts are detected with FFmpeg (pixel difference >27%, min separation 4s). The model is trained with a higher loss weight for CHORD tokens. Core assumption: musical chords structurally align with scene cuts. Evidence: described in Section 4.1 and 6.2; novel compared to continuous motion features. Break conditions: too many/few scene cuts or insufficient loss weighting can degrade alignment.

### Mechanism 3: Frozen pretrained features with cross-attention fusion
VEMOCLAP leverages frozen pretrained networks (CLIP, BEATs, facial expression classifier, OCR/ASR) to extract rich multimodal semantics. These features are fused via cross-attention modules that model correspondences across modalities, and only the fusion layers and classifier are trained. This design reduces computational cost and overfitting while benefiting from large-scale pretraining. It yields state-of-the-art results on Ekman-6 (65.28% accuracy) and MovieNet (66.02% mAP). Evidence: abstract, Section 5.2.2.2, and 5.2.2.6. Break condition: pretrained features may be irrelevant to emotion or have mismatched temporal resolutions.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map** (data flow):  
Video input → VEMOCLAP → discrete emotion probabilities → Gaussian mixture → continuous valence/arousal (conditioning).  
Video input → Scene cut detector (FFmpeg) → scene cut timestamps → boundary offset calculator → boundary offset encodings (conditioning).  
Both conditionings + token stream → Music generator (transformer) → event-based tokens → MIDI converter → Audio synthesizer (Fluidsynth) → audio output.

**Critical path** (inference):
1. Extract frames (1 fps) and audio (16 kHz mono) from video.
2. Run VEMOCLAP to obtain emotion probabilities (6 Ekman classes).
3. Detect scene cuts with FFmpeg (pixel difference >27%, min separation 4s).
4. Map emotion probabilities to continuous valence/arousal using Gaussian mixture.
5. For each token generation step, compute boundary offset (time until next scene cut, clamped).
6. Condition transformer on emotion vector and boundary offset; generate tokens via nucleus sampling (p=0.7, temperature 1.2) until total TIME-SHIFT durations reach video duration + 3s fade-out.
7. Convert tokens to MIDI, synthesize with Fluidsynth, apply fade-out.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **Frozen features vs fine-tuning**: Freezing reduces overfitting and compute but may limit adaptation to emotion-specific cues.
- **Continuous vs discrete emotion conditioning**: Continuous allows fine-grained control but adds mapping complexity; discrete is simpler but coarse.
- **Soft vs hard temporal alignment**: Boundary offsets provide soft guidance preserving rhythm; hard forcing would disrupt musical flow.
- **Event-based vs grid-based timing**: Event-based preserves expressive timing but complicates alignment; offsets bridge the gap.
- **Multi-instrument generation**: Increases richness but enlarges vocabulary and model complexity.

**Failure signatures**:
- **Emotion mismatch**: Music emotion does not match video → check VEMOCLAP predictions, Gaussian mapping, and training labels.
- **Poor temporal sync**: Chords not near scene cuts → verify scene cut detection parameters, loss weight for CHORD tokens, offset calculations.
- **Degenerate outputs**: Repetitive or nonsensical music → inspect sampling parameters (nucleus p, temperature), training data quality, model capacity.
- **High compute during inference**: May arise from using many pretrained extractors → consider reducing modalities or optimizing extraction.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Train VEMOCLAP on Ekman-6 train split using cross-attention fusion; evaluate on test split; expect >65% accuracy.
2. Train music generator with emotion-only conditioning on Lakh-MIDI (Spotify-labeled); compare vanilla, discrete-token, continuous-token, continuous-concat models via NLL and emotion regression error; continuous-concat should best capture emotion.
3. Full system integration: Run EMSYNC on sample videos from EmoMV-C and Ads; compare against Video2Music and CMT via user study; expect EMSYNC consistently best across metrics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Large-scale emotion-labeled MIDI datasets**  
   *Question*: How can we acquire large-scale, multi-instrument MIDI datasets with continuous emotion labels?  
   *Basis*: Explicitly mentioned in the introduction.  
   *Why unresolved*: Existing emotion-labeled MIDI datasets are too small for deep neural networks with millions of parameters.  
   *Resolution*: A new dataset containing orders of magnitude more samples, created by automated labeling (e.g., audio features, lyrics) and validated by human raters.

2. **Robust audio bandwidth extension**  
   *Question*: How can audio bandwidth extension models be made robust to any low-pass filter, ensuring generalization to real-world degradation?  
   *Basis*: Explicitly raised regarding overfitting to synthetic data in audio generation.  
   *Why unresolved*: Although data augmentation with multiple filters improves performance, models still show a gap on unseen filters.  
   *Resolution*: Consistent high signal-to-noise ratio across diverse unseen filters in testing, without compromising seen-filter performance.

3. **High-fidelity multi-instrument audio from MIDI**  
   *Question*: How can high-fidelity, multi-instrument audio be directly generated from symbolic MIDI representations without paired human performance data?  
   *Basis*: Inferred from the absence of multi-instrument MIDI-audio paired datasets and the challenge of producing realistic audio.  
   *Why unresolved*: Current synthesis relies on simple soundfonts or piano-only models; no end-to-end method for convincing multi-instrument audio generation.  
   *Resolution*: Objective and subjective evaluations showing generated audio is perceptually indistinguishable from real performances across instruments/ensembles.

4. **Scalable video emotion classification**  
   *Question*: How can video emotion classification be scaled to large, diverse datasets while maintaining accuracy and handling full-length videos?  
   *Basis*: Inferred from the scarcity of large video emotion datasets and the use of a small fixed number of frames due to overfitting.  
   *Why unresolved*: The largest widely used dataset (Ekman-6) has only 1,637 videos, limiting model capacity and preventing processing longer videos.  
   *Resolution*: A large-scale, diverse video emotion dataset and a model that processes whole videos end-to-end, matching/exceeding SOTA accuracy without overfitting.

## Limitations
- Fixed Gaussian parameters for emotion mapping may not generalize to all video content; scene cut detection threshold is heuristic and may miss/add spurious boundaries.
- Music annotations via Spotify API are automatic and potentially noisy, especially for non-Western or classical music.
- User study is moderate (n=153) and restricted to trailers/ads, limiting the generalizability of performance claims.

## Confidence
- **VEMOCLAP achieves SOTA on Ekman-6 and MovieNet**: High
- **Continuous emotion conditioning and boundary offset mechanisms are key advances**: Medium
- **EMSYNC outperforms baselines in user studies**: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Ablate emotion mapping: Replace Gaussian mixture with a linear projection from discrete emotions to valence/arousal. Evaluate user-rated emotion match and objective tag alignment (KL) on a held-out set.
2. Stress-test boundary offsets: Vary scene cut threshold (0.2–0.35) and minimum separation (2–6s). Measure alignment (chord-cut proximity) and user timing preference.
3. Cross-domain test: Run EMSYNC on new video domains (e.g., vlogs, documentaries) without retraining. Compare emotion/timing metrics to the original test set to assess overfitting.
