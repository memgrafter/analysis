# Completing Missing Annotation: Multi-Agent Debate for Accurate and Scalable Relevant Assessment for IR Benchmarks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06526
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06526
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: DREAM fills missing relevance annotations with 95.2% accuracy while reducing human effort to 3.5%.

## Executive Summary
Information retrieval (IR) benchmarks often suffer from incomplete relevance annotations—many truly relevant document chunks remain unlabeled, leading to systematic underestimation of retrieval systems and misalignment with downstream generation tasks. This paper introduces DREAM, a multi-agent debate framework where two LLM agents with opposing initial stances (relevant vs. irrelevant) engage in reciprocal critique over multiple rounds. Agreement between agents is used as a high-confidence signal for automatic labeling; disagreements are escalated to human annotators with the debate history as context. Applied to BEIR and RobustQA, DREAM constructs BRIDGE by adding 29,824 missing relevant chunks, a 428% increase. Re-evaluating 25 retrieval systems on BRIDGE shows prior benchmarks underestimate performance; retrieval-generation alignment (RAGAlign@10) improves from 0.70 to 0.84. DREAM achieves 95.2% balanced accuracy with only 3.5% human effort.

## Method Summary
DREAM addresses annotation holes by automating relevance labeling via debate. For a given query and its gold answers, candidate chunks are pooled from 25 retrieval systems (sparse, dense, rerank, rewrite). An initial filter using a 3-LLM vote discards obviously irrelevant chunks. Each remaining chunk is labeled by two LLM agents: one initially arguing relevance, the other irrelevance. They exchange arguments over up to two rounds, using full history. If both agents converge to the same label, it is accepted; otherwise the case is escalated to human annotators (MTurk) who adjudicate based on the debate transcript. The process yields a high-accuracy labeled dataset (BRIDGE) with minimal human involvement. The paper evaluates DREAM on 700 expert-labeled triples, reporting balanced accuracy and escalation ratio. It then measures the impact of BRIDGE on retrieval evaluation bias via growth rate, marginal contribution, and RAGAlign.

## Key Results
- DREAM achieves 95.2% balanced accuracy on relevance labeling with only 3.5% of cases escalated to humans.
- BRIDGE fills 29,824 missing relevant chunks across 3,657 queries, a 428% increase in coverage.
- Retrieval-generation alignment (RAGAlign@10) increases from 0.70 to 0.84 when systems are re-evaluated on BRIDGE, demonstrating that annotation holes cause significant misalignment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
DREAM leverages the adversarial nature of multi-agent debate to surface nuanced reasoning about relevance. By forcing agents to adopt opposing stances, each must critically examine the chunk's support for any gold answer, reducing superficial or biased judgments. Iterative argumentation allows agents to refine their positions based on the other's points, leading to convergence on a consensus when evidence is clear. Agreement serves as a high-confidence signal: if two independently initialized agents arrive at the same conclusion after debate, the likelihood of error is low. Disagreements, which likely involve ambiguous or borderline cases, are escalated to humans, thus allocating expert effort where it matters most. This mechanism efficiently scales labeling while maintaining accuracy. The reliance on gold answers ensures the relevance definition is consistent with downstream RAG tasks, where a generator must produce answers grounded in retrieved content. The 3-LLM filter further reduces load by pre-removing trivially irrelevant chunks, allowing the debate to focus on uncertain candidates.

## Foundational Learning

### Annotation Holes
**Why needed:** Missing relevant chunks in IR benchmarks cause evaluation bias and retrieval-generation misalignment.
**Quick check:** For a query, compare its gold answers against retrieved top-k chunks; if some gold-supported chunks are absent from the judgment pool, a hole exists.

### Multi-Agent Debate
**Why needed:** Provides a scalable, high-accuracy labeling protocol by exploiting agent disagreement and consensus.
**Quick check:** Run a two-agent debate on a small labeled set; compute accuracy when agents agree vs. disagree to verify correlation.

### Balanced Accuracy
**Why needed:** Accounts for class imbalance (fewer relevant chunks) and gives a fair performance measure.
**Quick check:** On a test set, compute (TPR + TNR)/2 to obtain bAcc; compare with standard accuracy if classes are imbalanced.

### Escalation Ratio
**Why needed:** Quantifies the proportion of cases requiring human intervention, reflecting system efficiency.
**Quick check:** Count the number of escalated instances divided by total instances; lower indicates higher automation.

### RAGAlign
**Why needed:** Measures consistency between retrieval effectiveness and generation success, key for RAG evaluation.
**Quick check:** For each query, compute binary retrieval success (e.g., Hit@10) and binary generation success (whether retrieved content supports the answer); compute proportion of matching outcomes.

### Candidate Pool Diversity
**Why needed:** A diverse set of retrievers ensures broader coverage of relevant chunks, enabling hole discovery.
**Quick check:** Incrementally add retrievers to a fixed query set and plot the growth rate of newly found relevant chunks; saturation indicates sufficient diversity.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Input queries + gold answers -> Candidate retrieval (25 systems) -> Merge & deduplicate -> 3-LLM irrelevant filter -> For each remaining chunk: DREAM debate (2 agents, max 2 rounds) -> Agreement? -> Yes: automatic label (consensus) -> No: escalate to human -> Final labeled dataset (BRIDGE)

### Critical Path
The sequential debates in DREAM dominate runtime; each query-chunk pair requires multiple LLM calls. The overall pipeline is memory-bound by candidate pool size but compute-bound by debate rounds. Parallelizing debates across chunks and queries is essential for scalability.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Agent homogeneity vs. heterogeneity:** Default uses two identical Llama3.3-70B-Instruct agents; mixing models was found less effective in limited experiments but might help in other settings.
- **Number of rounds:** Fixed R=2 balances accuracy and cost; more rounds may cause spurious consensus without gains.
- **Filter strictness:** The 3-LLM irrelevant filter reduces debate load but risks discarding borderline relevant chunks (false negatives). Threshold choice affects hole-filling coverage.
- **Escalation policy:** DREAM escalates only on disagreement; this minimizes human effort but may propagate agent errors when they spuriously agree.

### Failure Signatures
- **Spurious agreement:** Agents agree on incorrect labels; detectable by sampling agreed cases and verifying against gold (expected ≤5% error).
- **Excessive escalation:** High disagreement rate (>10%) indicates agents are brittle or prompts are ambiguous; inspect debate logs for circular reasoning.
- **Pool saturation failure:** If growth rate does not plateau as retrievers are added, the candidate pool lacks diversity, risking unfilled holes.
- **RAGAlign mismatch:** Unexpectedly low improvement in alignment suggests labeled holes may not be truly relevant or that the generator/evaluator setup is inconsistent.

### First Experiments
1. **Baseline replication:** Run DREAM on the 700-sample gold-labeled set; measure balanced accuracy and escalation ratio; compare against LLMJudge and LARA to confirm reported numbers.
2. **Filter sensitivity sweep:** Vary the 3-LLM filter threshold (e.g., number of models voting irrelevant) and evaluate impact on number of holes filled and final labeling accuracy.
3. **Debate round ablation:** Test R=1,2,3 on a subset; record agreement rates and spurious agreement to validate that two rounds are optimal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does increasing model diversity in terms of architecture, size, or training data improve the accuracy and reliability of multi-agent debate for relevance assessment?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] From Section 3.2.3 and Appendix B.4, the authors experiment with increasing diversity by using more agents, heterogeneous LLM families, and higher temperature but find no improvement over their default setting (two homogeneous Llama3.3-70B-Instruct agents). However, these experiments are limited to two model families (Llama and Qwen) and specific configurations. It remains unclear whether other diversity dimensions (e.g., models with different training objectives, or including smaller models) could yield benefits.
- **Why unresolved:** The current empirical exploration of diversity is narrow; many factors such as model scale (e.g., mixing large and small models), architectural differences (e.g., encoder-decoder vs. decoder-only), and training data diversity have not been investigated. Theoretically, diverse perspectives might improve debate quality, but the paper's results suggest diminishing returns or negative effects, leaving the optimal diversity level unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments that vary model diversity along multiple axes (e.g., model size, architecture, training data) while controlling for other variables, measuring consensus accuracy, stability, and cost-effectiveness. Additionally, qualitative analysis of debate logs to understand how diversity influences argumentation and agreement patterns.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can DREAM be extended to answer-unaware relevance assessment, where gold answers A(q) are not provided?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper's formulation (Section 3) defines relevance as support for at least one answer in A(q). This answer-aware setting is necessary for constructing BRIDGE because the source benchmarks (BEIR, RobustQA) include query-answer pairs. The authors state that they prioritize benchmarks with query-answer pairs to facilitate RAG evaluation.
- **Why unresolved:** Many IR benchmarks (e.g., ad hoc retrieval datasets like TREC) do not provide gold answers; they only have relevance judgments. DREAM's reliance on A(q) limits its applicability to such benchmarks. It is unclear how the debate protocol would function without answers—for example, would agents need to generate hypothetical answers or use a different relevance criterion?
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a variant of DREAM that does not require gold answers. Possibilities include having agents debate over direct query-chunk relevance without answer guidance, or generating candidate answers on the fly and then assessing support. Evaluate such a variant on benchmarks without answers and compare labeling accuracy and human escalation rate with DREAM (answer-aware) and existing methods.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent do missing relevant chunks (holes) in IR benchmarks affect the evaluation of IR systems for tasks beyond RAG, such as document summarization, question answering, or search result diversification?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper demonstrates that holes systematically underestimate retrieval performance and cause retrieval-generation misalignment in RAG (Section 5). However, the study is confined to RAG; the authors note that "retrieval serves as the key downstream task," but do not examine other applications.
- **Why unresolved:** It is plausible that holes could bias evaluation in any task that relies on IR benchmark annotations. For instance, in summarization, if relevant chunks are missing, retrieved content might be deemed insufficient leading to underestimation of retrieval's contribution. Similarly, for multi-faceted queries, holes might affect diversification metrics. The broader impact of holes on the IR ecosystem remains unquantified.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Select several downstream tasks that depend on IR benchmarks (e.g., summarization using retrieved documents, multi-answer QA, search result diversification). Using a benchmark with holes filled by DREAM (like BRIDGE) vs. the original version, re-evaluate state-of-the-art systems for these tasks. Measure performance differences and analyze whether the relative ranking of systems changes, indicating evaluation bias.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the optimal stopping condition for the multi-round debate, and can an adaptive stopping rule improve efficiency without sacrificing accuracy?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors set the maximum debate rounds to 2 based on ablation results (Section 3.2.2) and note that additional rounds do not yield meaningful gains and may increase spurious consensus (Appendix B.3). However, they use a fixed round limit and escalate all non-agreed cases after 2 rounds. This suggests that some cases might converge earlier, while others might need more rounds but risk spurious agreement.
- **Why unresolved:** A fixed round limit is a heuristic. It is unknown whether an adaptive criterion—such as monitoring argument novelty, inter-agent disagreement metrics, or confidence—could decide dynamically when to stop, potentially reducing unnecessary rounds and improving accuracy by avoiding spurious consensus from over-debating.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design and implement an adaptive stopping criterion. For example, track the similarity between successive arguments or the change in labels. Compare the adaptive approach against fixed-round debate (R=2) on accuracy, human escalation rate, and average number of rounds. Also, analyze the characteristics of cases where adaptive stopping leads to different outcomes.

## Limitations

- Candidate pool construction details (deduplication, system failure handling) are unspecified, risking incomplete hole coverage.
- Human escalation protocol (format/prompt for debate history) is not fully described, hindering replication of adjudication.
- Prompt sensitivity: DREAM's performance depends on exact debate prompts; minor variations may alter consensus rates.

- BRIDGE scale (29,824 chunks): **Medium**
