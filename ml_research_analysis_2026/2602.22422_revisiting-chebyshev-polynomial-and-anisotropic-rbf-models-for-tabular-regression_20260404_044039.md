# Revisiting Chebyshev Polynomial and Anisotropic RBF Models for Tabular Regression

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.22422
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22422
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Smooth-basis models (Chebyshev polynomials, anisotropic RBF) match tree ensembles in accuracy on tabular regression while achieving significantly tighter generalisation gaps; TabPFN leads in accuracy but requires GPU.

## Executive Summary
This paper benchmarks eight regression models—including novel smooth-basis approaches (erbf, chebypoly, chebytree), tree ensembles, and the transformer-based TabPFN—on 55 diverse tabular regression datasets. After standard preprocessing, the study evaluates models using nested cross‑validation, focusing on adjusted R², generalisation gap, and computational cost. Results show that TabPFN attains the highest accuracy but depends on GPU hardware. Among CPU‑compatible methods, smooth models and tree ensembles are statistically tied in predictive performance; however, smooth models exhibit substantially smaller generalisation gaps (e.g., chebypoly median gap 0.014 vs. XGBoost 0.079), winning 87% of matched accuracy comparisons. Smooth models also offer lower tuning cost (chebypoly, chebytree) and fastest inference (erbf). The authors recommend integrating smooth‑basis models into regression pipelines, particularly when generalisation robustness or smooth prediction surfaces are desired.

## Method Summary
The benchmark draws datasets from OpenML, UCI, PMLB, and MoleculeNet, applying uniform preprocessing (filtering, imputation, categorical encoding, feature selection capped at 50 features and 50 000 samples). Models compared are: erbf (anisotropic RBF with Lipschitz‑guided centres), chebypoly (ridge‑regularised Chebyshev polynomial), chebytree (tree‑guided Chebyshev hybrids), random forest, XGBoost, TabPFN (zero‑shot), ridge regression, and decision tree. Evaluation uses nested 5‑fold cross‑validation; hyper‑parameters are tuned via Optuna in the inner loop (20–50 trials) with fixed seeds. Primary metrics are adjusted R², generalisation gap (train–test R²), and computational measurements (tuning, training, inference times). The full implementation is released as open‑source software.

## Key Results
- Smooth‑basis models and tree ensembles achieve statistically indistinguishable adjusted R² on CPU, while smooth models yield markedly smaller generalisation gaps (median gap: chebypoly 0.014, XGBoost 0.079).
- TabPFN delivers the highest accuracy but requires a GPU; among CPU‑only models, the best accuracy‑gap trade‑off varies by dataset domain.
- Chebypoly and chebytree are the fastest to tune, and erbf provides the lowest inference latency, making smooth models computationally efficient alternatives to tree ensembles.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The superior generalisation of smooth‑basis models is explained by three interlinked mechanisms.

**Mechanism 1: Smoothness induces algorithmic stability.** The continuously differentiable prediction surfaces of Chebyshev polynomials and Gaussian RBF networks satisfy a bounded Lipschitz condition. According to stability theory (e.g., Bousquet & Elisseeff), such models output similar predictions for nearby inputs, which reduces leave‑one‑out error and tightens the generalisation gap. This effect holds for moderately parameterised models outside the interpolation regime. If the true target is highly discontinuous (e.g., step functions), approximating it with smooth bases may require many basis functions, increasing effective capacity and potentially eroding the gap advantage.

**Mechanism 2: Lipschitz‑guided centre allocation in erbf.** The anisotropic RBF network estimates a local Lipschitz constant at each training point to measure how rapidly the target changes. It samples basis function centres with probability proportional to this estimate, concentrating modelling capacity in high‑gradient regions while using fewer basis functions in flat areas. This adaptive allocation improves efficiency and reduces overfitting in smooth domains (e.g., engineering simulations). However, if noise dominates the Lipschitz estimate, centres may become over‑concentrated in noisy regions, harming performance.

**Mechanism 3: Ridge‑regularised Chebyshev expansion controls complexity.** Chebyshev polynomials form an orthogonal basis on the unit hypercube, yielding a well‑conditioned design matrix. Adding an L2 penalty on the coefficients (ridge regression) shrinks them toward zero, favouring smoother fits with smaller derivatives and lower variance. This directly regulates the smoothness of the fitted surface, leading to tighter generalisation gaps. The combination of orthogonality and regularisation explains chebypoly’s frequent best‑gap performance. Nevertheless, for genuinely non‑smooth targets, global polynomials may still require high degree, making piecewise hybrids like chebytree preferable.

## Foundational Learning
- **Generalisation gap**: The difference between training and test R² scores quantifies overfitting. In this paper it is the primary metric for comparing models’ stability. *Quick check*: If a model has training R² = 0.9 and test R² = 0.7, what is its generalisation gap? (Answer: 0.2)
- **Smoothness (Lipschitz continuity)**: A function is Lipschitz continuous if its derivative is bounded globally, implying that small input changes cannot cause arbitrarily large output jumps. Smooth models produce such surfaces, which theory links to reduced leave‑one‑out error. *Quick check*: Can a function with a discontinuous jump be Lipschitz continuous? (Answer: No, because a jump violates the Lipschitz condition at the discontinuity.)
- **Bias‑variance tradeoff**: As model complexity increases (e.g., polynomial degree), bias decreases but variance increases. Smooth models with moderate complexity achieve lower variance (and thus smaller generalisation gap) at a slight bias cost, still matching tree ensembles in overall accuracy. *Quick check*: As you increase the degree of a polynomial regressor, what happens to bias and variance? (Answer: bias decreases, variance increases.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (execution pipeline):
- *erbf*: centre placement (Lipschitz‑guided or k‑means) → width initialization (local ridge or local variance) → width optimization (L‑BFGS‑B in log‑space) → output weights (ridge regression).
- *chebypoly*: input scaling → univariate Chebyshev expansion per feature → optional interaction terms → ridge regression.
- *chebytree*: decision tree splits → leaf‑wise low‑degree chebypoly fitting → prediction (tree routing + polynomial evaluation).

**Critical path**: Preprocess data (imputation, encoding, scaling) → select model → tune hyperparameters via nested CV (inner Optuna, outer 5‑fold) → refit on full training set → predict. Model‑specific tips: keep centre and width stages of erbf decoupled; monitor basis size for chebypoly to avoid combinatorial explosion; set min_samples_leaf appropriately in chebytree to prevent leaf overfitting.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Smoothness vs discontinuities: Smooth bases assume underlying continuity; chebytree hybridises to handle regime changes.
- Cost vs accuracy: chebypoly/chebytree tune fastest; erbf tuning is slower but inference is fastest; XGBoost is moderate; TabPFN has high inference cost.
- Interpretability: chebypoly gives explicit coefficients; erbf offers geometric centres/widths; trees provide rules; chebytree combines both.
- Scalability: chebypoly basis grows combinatorially with degree/interactions; erbf scales O(nKd) per iteration; tree ensembles scale well; TabPFN limited to ≤50 000 samples.

**Failure signatures**:
- *erbf*: basis widths collapse to near‑zero (NaNs), poor local fits → check initial width distribution and Lipschitz estimate robustness.
- *chebypoly*: ill‑conditioned design matrix (high degree without ridge), extrapolation artefacts → monitor condition number, consider clipping predictions.
- *chebytree*: leaf overfitting (tiny leaf size, high polynomial degree) or underfitting → inspect leaf sample counts and coefficient magnitudes.
- *General*: if generalisation gap remains comparable to XGBoost, the target may not be smooth; consider chebytree or tree ensembles.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Synthetic smooth function test**: Fit erbf, chebypoly, chebytree, and XGBoost to a known smooth surface (e.g., sin(x)·cos(y) + noise). Expect smooth models to achieve similar test R² but smaller gaps than XGBoost.
2. **Discontinuous function test**: Repeat with a step function (1 if x>0 else 0 plus noise). Here tree‑based models should show smaller generalisation gaps; smooth models may require many basis functions and exhibit larger gaps, while chebytree should partially recover performance.
3. **Computational scaling test**: Measure tuning and prediction times on datasets of increasing size (n=1 k, 10 k, 50 k). Confirm chebypoly/chebytree maintain low tuning time; erbf tuning grows with data; XGBoost is moderate; check memory usage for high‑dimensional inputs.

## Limitations
- Dataset versions are not pinned; future downloads may drift, compromising reproducibility.
- TabPFN’s GPU requirement limits fair CPU‑only comparison and may bias resource‑usage conclusions.
- The smoothness‑stability mechanism may fail for highly discontinuous or very noisy targets; Lipschitz‑guided centre allocation in erbf can be sensitive to noise.

## Confidence
- Smooth models achieve tighter generalisation gaps: **Medium**
- Lipschitz‑guided centre allocation (erbf) improves performance: **Medium**
- Ridge‑regularised Chebyshev (chebypoly) controls complexity: **Medium‑High**
- TabPFN achieves highest accuracy; smooth models competitive on CPU: **High** (accuracy ordering), **Medium** (gap stability)
