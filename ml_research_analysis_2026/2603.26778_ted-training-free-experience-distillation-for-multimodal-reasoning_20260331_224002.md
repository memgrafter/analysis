# TED: Training-Free Experience Distillation for Multimodal Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26778
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26778
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A training-free framework that improves multimodal reasoning accuracy (e.g., +0.075 on MathVision) using only 100 training samples, reducing distillation cost by over 20× compared to parameter-based methods.

## Executive Summary
TED reimagines knowledge distillation as a training-free process by transferring teacher expertise into a reusable in-context "experience" set, avoiding costly parameter updates. The student model generates diverse reasoning trajectories for each problem, while the teacher independently produces a correct solution. The teacher then critiques the student's paths, extracting generalized reasoning principles as experiences. A compression mechanism curates these experiences to prevent prompt bloat. Evaluated on multimodal benchmarks with Qwen3-VL-8B, TED yields significant accuracy gains (e.g., MathVision: 0.627→0.702) with minimal data, offering an efficient alternative to traditional distillation.

## Method Summary
TED operates without modifying model weights. For each training sample, the student samples multiple reasoning trajectories conditioned on a current experience set \(E\). The teacher independently solves the problem and compares its solution to the student's condensed trajectories. Based on this critique, the teacher selects a discrete action (Add, Modify, Delete, None) to update \(E\) with generalized principles. Usage statistics track which experiences are invoked; when a token or item budget is exceeded (4000 tokens or 15 items), a compression phase retains the most frequently used items and uses the teacher to merge, rewrite, or remove low-utility entries. After iterating over the training set, the final \(E\) is injected into the student's system prompt for inference, enhancing performance on held-out tasks.

## Key Results
- Using Qwen3-VL-8B on MathVision, accuracy increases from 0.627 (direct inference) to 0.702 with TED using 100 training samples.
- On VisualPuzzles, accuracy improves from 0.517 to 0.561 under the same setup.
- Compared to parameter-based naive knowledge distillation, TED reduces training cost by over 20× while achieving competitive or superior results on these benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Critique-Driven Experience Extraction**
- **Claim:** TED extracts reusable reasoning principles by comparing multiple student trajectories with a teacher’s correct solution.
- **Mechanism:** The student generates diverse reasoning paths; the teacher produces an independent solution. The teacher critiques differences, identifying effective patterns and failure modes to distill into experience items.
- **Core assumption:** The teacher’s solution is correct and its critique captures generalizable knowledge applicable to future problems.
- **Evidence:** Abstract describes trajectory generation and teacher critique; Section 4.1 details condensation and filtering; corpus shows TED’s focus on critique versus trajectory reuse.
- **Break condition:** If the teacher’s solution is incorrect or no student trajectory is correct, critique may yield negative or misleading experiences.

**Mechanism 2: Teacher-Guided Experience Updates**
- **Claim:** The experience set evolves via teacher-selected discrete actions (Add, Modify, Delete, None) without parameter changes.
- **Mechanism:** After critique, the teacher decides which action to apply to the existing experience set, enabling continuous refinement.
- **Core assumption:** The teacher accurately judges experience utility and correctness.
- **Evidence:** Abstract mentions continuous refinement; Section 4.2 defines action selection; corpus contrasts with memory methods lacking teacher-guided updates.
- **Break condition:** Poor action selection (e.g., deleting useful items) can degrade the experience set over time.

**Mechanism 3: Usage-Based Compression**
- **Claim:** To avoid context bloat, TED compresses the experience using usage statistics and teacher-guided merging/rewriting.
- **Mechanism:** Usage frequency is tracked; when budgets are exceeded, the top-R most used items are retained. The teacher then merges redundant items, rewrites vague ones, or deletes low-utility entries.
- **Core assumption:** Frequently used items are more valuable; the teacher can abstract overlapping patterns.
- **Evidence:** Abstract describes compression; Section 4.3 provides algorithmic details; corpus notes TED’s integration of usage tracking and teacher guidance versus simpler compression.
- **Break condition:** Inaccurate usage tracking or over-aggressive merging may discard valuable nuances, harming performance.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Student (S) generates trajectories → Experience buffer (E) stores textual items → Teacher (T) critiques trajectories, selects actions to update E, and performs compression → Usage tracker logs invoked items → Prompt injector constructs final input `[sys; E; x]`.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. For each training sample, construct prompt with current E.  
  2. Student samples N trajectories; teacher generates its own.  
  3. Condense all trajectories to structured format; filter teacher correctness.  
  4. Partition student trajectories into correct/incorrect sets, enforce balance.  
  5. Teacher critiques, selects action (Add/Modify/Delete/None), and updates E.  
  6. Update usage counters for invoked experiences.  
  7. After epoch, if compression trigger met (tokens>4000 OR items>15), retain top-R items, then merge/rewrite/delete via teacher.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Experience capacity: Larger E risks overflow; compression mitigates but may lose detail.  
  - Teacher strength: Stronger teachers yield higher-quality experiences but increase cost.  
  - Number of trajectories (N): More trajectories provide diverse critique data but raise compute.  
  - Compression aggressiveness: Aggressive merging reduces size but may obscure distinctions.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Performance drops when E grows without compression (Table 7: 0.594 vs. 0.702).  
  - Weak teacher (e.g., student self-critique) yields smaller gains; teacher errors may mislead.  
  - Absence of correct student trajectories leads to only negative experiences.  
  - Usage tracking errors cause frequent items to be discarded, harming utility.
- **Key experimental comparisons:**  
  1. Baseline: Direct inference with no experience.  
  2. Full TED: With experience and compression, showing improvement on MathVision.  
  3. Ablation: TED without compression, resulting in degraded performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How does TED’s in-context experience concretely reduce the student’s reasoning search space?**  
  *Basis:* Authors speculate in the Limitation section but lack empirical proof.  
  *Why unresolved:* Without a verified mechanism, generalizing TED or explaining scale benefits is difficult.  
  *Needed evidence:* Measure trajectory diversity with/without experiences; track invoked items during corrections; compare across model scales.
- **Can TED handle large training datasets (e.g., >10k samples) without performance degradation due to context bloat?**  
  *Basis:* Study uses up to 3k samples; parameter-based KD catches up at larger scales (Table 2); authors claim TED suits low-data regimes.  
  *Why unresolved:* Scaling would broaden applicability, but fixed-size experience buffers may become insufficient or noisy.  
  *Needed evidence:* Run experiments with increasing dataset sizes, monitor experience growth and compression efficacy; explore adaptive compression.
- **What characteristics of distilled experiences enable cross-modal transfer, and how can we design maximally general experiences?**  
  *Basis:* Cross-modal results (MathVerse ↔ AIME25, Table 8) show positive transfer, but reasons are unanalyzed.  
  *Why unresolved:* Identifying transferable features would enable universal reasoning templates.  
  *Needed evidence:* Manually code experiences by abstraction level; correlate with cross-task gains; test synthetic experiences at varying generality.

## Limitations
- Teacher correctness drift: Reliance on teacher’s accurate solutions means teacher errors can introduce misleading experiences, especially when student trajectories are all incorrect.
- Prompt sensitivity: Performance likely depends on precise critique and compression prompt wording; minor variations may break the mechanism.
- Black-box teacher dependence: Using proprietary teachers (e.g., Kimi-K2.5) introduces cost, latency, and reproducibility challenges, limiting fine-grained analysis.

## Confidence
- **High:** Reported performance gains on MathVision/VisualPuzzles are empirically supported within the paper’s fixed setup (same student/teacher, 100 samples).
- **Medium:** The critique-driven update mechanism is plausible but hinges on unverified implementation details (usage tracking, action parsing); generalization to other model pairs/datasets is uncertain.
- **Low:** Claims about continuous refinement and compression efficacy are based on a single trigger and small experience budget; scaling behavior remains unknown.

## Next Checks
1. **Ablate teacher strength:** Replace Kimi-K2.5 with a weaker teacher (e.g., the student itself) and measure performance drop; a sharp decline confirms dependence on teacher correctness.
2. **Reimplement usage tracking:** Explicitly log which experience items are cited during student inference (via prompt inspection or logit analysis); verify that top-used items align with compression retention.
3. **Qualitative experience audit:** Manually inspect 50 generated experience items for correctness, generality, and redundancy; correlate utility scores with human judgment to validate compression logic.
