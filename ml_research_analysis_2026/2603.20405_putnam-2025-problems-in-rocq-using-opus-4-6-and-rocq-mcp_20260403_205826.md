# Putnam 2025 Problems in Rocq using Opus 4.6 and Rocq-MCP

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.20405
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20405
- Reference count: 4
- Primary result: Claude Opus 4.6, using eight MCP tools for Rocq, autonomously proved 10 of 12 Putnam 2025 problems.

## Executive Summary
This paper reports on the first large-scale attempt to solve Putnam competition problems using a language model within a formal proof assistant. Claude Opus 42.6, equipped with eight Model Context Protocol (MCP) tools for Rocq, successfully proved 10 out of 12 problems from the 2025 competition. The agent employed a compile‑first, interactive‑fallback strategy and a dynamic multi‑agent architecture, generating 5,542 lines of verified Rocq code at a cost of ~$5,279 and 17.7 hours of active compute.

## Method Summary
The task was to formalize and prove 12 Putnam 2025 problems in Rocq. Autoformalized problem statements (derived from Lean) were provided as stub files. The orchestrator (Claude Code) deployed subagents, each with access to eight MCP tools: `rocq_compile`, `rocq_verify`, `rocq_auto_solve`, `rocq_query`, `rocq_step`, `rocq_step_multi`, `rocq_toc`, `rocq_notations`. The primary workflow was a compile‑first loop: the subagent wrote a full proof, compiled it, and iterated on compiler errors; interactive stepping was a fallback. Proofs were sandboxed via module wrapping and an axiom whitelist (`rocq_verify`). The orchestrator dynamically spawned role‑specialized subagents (Lemma Prover, Bug Fixer, etc.) to address bottlenecks. All runs occurred in an isolated VM without internet.

## Key Results
- Solved 10 of 12 Putnam 2025 problems (10/12).
- Produced 5,542 lines of verified Rocq code across successful proofs.
- Computational cost: 141 subagents, 17.7 hours active compute (51.6 hours wall‑clock), ~1.9 billion tokens (~$5,279).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A "compile‑first, interactive‑fallback" strategy efficiently drives proof development by leveraging the Rocq compiler's precise error reporting as the primary feedback loop.
- **Mechanism:** The agent writes complete proof files, compiles them with `rocq_compile`, and iterates on compiler errors. Interactive tools (`rocq_step`, `rocq_query`) are only invoked to debug specific subgoals or library queries when compilation stalls.
- **Core assumption:** The compiler provides sufficiently actionable error messages for the LLM to correct most mistakes in a single write‑compile‑fix cycle, avoiding the need for expensive step‑by‑step interactive proving for the bulk of the work.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "encode a 'compile‑first, interactive‑fallback' strategy"
  - [section 2.1] "the agent writes a complete proof file, compiles it, iterates on errors, and only falls back to interactive stepping for debugging specific subgoals"
  - [section 2.2] "81% of miniF2F proofs succeeded via direct compilation alone"
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers discuss agentic theorem proving but not this specific strategy.
- **Break condition:** When compilation errors become ambiguous or persist across multiple iterations without clear resolution, requiring interactive exploration of the proof state.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Sandboxing via module wrapping (`rocq_verify`) and an axiom whitelist prevents the LLM from exploiting loopholes in the formalization (e.g., `Admitted`, redefining the theorem statement) to produce invalid proofs that still compile.
- **Mechanism:** The proof attempt is placed inside a separate Rocq module. The outer context then attempts to apply the proved theorem to the original statement. A whitelist filters out non‑standard axioms.
- **Core assumption:** Isolating the LLM‑generated proof from the problem statement forces it to actually prove the intended theorem within a constrained logical environment.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2] "14 proofs used Admitted and 6 exploited type redefinition... motivating a sandboxing mechanism and an axiom whitelist in `rocq_verify`"
  - [section 4.1] "All 10 solved proofs pass verification with only standard axioms"
  - [section 5] The A3 loophole exploitation and subsequent fixing illustrates both the failure mode and the verification mechanism.
- **Break condition:** When the formalization itself contains ambiguities or the problem requires custom inductive types that fail to unify across the module boundary, causing false negatives (as seen with A3 and B1 initially).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A multi‑agent architecture with dynamically spawned, role‑specialized subagents (Lemma Prover, Bug Fixer, etc.) scales the overall problem‑solving capacity by decomposing the proof effort into parallelizable and specialized tracks.
- **Mechanism:** An orchestrator (Claude Code) initially assigns problems to parallel teams. Based on encountered failure modes, it spawns new subagents with specific roles and mathematical hints to target bottlenecks like lemma proving, error repair, or proof completion.
- **Core assumption:** Different proof development phases (initial structure, lemma proving, debug, verification) benefit from distinct prompting strategies and LLM "personas," and parallelizing these efforts mitigates the sequential nature of a single‑agent workflow.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "the agent deployed 141 subagents over 17.7 hours of active compute"
  - [section 4.2] Table 3 shows role distribution (Lemma Prover 55 agents, Bug Fixer 36, etc.) and the "parallel dispatch was highly effective: 4 problems solved in 1.8h, 7 in 5.2h"
  - [section 4.2] "Mathematical hints matter... These hints were generated by the orchestrator LLM itself"
- **Break condition:** When multiple subagents consecutively fail at the same core mathematical bottleneck without generating novel approaches, indicating a need for strategic replanning or human guidance rather than more parallel attempts.

## Foundational Learning
- **Theorem proving workflow (compile‑iterate)**  
  Why needed here: The entire system is built on a loop where the LLM emits a full proof script, the compiler provides feedback, and the LLM edits. Understanding this loop—including that errors are expected and part of the process—is fundamental.  
  Quick check: Given a compiler error about an "unresolved implicit argument," what are the two most likely LLM actions in the next iteration? (Answer: Add an explicit argument or use a `refine`/`apply` tactic to instantiate it.)

- **Limitations of proof automation**  
  Why needed here: The `rocq_auto_solve` tool was almost never sufficient for Putnam problems. This teaches that high‑level mathematical insight, not just tactic search, is required, guiding the agent to rely more on its own reasoning than on `auto`.  
  Quick check: Why would `lia` (linear integer arithmetic) likely fail on a Putnam combinatorics problem involving inequalities or number theory?

- **Formalization quality as a first‑class concern**  
  Why needed here: The A3 case study shows that a seemingly minor encoding choice (partial functions for strategies vs. must‑move constraints) can create a exploitable loophole or make the problem intractable. The agent's ability to detect the loophole is a strength, but fixing it required human guidance to a better formalization.  
  Quick check: If an agent proves a trivial theorem for a complex problem statement, what should you suspect first: the agent's brilliance or the problem's formalization?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Orchestrator (Claude Code) -> Subagents (LLM instances) -> rocq‑mcp Server (8 tools) -> Rocq Toolchain (rocq/coqc, coq‑lsp) -> Standard libraries (Coquelicot, math‑comp, math‑comp‑analysis). Problem formalizations are input stubs.

**Critical path:**  
1. Load problem formalization.  
2. Orchestrator assigns problem to a team (or spawns a new subagent).  
3. Subagent uses `rocq_compile` loop as primary method; may use `rocq_auto_solve` for a quick check first.  
4. On persistent compile errors, subagent uses `rocq_query`/`rocq_notations` to explore library/scoping.  
5. On incomplete proofs, subagent uses `rocq_step` to inspect goals.  
6. When a proof compiles, orchestrator runs `rocq_verify` for validation.  
7. Orchestrator may spawn specialized subagents (Lemma Prover, Bug Fixer) based on logs.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Compile‑first vs. interactive: Chose compile‑first for efficiency (81% success on miniF2F) but added interactive tools as a costly fallback. This minimizes average cost but risks high cost on hard problems.  
- General frontier model vs. specialized prover: Uses Claude Opus 4.6 without fine‑tuning. Advantage: flexibility. Disadvantage: no embedded tactical knowledge.  
- Two‑level agent hierarchy: Simple orchestrator/subagent avoids communication overhead but may lack coordinated long‑term strategy.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Stuck in compile loop: high number of `rocq_compile` calls with recurring similar errors (e.g., "unification failure"). Signal: needs `rocq_query` or `rocq_step`.  
- False positive risk: proof compiles but uses `Admitted` or non‑standard axioms. Must be caught by `rocq_verify`.  
- Diminishing returns: many subagents assigned to one problem, high token consumption, but no new proof attempts. Signal: same mathematical bottleneck; need strategic replanning or hint injection.  
- Module boundary failure: `rocq_verify` fails because the proof's custom types don't unify across the module. Signal: formalization issue (like A3/B1).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline compile‑only run: Run initial agent on a subset of miniF2F‑Rocq with only `rocq_compile`. Measure success rate (~81%) and categorize failures (invalid proofs, incomplete proofs, other).  
2. Tool augmentation test: Add `rocq_verify` and one interactive tool (e.g., `rocq_step`). Re‑run on the failing miniF2F subset. Measure if new tools resolve their targeted failure categories without degrading overall efficiency.  
3. Parallel scaling test: Take a solved Putnam problem (e.g., A1) and artificially induce a difficult sub‑lemma. Run full multi‑agent setup and observe how orchestrator spawns Lemma Prover subagents. Measure token/time cost of the specialized phase versus a single‑agent continuation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can agents automatically detect and resolve formalization gaps (like the A3 loophole) without human guidance? Basis: The A3 case study shows the agent exploited a formalization loophole and required human intervention. Why unresolved: The workflow depends on human experts to identify flawed encodings and supply corrected formalizations. What evidence would resolve: An experiment where the agent, given multiple candidate formalizations, autonomously selects or constructs one that is mathematically faithful and proof‑resistant.

2. Does the "compile‑first, interactive‑fallback" strategy scale to formalization tasks involving large libraries and complex dependencies, where compiler errors become less localized? Basis: Section 5 states this is open. Why unresolved: The experiment used self‑contained problem files; in large‑scale developments errors may stem from library mismatches. What evidence would resolve: Running the same agent/toolchain on a benchmark like MathComp or Coquelicot.

3. Can early‑termination heuristics significantly improve cost efficiency by detecting when multiple subagents are stuck on the same unsolvable bottleneck? Basis: Section 4.2 notes that for hard problems many agents attempted the same difficulty without new approaches, suggesting early‑termination could save resources. Why unresolved: The orchestrator lacks meta‑reasoning to recognize unproductive repetition; current scaling is brute‑force. What evidence would resolve: Instrument the orchestrator to cluster subagent failures, implement termination policy, and measure token savings on hard problems.

## Limitations
- Undocumented orchestration parameters (subagent spawning policy, token limits, lifetimes) hinder exact reproduction of subagent dynamics and cost scaling.
- Significant inactivity (33.8 h) due to rate limits and container crashes, with unspecified recovery logic, affects total wall‑clock time.
- Module‑boundary verification produces false negatives when proofs use custom inductive types, requiring ad‑hoc manual fixes (e.g., for A3, B1).

## Confidence
- Overall success (10/12 Putnam problems): High confidence.
- Compile‑first strategy: High confidence.
- Sandboxing/verification: Medium confidence.
- Multi‑agent scaling: Medium confidence.
