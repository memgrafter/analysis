# RelayS2S: A Dual-Path Speculative Generation for Real-Time Dialogue
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.23346
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23346
- Reference count: 13
- Primary result: Achieves near‑zero response‑onset latency (81 ms P90) while preserving 99% of cascaded quality (4.78 vs 4.83), with only a slight increase in low‑quality rate (5.5%→7.4%).

## Executive Summary
Real‑time spoken dialogue systems face a fundamental trade‑off: end‑to‑end speech‑to‑speech (S2S) models provide low latency but weaker semantic quality, while cascaded ASR→LLM pipelines deliver higher quality at the cost of increased latency. RelayS2S introduces a hybrid architecture that runs two parallel paths upon user‑turn detection. A fast duplex S2S model speculatively generates a short response prefix that is streamed immediately to TTS, achieving low‑latency audio onset. A slow cascaded ASR→LLM pipeline generates a higher‑quality continuation conditioned on the committed prefix. A lightweight learned verifier decides whether to commit the prefix or fall back entirely to the slow path. Evaluations show RelayS2S achieves a P90 onset latency of 81 ms—comparable to the pure S2S model’s 71 ms and far below the cascaded baseline’s 1091 ms—while retaining 99% of the cascaded system’s average quality score (4.78 vs 4.83). The low‑quality rate increases only slightly from 5.5% to 7.4%. The verifier adds only ~10 ms overhead and operates at an 8% fallback rate. The system requires no architectural modifications to existing cascaded pipelines, serving as a drop‑in enhancement.

## Method Summary
RelayS2S combines a duplex S2S model with a traditional cascaded ASR→LLM pipeline. Upon detection of a user turn end, the duplex S2S model simultaneously processes incoming audio on its main online stream (for barge‑in detection) and, via a forked speculative stream, generates a short text prefix at maximum speed. A lightweight verifier—trained on out‑of‑fold prefix data using calibration signals like entropy and token probability—scores each prefix. If the score exceeds a threshold, the prefix is committed: it is immediately sent to a streaming TTS engine and also prepended as forced context to the slow‑path LLM, which continues generation. If the verifier falls back, the system ignores the prefix and relies solely on the ASR→LLM pipeline. The duplex S2S model is trained in three stages on a large synthetic dataset of 104 k conversations with programmatically injected backchannels and interruptions. The verifier reuses decoder hidden states, adding minimal overhead (~10 ms). The entire relay mechanism operates transparently, requiring no changes to either the S2S or cascaded components.

## Key Results
- P90 onset latency: 81 ms (vs 71 ms pure S2S, 1091 ms cascaded with GPT‑4o).
- Average quality: 4.78 (99% of cascaded’s 4.83).
- Low‑quality rate: 7.4% (vs 5.5% cascaded); verifier fallback rate 8%; verifier overhead ~10 ms.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Forked speculative generation decouples fast prefix creation from real‑time audio monitoring. Upon turn detection, the duplex S2S model splits into a main online stream (continues ingesting audio every 160 ms and updates its KV‑cache for barge‑in detection) and a speculative stream (stops audio input and decodes autoregressively at full speed). Once the prefix reaches the minimum TTS chunk size (e.g., 5 words), it is streamed to TTS, overlapping with the slower ASR→LLM path. This leverages the S2S model’s ability to produce a coherent prefix faster than the cascaded pipeline’s first *N* words. Evidence includes the abstract’s latency numbers and Section 2.3’s description of freeing the speculative branch from the speech‑tick constraint. The break condition is when the S2S decoding speed does not outpace the cascaded path (e.g., due to hardware limits or large TTS chunk requirements), which would erode the latency advantage.

### Mechanism 2
A lightweight learned verifier gates the speculative prefix, balancing latency and quality. The verifier consumes per‑token decoder hidden states and calibration features (entropy, token probability, top‑two margin) to produce a confidence score. A threshold determines whether to commit the prefix (stream to TTS and hand off to the slow path) or fallback entirely. The verifier reuses already computed states, adding only ~10 ms overhead. Evidence: abstract and Section 2.4 detail the architecture (projection, multiplicative gate, attention pooling, sigmoid head). The break condition is misclassification: a high good‑prefix fallback rate unnecessarily raises latency, while a high bad‑commit rate degrades quality (Table 5 shows quality drop without the verifier).

### Mechanism 3
The slow‑path LLM generates a high‑quality continuation conditioned on the committed prefix, ensuring seamless utterance extension. Upon commit, the ASR→LLM pipeline receives the user transcript and prepends the prefix as forced context in the LLM prompt. The LLM continues generation from the exact end of the prefix. The shared response buffer allows the streaming TTS to play the prefix and then switch to the continuation without gap, provided the continuation arrives before the prefix audio exhausts (positive relay margin). A 5–7 word prefix yields 2–3 seconds of speech, which typically exceeds the slow‑path startup time. Evidence: abstract, Section 2.5 (forced context), and Section 2.6 (relay margin). Break conditions: prefix too short (negative margin) or verifier failure allows a bad prefix, causing semantic disruption.

## Foundational Learning
### Duplex Speech‑to‑Speech Models
- **Why needed:** The fast path relies on a single model that simultaneously processes incoming user speech and generates agent control tokens in real time, enabling immediate response initiation and interruption handling.
- **Quick check:** What are the five special control tokens and their meanings? *Answer:* [SIL] continued listening, [BOC] begin backchannel, [BOS] begin substantive response, [STP] terminate ongoing response, [EOS] complete response.

### Speculative Decoding (Forked Generation)
- **Why needed:** Allows the S2S model to produce a prefix much faster than the real‑time tick rate by temporarily freeing a branch from audio‑input constraints.
- **Quick check:** How does the speculative stream differ from the main online stream? *Answer:* The main stream continues observing audio every 160 ms and updates its KV‑cache; the speculative stream stops receiving audio after the fork and runs continuous autoregressive decoding.

### Prefix Conditioning in LLMs
- **Why needed:** The slow‑path LLM must continue exactly from the committed prefix without altering its wording, ensuring seamless concatenation with the already spoken audio.
- **Quick check:** What ensures the LLM continues naturally from the forced prefix? *Answer:* The prefix is inserted as part of the prompt at the correct turn position; generation starts immediately after the last prefix token, with no extra separators.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
The system consists of: a duplex S2S model (streaming speech encoder → CNN adapter → LLM backbone) that upon turn detection forks into two branches: a main online stream (continues audio processing for barge‑in) and a speculative stream (generates a short text prefix). A lightweight verifier scores each draft prefix. A shared response buffer holds emitted tokens. The slow path comprises an ASR module followed by a text LLM, which when the prefix is committed, receives the user transcript and the prefix as forced context to generate the continuation. A streaming TTS engine consumes text from the buffer in small chunks, starting once the prefix reaches minimum size.

### Critical path
1. User’s turn is detected (e.g., pause or [STP] token).
2. Duplex S2S model predicts a response‑initiation token ([BOS] or [BOC]).
3. Fork creates speculative and main streams.
4. Speculative stream drafts a short prefix (e.g., 5 words) at maximum speed.
5. Verifier scores the prefix.
6. **If commit:** Prefix sent to TTS; ASR begins transcription; LLM starts continuation from forced prefix.  
   **If fallback:** Skip prefix; ASR→LLM generates full response.
7. TTS plays prefix while slow path produces first continuation chunk; seamless handoff if relay margin positive.
8. Main stream continues monitoring; if [STP] detected, TTS stops and remaining buffered prefix is discarded.

### Design tradeoffs
- Prefix length: longer prefixes increase audio buffer (reducing underrun risk) but raise the fraction of low‑quality openings.
- Verifier threshold: lower threshold commits more good prefixes but also lets more bad ones through; higher threshold reduces bad commits but increases fallback rate.
- Fast‑path model strength: weaker S2S yields more inappropriate prefixes, forcing fallbacks or quality loss.
- Slow‑path scale: benefits of RelayS2S grow with stronger slow‑path models because the quality gap narrows while latency remains low.

### Failure signatures
- High fallback rate (>15%): verifier too conservative or S2S quality poor.
- High bad‑commit rate: verifier not filtering well; monitor low‑quality rate (>10% with GPT‑4o).
- TTS underruns: negative relay margin; check that continuation arrives before prefix audio ends.
- Turn‑taking errors: main stream missing interruptions; check F1 of [STP] prediction (<95% problematic).
- ASR errors: cascading failure; monitor word error rate.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Latency validation:** Measure P90 onset latency across different slow‑path LLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5‑0.5B, 7B, GPT‑4o) to confirm RelayS2S latency stays near pure S2S baseline (~80 ms) irrespective of backend.
2. **Quality‑latency trade‑off:** Ablate prefix length (3, 5, 7 words) with and without the verifier; compute average quality and low‑quality rate to select an operating point.
3. **Verifier calibration:** Compute AUROC and precision‑recall on held‑out data; adjust decision threshold to achieve target fallback rate (~8%) while maximizing good‑prefix commit.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to real conversations:** The system is trained and evaluated on fully synthetic data with programmatically injected duplex events. Performance on natural human speech with spontaneous turn‑taking, diverse accents, and acoustic variability is unknown. No human studies or out‑of‑distribution tests are reported.
2. **Impact of ASR errors on conditioned continuation:** The slow path relies on ASR transcripts to condition the LLM. ASR errors (e.g., from Whisper v2) may misalign the user’s actual intent with the forced prefix, causing incoherent continuations even if the prefix is sensible. The paper does not decompose failures by ASR accuracy.
3. **Verifier behavior with scaled fast‑path models:** The verifier is trained on prefixes from a 0.5B S2S model. Scaling the fast‑path model could change prefix quality distribution and calibration signals, potentially requiring verifier retraining. The paper does not explore this.
4. **Alignment of automated quality judgments with human perception:** All quality evaluations use an LLM judge (Gemini‑3) with a custom rubric. LLM judges may have biases; backchannel F1 is only 50.8%. Human evaluations are absent, so the real‑world conversational quality remains uncertain.
5. **Handling variable slow‑path latency (relay margin):** The system assumes a 5–7 word prefix provides enough audio buffer for the slow path to produce its first chunk. Under network jitter or on resource‑constrained devices, slow‑path latency may increase, causing negative relay margins and audible gaps. The paper does not study sensitivity to latency variations.

## Limitations
- Synthetic data domain gap: Performance on real human conversations with natural acoustic and turn‑taking variability is unproven.
- Implementation opacity: Critical details for forked speculative generation (KV‑cache handling) and synthetic duplex injection are not provided, hindering exact replication.
- Slow‑path dependency: The claimed widening benefits rely on a high‑quality slow‑path LLM; for weaker backends the trade‑off may not justify the complexity.

## Confidence
- Latency reduction: **High**
- Quality retention: **High**
- Verifier efficiency: **High**
- Scalability with slow‑path strength: **Medium**
- Robustness to real speech & speaker variation: **Low**

## Next Checks
1. **Real‑World Generalization Test:** Replicate the RelayS2S pipeline and evaluate it on a held‑out set of real human‑human or human‑AI duplex conversations (e.g., Switchboard, Fisher, or commercial voice assistant data). Compare P90 onset latency and quality scores against the cascaded baseline on this real data.
2. **Verifier Threshold Sensitivity Analysis:** Sweep the verifier’s decision threshold on a validation set and plot the trade‑off curve between system P90 latency (should increase with higher fallback) and low‑quality rate (should decrease). Identify the Pareto frontier and check if the paper’s operating point (~8% fallback) is near‑optimal.
3. **Relay Margin Under Variable Slow‑Path Latency:** Measure the relay margin (time between prefix TTS finish and slow‑path first continuation token) across slow‑path LLMs of varying latency (e.g., different sizes, quantization, or hardware). Systematically vary prefix length (3,5,7,9 words) to find the minimum safe prefix length for each configuration that guarantees ≤5% TTS underruns.
