# A Two-Stage Architecture for NDA Analysis: LLM-based Segmentation and Transformer-based Clause Classification

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.09990
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09990
*   **Reference Count:** 6
*   **Primary Result:** A two-stage system (LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct segmentation + fine-tuned Legal-Roberta classification) achieves strong segmentation (ROUGE F1 0.95) and effective multi-label clause labeling (Weighted F1 0.85) on the Kleister-NDA2 dataset despite significant class imbalance.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of automating Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA) review, which is traditionally slow and error-prone due to document variability. The authors propose a two-stage architecture. First, an instruction-tuned large language model (LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct) segments raw NDA text into individual clauses. Second, a domain-specific transformer model (Legal-Roberta-Large), fine-tuned for multi-label classification, assigns each clause to one or more of 14 predefined legal categories. Evaluated on the public Kleister-NDA2 dataset, the system demonstrates high segmentation accuracy and robust classification performance on majority clause types, with performance degradation limited to a few underrepresented classes.

## Method Summary
The method processes 322 annotated NDAs from the Kleister-NDA2 dataset. **Stage 1 (Segmentation):** A prompted LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct model parses the full, unstructured NDA text and outputs clauses marked with `[INIT_CLAUSE]` and `[END_CLAUSE]` tags. Its general language understanding handles structural variability. **Stage 2 (Classification):** A `lexlms/legal-roberta-base` model is fine-tuned on the extracted clauses for multi-label classification over 14 classes, using Focal Loss to combat severe class imbalance. Performance is evaluated using ROUGE, semantic similarity, and factual correctness for segmentation, and Weighted/Macro F1 for classification. A Needleman-Wunsch alignment algorithm with a similarity pre-filter is used to match predicted and reference clauses for robust metric computation.

## Key Results
*   **Segmentation:** Achieved ROUGE F1 of 0.95 ± 0.0036, Factual Correctness of 0.95, and Semantic Similarity of 0.98.
*   **Classification:** Achieved a Weighted F1 of 0.85, demonstrating strong performance on frequent classes, though Macro F1 was lower at 0.69.
*   **Inequality:** Performance drop in Macro F1 is attributed to four clause classes with fewer than 100 training samples each, highlighting the impact of severe class imbalance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Using an instruction-tuned LLM (LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct) for segmentation effectively handles the high variability in NDA structure and writing style.
*   **Mechanism:** The LLM is prompted to parse a raw, unstructured NDA document and output clauses wrapped in `[INIT_CLAUSE]` / `[END_CLAUSE]` tags. Its general language understanding and instruction-following capability allow it to identify logical clause boundaries based on semantic shifts and legal conventions, overriding format inconsistencies.
*   **Core assumption:** A sufficiently capable instruction-tuned LLM can infer clause boundaries from semantic and contextual cues in heterogeneous legal documents without needing layout-specific rules.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "...LLM-based segmentation... addressing... document variability."
    *   [section 3.5] "The segmentation of legal documents poses a significant challenge, as such texts generally lack a standardized structure."
    *   [corpus] Weak signal. Neighbors focus on other segmentation tasks (SQL, images) but confirm multi-stage architectures are a pattern.
*   **Break condition:** The mechanism fails if the LLM's context window is insufficient for very long documents or if the prompt engineering cannot sufficiently disambiguate complex, multi-topic clauses. Evidence: Input NDAs averaged 3,401 tokens, near the model's capacity.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Employing a domain-specific, fine-tuned transformer (Legal-Roberta-Large) for multi-label classification accurately maps segmented clauses to a predefined legal taxonomy.
*   **Mechanism:** Each extracted clause text is fed into Legal-Roberta-Large, which has been fine-tuned on the annotated NDA clause dataset. The model learns to predict a multi-label vector over the 14 clause classes, leveraging its legal-domain pre-training to understand specialized terminology and relationships.
*   **Core assumption:** Fine-tuning a domain-adapted BERT-variant on a modest, imbalanced multi-label dataset can learn sufficient discriminative patterns for the majority of clause types, even with severe class imbalance.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "...Transformer-based Clause Classification... weighted F1 0.85."
    *   [section 3.6] "For classification, fine-tuning was performed on the legal-roberta-base model... The dataset... characterizes a multi-label classification problem, which means... significant class imbalance."
    *   [section 4.2] High Weighted F1 (0.85) but low Macro F1 (0.69) directly evidence the trade-off.
*   **Break condition:** The mechanism underperforms on minority classes (e.g., those with <100 samples). Evidence: "four classes were identified with fewer than 100 samples each, and this scarcity negatively impacted the macro F1 performance."

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Using the Needleman-Wunsch sequence alignment algorithm for evaluation enables robust, automated scoring of segmentation quality by reliably matching predicted clauses to reference annotations.
*   **Mechanism:** It aligns the sequence of predicted clauses with the sequence of reference clauses, scoring matches/mismatches/gaps. A similarity threshold (0.7) pre-filters obvious non-matches, drastically reducing the O(N*M) comparison cost, allowing for expensive downstream metrics (factual correctness, semantic similarity) to be computed on aligned pairs.
*   **Core assumption:** Treating clauses as sequences and applying a biological sequence alignment algorithm with a tuned threshold provides a reliable approximation of clause-level correspondence for evaluation purposes.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 3.5] "One of the main challenges... was the absence of prior alignment... To mitigate the quadratic computational complexity... we implemented the Needleman-Wunsch algorithm."
    *   [section 3.5] "By applying a similarity threshold of 0.7... we observed an average reduction of 92.5% in the number of clause comparisons."
    *   [section 4.1] Metrics are reported *after* this alignment step ("In the subset of samples that satisfied this condition...").
*   **Break condition:** The alignment heuristic may mis-align clauses with substantial paraphrasing but identical meaning, penalizing semantically correct but lexically divergent segmentations.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Multi-Label Classification**
    *   **Why needed here:** The classification task is multi-label (a clause can belong to multiple classes, e.g., "Purpose" and "Term"), requiring models and metrics (like Hamming Loss, F1) that handle non-exclusive label sets, unlike single-label classification.
    *   **Quick check question:** How does the evaluation strategy (e.g., Macro vs. Weighted F1) differently reflect performance on frequent vs. rare clause types in an imbalanced multi-label setting?
*   **Concept: Fine-tuning a Pre-trained Language Model**
    *   **Why needed here:** Legal-Roberta is pre-trained on general legal text but must adapt to the specific NDA clause taxonomy and multi-label structure of the Kleister-NDA dataset. The process involves task-specific head modification and learning rate management (e.g., 1e-5, warmup).
    *   **Quick check question:** Why might a very small dataset (3,714 clauses) for fine-tuning risk overfitting, and how might techniques like Focal Loss (γ=2) and weight decay (0.01) mitigate this?
*   **Concept: Document Pre-processing & Prompt Engineering for LLMs**
    *   **Why needed here:** The raw PDF-converted TXT NDAs are noisy and variable. The segmenter's prompt (499 tokens) is the primary control mechanism to elicit consistent clause-extraction behavior from the LLM, compensating for lack of standardization.
    *   **Quick check question:** What are two specific challenges in prompt design for this task, based on the text (e.g., handling headers, tables, or defining clause boundaries)?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Raw NDA .txt -> LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct (Segmenter) -> Parsed Clause List -> Legal-Roberta-Large (Classifier) -> Labeled Clauses.
*   **Critical path:** Raw Text → Segmenter → Clause List → Classifier → Labeled Clauses. The classifier **cannot** start until the segmenter's output is parsed. Metric calculation (ROUGE, alignment) is an offline evaluation step on a labeled test set.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Segmentation:** LLM (flexible, context-aware) vs. rule-based (precise but brittle). LLM chosen for variability but requires careful prompting and is slower/more costly than a lightweight rule parser.
    *   **Classification:** Domain-specific BERT (Legal-Roberta) vs. generic LLM. BERT chosen for efficiency on short texts and strong performance with fine-tuning, but its 512-token limit necessitated pre-segmentation.
    *   **Evaluation:** Alignment algorithm (Needleman-Wunsch) vs. exact string match. Alignment tolerates minor segmentation errors but adds complexity and a threshold dependency.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **Segmenter:** Output contains malformed tags, merges distinct clauses, or splits a single clause into fragments. High ROUGE but low Semantic Similarity/Factual Correctness *after alignment* indicates boundary errors.
    *   **Classifier:** Consistently low scores on specific classes (check class-wise F1). High Hamming Loss but decent Weighted F1 indicates errors concentrated on minority classes.
    *   **Pipeline:** Classifier receives empty or nonsensical clause text from a failed segmentation. Monitor average clause length output from segmenter.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Segmentation Sanity Check:** Run segmenter on 5-10 known NDAs. Visually inspect output for correct tag placement and clause coherence. Does it handle a two-column layout conversion artifact?
    2.  **Classifier Baseline Replication:** Fine-tune Legal-Roberta-base on the provided train/val split (80/10) using the reported hyperparameters (3 epochs, lr=1e-5, Focal Loss). Replicate Table 3 validation metrics.
    3.  **End-to-End Smoke Test:** Process 2-3 full NDAs through the complete pipeline (segment → classify). Manually verify if predicted labels for obvious clauses (e.g., "Governing Law") are plausible. This tests integration, parsing, and model inference stability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
*   **Question:** What specific data augmentation techniques (e.g., paraphrase generation, synthetic sample creation) and alternative loss function modifications beyond Focal Loss most effectively improve multi-label classification performance on minority legal clause classes with fewer than 100 training samples each?
*   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors identify severe class imbalance (four classes with <100 samples) as causing low macro F1 and state future work will explore "data augmentation, paraphrase generation with generative models, and additional strategies beyond Focal Loss."
*   **Why unresolved:** The paper only applies standard Focal Loss (α=0.25, γ=2) without comparing other configurations or augmentation methods, leaving the optimal approach unknown for this specific imbalanced multi-label legal dataset.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments comparing various augmentation techniques (e.g., back-translation, LLM-based paraphrasing) and loss functions (e.g., class-weighted BCE, Label Smoothing) on the existing dataset, measuring macro F1 improvement on minority classes while monitoring overfitting risks.

### Open Question 2
*   **Question:** What architectural and accuracy challenges arise when integrating the LLM-based segmenter and transformer-based classifier into a single end-to-end system with specialist agents for automated NDA review, and how can error propagation between stages be minimized?
*   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states intent to "integrate segmentation and classification stages into a complete system to automate the review and correction of NDAs" with "specialist agents responsible for analyzing specific clauses."
*   **Why unresolved:** The current two-stage system is evaluated independently; no analysis of how segmentation errors affect classification performance in a pipelined setup, nor how agent-based correction might interact with model outputs.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Building a prototype integrated system, measuring end-to-end clause labeling accuracy with and without agent interventions, and analyzing error propagation patterns (e.g., mis-segmented clauses causing classifier failures) through ablation studies.

### Open Question 3
*   **Question:** How sensitive are the reported segmentation metrics (ROUGE, Factual Correctness, Semantic Similarity) to the Needleman-Wunsch alignment threshold of 0.7, and does this threshold introduce bias in evaluating clause boundary detection for structurally diverse NDAs?
*   **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method uses a fixed 0.7 threshold to filter clause alignments, claiming it reduces comparisons by 92.5%, but provides no justification for this value or analysis of metric stability across thresholds.
*   **Why unresolved:** The threshold is chosen arbitrarily without validation; it may exclude valid but lexically divergent clause matches, potentially inflating precision metrics and masking segmentation failures on non-standard clause phrasings.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-running evaluations across a range of thresholds (e.g., 0.5-0.9) and correlating threshold changes with metric variances, ideally on a held-out set with expert-validated clause alignments to determine the optimal, unbiased threshold.

### Open Question 4
*   **Question:** To what extent do document layout complexities (e.g., tables, multi-column formats, headers/footers) degrade the segmentation model's clause extraction accuracy compared to plain-text NDAs, and can prompt engineering or preprocessing mitigate these effects?
*   **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper notes "discrepancies between input and output lengths are partly due to the LLM’s difficulty reproducing elements like tables and headers," implying layout issues but quantifying their impact.
*   **Why unresolved:** No ablation study isolating layout effects; the dataset includes varied formats but results are aggregated, so performance on complex layouts vs. simple ones is unknown. Prompt adaptations are mentioned but not systematically evaluated for layout robustness.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Curating a controlled subset of the dataset with annotated layout types (simple vs. complex), re-evaluating segmentation metrics per layout category, and testing modified prompts or preprocessing steps (e.g., layout-aware text extraction) to measure performance gains on complex documents.

## Limitations
*   The exact 499-token prompt for the segmentation LLM is undisclosed, making full replication of its specific performance dependent on prompt engineering.
*   Per-class performance metrics and the exact distribution of the 14 clause types are not reported, preventing a full analysis of classification behavior on minority classes.
*   The Needleman-Wunsch alignment process details (e.g., scoring matrix) beyond the 0.7 threshold are vague, introducing potential variability in evaluation reproducibility.

## Confidence
*   **Segmentation generalization:** Medium. High ROUGE (0.95) supports effectiveness, but prompt dependency and context-window limits (3,401 tokens) reduce certainty.
*   **Classification on frequent classes:** High. Weighted F1 (0.85) indicates strong learning, though minority class performance is unreliable (Macro F1 0.69).
*   **Alignment-based evaluation:** Medium. Efficient and correlates with semantic metrics, but may fail on paraphrased clauses as noted in break condition.
