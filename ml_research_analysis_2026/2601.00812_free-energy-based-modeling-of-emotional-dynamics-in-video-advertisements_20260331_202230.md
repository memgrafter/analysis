# Free Energy-Based Modeling of Emotional Dynamics in Video Advertisements

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.00812
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00812
- **Reference count**: 37
- **Primary result**: Using the Free Energy Principle, the study derives scene-level emotional metrics (KLD, BS, UN) from expression features, clusters ads into three dynamic patterns, and demonstrates robustness and cross-dataset generalization.

## Executive Summary
Video advertisements are designed to evoke specific emotional responses, but measuring these dynamics without relying on external physiological data remains challenging. This paper introduces a novel framework based on the Free Energy Principle (FEP) to quantify emotional fluctuations in ads directly from their audiovisual content. By extracting scene-level expression features via vision and language models, the method derives three Free Energy components—Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD), Bayesian surprise (BS), and uncertainty (UN)—which map onto pleasantness and surprise dimensions. These scene metrics are aggregated into video-level indices that characterize emotional trajectories. The approach is evaluated on a large corpus of food advertisements and tested for generalization across Japanese ad genres and durations.

The results demonstrate that KLD aligns with brand presentation (pleasantness), BS with informational complexity, and UN with element variability. Clustering on video-level indices reveals three prototypical emotional dynamics: uncertain stimulus, sustained high emotion, and momentary peak-and-decay. Robustness analyses show stable correlations across hyperparameter settings, and cross-dataset tests confirm similar cluster structures and significant genre-specific distributions. This work shows promise for computational emotion modeling in advertising without the need for human-labeled training data.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with scene segmentation using PySceneDetect, extracting the middle frame per scene. Visual expression elements are obtained from these frames using a vision-language model (MiniCPM-V2.6) prompted to output binary presence of categories (Attract, Brand, Connect, Direct). Audio elements are extracted by separating speech with Demucs, transcribing via Whisper, and analyzing the transcript with an LLM (phi-4). The resulting modality-specific binary vectors are aligned temporally and fed into a variational Bayesian Hidden Markov Model (HMM) with Dirichlet priors; the model learns state transitions and observation probabilities, optionally separating or combining modalities. For each scene, Free Energy components are computed: KLD (perception error) reflects divergence between prior and posterior; BS and UN measure learning updates. To characterize the whole ad, scene-level metrics are aggregated into peak, end, skew, and decay rate (the latter via simulating repeated viewings). Finally, k‑means clustering on these four indices identifies emotional patterns, and the system’s validity is checked through correlations with expression features, hyperparameter stability, and generalization across ad types.

## Key Results
- Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD) correlated with brand presentation, interpreted as pleasantness.
- Bayesian surprise (BS) correlated with informational complexity, capturing surprise; uncertainty (UN) correlated with element variability.
- Video-level metrics (peak, end, skew, decay) clustered ads into three distinct emotional dynamics patterns. Robustness checks across hyperparameters showed stable correlations (audio ~1.0, visual ≥0.36). Generalization to six Japanese ad types confirmed similar cluster structures and genre-specific differences (χ²=341.41, p<.001).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD) serves as an indicator of pleasantness in video advertisements.
- **Mechanism**: KLD quantifies the information gain when the internal model (Hidden Markov Model) updates its prior beliefs to match observed scene features. Scenes containing brand-oriented elements (e.g., logos, product images) are associated with higher KLD values. The paper posits that these prediction errors align with subjective pleasantness, based on prior Free Energy Principle (FEP) literature linking valence to free energy fluctuations.
- **Core assumption**: The KLD component of variational free energy corresponds to experienced pleasantness, and brand presentation deliberately used by creators reliably evokes this pleasantness.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD) correlated with brand presentation (pleasantness)"
  - [section V.A] "KLD exhibited positive associations with brand-oriented elements" across both visual and audio modalities.
  - [corpus] Nearby papers (e.g., "Saliency-guided Emotion Modeling") also explore emotion prediction but do not directly validate the KLD–pleasantness link; this paper provides initial correlative evidence.
- **Break condition**: If future subjective rating studies fail to confirm the relationship between KLD and pleasantness, or if the correlation with brand elements disappears in other cultural contexts, this mechanism would be undermined.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Bayesian surprise (BS) captures surprise arising from informational complexity.
- **Mechanism**: BS measures the update required for the prior distribution after observing a scene, effectively reflecting how much beliefs must change to accommodate new information. Scenes with many co-occurring expression elements (high total feature count, brand and direct-action elements) yield larger BS, indicating higher perceived surprise due to complexity.
- **Core assumption**: BS corresponds to subjective surprise, as established in prior FEP work (e.g., Itti & Baldi), and informational complexity in ads drives this surprise.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Bayesian surprise (BS) with informational complexity (surprise)"
  - [section V.A] "BS: positive associations with the total number of visual features and with brand-oriented or direct action–inducing elements."
- **Break condition**: Weak or absent correlations between BS and complexity metrics in new datasets, or mismatch with human surprise ratings, would challenge this mechanism.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Uncertainty (UN) reflects surprise driven by element variability and quantity.
- **Mechanism**: UN represents the entropy of the posterior distribution over hidden states after observation, indicating model uncertainty. Higher UN occurs when scenes contain diverse element types (e.g., many direct-action cues) or when element arrangements are variable. Negative associations with attention-attracting elements (visual) and out-of-category audio suggest that simple, stereotypical elements reduce uncertainty.
- **Core assumption**: UN indexes a form of surprise related to ambiguity or unpredictability, consistent with FEP definitions of surprise components.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "uncertainty (UN) with element variability (surprise)"
  - [section V.A] "UN: negative association with attention-attracting elements and positive association with the total number of visual features and direct action–inducing elements."
  - [section V.A] Audio modality: "UN demonstrated positive associations with ... total number of audio expression features."
- **Break condition**: Inconsistent associations between UN and element variability across genres or durations, or failure to predict subjective uncertainty, would weaken this mechanism.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Video advertisement -> Scene segmentation (PySceneDetect) -> Feature extraction (VLM: MiniCPM-V2.6 for visual; LLM: phi-4 for audio via Whisper+Demucs) -> Scene-level binary vectors (Attract, Brand, Connect, Direct) -> HMM training (variational Bayesian) -> FE computation (KLD, BS, UN) -> Video-level aggregation (peak, end, skew, decay) -> Analysis (clustering, robustness checks, generalization).

### Critical Path
Feature extraction → HMM training → FE computation → video-level indices. Errors in feature extraction propagate directly; HMM training must converge reliably.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **HMM hyperparameters** (number of hidden states, Dirichlet prior scale): More states increase model capacity but risk overfitting; prior scale affects smoothing. Optimal values (5 states, 0.2 scale) were tuned on food dataset.
- **Modality separation vs. fusion**: Paper often computes FE per modality then combines; could miss cross-modal interactions. A joint multimodal HMM might capture deeper integration but increases complexity.
- **Expression element taxonomy**: Fixed set based on ABCD framework; may omit genre-specific expressions. Generalizability to other ad types might require expanding the list.

### Failure Signatures
- **Unstable clustering**: Low Adjusted Rand Index (<0.2) across hyperparameter perturbations indicates that emotional patterns are not robustly captured.
- **Weak scene-level correlations**: Low absolute Pearson r (<0.2) between FE metrics and expression features suggests the FE components are not aligning with intended expressive structures.
- **Poor cross-dataset generalization**: Matrix correlation <0.3 between visual modality patterns across genres indicates overfitting to specific visual styles (e.g., car ads).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Scene-level validation**: On a held-out set of food ads, compute Pearson correlations between FE metrics and expression element frequencies; expect positive KLD–brand, BS–complexity, UN–variability patterns.
2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Vary hidden states (±2) and Dirichlet scale (±0.1) around optimal; recompute correlations and clustering ARI to ensure stability (confidence intervals narrow, ARI >0.2).
3. **Cross-condition generalization**: Train HMMs on cosmetics and car ads (both durations); compute correlation matrices between FE metrics and expression features; test similarity with food-reference matrices using vectorized matrix correlation (expect audio ~1.0, visual ≥0.36).

## Limitations
- FE-to-emotion mappings are inferential, lacking direct validation with human subjective ratings; cultural generalizability is limited (only six Japanese ad types tested).
- Feature extraction depends on undisclosed VLM/LLM prompts and metadata handling; binary expression tagging reproducibility is unclear.
- Original dataset (1,059 food ads) is inaccessible; proxy datasets may not capture identical expressive structures, risking dataset shift.

## Confidence
- **Scene-level FE-emotion mappings**: Medium
- **Video-level clustering patterns**: Medium
- **Cross-dataset generalization**: Medium-Low
