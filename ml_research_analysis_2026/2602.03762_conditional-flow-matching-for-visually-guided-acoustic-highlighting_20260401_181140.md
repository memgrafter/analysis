# Conditional Flow Matching for Visually-Guided Acoustic Highlighting

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.03762
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03762
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: VisAH-FM achieves state-of-the-art on Muddy Mix, improving IB Score to 29.1, reducing KLD to 9.7, LDif to 7.77, with a 60% subjective win rate over VisAH.

## Executive Summary
Visually-guided acoustic highlighting aims to rebalance audio to align with video content. Existing discriminative models struggle due to the many-to-many ambiguity in audio remixing: a single poorly-balanced input may correspond to multiple plausible well-balanced outputs.

This work reformulates the task as a generative problem using Conditional Flow Matching (CFM). Two key innovations are introduced: (1) a rollout loss that penalizes deviation at the final step of multi-step flow integration to prevent error accumulation, and (2) a conditioning module that fuses audio (CLAP) and visual (CLIP) cues via adapter layers for explicit cross-modal source selection. On the Muddy Mix dataset, VisAH-FM outperforms the prior state-of-the-art VisAH on all objective metrics (IB Score +0.2, KLD -2, LDif -1.9) and achieves a 60% win rate in subjective listening tests. Ablations confirm the contribution of both components.

## Method Summary
VisAH-FM employs a Conditional Flow Matching framework to learn a velocity field that transforms a poorly-balanced audio mixture into a well-balanced target. A CLIP image encoder extracts per‑frame visual features, while a CLAP audio encoder processes the input audio. An adapter module injects audio features into intermediate CLIP layers (e.g., layer 18) via cross‑attention, yielding audio‑aware visual tokens. A DEMUCS‑based U‑Net (vector field estimator) takes a noised sample \(x_t\) and the conditioning tokens, predicts \(u_\theta\), and computes the velocity as \(v_\theta = x_0 - u_\theta\) (warm start). Training combines a standard CFM loss and a rollout loss: after *T* flow steps from the source, the final output is compared to the target via MSE, encouraging self‑correcting trajectories. Data are generated on‑the‑fly from source stems (speech, music, effects) with random adjustments. Inference uses 4 integration steps.

## Key Results
- IB Score improved from 28.84/28.92 to 29.1; KLD reduced from 11.37/11.71 to 9.7; LDif dropped from 9.66/9.63 to 7.77.
- Subjective test: 60% win rate versus VisAH.
- Ablations confirm that rollout loss and audio‑visual conditioning each contribute positively to performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Reformulating visually-guided acoustic highlighting as a generative task using Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) better captures the inherent many-to-many mapping between poorly-balanced and well-balanced audio, overcoming limitations of discriminative models.

**Mechanism:** CFM learns a continuous, time-dependent velocity field that transports samples from the source (poorly-balanced) audio distribution to the target (well-balanced) distribution. This distributional alignment allows multiple plausible outputs for a given input, rather than forcing a single deterministic mapping.

**Core assumption:** The acoustic highlighting task has fundamental ambiguity: for a fixed video, there exist several valid ways to rebalance audio sources, and the poorly-balanced input can correspond to many well-balanced targets.

**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract]: “we reframe this task as a generative problem and introduce a Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) framework” and “establishing that visually-guided audio remixing is best addressed through generative modeling.”
- [section 2.2]: “Given its many-to-many nature we argue that a generative approach is better suited than a purely discriminative one.”
- [corpus]: Corpus contains flow matching applications to other ambiguous audio tasks (e.g., FlowTSE for target speaker extraction), suggesting broader relevance; however, direct evidence for acoustic highlighting is solely from this paper.

**Break condition:** If the mapping were truly one-to-one (e.g., a fixed rule for remixing), a discriminative model could suffice and be more efficient; but the paper’s analysis of the task’s ambiguity makes this unlikely.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** The rollout loss stabilizes long-range flow integration by penalizing deviation at the final step, promoting self-correcting trajectories and preventing error accumulation across iterative steps.

**Mechanism:** During training, the model performs a full rollout of *T* steps starting from the source audio. An auxiliary MSE loss compares the final output after these steps to the ground-truth target. This end-to-end supervision forces the learned velocity field to produce coherent multi-step trajectories that can recover from early prediction errors, rather than compounding them.

**Core assumption:** In iterative flow matching, small inaccuracies in the early steps’ velocity predictions can compound over time, pushing samples off the data manifold and degrading final output quality.

**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract]: “A key challenge is error accumulation over iterative steps; the proposed rollout loss penalizes deviation at the final step, promoting self-correcting trajectories and stabilizing long-range flow integration.”
- [section 2.2.1]: Detailed description of rollout loss and comparison to consistency loss; Figure 4a shows that without rollout, cosine similarity between predicted and ground-truth vector fields degrades over steps, while with rollout it remains high.
- [section 3.2.2]: Quantitative analysis of curvature and variance demonstrates that rollout reduces non-linear deviations in trajectories.
- [corpus]: The concept relates to consistency models and D-Flow, but the specific rollout formulation is novel; corpus does not provide direct evidence for this exact mechanism.

**Break condition:** The rollout loss is most beneficial when multiple inference steps are used. With a single-step model (or if the velocity field is already highly accurate), the loss may be unnecessary. Additionally, an excessively high rollout weight can force trajectories to become overly non-linear (as shown in Figure 4b), reducing performance.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The proposed conditioning module, which fuses audio and visual cues before vector field estimation via adapter layers, improves cross-modal source selection and leads to better audio‑visual alignment in the output.

**Mechanism:** An adapter layer injects audio features (from a CLAP encoder) into intermediate layers of the CLIP vision encoder through cross‑attention. This produces audio‑aware visual representations that explicitly encode which sound sources are present and should be enhanced/suppressed based on the video context. The U‑Net (vector field estimator) then receives conditioning that already indicates target sources, allowing it to focus on precise audio regression rather than solving cross‑modal reasoning.

**Core assumption:** Identifying the correct audio sources to highlight is primarily a cross‑modal reasoning task that can be solved in the conditioning pathway, rather than burdening the main regression network.

**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract]: “a conditioning module fuses audio and visual cues before vector field estimation, enabling explicit cross-modal source selection.”
- [section 2.2.2]: “the burden of identifying the target source is largely shifted to the U‑Net… we aim for the conditioning module to determine the source to enhance.”
- [section 3.2.1]: Table 2 shows that conditioning with vision + audio (CLAP) via adapter yields the best metrics (IB Score 29.12, KLD 9.70, LDif 7.77), outperforming vision‑only or vision+text.
- [corpus]: While corpus includes audio‑visual source separation works, the adapter‑based fusion strategy is unique to this paper; evidence comes from the paper’s own ablations.

**Break condition:** If the visual context alone is sufficiently informative (e.g., a clear close‑up of a speaker with no competing audio), the adapter may provide marginal gains. However, the consistent improvements across conditions in Table 2 suggest the adapter is generally beneficial.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
- Visual encoder: CLIP (image encoder) extracts per‑frame features.
- Audio encoder (conditioning): CLAP extracts features from the input (poorly‑balanced) audio.
- Conditioning module: Adapter layers inserted into CLIP (e.g., at layer 18) perform cross‑attention between CLIP and CLAP features, producing audio‑aware visual tokens. A learned scalar λ controls fusion strength.
- Timestep encoding: Sinusoidal embeddings of the flow timestep *t* are added to the conditioning tokens.
- Vector field estimator: DEMUCS‑based U‑Net (latent transformer + decoder) takes a noisy sample \(x_t\) and conditioning tokens, predicting \(u_\theta(x_t, t, c)\). The velocity is computed as \(v_\theta = x_0 - u_\theta\) (warm start).
- Losses:
  - CFM loss: \(\| (x_1 - x_t)/(1-t) - v_\theta(x_t, t, c) \|^2\)
  - Rollout loss: after *T* recurrent steps from \(x_0\), MSE between final output \(\hat{x}_T\) and target \(x_1\).
- Data pipeline: On‑the‑fly generation of poorly‑balanced mixes from source stems (speech, music, effects) using random adjustments.

### Critical path
1. For each sample, load video frames and the corresponding well‑balanced audio \(x_1\); generate poorly‑balanced input \(x_0\) via random source adjustments.
2. Extract CLIP features from video frames; extract CLAP features from \(x_0\).
3. Fuse CLIP and CLAP features via adapter layers to obtain audio‑aware visual conditioning.
4. Sample a timestep \(t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)\) and compute \(x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1\).
5. Pass \(x_t\) and conditioning to U‑Net, obtain \(u_\theta\), then \(v_\theta = x_0 - u_\theta\).
6. Compute CFM loss between \(v_\theta\) and the true velocity \((x_1 - x_t)/(1-t)\).
7. For rollout loss: start from \(x_0\), apply the flow for *T* steps (using fixed or sampled timesteps) to get \(\hat{x}_T\), then compute MSE with \(x_1\).
8. Backpropagate total loss \(L = L_{\text{CFM}} + \lambda L_{\text{rollout}}\).

### Design tradeoffs
- **On‑the‑fly vs fixed remixing:** On‑the‑fly increases training diversity but adds computational overhead; fixed dataset is faster but may overfit to specific mixes.
- **Adapter insertion layer:** Early layers preserve more pretrained structure but may limit fusion depth; the paper uses layer 18 of CLIP as a compromise.
- **Rollout loss weight λ:** Low λ gives minimal stabilization; high λ can force trajectories to become overly non‑linear (see Table 11). A moderate value (λ=0.3) works best.
- **Number of inference steps:** More steps can improve quality but increase latency; the paper uses 4 steps, which is a tradeoff between performance and efficiency.
- **Warm start:** Defining \(v_\theta = x_0 - u_\theta\) helps initialize from a pretrained discriminative model but requires careful handling of the skip connection.

### Failure signatures
- **No rollout loss:** Trajectory curvature increases over steps (Figure 4b), cosine similarity with ground‑truth velocity drops (Figure 4a), and final metrics degrade (Table 3: KLD ↑, LDif ↑).
- **No audio in conditioning:** Model fails to select correct sources, especially in ambiguous scenes; metrics worsen (Table 2: ✗V or ✗T).
- **Rollout loss only (λ large, no CFM):** Trajectories become highly non‑linear (Table 12, high curvature), leading to suboptimal results (Table 3: Rollout only slightly worse than FM+Rollout).
- **Incorrect λ initialization for adapter:** If λ_E is not initialized to zero, the pretrained CLIP features may be corrupted early in training, causing instability.
- **Overly high rollout horizon or steps:** May introduce excessive regularization or noise; Table 11 shows that rolling out over 10 steps (vs 4) slightly increases LDif.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline reproduction:** Train the original VisAH model on Muddy Mix to confirm data generation, metrics, and implementation parity. This establishes a reference point.
2. **Ablate conditioning audio:** Train VisAH‑FM with vision‑only conditioning (no adapter) vs vision+audio (CLAP) via adapter. Expect the latter to improve IB Score, KLD, and LDif.
3. **Ablate rollout loss:** Train with and without rollout loss (keeping other fixes constant). Measure trajectory stability (e.g., cosine similarity over steps) and final metrics; without rollout, expect error accumulation and worse performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Computational efficiency:** How can the computational efficiency of flow matching for acoustic highlighting be improved to enable practical deployment? (VisAH‑FM is computationally more demanding due to iterative steps and large encoders.)
2. **Robustness to weak/ambiguous cues:** How can the model be made robust to weak or ambiguous audio‑visual cues that lead to failure cases? (CLIP/CLAP may lack fine‑grained alignment.)
3. **Unpaired training:** Can the conditional flow matching framework be extended to unpaired training for greater real‑world applicability? (Current reliance on artificially mixed paired data restricts learning from natural content.)
4. **Generalization to in‑the‑wild data:** How does VisAH‑FM generalize to diverse in‑the‑wild video content beyond the controlled Muddy Mix dataset? (Muddy Mix uses pseudo‑data generation and may not capture full variability.)

## Limitations
- Evaluation limited to Muddy Mix; generalization to other datasets and domains remains uncertain.
- Conditioning relies on frozen CLIP/CLAP, which may degrade performance under domain shifts (e.g., unseen objects, non‑speech sounds).
- Rollout loss hyperparameters (λ, T) lack theoretical guidance and require manual tuning.
