# Less Data, Faster Convergence: Goal-Driven Data Optimization for Multimodal Instruction Tuning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.12478  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12478  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: GDO reduces training data by 14‑19× while matching or exceeding the accuracy of a 512k‑sample baseline across four multimodal video benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Multimodal instruction tuning suffers from uneven sample utility, leading to wasted computation on low‑value data. The authors propose Goal‑Driven Data Optimization (GDO) to address this inefficiency. GDO operates on a candidate pool of image and video QA samples. For each sample, it extracts six probe‑derived descriptors—optical flow, video‑dependence, temporal necessity, self‑consistency, perplexity‑like difficulty, and coverage—using a frozen Qwen3‑VL‑8B‑Instruct probe. These descriptors are normalized, combined via a fixed scoring function, and filtered through feasibility constraints to meet goal‑specific distribution targets (e.g., minimum video ratio). Under a strict one‑epoch SFT protocol with Qwen3‑VL‑8B‑Instruct, GDO achieves the same accuracy as a 512k‑sample baseline using only 26k–35k samples on MVBench, VideoMME, MLVU, and LVBench, and even surpasses the baseline by up to 3.08 percentage points. Four goal profiles—MinLoss, Diverse, Temp, and Temp+—demonstrate clear trade‑offs, with Temp/Temp+ yielding the largest gains on motion‑ and order‑focused benchmarks.

## Method Summary
GDO’s procedure is fully specified under a fixed training and evaluation contract to ensure comparability. Starting from a shared candidate pool comprising ~512k samples from LLaVA‑OneVision (image QA) and LLaVA‑Video (short‑video QA), the method computes six descriptors per sample using a frozen Qwen3‑VL‑8B‑Instruct probe: *mflow* (average RAFT optical flow magnitude), *mvds* (video‑dependence score, i.e., loss difference between blind and visual prompts), *mtnc* (temporal necessity via model judgment), *msc* (self‑consistency from stochastic decode Jaccard similarity), *mppl* (exponentiated video loss), and *mcov* (coverage from text/vision embedding density and source repetition). Each descriptor is normalized across the pool. For each goal profile (MinLoss, Diverse, Temp, Temp+), a predefined scoring function (ρvid for videos, ρimg for images) combines normalized descriptors with heuristic terms (text quality, difficulty bin alignment, temporal bonus, source rarity). Feasibility control Cg imposes constraints such as total budget, minimum video ratio, required VDS+ count, floor on temporally positive samples, and source floors. Subset selection proceeds by ranking candidates, then applying staged reservoir filling with deduplication and QA‑per‑video caps to produce a 1× subset. The subset is used to train Qwen3‑VL‑8B‑Instruct via one‑epoch supervised fine‑tuning (8×H20, batch 1/GPU). Checkpoints are evaluated on MVBench, VideoMME, MLVU, and LVBench to track accuracy versus training samples.

## Key Results
- GDO reaches the 512k‑sample baseline’s accuracy with far fewer samples: 35.4k on MVBench (14.5× reduction), 26.6k on VideoMME (19.2×), 27.3k on MLVU (18.8×), and 34.7k on LVBench (14.8×).
- It surpasses the baseline’s final performance: MVBench +1.38 percentage points, VideoMME +1.67 pp, MLVU +3.08 pp, LVBench +0.84 pp.
- Four goal profiles offer trade‑offs: MinLoss maximizes sample efficiency; Diverse maintains broader coverage; Temp and Temp+ progressively enhance temporal understanding, with the strongest gains on motion‑ and order‑focused benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of GDO stems from its multi‑faceted utility estimation. Optical flow (mflow) identifies visually dynamic content, which often requires richer visual reasoning. Video‑dependence (mvds) directly measures how much the question relies on visual input; a high score indicates the answer cannot be derived from text alone. Temporal necessity (mtnc) explicitly targets questions that require understanding of time, crucial for video tasks. Self‑consistency (msc) captures semantic ambiguity; low consistency suggests the sample is noisy or underspecified, which may harm learning. Perplexity (mppl) reflects language difficulty, helping to balance the curriculum. Coverage (mcov) promotes diversity and prevents over‑representation of any single source. The scoring function aggregates these signals with goal‑specific weighting, while feasibility constraints guarantee that the selected subset respects distributional minima (e.g., enough temporally positive samples). By focusing the limited training budget on samples that score highly across these axes, GDO ensures every training token is maximally informative, leading to faster convergence and higher final performance than a random or naively mixed large dataset.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multimodal instruction tuning**  
  *Why needed*: To adapt large vision‑language models to follow human instructions over arbitrary images and videos, enabling practical interactive AI.  
  *Quick check*: After training on a small instruction set, the model should correctly answer questions such as “Describe what is happening in this video.” or “What object is the person holding?”.

- **Sample utility estimation**  
  *Why needed*: Training data varies in its contribution to model capabilities; identifying high‑utility samples maximizes learning per compute unit and reduces waste.  
  *Quick check*: Compute the loss reduction on a held‑out set when a sample is added to a batch; high‑utility samples should yield larger drops.

- **Handcrafted descriptors (e.g., VDS, TNC)**  
  *Why needed*: They provide interpretable, cheap‑to‑compute proxies for a sample’s instructional value without requiring full training runs.  
  *Quick check*: On a random subset, verify that VDS is positive for most video‑dependent questions, TNC is higher for temporal questions, and mflow is non‑zero for videos but zero for images.

- **Goal‑driven subset construction**  
  *Why needed*: Different downstream tasks demand different skill distributions; constructing subsets tailored to a goal (e.g., temporal reasoning) allows targeted improvement.  
  *Quick check*: After subset selection, check that constraint metrics (e.g., minimum video ratio, VDS+ count) meet the feasibility control values; also evaluate the trained model on a goal‑relevant benchmark.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Candidate Pool -> Descriptor Extraction (frozen Qwen3‑VL‑8B‑Instruct) -> Normalization -> Scoring (ρvid/ρimg) -> Feasibility Control (Cg) -> Subset Selection -> Model Training (Qwen3‑VL‑8B‑Instruct SFT) -> Evaluation (MVBench, VideoMME, MLVU, LVBench)

**Critical path**  
1. Compute all six descriptors for every sample in the candidate pool.  
2. Normalize each descriptor dimension to zero mean and unit variance across the pool.  
3. Apply the goal‑specific scoring function to produce a utility score for each sample.  
4. Enforce feasibility constraints (budget, minimum video ratio, VDS+ target, temporally positive floor, source floors) while selecting the top‑ranked samples, using staged reservoir filling, deduplication, and per‑video QA caps.  
5. Train a fresh Qwen3‑VL‑8B‑Instruct model from the selected subset using the exact one‑epoch SFT configuration.  
6. Evaluate checkpoints on the four target benchmarks to measure convergence and final accuracy.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Fixed scoring function vs. learned**: A handcrafted formula is transparent and avoids extra training, but may not capture interactions between descriptors or adapt to novel goals.  
- **Single candidate pool vs. specialized pools**: Using existing image‑video datasets simplifies data collection but may lack coverage for certain skills (e.g., ultra‑long video reasoning).  
- **Frozen probe model**: Using the same architecture as the target model avoids domain gap but the probe’s frozen state may not reflect the model’s evolving representations during training.  
- **Heuristic terms in scoring**: Terms like temporal bonus and source rarity allow explicit balancing but introduce hyperparameters that must be set manually, potentially overfitting to the baseline.

**Failure signatures**  
- Subset statistics (selected video ratio, VDS+ count, temporally positive floor) differ from reported Table 6 by more than 2%.  
- Descriptor distributions (e.g., mean mflow for videos, VDS positivity rate) are far outside expected ranges (e.g., VDS < 0 for most videos).  
- Training loss curve shows instability (spikes, divergence) or significantly slower decline compared to Figure 5.  
- Final benchmark scores do not improve over the baseline despite using a smaller subset, indicating likely errors in scoring or selection.

**First experiments**  
1. **Descriptor sanity check**: Extract descriptors on 100 random samples; confirm that mflow > 0 for videos and ~0 for images; VDS is positive for a majority of video samples; TNC correlates with manually annotated temporal questions.  
2. **Subset construction audit**: Build the MinLoss subset and compute its composition (video ratio, VDS+ count, etc.). Compare to the paper’s Table 6; if mismatched, revise normalization or constraint implementation.  
3. **Convergence trajectory validation**: Train on the MinLoss subset, checkpoint at regular intervals, and evaluate MVBench. Plot accuracy vs. training samples and compare to Figure 5; substantial deviations indicate recipe or data differences.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can we design goal‑specific scoring functions that adapt to specialized objectives like ultra‑long video understanding?  
- How can we construct training pools with sufficient native ultra‑long video coverage to alleviate the LVBench mismatch?  
- Can we obtain more reliable temporal necessity signals than the current question‑level proxy?  
- Are the six handcrafted sample descriptors sufficient, or are there additional cues (e.g., audio, scene complexity) that could improve utility prediction?

## Limitations
- Probe configuration (model version, prompts, decoding settings) for key descriptors (VDS, TNC, SC) is not documented, making exact replication difficult.  
- The scoring function’s heuristic terms and normalization steps are opaque; the exact `quality_score` merge formula is missing.  
- No ablation study isolates the contribution of individual descriptors; their necessity remains unproven.  

## Confidence
