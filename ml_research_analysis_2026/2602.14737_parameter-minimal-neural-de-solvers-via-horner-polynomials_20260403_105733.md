# Parameter-Minimal Neural DE Solvers via Horner Polynomials

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14737
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14737
- **Reference Count:** 31
- **Primary Result:** Horner polynomial networks solve ODEs/PDEs with minimal parameters (tens) while achieving lower RMSE (e.g., 4.6e‑6) than larger MLP/SIREN baselines.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a neural architecture that uses Horner-factorized polynomials as trial solutions for differential equations. By restricting the hypothesis class, the number of learnable parameters is drastically reduced. Initial conditions are hard-coded exactly, and a spline-like extension uses multiple small Horner models on subintervals with continuity constraints. On ODE benchmarks and a 2D heat equation, the approach achieves orders-of-magnitude lower root mean square error with far fewer parameters compared to standard MLP and SIREN baselines. The spline extension further improves accuracy by an order of magnitude with minimal parameter growth, demonstrating an effective trade‑off for resource‑efficient scientific machine learning.

## Method Summary
The core building block is the Horner polynomial: \(P(t) = a_0 + t\cdot(a_1 + t\cdot(\dots + t\cdot a_m)\dots)\). The first \(n\) coefficients (where \(n\) is determined by initial conditions) are fixed to satisfy the DE’s initial conditions exactly, leaving only \(m-n+1\) free parameters. During training, collocation points are sampled in the domain (e.g., 200 uniformly for ODEs). The network’s output and its derivatives (via automatic differentiation) are used to compute the mean‑squared residual loss, minimized with Adam (learning rate \(10^{-3}\), 10 000 epochs). For the heat equation, a nested Horner structure (outer over \(y\), inner over \(x\)) with 45 total parameters is used; boundary/initial conditions are enforced by soft penalties. The spline extension partitions the domain into subintervals; each interval uses an independent Horner net (degree 7). Continuity at the knots is promoted via absolute‑difference penalties on function value and derivative (weights \(\mu=\nu=0.5\)), while the residual loss and an exact initial‑condition term (weight \(\lambda_0=1\)) are also included.

## Key Results
- **Type A ODE:** Horner network (10 learnable parameters) achieves RMSE \(4.6\times10^{-6}\) vs. MLP (106 parameters) RMSE \(2.7\times10^{-5}\).
- **Spline extension:** Using 4 subintervals with degree‑7 Horner models reduces RMSE by an order of magnitude relative to a single Horner net with modest parameter growth.
- **2D heat equation:** Solved with only 45 parameters and competitive accuracy, demonstrating parameter efficiency in higher dimensions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Horner’s method provides a compact representation of polynomials; fixing low‑order coefficients enforces initial conditions exactly, eliminating the need for soft penalties and reducing hyperparameter sensitivity. The resulting parameter‑minimal architecture acts as a strong inductive bias toward smooth, polynomial‑like solutions. The spline‑like extension partitions the domain, allowing local adaptivity while continuity constraints maintain global smoothness. This combination yields high accuracy with very few parameters, especially effective for low‑dimensional DEs where smooth solutions can be approximated by modest‑degree polynomials.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Coefficients (a₀…aₘ) → Horner block (recursive multiplication‑addition) → Network output \(N(t)\) → Automatic derivatives → Residual loss (plus penalties for spline/PDE) → Adam update.

**Critical path:** Input \(t\) (or \((x,y)\) for PDE) is fed into the Horner evaluation to produce the solution. Derivatives are obtained via autograd. The residual \(r = dN/dt + 2N - 1\) (Type A) is computed, and its mean square forms the primary loss. For splines, outputs at interval boundaries are additionally fed into absolute‑difference penalties. The total loss is back‑propagated to update the free coefficients.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Polynomial degree*: Higher degree increases expressive power but risks numerical instability and overfitting; requires careful initialization.  
- *Spline granularity*: More subintervals raise parameter count but improve local accuracy; continuity penalties balance smoothness.  
- *Parameter vs. accuracy*: Horner achieves high accuracy with few parameters; for complex/high‑dimensional DEs, higher degree may be needed, potentially reducing the parameter advantage.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Initial condition not exactly satisfied ⇒ coefficient fixing or Horner recursion bug.  
- Exploding coefficients/NaNs during training ⇒ degree too high or learning rate inadequate.  
- Visible jumps at subinterval boundaries ⇒ continuity penalties too weak or poorly tuned weights.  
- Poor validation accuracy ⇒ underfitting (degree too low) or insufficient collocation points.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Implement Horner network (degree 10, fix \(a_0=1\) for Type A ODE). Train on 200 uniform collocation points with Adam (\(lr=10^{-3}\), 10 000 epochs). Verify \(N(0)=1\) and compute RMSE on a dense grid; expect \(\approx 4.6\times10^{-6}\).  
2. Replicate SIREN baseline (4 hidden layers of 5 neurons, 106 parameters) with soft IC penalty \(\lambda_0=0.1\); train under same settings. Expect RMSE \(\approx 2.7\times10^{-5}\), confirming Horner’s advantage.  
3. Build spline model: 4 subintervals, each subnet degree 7, with \(\lambda_0=1\), \(\mu=\nu=0.5\). Check continuity at knots and verify RMSE improves by at least \(10\times\) relative to single Horner.
