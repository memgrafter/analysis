# Who Deserves the Reward? SHARP: Shapley Credit-based Optimization for Multi-Agent System

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.08335  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08335  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: SHARP achieves an average performance of 32.56 on four benchmarks, improving over single-agent baselines by 23.66% and over multi-agent baselines by 14.05%.

## Executive Summary
SHARP addresses the credit assignment problem in LLM-based multi-agent systems by decomposing rewards into three orthogonal components: a global broadcast accuracy reward (terminal task success), a Shapley-based marginal credit reward that estimates each agent’s contribution via counterfactual masking, and a tool-process reward ensuring valid tool usage. These components are normalized across sampled trajectory groups using group-relative advantages to stabilize policy updates. Evaluated on MuSiQue, GAIA-text, WebWalkerQA, and FRAMES with a Qwen3-8B backbone, SHARP achieves an average score of 32.56, representing improvements of 23.66% over single-agent and 14.05% over multi-agent baselines. The framework also reduces harmful sub-agent interactions from 5.48% to 4.40% and shows scaling benefits with larger models.

## Method Summary
SHARP employs a shared LLM policy instantiated with role-specific prompts: a planner that decomposes queries into subtasks, and workers that execute subtasks using tools (e.g., Python execution, web search). The reward for each agent *m* in trajectory *τ* aggregates:  
1. **Broadcast accuracy reward** `R_acc(τ)` (binary terminal signal for correct final answer).  
2. **Shapley-based marginal credit** `R_mc,m = R_acc(τ) - R_acc(τ\m)`, where `τ\m` masks the agent’s actions while preserving others; for the planner, credit is the average of positive worker credits scaled by λ.  
3. **Tool-process reward** `R_tool` as the average validity score `φ(a,s)` across the agent’s tool calls.  
The aggregated reward `¯R = α·R_b + β·R_mc + γ·R_tool` is normalized across a group of *G* sampled trajectories for the same query: advantage `ˆA = (¯R - μ_m)/(σ_m + δ)`, where `μ_m` and `σ_m` are the mean and standard deviation of `¯R` across the group for agent *m*. The policy is updated via a PPO-like clipped surrogate objective maximizing `J_SHARP(θ) = E[min(R·ˆA, clip(R,1-ϵ,1+ϵ)·ˆA)]` with AdamW. Key hyperparameters: `α=0.9`, `β=0.9`, `γ=0.1`, `ϵ=0.2`, `δ=1e-6`, group size `G=8`, batch size 256, learning rate 1e-5, and 180 training steps.

## Key Results
- **Average performance**: 32.56 across MuSiQue, GAIA-text, WebWalkerQA, and FRAMES.  
- **Relative gains**: +23.66% over single-agent baselines, +14.05% over multi-agent baselines.  
- **Harmful interactions**: Reduced from 5.48% to 4.40%.  
- **Scaling**: Performance increases with model size up to 8B parameters.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: SHARP enables precise credit assignment by decomposing the reward into three orthogonal components: global task success, individual marginal contribution, and tool execution validity.
- **Mechanism**: The global broadcast accuracy reward (binary terminal signal) aligns with final outcome. The Shapley-based marginal credit reward is computed by counterfactually masking each agent’s actions in a trajectory and measuring the drop in global reward, approximating Shapley value. The tool process reward aggregates per-call validity scores (e.g., correct tool usage). These are linearly combined with tunable weights (α, β, γ) to form the total surrogate reward per agent.
- **Core assumption**: A single-trajectory counterfactual ablation provides a computationally feasible approximation of Shapley value, which in cooperative game theory fairly distributes total value based on marginal contributions over all coalitions.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract]: “tripartite reward mechanism comprising a global broadcast-accuracy reward, a Shapley-based marginal-credit reward, and a tool-process reward”  
  - [section 4.2]: Formal definitions in Eq. (6)–(9), including the counterfactual credit in Eq. (11).  
  - [corpus]: Neighbors include “Shapley Value Based Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning” (paper_id 125828, FMR 0.73), which applies Shapley to MARL credit assignment, supporting the approach’s validity.
- **Break condition**: If agents are highly interdependent, masking one may alter the counterfactual contributions of others, violating the *ceteris paribus* assumption and biasing marginal estimates.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Normalizing advantages within groups of sampled trajectories stabilizes policy updates by reducing variance in the learning signal across heterogeneous task instances.
- **Mechanism**: For each agent identity *m*, SHARP computes the mean `μ_m` and standard deviation `σ_m` of the aggregated rewards `¯R_i,m` over *G* trajectories per query. The group-relative advantage is then `ˆA_i,m = (¯R_i,m − μ_m) / (σ_m + δ)`, effectively a z-score normalization within the group.
- **Core assumption**: Trajectory groups for a given query share similar difficulty; normalizing per-agent rewards across the group removes query-specific biases and yields a low-variance gradient estimate.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract]: “normalizing agent-specific advantages across trajectory groups”  
  - [section 4.4]: Explicit equation for group-relative advantage (Eq. 14) with `μ_m` and `σ_m`.  
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; corpus includes return decomposition methods (e.g., STAS, paper_id 209255) but not group-relative normalization. Assumption: This technique extends group-based RL baselines like GRPO to multi-agent settings.
- **Break condition**: If the group size *G* is too small, the mean and std become noisy, leading to unstable advantage estimates; if group diversity is low, normalization may not effectively mitigate variance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Counterfactual masking isolates each agent’s causal impact on task success, enabling fine-grained policy updates even in hierarchical planner-worker systems.
- **Mechanism**: For a worker agent *m* in trajectory `τ_i`, the system constructs a counterfactual trajectory `τ\m,i` by masking *m*’s actions while preserving the planner’s plan, other workers, and environmental transitions. The marginal credit is `R_acc(τ_i) − R_acc(τ\m,i)`. For the planner, credit is the average of positive worker credits, reflecting its indirect influence.
- **Core assumption**: The difference in terminal reward upon removing an agent approximates that agent’s marginal contribution, assuming other agents’ policies and environment dynamics remain unchanged.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [section 4.3]: “counterfactual marginal contribution formulation… `τ\m` represents the trajectory via ablating agent *m*” and Eq. (11).  
  - [section 4.3]: Planner credit aggregation via Eq. (13) with λ scaling.  
  - [corpus]: Related to causality detection in MARL (paper_id 206206, “Causality Detection for Efficient MARL”) which uses ablated trajectories to identify influential agents.
- **Break condition**: In tightly coupled systems where agents dynamically adapt to each other, masking one may cause cascading behavior changes, making the ablation non-comparative and the marginal credit inaccurate.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Shared policy πθ (LLM) instantiated with role-specific prompts: planner (task decomposition) and workers (subtask execution). Reward decomposer, group statistics module, advantage normalizer, clipped policy gradient updater  
- **Critical path**: Input query → planner generates subtask actions → for each subtask, a worker executes with tools → collect full trajectory τ → compute R_acc, counterfactual R_mc for each agent, and R_tool → aggregate ¯Ri,m → compute group-relative ˆAi,m → update πθ via J_SHARP(θ)  
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Credit precision vs. computational cost: Exact Shapley over all coalitions is exponential; SHARP’s single-trajectory ablation trades theoretical purity for scalability.  
  - Reward weighting (α,β,γ): Increasing β emphasizes individual contribution but may destabilize if marginal credits are noisy; γ ensures basic tool competence.  
  - Group size G: Larger G improves normalization reliability but increases rollouts per query; balance based on available compute.  
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Policy collapse: If σm → 0 for an agent, advantage normalization divides by near-zero, causing gradient explosion. Monitor σm and increase δ if needed.  
  - Low marginal credits: If most agents get zero or negative R_mc, either the Shapley approximation is failing (e.g., due to strong agent interdependence) or many agents are redundant; consider architectural changes.  
  - Reward hacking: If tool process reward is weighted too heavily (γ↑), agents may make valid but irrelevant tool calls to inflate scores; adjust γ based on validation performance.  
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Benchmark evaluation**: Run SHARP on MuSiQue, GAIA-text, etc., comparing to single-agent GRPO and multi-agent MATPO to verify the claimed 23.66% and 14.05% gains (Table 1).  
  2. **Ablation of Shapley component**: Set β=0 to remove marginal credit and observe accuracy drop (Figure 3 left), confirming Mechanism 1’s contribution.  
  3. **Scaling test**: Vary model size from 0.6B to 8B on MuSiQue (Figure 4) to check if SHARP’s advantage grows with capacity, indicating robust scalability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question**: How can we more aggressively prune low-utility tool calls in multi-agent LLM systems?  
**Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors observe that "useful subagents remain a minority of total calls" and state that this "illustrates a fundamental bottleneck in multi-agent designs, highlighting the need for future research into more aggressive pruning of low-utility execution paths." (Section 5.5, RQ4 discussion)  
**Why unresolved**: Current approaches like SHARP reduce harmful interactions but only modestly increase useful ones (from 11.03% to 12.96%). A systematic method to automatically identify and eliminate neutral or redundant tool invocations remains lacking, limiting efficiency.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: A credit-based pruning mechanism integrated into training that dynamically suppresses low-marginal-contribution tool calls, evaluated across multiple benchmarks for both accuracy and inference cost. Demonstrating a significant reduction in neutral calls without task performance degradation would validate the approach.

### Open Question 2
**Question**: What are the computational limits of Shapley-based credit assignment in large-scale multi-agent systems, and how can we approximate it efficiently without sacrificing fairness?  
**Basis in paper**: [inferred] SHARP approximates Shapley values via leave-one-out counterfactuals, requiring rerunning trajectories for each ablated agent. Appendix D shows full credit computation (p=1) increases training latency by ~97% (684s → 1345s per batch), indicating a scalability bottleneck.  
**Why unresolved**: The cost-performance trade-off is identified but no adaptive approximations are proposed. Efficiently selecting which agents to credit (e.g., via sampling) while preserving Shapley’s fairness properties (e.g., symmetry, dummy) is an open problem.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Propose an adaptive Monte Carlo or gradient-based Shapley estimator and show it matches full leave-one-out performance at lower computational cost, while maintaining fairness metrics on synthetic and real benchmarks.

### Open Question 3
**Question**: How valid is the leave-one-out approximation of Shapley values in hierarchical planner-worker systems, and what biases does the counterfactual construction introduce?  
**Basis in paper**: [inferred] SHARP defines agent credit as ΔR_acc between full and masked trajectories, not the full Shapley average over subsets. The counterfactual τ\m masks an agent’s actions while preserving others, potentially breaking sequential dependencies (e.g., a worker’s failure may alter planner’s later decisions).  
**Why unresolved**: No analysis of approximation bias or counterfactual validity exists. In hierarchical settings, masking a worker leaves the planner’s subtask instructions unchanged, creating unrealistic state trajectories that could distort marginal contributions.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Compare leave-one-out credits against exact Shapley values on small-scale synthetic tasks; vary counterfactual designs (e.g., re-planning after masking) and measure impact on final performance and credit fairness.

### Open Question 4
**Question**: How does SHARP generalize to communication topologies beyond planner-worker hierarchies (e.g., dynamic graphs, peer-to-peer)?  
**Basis in paper**: [inferred] Contributions claim applicability to "sequential chains, communication graphs, and hierarchical planner-worker paradigms" but experiments solely use a single planner and workers. The planner credit formula (Eq. 13) assumes a clear hierarchy, averaging worker credits—unsuitable for symmetric topologies.  
**Why unresolved**: Credit aggregation rules for non-hierarchical structures are underspecified. Without a general principle (e.g., treating all agents uniformly), extension to arbitrary graphs may fail or require ad-hoc adaptations.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Adapt SHARP’s credit assignment to architectures like G-Designer or CARD (e.g., by computing pairwise Shapley interactions or using graph Laplacian regularization) and evaluate on benchmarks where those topologies are standard.

## Limitations
- **Counterfactual masking implementation**: The exact replacement policy for masked agents (e.g., no-op, re-roll, placeholder) is unspecified, critically affecting Shapley approximation accuracy.
- **Tool-process reward granularity**: The reward function φ(a,s) is not detailed; whether it is binary (execution success) or continuous may lead to reward hacking or unstable incentives.
- **Independence assumption in ablations**: SHARP assumes other agents’ policies remain constant when one is masked, but in practice LLM agents may adapt dynamically, biased marginal contributions.

## Next Checks
1. **Define and audit counterfactual trajectory construction** τ\m. Implement a version with explicit replacement policies (e.g., “no-op” vs. “repeat last valid action” vs. “sample from prior policy”). Measure variance in marginal credits and final accuracy sensitivity to these design choices.
2. **Measure tool reward granularity impact**. Replace φ(a,s) with (a) binary execution success; (b) syntax-only check; (c) a continuous score including answer relevance (e.g., via LLM judge). Compare training stability and final tool misuse rates to isolate which aspects of tool validity matter.
3. **Quantify Shapley approximation bias via human evaluation**. For a held-out set of queries, have human annotators rank agent contributions (planner vs. workers). Compute correlation between SHARP’s marginal credits and human judgments. Low correlation would confirm the break condition and indicate a need for richer coalition sampling.
