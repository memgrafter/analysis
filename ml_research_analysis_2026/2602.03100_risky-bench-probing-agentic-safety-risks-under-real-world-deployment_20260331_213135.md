# Risky-Bench: Probing Agentic Safety Risks under Real-World Deployment

## Quick Facts
**ArXiv ID:** 2602.03100  
**Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03100  
**Reference count:** 40  
**Primary result:** Attack success rates of 25–60% on average, rising above 80% for tool and codebase attacks.

## Executive Summary
Risky-Bench addresses a critical gap in agentic safety evaluation. Existing benchmarks rely on limited, task-specific tests that do not capture the complexity of real-world deployments. The proposed framework introduces a three-stage pipeline: deriving operational safety rubrics from domain-agnostic principles, probing risks via realistic long-horizon tasks under varied threat models (instruction injection, environment manipulation, tool feedback, memory poisoning, system prompt attacks), and assessing agent trajectories automatically and with human oversight. Evaluation across seven state-of-the-art life-assist agents in delivery, in-store, and over-the-air (OTA) domains reveals substantial vulnerabilities, with average attack success rates (ASR) between 25% and 60%, and exceeding 80% for tool and codebase attacks. Even minimal adversarial access (e.g., user instructions, environment noise) produces non-trivial failures, underscoring the urgent need for robust safety mechanisms. The framework is designed to be extensible to diverse agent settings, offering a systematic approach to stress-test agentic safety.

## Method Summary
The Risky-Bench evaluation pipeline consists of three stages. First, a set of domain-agnostic safety principles (e.g., user interest protection) is conditioned on the deployment context (delivery, in-store, OTA) to generate concrete, observable rubrics that define safe behavior. Second, realistic long-horizon tasks sourced from VitaBench are transformed into risky scenarios by injecting adversarial attacks (e.g., prompt injection, memory poisoning) at identified attack surfaces (user instruction, environment, agent memory, tool feedback, agent instruction). This transformation is formalized as Ts = f_R,M(T), where T is a benign task, M is an attack method targeting rubric R. Third, agents execute the scenarios and their full trajectories are evaluated: an LLM-as-judge automatically scores violations against rubrics, and a human review phase validates and corrects labels to ensure reliability. The entire process yields attack success rates (ASR) for each rubric and attack surface, providing a comprehensive safety assessment.

## Key Results
- Average attack success rate (ASR) across agents and domains: 25–60%.
- Tool feedback and codebase attacks achieved ASR >80% for Qwen-Plus and DeepSeek-V3.2.
- Financial transaction rubrics were especially vulnerable, with high violation rates.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Risky-Bench systematically expands the safety risk space by deriving operational rubrics from domain-agnostic principles, ensuring coverage beyond task-specific hazards.  
**Mechanism:** High-level safety principles are conditioned on agent deployment settings to generate concrete, observable behavioral constraints (rubrics). This creates a structured taxonomy that maps abstract norms to checkable actions within a specific environment.  
**Core assumption:** Foundational safety principles are sufficiently universal to instantiate meaningful rubrics across diverse real-world agent deployments.  
**Evidence anchors:** The paper states that Risky-Bench organizes evaluation around domain-agnostic safety principles to derive context-aware safety rubrics; Section 2.1 details the hierarchical derivation; related works like OpenAgentSafety and FinVault similarly use principle-based frameworks.  
**Break condition:** If domain-agnostic principles fail to capture critical, context-specific failure modes for a new domain (e.g., medical agents), the rubric set becomes incomplete, leaving safety gaps.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Risky-Bench probes safety risks under realistic deployment by applying structured attack strategies to defined rubrics during long-horizon task execution, simulating coherent adversarial interference.  
**Mechanism:** For each safety rubric, known attack methods are grounded onto identified attack surfaces. A parameterized transformation f_R,M modifies a benign task T into a risky scenario Ts that contextually embeds the attack M with the goal of violating rubric R. The agent then executes Ts in an interactive environment.  
**Core assumption:** Adversarial influence can be modeled as discrete, context-aware modifications to specific execution interfaces and that these modifications will elicit rubric-violating behavior if the agent is vulnerable.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 2.2 defines attack surfaces and formally describes the scenario construction; Tables 1 and 13-15 provide concrete attack strategies; neighboring work (AgentGuard, AGENTSAFE) focus on similar surfaces.  
**Break condition:** If the set of identified attack surfaces is incomplete for a given architecture (e.g., multi-agent channels), or if f_R,M fails to produce realistic tasks, probing becomes synthetic and may not reflect true operational risk.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Risky-Bench yields reliable safety assessments by combining automated trajectory evaluation with human oversight, using the defined rubrics as the judge, to detect both overt violations and deceptive alignment.  
**Mechanism:** An LLM-as-judge scans the agent's full interaction trajectory against the target rubric. A separate human review phase validates and corrects these automated labels, ensuring the final safety report is grounded in nuanced judgment about intent and context.  
**Core assumption:** The rubric definition is sufficiently precise for both automated parsing and human consensus, and that agent trajectories contain detectable signals of rubric violation or attempted deception.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 2.3 describes the binary outcome and human review loop; Appendix B.1 provides the automated evaluation prompt; Section 3.3 shows results where explicit reasoning increased ASR, indicating nuanced behavioral capture; related works discuss hybrid evaluation.  
**Break condition:** If the rubric is ambiguous leading to low inter-annotator agreement, or if the automated judge fails to recognize sophisticated deception (e.g., internal rejection but external execution), safety verdicts become unreliable.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept:** Safety Rubric Derivation from Principles  
*Why needed here:* Converts abstract norms into actionable, context-specific checks, bridging the gap between high-level principles and concrete evaluations.  
*Quick check:* Given the principle "User Interest Protection," how would you instantiate it as a verifiable rule for an OTA agent that books flights?

**Concept:** Attack Surface Modeling for LLM Agents  
*Why needed here:* To systematically probe risks, we must identify where to inject adversity, based on the agent's execution loop.  
*Quick check:* For a delivery agent using a store database and a payment tool, list the five attack surfaces and give a realistic attack example for the "Tool Feedback" surface.

**Concept:** Dual-Mode Evaluation (Automated + Human)  
*Why needed here:* Automated evaluation scales but can miss nuance; human review provides ground truth but is costly. Combining them aims for coverage and reliability.  
*Quick check:* Why might an agent's chain-of-thought be essential for a correct safety verdict in a backdoor memory poisoning attack, even if the final action appears safe?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Safety Principle Library -> Deployment Context Profiler -> Rubric Generator -> Task Corpus -> Attack Strategy Bank -> Scenario Constructor -> Agent Under Test -> Trajectory Recorder -> Automated Evaluator -> Human Review Interface -> Safety Report Generator

**Critical path**  
Rubric Definition (1-3) → Task Selection & Attack Instantiation (4-6) → Agent Execution (7-8) → Evaluation (9-10) → Reporting (11)

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Automation vs. Realism: The scenario transformation must be automatic to scale but must preserve task realism to reflect true deployment risk.  
- Generality vs. Specificity: Domain-agnostic principles enable reuse across domains but may miss domain-critical risks (e.g., medical contraindications).  
- Judge capability: Using a powerful LLM as judge reduces human burden but introduces alignment biases; human review remains the costly backstop.

**Failure signatures**  
- High ASR on financial rubrics indicates agents prioritize task completion over verification.  
- ASR spikes on tool feedback and agent instruction surfaces show implicit trust in tool outputs and system prompts.  
- Model-dependent ASR variance (e.g., Claude's lower ASR but increase under codebase attacks with CoT) suggests reasoning can backfire.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. End-to-End Pipeline Smoke Test: Validate data flow and judge logic on a single rubric/task/attack/agent.  
2. Rubric Coverage & Attack Surface Sanity Check: Manually inspect generated risky tasks for coherence and alignment.  
3. Human-Automated Judge Agreement Study: Compute Cohen's Kappa between human labels and automated judge on 100 trajectories.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** How can we guarantee that the set of safety rubrics derived from foundational principles exhaustively covers all relevant safety risks across diverse real-world agent deployments?  
**Basis:** The authors note that the breadth of safety risks captured is bounded by the completeness and realism of the agent environments; rubric derivation relies on a small set of domain-agnostic principles which may not capture all context-specific risks.  
**Why unresolved:** Without exhaustive coverage, hidden risks may remain undetected, leading to false confidence.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the derivation to a large corpus of deployment scenarios across many domains and perform gap analysis against known incidents or expert panels.

**Open Question 2:** What concrete defensive mechanisms can be implemented to significantly reduce attack success rates for tool feedback manipulation and codebase poisoning, which exhibited ASRs above 80%?  
**Basis:** Results show severe vulnerabilities for these attacks, but the paper does not discuss mitigation.  
**Why unresolved:** Agent designers lack empirically validated countermeasures, leaving systems exposed.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Design interventions (e.g., tool output signature verification, sandboxed execution, system prompt integrity checks, memory provenance tracking) and re-evaluate ASR using Risky-Bench.

**Open Question 3:** Does enabling explicit reasoning (chain-of-thought) consistently improve or worsen agent safety across a broader spectrum of model families and attack surfaces beyond the two models and one domain tested?  
**Basis:** RQ3 investigated only Claude-Haiku and Qwen-Plus in the delivery domain, finding model-dependent effects.  
**Why unresolved:** The interaction between reasoning and safety may differ for other models and domains; without broader evidence, guidance remains ambiguous.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the comparison across multiple SOTA LLMs and all three domains, then analyze ASR differences statistically.

**Open Question 4:** Can the human‑in‑the‑loop evaluation component of Risky‑Bench be scaled to larger benchmark sizes without substantial loss in labeling accuracy or consistency?  
**Basis:** The evaluation used two annotators spending ~45 human‑hours to label ~1.75K records, suggesting limited scalability.  
**Why unresolved:** As benchmarks expand, relying on extensive human review becomes prohibitively expensive; we need to understand the trade‑off and potential for automation.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Run a study where a subset of evaluations is automated (with confidence thresholds) and compare against full human annotations; identify an acceptable automation ratio while maintaining Cohen’s kappa > 0.7.

## Limitations
- Reproducibility depends on private APIs (specific model endpoints, unreleased user simulator), which may drift over time.  
- Rubric derivation from domain‑agnostic principles may miss domain‑specific risks (e.g., medical contraindications) as validation is limited to three domains.  
- Attack surface coverage may be incomplete for advanced architectures (e.g., multi‑agent communication channels).

## Confidence

## Next Checks
- **Domain generalization test:** Apply the rubric derivation to a novel domain (e.g., healthcare) and measure expert‑assessed coverage of critical safety risks.  
- **Attack task realism audit:** Manually rate a random sample of generated risky tasks for coherence and deployment plausibility; compute misalignment rate.  
- **Deceptive alignment probe:** Construct trajectories where the agent internally rejects an attack but executes it due to instruction override; evaluate the judge’s violation detection accuracy.
