# PingPong: A Natural Benchmark for Multi-Turn Code-Switching Dialogues

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17277
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17277
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces PingPong, a benchmark of 500 human-authored multi-turn code-switching dialogues across five language combinations, demonstrating poor performance of state-of-the-art models and superiority of region-specific models.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents PingPong, a novel benchmark designed to address the scarcity of realistic evaluations for multi-turn code-switching dialogues. The dataset comprises 500 conversations among 2–4 speakers, collected via crowdsourcing to ensure natural code-switching across five language pairs (including one trilingual combination). It is annotated for three downstream tasks: question answering (with both answerable and unanswerable questions), dialogue summarization, and topic classification. Evaluation of leading multilingual models (e.g., Qwen, Gemma, Sailor) reveals generally weak performance, with regional models tailored to specific languages consistently outperforming general-purpose ones. Additionally, human-written dialogues exhibit greater naturalness and structural diversity than those generated by GPT-4o, as measured by automated metrics and human preference.

## Method Summary
The dataset was constructed through a crowdsourcing protocol where participants engaged in spontaneous conversations on diverse topics while naturally switching languages. The resulting dialogues were annotated for three tasks: multiple-choice QA (including unanswerable questions requiring context reasoning), abstractive summarization, and topic classification. Model performance was evaluated on these tasks using standard metrics (accuracy, ROUGE-L) for a range of open-weight multilingual and regional models. A comparative analysis between human-authored and GPT-4o-generated dialogues employed statistical measures (utterance length variance, reply distance, speaker imbalance) and human preference scoring.

## Key Results
- State-of-the-art models show limited proficiency on PingPong: QA accuracies range from 5% to 91% (most below 50%), summarization ROUGE-L scores are mostly below 0.3, and topic classification accuracies hover between 40–60%.
- Regional models (e.g., Sailor for Southeast Asian languages) consistently outperform general multilingual models (e.g., Qwen, Gemma) across tasks and language combinations.
- Human-authored dialogues demonstrate greater naturalness and structural diversity than GPT-4o-generated ones, with higher variance in utterance length, longer reply distances, and increased speaker imbalance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
PingPong provides a realistic testbed for code-switching because it captures naturally occurring multi-turn interactions between multiple speakers, a scenario that synthetic or single-turn datasets often miss. The inclusion of diverse language combinations—including a trilingual setting—and varied topics ensures the benchmark reflects the complexity of real-world code-switching. By annotating for multiple tasks (QA, summarization, classification), it probes different facets of model understanding, from contextual reasoning to content compression and topic coherence. The human-authored origin guarantees authentic patterns of alternation, making it a reliable yardstick for evaluating model robustness in multilingual dialogue.

## Foundational Learning
- **Code-switching:** Alternation between languages within a conversation; needed because models must handle mixed-language input and understand context-dependent language choices.
- **Multi-turn dialogue:** Conversations with extended exchanges; needed to test context tracking and coherence over multiple speaker turns.
- **Benchmark construction via crowdsourcing:** Ensures natural, diverse data; quick check: verify inter-annotator agreement for task annotations (e.g., QA answer consistency).
- **Evaluation metrics:** Accuracy for QA/classification, ROUGE-L for summarization; quick check: compare model scores against simple baselines (e.g., random guessing, majority class).
- **Regional vs. multilingual models:** Models trained on specific language pairs vs. broad multilingual corpora; quick check: assess performance gap on language-specific code-switching patterns.
- **Reasoning traces (chain-of-thought):** Intermediate reasoning steps to improve QA; quick check: evaluate whether explicit reasoning consistently boosts performance on answerable vs. unanswerable questions.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Crowdsourcing platform -> Raw dialogue collection -> Annotation tasks (QA, summarization, topic) -> Dataset curation -> Train/test split -> Model inference -> Metric computation -> Analysis

Critical path:
The core workflow hinges on collecting natural dialogues, accurately annotating them for the three tasks, and then evaluating models on these standardized splits. Any weakness in annotation quality or split representativeness directly impacts benchmark validity.

Design tradeoffs:
- Human collection vs. generation: Humans provide authenticity but are costly and limited in scale; GPT-4o generation scales cheaply but sacrifices naturalness and diversity.
- Language pair selection: Five combinations (including trilingual) cover common switching patterns but exclude many possible pairs, trading off breadth for depth.
- Task design: Including unanswerable QA questions tests reasoning but increases difficulty and may obscure performance on purely answerable questions.

Failure signatures:
- Models scoring near random chance (e.g., ~25% for 4-choice QA) indicate inability to grasp code-switching context.
- Large performance drops when moving from single-language to code-switching inputs signal lack of robustness.
- Regional models outperforming multilingual ones by wide margins highlights deficiencies in multilingual pretraining for code-switching.

First three experiments:
1. Replicate human vs. GPT-4o dialogue comparison using the reported automated metrics (utterance length variance, reply distance, speaker imbalance) and validate the naturalness claim.
2. Run baseline models (e.g., a small multilingual model) on all three tasks to establish a performance floor and identify the most challenging task.
3. Test the effect of providing reasoning traces (chain-of-thought) on QA performance, specifically comparing answerable vs. unanswerable question accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly list open research questions. However, the analysis suggests implicit directions: How can models be improved for code-switching? What makes human dialogues more natural than synthetic ones? How can the benchmark be extended to more language pairs?

## Limitations
- **Generalizability:** Dataset covers only five specific language combinations (including one trilingual), limiting conclusions about broader code-switching phenomena.
- **Naturalness evaluation:** Comparison between human and GPT-4o dialogues relies on limited automated metrics and a single human preference score, lacking deeper qualitative analysis.
- **Benchmark adoption:** Long-term impact and community uptake of PingPong remain unknown; no follow-up studies or integration into standard evaluation suites are reported.

## Confidence
- Dataset construction & annotation: **High** (detailed crowdsourcing protocol, clear task definitions).
- Model performance results (QA, summarization, classification): **Medium** (empirical scores provided, but limited model selection per task may not generalize).
- Regional model superiority: **Medium** (consistent trend across experiments, but few regional models tested per language pair).
- Human dialogues more natural than GPT-4o: **Medium** (statistical differences noted, but naturalness is subjective; human evaluation sample size unspecified).

## Next Checks
1. Verify public release and reproducibility: Confirm dataset and code availability and attempt to replicate at least one baseline model’s reported scores.
2. Expand naturalness assessment: Conduct a larger-scale human evaluation (e.g., pairwise comparison) between human and GPT-4o dialogues across all language combinations, probing structural and pragmatic aspects.
3. Test cross-lingual generalizability: Evaluate top-performing models from PingPong on an external code-switching benchmark (e.g., CS-Dialogue) to assess transferability to different language pairs and tasks.
