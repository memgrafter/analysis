# CWoMP: Morpheme Representation Learning for Interlinear Glossing

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18184  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18184  
- Reference count: 25  
- Primary result: CWoMP reduces Morpheme Error Rate vs. prior SOTA (e.g., 0.26→0.14–0.19 on Lezgi; 0.68→0.54 on Gitksan with 31 training sentences) while using ~8.5× fewer FLOPs and enabling inference-time lexicon expansion without retraining.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses automated interlinear glossing—a key task in documentary linguistics—by explicitly modeling morpheme structure. The proposed CWoMP framework uses contrastive pre‑training to align words‑in‑context with their constituent morphemes in a shared embedding space. A lightweight autoregressive decoder then generates glosses by retrieving morpheme entries from a mutable codebook, guaranteeing a 1:1 segment‑to‑gloss correspondence and allowing users to expand the lexicon at inference time. Evaluated on seven low‑resource languages, CWoMP achieves state‑of‑the‑art Morpheme Error Rate reductions (e.g., 0.26→0.14–0.19 on Lezgi; 0.68→0.54 on Gitksan with only 31 training examples) while cutting computational cost by a factor of 8.5 compared to byte‑level baselines.

## Method Summary
CWoMP employs a two‑stage training pipeline. Stage 1 learns a shared BoM encoder (initialized from LaBSE) that maps a word‑in‑context (including surrounding transcription and translation) and individual morpheme segment+gloss pairs into a normalized vector space. Training uses a multi‑positive contrastive loss (InfoNCE) that pulls each word closer to all its true morphemes while pushing away other morphemes in the batch. Stage 2 freezes the BoM encoder and constructs a codebook of morpheme embeddings from the training set. A small autoregressive transformer (IGT decoder) is then trained with teacher forcing: given the word embedding followed by previously generated morpheme embeddings, it predicts the next morpheme by computing dot‑product similarity with the codebook. At inference, the model selects the nearest neighbor for each step, producing a sequence of morpheme entries that yields both segmentation and glosses. The codebook can be extended with new morphemes at test time without retraining.

## Key Results
- MER reduced vs. prior SOTA on all seven languages: e.g., Lezgi 0.26→0.14–0.19, Gitksan 0.68→0.54 (with only 31 training sentences).
- ~8.5× fewer FLOPs compared to GlossLM (byte‑level decoder).
- Lexicon expansion at inference recovers errors due to out‑of‑vocabulary morphemes, especially in the lowest‑resource language (Gitksan: ∆MER = 0.133).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Contrastive alignment between words‑in‑context and their constituent morphemes creates a shared embedding space where morphological relationships are geometrically structured, enabling accurate morpheme retrieval.
- **Mechanism:** A dual encoder (BoM Encoder) maps a word‑in‑context (transcription + surrounding context + translation) and individual morphemes (segment + gloss pair) into a normalized vector space. Training uses a multi‑positive InfoNCE loss that minimizes the distance between a word's embedding and embeddings of its true morphemes while maximizing distance to other morphemes. This forces the model to learn representations sensitive to morphological composition.
- **Core assumption:** Morphologically related words and their constituent morphemes share underlying latent properties that can be captured in a Euclidean space via contrastive objectives. This assumes consistent, canonical morpheme segmentation/gloss pairs exist in the training data.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states contrastive alignment; Section 3.2 details the dual encoder and loss; Section 4.7 demonstrates morphological analogies in Tsez embeddings (mean cosine similarity of difference vectors ~0.79).
- **Break condition:** Degrades if annotations are highly inconsistent (e.g., Uspanteko variable surface forms) or for non‑concatenative morphology (under‑represented).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Constraining generation to a precomputed, mutable codebook of morpheme embeddings eliminates hallucination of unattested morpheme types and guarantees a 1:1 segment‑to‑gloss alignment.
- **Mechanism:** The autoregressive IGT decoder outputs an embedding vector at each step, which is matched via dot‑product similarity to the nearest neighbor in a frozen lexicon of morpheme embeddings (the codebook). The codebook is built offline from all morphemes seen during training. During inference the decoder selects only from known form‑meaning pairs.
- **Core assumption:** The space of possible morphemes is largely covered by the training lexicon (plus user‑added entries). New morphological forms can be approximated by retrieving existing, contextually appropriate morphemes. Assumes glosses follow standard conventions and are atomic.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions interpretable predictions and inference‑time lexicon expansion; Section 3.3 describes the constraint; Section 4.3 shows GlossLM hallucinating vs. CWoMP's attested outputs; Table 2 shows OOV morphemes account for a significant portion of MER recoverable with lexicon expansion.
- **Break condition:** Performance drops for morphemes with high allomorphy if canonical forms are missing; cannot generate novel morpheme pairs not in the codebook.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The learned morpheme embedding space encodes systematic morphological relations (e.g., inflectional paradigms), enabling robust performance in extremely low‑resource settings by leveraging geometric compositionality.
- **Mechanism:** Because the contrastive objective aligns words with their parts, morpheme embeddings place morphologically related items in consistent relative positions. This allows generalization: if a stem's embedding is known, affix embeddings can be applied correctly even if a specific stem‑affix combination is rare.
- **Core assumption:** Morphological processes are productive and follow regular, compositional patterns that manifest as linear transformations in the embedding space. More plausible for concatenative/fusional systems than non‑concatenative or suppletive morphology.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.7 shows PCA visualizations of Tsez embeddings with parallel vectors for transformations (e.g., POSS.ABL→POSS.LAT) and high mean cosine similarity (0.70–0.79) of difference vectors; Section 4.5 shows CWoMP's advantage over ByT5 in lowest‑resource regimes (e.g., Gitksan with 31 sentences).
- **Break condition:** Linear analogy property may not hold for non‑linear morphology (e.g., Semitic root‑and‑pattern). Extremely sparse data may prevent reliable relation capture.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Contrastive Learning with Multiple Positives per Anchor
- **Why needed here:** Standard InfoNCE treats all in‑batch samples as negatives, but in morphology multiple words share the same morpheme (e.g., plural suffix `-s`). The multi‑positive variant accounts for this by identifying all valid positive pairs within a batch and averaging, preventing penalties for correct shared‑morpheme recognition.
- **Quick check question:** How would standard InfoNCE loss degrade performance for a morpheme like English `-s` that appears in many words (`cat-s`, `dog-s`) within a single training batch?

### Concept: Dual Encoder Architecture for Cross‑Modal Alignment
- **Why needed here:** The task requires matching two different "modalities": a word‑in‑context (a longer string with context) and a morpheme (a short segment/gloss pair). A shared‑weight transformer encoder processes both types of prompts, producing embeddings in the same space. This architecture is standard for retrieval tasks but here adapted for linguistic units.
- **Quick check question:** Why is a single shared‑weight encoder used for both `pθ(w)` and `qθ(m)` instead of two separate encoders? What is the implicit assumption?

### Concept: Autoregressive Generation via Nearest‑Neighbor Retrieval from a Codebook
- **Why needed here:** To generate a sequence of morphemes, the decoder must output an embedding that corresponds to a valid morpheme. Instead of generating discrete tokens, it retrieves from a precomputed set of morpheme embeddings. This integrates retrieval directly into the generation mechanism, ensuring validity and enabling post‑hoc codebook expansion.
- **Quick check question:** How does this decoding scheme differ from typical autoregressive decoding in sequence‑to‑sequence models (like GlossLM)? What are the implications for the output vocabulary size and computational cost during decoding?

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component map  
BoM Encoder (pretrained/fine‑tuned) → Lexicon/Codebook Builder (offline) → IGT Decoder (training). Inference: word → BoM Encoder → IGT Decoder (autoregressive with codebook lookup) → output morpheme sequence.

#### Critical path  
The overall success hinges on BoM encoder quality. If the encoder does not place a word close to its true morphemes in the shared space, the decoder receives poor initial conditioning and retrieval fails. The decoder's role is to order the retrieved morphemes correctly, but it cannot compensate for a miss in the first retrieval step.

#### Design tradeoffs  
- *Constraint vs. Flexibility:* The codebook constraint prevents hallucination and enforces alignment but prevents generating novel morpheme forms (e.g., a new irregular plural). Trade‑off for reliability in documentary contexts.  
- *Two‑stage vs. End‑to‑End:* Separate training stages simplify training (decoder learns a simpler ordering task) and enable inference‑time lexicon expansion. However, may cause distributional mismatch if the codebook is static while the decoder's internal representation drifts.  
- *Efficiency vs. Representation Power:* Using a frozen large encoder (LaBSE) is compute‑heavy but transfers strong multilingual knowledge. The lightweight decoder keeps generation cheap. The paper reports ~8.5× FLOP reduction vs. byte‑level decoding.

#### Failure signatures  
- **BoM Encoder Failure:** Low recall@k in morpheme retrieval (Appendix B.1); many true morphemes not in top‑k neighbors.  
- **Decoder Ordering Failure:** High MER even when all correct morphemes are in the codebook (i.e., "Train Lexicon" setting). Indicates the decoder struggles with morpheme sequencing/templatics.  
- **Lexicon Mismatch Failure:** Large gap between "Train Lexicon" and "Extended Lexicon" MER, especially in high‑OOV languages (e.g., Gitksan pOOV=0.446, ∆MER=0.133). Indicates errors due to missing morphemes, not misordering.  
- **Annotation Consistency Failure:** Poor performance on languages with high surface variation in morpheme forms (Uspanteko), where the same gloss may have multiple segment strings.

#### First 3 experiments
1. **Verify BoM Encoder Retrieval in Isolation:** Pre‑train the BoM encoder and evaluate word→morpheme recall@k on validation sets. Expect P@1 > 0.6 for most languages; lower indicates encoder alignment issues.  
2. **Check Decoder Generalization with a "Perfect" Lexicon:** Replace the codebook with ground‑truth morpheme embeddings during decoder training. If MER remains high, the decoder fails at ordering, pointing to architectural or data problems.  
3. **Test Lexicon Expansion Workflow:** Run inference, then manually add a handful of frequent OOV morphemes (from the dev set) to the codebook *without* retraining. Measure MER change on a dev subset containing those morphemes. Expect a measurable drop, validating the mutable lexicon claim; no change suggests decoder insensitivity or embedding mismatch.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
#### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can CWoMP be effectively integrated into real‑world, human‑in‑the‑loop documentation workflows with field linguists?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states: "In the future, we will investigate how CWoMP might support real users through human‑in‑the‑loop documentary linguistic workflows."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only simulates lexicon expansion via an oracle setting (adding ground‑truth morphemes). Practical usability, user interaction model, and error correction mechanisms with non‑expert users remain unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A user study with documentary linguists using CWoMP on unseen low‑resource language data, measuring workflow efficiency, error correction rates via lexicon expansion, and qualitative feedback on interpretability.

#### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent can CWoMP's learned morpheme embedding space capture and support generalization for typologically diverse morphological phenomena, such as non‑concatenative or templatic morphology?
- **Basis in paper:** The limitations section notes the need for testing on languages with "additional typological features that are under‑represented in the SIGMORPHON 2023 dataset, such as extensive non‑concatenative morphology." Section 4.7 shows linear structure for concatenative transformations but is limited to Tsez.
- **Why unresolved:** Evaluation was on predominantly concatenative languages from SIGMORPHON. The geometric properties demonstrated may not hold for morphologies where morpheme boundaries are less clear or involve root‑and‑pattern systems.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Testing CWoMP on a curated set of languages with strong non‑concatenative morphology (e.g., Arabic, Hebrew, Persian). Evaluation would include probing the embedding space for analogous transformation consistency and measuring glossing/segmentation performance compared to concatenative baselines.

#### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the practical impact of annotation inconsistency (e.g., surface vs. canonical segmentation, spelling variation) on CWoMP's performance and lexicon expansion, and can the system be made more robust to such noise?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 4.3 discusses Uspanteko's poor performance due to "large variation in attested forms of morphemes," and the limitations section states CWoMP "may be sensitive to inconsistent morpheme annotations or use of surface segmentation."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies this as a key trade‑off but does not quantify the impact of different annotation styles or propose methods to handle variation. The mutable lexicon may exacerbate issues if inconsistent forms are added as separate entries.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments where training data for a language is systematically perturbed to introduce spelling variations or mixed segmentation conventions. Metrics would track MER and the effectiveness of lexicon expansion under different noise conditions. Potential solutions could include clustering similar morpheme forms or learning canonical representations.

#### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the "extended lexicon" paradigm—where users add morphemes at inference time—move beyond the oracle setting to achieve tangible improvements when users expand the lexicon based on model errors without access to ground truth?
- **Basis in paper:** The extended lexicon setting (Section 4.2) adds *ground truth* evaluation morphemes to the lexicon, simulating a user noticing missing morphemes. This is an oracle that assumes perfect user knowledge of what is missing and correct.
- **Why unresolved:** Real users would add morphemes based on predicted errors, which may be incorrect or incomplete. The benefit of non‑oracle lexicon expansion, and the user effort required to achieve gains, is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An empirical simulation where a user (or heuristic) adds the model's top uncertain or novel predictions to the lexicon. Measure the change in MER after multiple rounds of such expansion on held‑out test data, comparing to the oracle ceiling.

#### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does the contrastive pre‑training objective in CWoMP induce linguistically meaningful organization in the shared embedding space beyond morphological analogies, and can this be leveraged for downstream documentary tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 4.7 analyzes morphological analogies and states that embeddings reflect morphology via geometric relations. The conclusions mention interpretability as a benefit.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis is preliminary and focused on one type of analogy. The extent to which the space encodes other linguistic dimensions (e.g., semantic fields, grammatical gender, syntactic valency) or could be used for tasks like morpheme classification or typological induction is not investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comprehensive probing study of the BoM encoder's embeddings, including: (a) clustering analysis by morpheme type (prefix vs. suffix, nominal vs. verbal), (b) accuracy of k‑NN classifiers for morpheme grammatical features, and (c) evaluation on a downstream task like identifying cognates or morpheme homographs across languages using the shared space.

## Limitations
- The approach relies on consistent, canonical morphemes and may degrade for languages with high surface form variation (e.g., Uspanteko) or non‑concatenative morphology.
- The frozen codebook prevents generation of novel morpheme types, limiting applicability to highly productive or irregular systems.
- Implementation details of the multi‑positive contrastive loss and per‑language hyperparameter tuning are not fully disclosed, raising reproducibility challenges.

## Confidence
- Contrastive alignment (Mechanism 1): Medium – supported by retrieval results and morphological analogies but vulnerable to annotation inconsistency.
- Codebook constraint (Mechanism 2): High – inherent architectural guarantee; empirical OOV recovery confirms.
- Geometric compositionality (Mechanism 3): Medium – strong low‑resource gains and Tsez analogies, but may not generalize to non‑linear morphology or extremely sparse data.
