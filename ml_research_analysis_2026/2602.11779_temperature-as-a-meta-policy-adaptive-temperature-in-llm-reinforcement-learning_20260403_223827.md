# Temperature as a Meta-Policy: Adaptive Temperature in LLM Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11779  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11779  
- **Reference count:** 14  
- **Primary result:** TAMPO improves average Pass@1 by 1.9% and Pass@8 by 1.7% over the best fixed-temperature baseline on five math reasoning benchmarks, with comparable complexity.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning for LLMs often uses a fixed or heuristically chosen sampling temperature, which cannot adapt to the evolving exploration–exploitation needs during training. This paper introduces TAMPO, a method that treats temperature selection as a learnable meta-policy in a hierarchical two-loop framework. The inner loop updates the LLM policy (e.g., via GRPO) using rollouts sampled at a temperature drawn from the meta-policy. The outer loop reuses those same rollouts to evaluate candidate temperatures via virtual likelihoods and updates the meta-policy to favor temperatures that yield high-advantage trajectories. Without requiring additional rollouts, TAMPO achieves consistent gains on math and commonsense reasoning tasks.

## Method Summary
TAMPO casts temperature control as a meta-policy π_ϕ over a discrete set of temperatures. In each step, the meta-policy samples a temperature Tₛ, and the inner RL algorithm (e.g., GRPO) generates a batch of trajectories using that temperature. After computing trajectory advantages (group-relative rewards in GRPO) and updating the LLM policy π_θ, the outer loop reuses the generated trajectories. For each virtual temperature Tᵏ in the candidate set, it computes the likelihood of each trajectory under π_θ with Tᵏ, scales the trajectory’s advantage by the normalized likelihood to obtain a temperature‑specific advantage, aggregates these across the batch, and updates π_ϕ using an exponentially weighted moving average (EMA) followed by min‑max normalization. This reuse of inner‑loop rollouts makes the approach computationally efficient and avoids extra sampling.

## Key Results
- **Benchmark improvements:** On five math reasoning benchmarks, TAMPO raises average Pass@1 by 1.9% and Pass@8 by 1.7% compared to the best fixed‑temperature GRPO (Ts=1.5).  
- **Generalization:** Gains also appear on ECQA with a 3B model, indicating broader applicability.  
- **Efficiency:** Training complexity remains nearly identical to fixed‑temperature baselines; no additional rollouts are needed.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Trajectory likelihood under virtual temperatures provides a differentiable, unimodal signal that correlates with temperature quality for generating high‑advantage rollouts.  
- **Mechanism:** For a fixed rollout τ, compute its average log‑likelihood across a discrete set of candidate temperatures. Likelihood is unimodal in T, so there exists a temperature T⋆ that maximizes ℓᵀ(τ). The temperature‑specific advantage is A(Tᵏ)ᵢ = ˆℓᵀᵏ(τᵢ) · Aᵢ, where Aᵢ is the trajectory’s advantage. This upweights temperatures that make high‑advantage rollouts more likely and downweights those that make low‑advantage rollouts likely.  
- **Core assumption:** The rollout’s intrinsic temperature preference (T⋆) is stable and can be estimated from a single generation without re‑sampling at each virtual temperature.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 3.3.1] Proves unimodality of trajectory likelihood in T and defines T⋆.  
  - [section 3.3.2] Derives A(Tᵏ)ᵢ = ˆℓᵀᵏ(τᵢ)·Aᵢ as the update signal.  
  - [Appendix B] Formal proof of unimodality.  
- **Break condition:** If likelihood is not unimodal (e.g., all tokens greedy or uniform), T⋆ estimation becomes ambiguous, weakening the signal.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Exponentially weighted moving average (EMA) and min‑max normalization of temperature‑specific advantages produce a stable, valid meta‑policy distribution that balances feedback from recent and historical rollouts.  
- **Mechanism:** Raw temperature advantages A(Tᵏ)ᴮ over a batch (Eq. 10) are smoothed via EMA to get ¯A(Tᵏ)ₛ (Eq. 11), reducing variance from batch noise. Min‑max normalization across temperatures then yields πₛ(Tᵏ) (Eq. 12), a valid probability distribution that favors high‑advantage temperatures while preserving relative ordering.  
- **Core assumption:** EMA with fixed decay α provides sufficient responsiveness to new data while filtering noise; min‑max prevents saturation and maintains discrimination even when advantages are tightly clustered.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 3.3.2] Explicit definitions of EMA and min‑max normalization.  
  - [section 4.3] Ablation shows α=0.05 best; too small (0.01) unstable, too large (0.10) mixes poorly.  
  - [meta‑learning literature, e.g., Meta‑SAC] Supports use of moving averages for meta‑parameter adaptation.  
- **Break condition:** Abrupt policy shifts may cause EMA to lag, reinforcing outdated temperatures. If all ¯A(Tᵏ) are nearly equal, min‑max normalization becomes unstable.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The hierarchical two‑loop structure enables online temperature adaptation without additional rollouts by reusing inner‑loop trajectories, aligning exploration directly with policy improvement.  
- **Mechanism:** Inner loop: sample prompts, generate G rollouts at Tₛ ∼ πₛ₋₁, compute advantages Aᵢ, update θ. Outer loop: for the same rollouts, compute A(Tᵏ)ᵢ for all candidate Tᵏ via virtual likelihood evaluation, aggregate to A(Tᵏ)ᴮ, update EMA and πₛ. The outer loop runs after each inner update, reusing all generated data.  
- **Core assumption:** Inner‑loop rollouts contain sufficient information to evaluate candidate temperatures; specifically, the likelihood difference under virtual temperatures reflects the temperature’s impact on generating that rollout’s advantage.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] Explicitly states “trajectory‑guided, reward‑driven mechanism enables online adaptation without additional rollouts.”  
  - [section 3.4] Algorithm 1 shows outer loop reusing inner‑loop rollouts; no extra sampling.  
  - [section 4.2] Complexity nearly identical to baselines; negligible training time difference.  
- **Break condition:** Using a very small G (e.g., G=1) yields high‑variance Aᵢ, making A(Tᵏ)ᵢ noisy. A full θ update after each batch may misrepresent virtual likelihoods if the policy changes drastically between inner and outer steps.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Sampling Temperature as Exploration Knob**  
*Why needed here:* Temperature directly modulates the randomness of text generation. TAMPO treats it as a decision variable rather than a fixed hyperparameter, requiring understanding of how temperature shapes exploration and exploitation.  
*Quick check question:* If you set temperature too low during RL, what happens to the diversity of rollouts and risk of premature convergence? If too high?

**Concept: Critic‑Free RL (GRPO)**  
*Why needed here:* TAMPO uses GRPO in the inner loop. GRPO computes trajectory advantages by normalizing rewards within a group of rollouts, avoiding a learned value network. This enables scalable LLM RL but fixes temperature.  
*Quick check question:* How does GRPO estimate advantage without a critic? (Hint: group‑relative normalization.)

**Concept: Meta‑Policy / Bilevel Optimization**  
*Why needed here:* TAMPO frames temperature selection as a meta‑policy over a discrete set. The inner loop optimizes the LLM policy; the outer loop optimizes the temperature distribution. This hierarchical framing is key to adaptive exploration.  
*Quick check question:* In a bilevel problem, what does the outer loop optimize vs. the inner loop? Why can’t we simply backprop through temperature to update it?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
π_ϕ (meta‑policy) -> Tₛ (sampled temperature) -> π_θ (LLM policy) -> trajectories τ -> (Likelihood Calculator: computes ℓᵀᵏ(τ) for virtual Tᵏ; Advantage Aggregator: computes A(Tᵏ)ᴮ, EMA ¯A(Tᵏ)ₛ, updates π_ϕ) -> back to π_ϕ

**Critical path:**  
1. Sample Tₛ ∼ πₛ₋₁.  
2. For each prompt, generate G rollouts at Tₛ.  
3. Compute per‑trajectory advantages Aᵢ (e.g., GRPO group‑relative).  
4. Update π_θ using Aᵢ (inner loop).  
5. For each virtual Tᵏ, compute ℓᵀᵏ(τᵢ) for all τᵢ from step 2.  
6. Set A(Tᵏ)ᵢ = ˆℓᵀᵏ(τᵢ) · Aᵢ.  
7. Aggregate to batch‑level A(Tᵏ)ᴮ.  
8. Update EMA ¯A(Tᵏ)ₛ with decay α.  
9. Apply min‑max normalization across temperatures to obtain new πₛ(Tᵏ).  
10. Repeat.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Discretized vs. continuous temperature set:** Discrete K (default 10) is simple and matches common practice; continuous would require differentiable sampling or additional meta‑optimization.  
- **Meta‑policy sampling strategy:** Nucleus top‑p (e.g., p=0.7) balances exploration over temperatures; greedy (p=0) may converge prematurely.  
- **EMA decay α:** Low α reacts fast but noisy; high α smooths but lags. Ablation shows α=0.05 effective.  
- **Warmup phase:** First 10% steps use fixed T=1.0 while meta‑policy updates, to bootstrap meaningful advantage estimates before letting meta‑policy control sampling.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Meta‑policy collapses to a single temperature quickly (entropy ≈ 0).  
- Validation Pass@1 degrades while training advantage improves (meta‑policy overfitting to batch noise).  
- High variance in sampled Tₛ with no clear trend (nucleus p too high or advantages too noisy).  
- No improvement over best fixed baseline (candidate set lacks optimum or unimodal assumption fails).  
- Instability when inner loop uses very small G (high‑variance advantages).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Warmup ablation:** Run TAMPO without warmup; expect unstable early performance due to poor advantage estimates.  
2. **EMA coefficient sweep:** Test α ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.10} on a single benchmark (e.g., MATH‑500); expect α=0.05 to yield best trade‑off.  
3. **Meta‑policy sampling strategy:** Compare nucleus top‑p ∈ {0.5, 0.7, 0.9} and greedy (p=0) on Pass@1; expect moderate p=0.7 to balance exploration/exploitation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does TAMPO generalize beyond math and commonsense reasoning?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Only five math benchmarks and ECQA are evaluated.  
- **Why unresolved:** Task specificity of gains remains unknown.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Test TAMPO on varied NLP tasks (e.g., MMLU, XSum) and compare to fixed‑temperature baselines.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can TAMPO adopt a continuous temperature distribution instead of discrete candidates?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The method uses a fixed set of temperatures; a continuous variant is not explored.  
- **Why unresolved:** Discretization may miss the truly optimal temperature and limit fine‑grained adaptation.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replace the discrete meta‑policy with a parameterized continuous distribution (e.g., Beta) and measure performance differences.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Is the meta‑policy update (EMA + min‑max) provably stable?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Algorithm 1 applies these updates without any convergence analysis.  
- **Why unresolved:** The heuristic could cause oscillations, bias, or collapse over time.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive theoretical convergence conditions and empirically track meta‑policy entropy across long training runs.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Could gradient‑based meta‑learning directly optimize temperature despite non‑differentiability?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors claim temperature is non‑differentiable; they avoid gradient‑based meta‑learning as “infeasible.”  
- **Why unresolved:** A differentiable approach might offer better sample efficiency and stronger guarantees.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Use reparameterization (e.g., Gumbel‑Softmax) or a straight‑through estimator to enable gradients, then compare sample efficiency to TAMPO.

## Limitations
- The unimodal likelihood assumption may fail for extreme token distributions (all greedy or uniform), breaking T⋆ estimation.  
- EMA may lag during abrupt policy shifts, causing the meta‑policy to reinforce outdated temperatures.  
- Reusing single‑rollout estimates assumes a stable temperature preference per trajectory; high‑variance advantages (small G) destabilize updates.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Unimodal signal):** High (proof in Appendix B), but edge cases not fully tested.  
- **Claim 2 (EMA+normalization stability):** Medium (ablations support α=0.05), but susceptible to distribution shifts.  
- **Claim 3 (Hierarchical reuse):** High (empirically efficient), yet relies on stable inner‑loop dynamics.

## Next Checks
1. **Test extreme token distributions:** Generate rollouts with near‑greedy (T→0) or uniform (T→∞) policies; verify if TAMPO’s meta‑policy degrades vs. fixed T.  
2. **Simulate abrupt policy shifts:** Introduce sudden reward structure changes mid‑training; monitor meta‑policy lag and recovery speed vs. adaptive baselines.  
3. **Expand candidate temperatures:** Add extreme Ts (e.g., 0.1, 2.0) and a continuous T meta‑policy (e.g., parametrized distribution) to assess coverage gaps.
