# Dreamer-CDP: Improving Reconstruction-free World Models Via Continuous Deterministic Representation Prediction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.07083
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07083
- **Reference count**: 9
- **Primary result**: Achieved a Crafter score of 16.2%±2.1%, matching DreamerV3 (14.5%±1.6%) and significantly outperforming other reconstruction-free methods.

## Executive Summary
Reconstruction-based world models in model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) often encode task-irrelevant pixel details, while existing reconstruction-free alternatives underperform on benchmarks like Crafter. This paper proposes Dreamer-CDP, a method that replaces reconstruction objectives with a JEPA-style predictor operating on continuous, deterministic representations. It separates the encoder into a deterministic feature extractor and a stochastic state encoder, and trains a predictor via negative cosine similarity loss on the deterministic embeddings, using a higher learning rate for the sequence model without EMA targets. On the Crafter benchmark, Dreamer-CDP matches the performance of the state-of-the-art reconstruction-based DreamerV3 and dramatically outperforms other reconstruction-free approaches, demonstrating that predictive learning on compressed features can supplant pixel-space reconstruction.

## Method Summary
Dreamer-CDP modifies the Dreamer architecture by removing the pixel-decoder and introducing a Continuous Deterministic Prediction (CDP) objective. The observation encoder is split: a feature extractor produces a deterministic, continuous embedding `u_t`, and a stochastic encoder samples a latent state `z_t` conditioned on `u_t` and the recurrent hidden state `h_t`. The recurrent state-space model (RSSM) dynamics operate on `(h_t, z_t)`. A predictor network takes `h_t` and outputsa prediction `û_t` of the *future* deterministic embedding `u_{t+1}`. This predictor is trained with a negative cosine similarity loss between `û_t` and a stop-gradient `u_{t+1}`. Crucially, the predictor and RSSM are trained with a higher learning rate (4×10⁻⁴) compared to the feature extractor (6×10⁻⁶), and no exponential moving average (EMA) is used for predictor targets. Standard Dreamer auxiliary losses (reward, continuation) and KL balancing terms for the stochastic state are retained.

## Key Results
- Dreamer-CDP achieved a Crafter score of 16.2%±2.1%, statistically matching DreamerV3 (14.5%±1.6%) and significantly outperforming other reconstruction-free baselines (MuDreamer: 7.3%±2.6%, DreamerPro: 4.7%±0.5%).
- Ablating the CDP loss (`L_CDP`) caused performance to collapse to 3.2%±1.2%, demonstrating the predictor's necessity.
- Removing reward gradients backpropagating into the world model reduced the score to 12.7%±1.6%, confirming the contribution of auxiliary tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Separation of Deterministic and Stochastic Representations
**Claim**: Splitting the encoder into a deterministic feature extractor (`u_t`) and a stochastic state encoder (`z_t`) isolates task-relevant features for prediction while discarding irrelevant pixel details.
**Mechanism**: `u_t` is a compressed, continuous embedding of the observation. The stochastic `z_t` handles partial observability and stochasticity, while the CDP predictor is trained to forecast `u_{t+1}`. This ensures the predictor only receives information relevant for future state prediction, avoiding the pressure to reconstruct every pixel.
**Evidence**: The architecture is explicitly defined with `u_t` as the prediction target [section 4]. The failure of other reconstruction-free methods that do not use this split (MuDreamer, DreamerPro) provides comparative evidence.
**Break condition**: If `u_t` still encodes irrelevant details, the predictor fails to learn useful dynamics, and performance degrades to the level of ablations (~3–5%).

### Mechanism 2: JEPA-Style Prediction with Negative Cosine Similarity
**Claim**: Training the predictor via negative cosine similarity on `u_t` prevents representational collapse and aligns features without contrastive learning or EMA.
**Mechanism**: The loss `L_CDP = -cos(SG(u_t), û_t)` uses a stop-gradient on `u_t` to prevent trivial solutions. Cosine similarity is scale-invariant and avoids contrastive negatives or batch statistics.
**Evidence**: The loss function is formally defined [section 4]. The effectiveness of similar losses in BYOL/SimSiam for static images provides conceptual support.
**Break condition**: If `u_t` loses variance (collapse) or the predictor outputs a constant, the loss provides no gradient, leading to performance collapse (observed in `L_CDP` ablation).

### Mechanism 3: Higher Learning Rate for Predictor Without EMA
**Claim**: Using a higher learning rate for the predictor (and RSSM) without EMA targets forces the sequence model to converge to a fixed point under moving representations, stabilizing training.
**Mechanism**: A faster-updating predictor must remain accurate despite shifts in `u_t` from the slowly updated feature extractor. The theoretical insight is that the predictor must be near a fixed point of its own prediction operator.
**Evidence**: Hyperparameters specify predictor LR=4e-4 vs. encoder LR=6e-6 and explicitly state "without exponential moving average (EMA) target network" [section 4, appendix A.1]. The work cites theoretical support (Tang et al., 2023).
**Break condition**: If the LR ratio is mis-tuned, the predictor either overfits to noisy `u_t` updates (too high) or lags and becomes uninformative (too low), harming performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)**
  *Why needed*: Defines the core predictive learning mechanism that replaces reconstruction; learns representations by predicting future embeddings in a joint space.
  *Quick check*: "How does JEPA avoid representational collapse without negative samples?"
- **Concept: Deterministic vs. Stochastic Latent States**
  *Why needed*: The deterministic `u_t` serves as the prediction target, while the stochastic `z_t` handles partial observability and uncertainty. Their separation is key to avoiding reconstruction pressures.
  *Quick check*: "What role does the stop-gradient on `u_t` play in the CDP loss, and why is `z_t` not used as the prediction target?"
- **Concept: KL Balancing in Dreamer**
  *Why needed*: The KL divergence terms (`L_dyn`, `L_rep`) regularize the stochastic state dynamics and are retained from Dreamer to prevent latent collapse, complementing the CDP objective.
  *Quick check*: "What is the effect of the stop-gradient in `L_dyn` and `L_rep`, and why is it necessary alongside the CDP loss?"
- **Concept: Negative Cosine Similarity Loss**
  *Why needed*: Provides an efficient, scale-invariant alignment signal between predicted and target embeddings without requiring contrastive negatives or batch-dependent operations.
  *Quick check*: "Why is cosine similarity preferred over mean squared error for aligning embeddings in CDP?"
- **Concept: Learning Rate Disparity in World Models**
  *Why needed*: The higher LR for the predictor/RSSM relative to the encoder is proposed to maintain predictive accuracy as representations evolve slowly, based on fixed-point theory.
  *Quick check*: "What could go wrong if the predictor's learning rate is set equal to the encoder's learning rate?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: CNN feature extractor -> u_t; Stochastic encoder (h_t, u_t) -> z_t; RSSM (h_{t-1}, z_{t-1}, a_{t-1}) -> h_t; CDP predictor (MLP, h_t) -> û_t; Auxiliary heads (h_t, z_t) -> ˆr_t, ˆc_t. No decoder.
- **Critical path**: Observation x_t -> feature extractor -> u_t (stop-gradient in L_CDP) -> stochastic encoder -> z_t -> RSSM -> h_t -> predictor -> û_t. Gradients: L_CDP updates predictor and RSSM; L_aux updates heads and RSSM; KL terms update encoder and RSSM.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *No decoder*: Saves memory/computation but loses direct pixel supervision; relies entirely on L_CDP and KL regularization.
  - *Deterministic u_t target*: Simplifies prediction but may lack stochasticity for exploration; compensated by stochastic z_t.
  - *Higher predictor LR*: Risks destabilizing RSSM if mis-tuned; requires careful scaling relative to encoder LR.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Crafter score falls below 5% (as in MuDreamer/DreamerPro or L_CDP ablation).
  - L_CDP remains high or plateaus without decreasing.
  - u_t embeddings show near-zero variance across a batch (representational collapse).
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Ablate L_CDP: Remove the predictor loss. Score drops to 3.2±1.2%, confirming its necessity.
  2. Ablate reward gradients: Inhibit backpropagation from L_aux into the world model. Score drops to 12.7±1.6%, showing auxiliary task contribution.
  3. Compare to DreamerV3: On Crafter, Dreamer-CDP (16.2%) matches DreamerV3 (14.5%) without reconstruction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization across benchmarks**: Dreamer-CDP's success is only shown on Crafter. It is unknown if its reconstruction-free approach generalizes to other MBRL benchmarks (e.g., Atari, DM Control). Future work should systematically evaluate on diverse suites.
- **Computational savings**: The removal of the pixel decoder is expected to reduce memory and compute, but the paper provides no empirical measurement of FLOPs, memory footprint, or training time. Controlled experiments are needed to verify this claim.
- **Data efficiency in specific settings**: The authors suggest reconstruction-free models may improve data efficiency in environments with simple action spaces and sparse, long-horizon rewards. This hypothesis is untested; sample complexity curves on environments varying action complexity and reward density are required.
- **Why CDP succeeds where other reconstruction-free objectives fail**: MuDreamer (action prediction) and DreamerPro (prototypical prediction) perform poorly on Crafter. The paper does not analyze which environmental properties favor continuous deterministic prediction over these alternatives. Diagnostic studies on Crafter variants or synthetic tasks are needed.
- **Stabilizing effect of high predictor LR and no EMA**: The theoretical justification (fixed-point insight) is cited but not empirically validated within this work. Isolating the contributions of learning rate disparity and EMA absence through targeted ablations is an open question.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is limited to the Crafter benchmark; performance on other domains (Atari, DMControl, Procgen) is unknown.
- Key design choices (predictor learning rate ×4, no EMA) lack comprehensive ablation; their sensitivity and isolated effects are unclear.
- The deterministic/stochastic representation split is proposed but not directly compared to alternative architectures.
- The feature extractor `u_t` is excluded from CDP gradients via stop-gradient, which may limit representation quality.
- The theoretical benefit of the higher predictor learning rate is not experimentally validated in this study.

## Confidence
- **CDP predictor as a sufficient replacement for reconstruction**: **High** on Crafter (ablation shows necessity, significant gains over other reconstruction-free methods); **Medium** for general claims due to single-benchmark evidence.
- **Deterministic/stochastic representation separation**: **Medium** (architecturally sound and consistent with results, but no direct ablation isolating its effect).
- **Higher predictor learning rate without EMA stabilizes learning**: **Low** (only theoretical support cited; no empirical validation within this work).

## Next Checks
1. **Ablate representation split**: Train variants where the CDP predictor targets `z_t` instead of `u_t`, or where the split is removed, keeping all else fixed. Measure impact on Crafter performance.
2. **Test on diverse benchmarks**: Run Dreamer-CDP on Atari 100k, DMControl, and Procgen. Compare to DreamerV3 and other reconstruction-free baselines to assess generalizability.
3. **Hyperparameter and EMA analysis**: Systematically vary the predictor-encoder learning rate ratio (e.g., 1×, 2×, 4×, 8×) and include conditions with EMA targets. Measure training stability, representation quality (e.g., eigenvalue spectra of `u_t`), and final scores across multiple seeds.
