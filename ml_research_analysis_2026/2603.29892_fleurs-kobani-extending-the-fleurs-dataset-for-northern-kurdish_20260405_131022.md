# FLEURS-Kobani: Extending the FLEURS Dataset for Northern Kurdish

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.29892
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29892
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Created the first public Northern Kurdish speech benchmark (5,162 utterances, 18h24m, 31 speakers) and achieved 28.11 WER / 9.84 CER on ASR via a two-stage Whisper v3-large fine-tuning strategy.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces FLEURS-Kobani, the first publicly available speech benchmark for Northern Kurdish. The dataset extends the FLEURS benchmark by recording native speakers read the Northern Kurdish sentences from the FLORES corpus, yielding 5,162 high-quality, semantically parallel utterances. The authors demonstrate that a two-stage fine-tuning strategy for OpenAI's Whisper v3-large—first on a larger, related Common Voice Northern Kurdish corpus (~68 hours), then on the new FLEURS-Kobani training set—significantly outperforms single-stage approaches, achieving a test Word Error Rate (WER) of 28.11. For low-resource speech-to-text translation (KMR→EN), a cascaded system using this ASR model followed by NLLB translation substantially outperforms an end-to-end model, scoring 19.60 BLEU versus 8.68 BLEU. The dataset is released under a CC BY 4.0 license.

## Method Summary
The core methodological contribution is the construction of the FLEURS-Kobani benchmark by recording 31 speakers read the Northern Kurdish sentences from the FLORES-200 corpus. This provides a standardized, multilingual evaluation framework with guaranteed parallel text references. For modeling, the authors employ a two-stage fine-tuning pipeline on Whisper v3-large: Stage 1 uses the Northern Kurdish subset of Common Voice (~68 hours) to learn general acoustic-phonetic patterns; Stage 2 further specializes the model on the smaller FLEURS-Kobani training partition (~3,870 utterances, 13h47m). For speech-to-text translation, they compare an end-to-end Whisper model (fine-tuned to output English) against a cascaded system using their best ASR model followed by the NLLB-1.3B machine translation model.

## Key Results
- Created FLEURS-Kobani: 5,162 utterances, 18h24m, 31 speakers (26F, 5M).
- Best ASR performance (two-stage fine-tuning): 28.11 WER, 9.84 CER on test set.
- End-to-end S2TT (KMR→EN): 8.68 BLEU.
- Cascaded S2TT (ASR + NLLB): 19.60 BLEU.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
A two-stage fine-tuning strategy (large related corpus → small target corpus) improves low-resource ASR by leveraging transferred acoustic-phonetic knowledge before specializing to the target dialect's distribution. Initial fine-tuning on a larger, related speech corpus (Common Voice Northern Kurdish, ~68hrs) warms the model's encoder and decoder to the broad phonotactic and lexical space. Subsequent fine-tuning on the smaller, carefully aligned FLEURS-Kobani data (~18.4hrs) then specializes the model to the specific sentences, vocabulary, and acoustic conditions of the benchmark without overwriting foundational representations. Evidence: Direct comparison shows two-stage (28.11 WER) significantly outperforms fine-tuning solely on Common Voice (37.02 WER) or solely on FLEURS-Kobani (38.09 WER). The core assumption is shared patterns between the general Common Voice recordings and the structured FLEURS-Kobani prompts allow beneficial transfer.

### Mechanism 2
Creating a speech benchmark by directly recording sentences from an existing, professionally translated text corpus (FLORES) ensures high-quality, semantically parallel textual references for multi-task evaluation (ASR, S2TT). By using FLORES sentences as exact prompts, every audio utterance is guaranteed to have a human-verified textual translation in multiple languages. This eliminates separate text alignment, ensuring evaluation against consistent, standardized references. The risk is that reading written prompts may produce speech not fully representative of spontaneous spoken norms if a significant diglossic gap exists.

### Mechanism 3
For low-resource end-to-end S2TT, direct fine-tuning on a small parallel speech-translation corpus underperforms a cascaded system (ASR + MT) if the ASR component is sufficiently strong. The cascaded system decomposes the problem: a dedicated ASR model (trained on potentially more speech data via transfer) produces text, which is then translated by a large, powerful text-based MT model (NLLB). The end-to-end model must learn a direct mapping from variable acoustic features to foreign language text from limited data. Evidence: Cascaded S2TT achieves 19.60 BLEU vs. end-to-end 8.68 BLEU. The cascade exploits separate, larger datasets (Common Voice for ASR, vast text for MT) that the single E2E system cannot.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Transfer Learning / Fine-tuning**
    *   **Why needed here:** Northern Kurdish has very limited in-domain speech data (~18h). Direct training from scratch fails. Using a larger, related pre-trained model (Whisper on 680k hrs) and adapting it via fine-tuning on in-domain data is essential.
    *   **Quick check:** Why is fine-tuning first on Common Voice (a general corpus) beneficial before fine-tuning on the small benchmark corpus, instead of just fine-tuning on the benchmark?

*   **Concept: Benchmark Construction via Corpus Extension**
    *   **Why needed here:** The contribution extends a pre-existing multilingual benchmark (FLEURS) to a new language. This requires aligning new data with an existing evaluation framework (using FLORES sentences and matching splits) to ensure comparability.
    *   **Quick check:** What are the risks of using a written-form-based text corpus (FLORES) to generate speech for a dialect that may have a significant spoken/written norm gap?

*   **Concept: Cascaded vs. End-to-End System Design for Low-Resource S2TT**
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper empirically demonstrates the trade-off for very low-resource S2TT. An engineer must understand *why* a modular cascade can outperform a unified E2E model when parallel speech-translation data is scarce.
    *   **Quick check:** What are the two primary sources of data that the cascaded system exploits, which the single E2E system cannot?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Common Voice (KMR, ~68h) -> Whisper v3-large (Stage 1 fine-tune) -> Whisper v3-large (Stage 2 fine-tune on FLEURS-Kobani train) -> ASR Output (KMR text)
    Whisper v3-large (Stage 2) -> E2E S2TT Output (EN text)
    ASR Output (KMR text) -> NLLB-1.3B (KMR->EN) -> Cascaded S2TT Output (EN text)

*   **Critical path:**
    1.  Data Alignment: Ensure every FLEURS-Kobani audio file is linked to its exact FLORES-derived Northern Kurdish prompt and corresponding English reference.
    2.  Acoustic Foundation (Stage 1): Fine-tune Whisper v3-large on the entire Common Voice KMR training set (68h) for 5 epochs (LR 1e-5, batch 4). Save best checkpoint.
    3.  Benchmark Specialization (Stage 2): Initialize from Stage 1 checkpoint. Fine-tune on FLEURS-Kobani train split (3,870 utt) with same hyperparameters. Select best checkpoint on dev set (491 utt).
    4.  Task-Specific Evaluation: Use final checkpoint for ASR test evaluation. For E2E S2TT, same model outputs English directly. For cascaded S2TT, use final checkpoint for ASR then NLLB-1.3B for translation.

*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Two-stage vs. Single-stage:** Two-stage requires two training runs and risks overfitting to Common Voice but yields major gains. Single-stage is simpler but performs worse due to data scarcity.
    *   **E2E vs. Cascaded:** E2E is elegant and avoids error propagation but needs more parallel speech-translation data. Cascaded leverages separate, larger corpora (CV for ASR, text for MT) but introduces dependency on ASR quality.
    *   **Dataset Scope:** FLEURS-Kobani uses written FLORES prompts, guaranteeing text alignment but potentially lacking ecological validity for spontaneous speech.

*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   High test WER but low dev WER: Potential speaker overlap between dev and train sets or overfitting to a small dev set.
    *   E2E S2TT BLEU << Cascaded BLEU: Classic sign of insufficient parallel speech-translation data for joint modeling; E2E model likely failing at one sub-problem.
    *   Poor performance on specific speakers/dialects: Given demographic skew (26F/5M) and potential regional variation, model may fail on underrepresented male voices or certain dialects.

*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Replicate Baseline ASR:** Fine-tune Whisper v3-large *only* on the FLEURS-Kobani train set (5 epochs, LR 1e-5, batch 4). Expected WER ~38.0 on test.
    2.  **Implement Two-Stage Pipeline:** Stage 1: Fine-tune on Common Voice KMR train. Stage 2: Continue fine-tuning from Stage 1 checkpoint on FLEURS-Kobani train. Select best on dev. Expected WER ~28.1 on test.
    3.  **Cascaded S2TT Reproduction:** Transcribe FLEURS-Kobani test set audio with the best two-stage ASR model. Pipe transcriptions through NLLB-1.3B (KMR->EN). Compute BLEU vs. English references. Expected BLEU ~19.6.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question 1:** How do regional dialect variability and speaker fluency impact ASR and S2TT model performance on FLEURS-Kobani? *Basis:* Conclusion notes two variables (fluency and dialect) and calls for granular speaker-level evaluation and dialectal analysis.
*   **Question 2:** What specific lexical and typological divergences cause poor E2E S2TT performance for Central Kurdish and Persian when using English as a pivot? *Basis:* Results show low BLEU for KMR-CKB and KMR-FA; text hypothesizes divergences from lexical/typological differences with the pivot language.
*   **Question 3:** Does the gender imbalance in FLEURS-Kobani's speaker population (26 female vs. 5 male) bias ASR model performance against male speakers? *Basis:* Data collection notes heavy gender skew and technical issues affecting recordings, suggesting potential demographic gaps.
*   **Question 4:** Can fine-tuning strategies beyond the two-stage Common Voice to FLEURS-Kobani approach further improve ASR performance for low-resource Northern Kurdish? *Basis:* Experiments compare three fine-tuning conditions (CV only, F-K only, two-stage) finding two-stage best, but other strategies (e.g., multi-stage, different pre-training corpora) are unexplored.

## Limitations
*   **Ecological validity:** Using FLORES written prompts creates a controlled benchmark but may not reflect spontaneous Northern Kurdish discourse due to potential diglossia.
*   **Demographic & acoustic bias:** Corpus is small (18.4h) and skewed (26F/5M speakers). Performance may degrade for male voices or speakers not represented. Single recording platform may introduce channel bias.
*   **Reproducibility blockers:** Exact file lists for the Common Voice subset and the precise train/dev split logic (especially the 149 "mismatched" dev sentences) are not publicly documented, blocking exact replication.

## Confidence
*   **Two-stage ASR improvement claim:** High. Directly supported by comparative results (28.11 vs. 37.02/38.09 WER).
*   **FLORES-based benchmark quality claim:** Medium-High. Method guarantees parallel text but the claim of *representative* speech depends on unverified written-spoken norm alignment.
*   **Cascade > E2E for low-resource S2TT claim (empirical result):** High. Clear result (19.60 vs. 8.68 BLEU). The proposed *mechanism* (separate data exploitation) is Medium as it is inferred, not directly ablated.

## Next Checks
1.  **Cross-lingual generalization test:** Apply the identical two-stage fine-tuning protocol (common voice → target FLEURS extension) to another low-resource FLEURS language (e.g., Kyrgyz, Uyghur). Does the performance gain (vs. single-stage) persist?
2.  **Speaker demographic ablation:** Retrain the best ASR model after stratified subsampling to balance speaker gender or exclude the most frequent speakers. Measure WER degradation on a held-out subset of *unseen* speakers.
3.  **Prompt-type noise injection:** Artificially corrupt the FLORES text prompts (simulating spontaneous disfluencies, dialectal variants) before ASR inference. Measure WER increase versus clean prompts to quantify benchmark sensitivity.
