# From Robotics to Sepsis Treatment: Offline RL via Geometric Pessimism

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08655
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08655
- **Reference Count:** 8
- **Primary Result:** Geo-IQL, by adding a pre-computed k-NN distance penalty to IQL, boosts Hopper-medium-replay scores by 18.85 points and reduces variance 4x, while on MIMIC-III Sepsis it shifts from behaviour cloning collapse to safe policy improvement with higher terminal clinician agreement and positive Q-improvement.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Geometric Pessimism (Geo-IQL), a method to stabilize offline reinforcement learning on datasets with sparse, fractured distributions. It addresses a core failure of prior methods like Implicit Q-Learning (IQL), which can overestimate value for out-of-distribution actions, by injecting a pre-computed, density-based penalty derived from k-nearest neighbour distances into the reward signal during training. This penalty is static and adds no training overhead. Evaluations on D4RL robotics tasks show dramatic improvements on the unstable Hopper benchmark and stability matching on well-covered tasks like HalfCheetah. Critically, on the real-world MIMIC-III Sepsis dataset, where IQL collapses to behaviour cloning, Geo-IQL yields a policy with higher terminal clinician agreement and positive estimated Q-value improvement, demonstrating its potential for safe policy optimization in critical, data-limited settings.

## Method Summary
Geo-IQL modifies the standard Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) pipeline by introducing a pre-computed OOD penalty. First, for every state-action pair `(s,a)` in the static dataset, it computes a geometric uncertainty score: the mean Euclidean distance to its 10 nearest neighbours in a joint embedding space `[normalized(s), a]`. This raw score is robustly standardized around a "safe core" threshold (30th percentile) using the median absolute deviation. An adaptive scaling factor, which grows aggressively in sparse regions and is negligible in dense ones, modulates a base penalty weight `λ_base`. The final penalty `p` is stored in a lookup table. During standard IQL training, each sampled transition's reward is replaced by `r_geo = r - p`. All IQL updates (expectile regression for the value, critic MSE, actor advantage-weighted regression) then proceed unchanged using this pessimistically shaped reward. The pre-computation is a one-time cost, making training overhead O(1) compared to methods that compute constraints online.

## Key Results
- On D4RL MuJoCo `medium-replay`, Geo-IQL boosts Hopper score from 80.09 (IQL) to 98.94 (+18.85) and reduces inter-seed standard deviation by 4×.
- On MIMIC-III Sepsis, IQL collapses to behaviour cloning (75.0% terminal clinician agreement, ΔQ = -0.0169), while Geo-IQL achieves 86.4% terminal agreement and positive ΔQ (+0.0138), with lower policy entropy.
- Geo-IQL matches IQL performance on stable, high-coverage tasks like HalfCheetah, demonstrating its adaptivity does not harm in-distribution performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Pre-computing a k-nearest neighbour (k-NN) distance penalty in state-action space and injecting it as a reward shaping term mitigates Q-value overestimation for out-of-distribution (OOD) actions without adding runtime overhead.
- **Mechanism:** For each `(s, a)` in the static dataset, compute the mean Euclidean distance to its `k=10` nearest neighbors in a joint embedding space (`[norm(s), a]`). This raw distance `Ũ(s,a)` is robustly standardized using the median absolute deviation (MAD) around a "safe core" threshold `τ` (30th percentile). The standardized score `U(s,a)` is then scaled by an adaptive factor `ρ(s,a)` that decays with density, producing a final penalty `p = λ_adapt * max(0, U(s,a))`. This penalty is subtracted from the original reward `r(s,a)` to form `r_geo`. During standard IQL training, the critic target uses `r_geo`, indirectly pessimizing Q-values for points far from the data manifold.
- **Core assumption:** Euclidean distance in the defined embedding space is a valid proxy for epistemic uncertainty (i.e., points far from training data are OOD and have uncertain, likely overestimated Q-values). The penalty magnitude correctly correlates with the degree of overestimation risk.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "augments standard IQL with a density-based penalty derived from k-nearest-neighbour distances... pre-computing the penalties... O(1) training overhead."
  - [section 4] Details the embedding (Eq 1), geometric uncertainty calculation (Eq 2), robust standardization (Eqs 3-4), and density-adaptive penalization (Eqs 5-8).
  - [corpus] Weak direct support; neighboring papers (e.g., PIQL) also explore support constraints for IQL, suggesting the broader research context values geometric/data-centric pessimism, but do not specifically validate the k-NN pre-computation approach.
- **Break condition:** If the state-action embedding is mis-specified (e.g., poor feature scaling, irrelevant features), Euclidean distance may not correlate with OOD risk. The penalty could then incorrectly suppress in-distribution actions or fail to suppress dangerous OOD actions.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The density-adaptive scaling factor `ρ(s,a)` creates a smooth, graded penalty surface that applies strong pessimism only in sparse regions while preserving performance in dense, in-distribution regions.
- **Mechanism:** `ρ(s,a) = 1 / (1 + max(0, U(s,a)))` maps the standardized OOD score `U(s,a)` (where U=0 is the safe core) to a scaling factor in `[0,1]`. This factor modulates the base penalty weight `λ_base` via `λ_adapt = λ_base * (2 - 1.5ρ)`, yielding `λ_adapt` in `[0.5λ_base, 2λ_base]`. Thus, `λ_adapt` is largest (2x base) when `U` is high (sparse region) and smallest (0.5x base) when `U` is near zero (dense core).
- **Core assumption:** The relationship between OOD distance and the required penalty strength for safe Q-value underestimation is monotonic and can be modeled by this specific sigmoid-like decay. The parameters (0.5, 1.5, τ=0.3) are sufficiently general across tasks.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "inject OOD conservatism via reward shaping... does not degrade performance on stable manifolds."
  - [section 4.4] Explains the design: "negligible in safe regions but grows aggressively in unknown regions" and presents Eq 5-7.
  - [section 6.1] Empirical validation: Geo-IQL matches IQL on stable tasks (HalfCheetah, Walker2d) while significantly improving unstable ones (Hopper), demonstrating the adaptivity.
- **Break condition:** If the true penalty required for safety is non-monotonic with density or depends on directional uncertainty rather than radial distance, this scalar, radially-symmetric penalty may be insufficient. Overly aggressive scaling in moderately OOD regions could cause unnecessary performance loss.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Subtracting `λ * d((s,a), D)` from the learned Q estimate `bQ(s,a)` provides a theoretically guaranteed pessimistic lower bound on the true optimal Q-function `Q*(s,a)`, under Lipschitz continuity assumptions.
- **Mechanism:** The proof (Appendix A) argues that if the optimal Q-function `Q*` and the learned Q-function `bQ` are both Lipschitz (with constants `L*` and `bL`), and `bQ` is accurate (error ≤ ε) on the dataset D, then the error at an OOD point `(s,a)` is bounded by `(bL + L*) * d + ε`, where `d` is the distance to the nearest neighbor in D. By setting the penalty parameter `λ ≥ (bL + L* + ε) / d_min` (where `d_min` is the minimum possible distance for any OOD query), the corrected estimate `˜Q = bQ - λ*d` is guaranteed to satisfy `˜Q(s,a) ≤ Q*(s,a)` for all `(s,a)`.
- **Core assumption:** The true optimal Q-function and the neural network approximator for Q are Lipschitz continuous over the entire state-action space. The distance metric used is the true, relevant metric for value function smoothness.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.7] "The geometric penalty is also theoretically grounded in Lipschitz continuity."
  - [appendix A] Provides the formal Theorem A.7 and proof steps, deriving the condition on λ.
  - [corpus] The concept of using Lipschitz properties to bound extrapolation error is established in prior work (e.g., Virmaux & Scaman, 2018 cited), but the specific application to pre-computed k-NN penalties in offline RL is novel to this paper.
- **Break condition:** The Lipschitz assumption may be violated if the Q-function has sharp discontinuities (e.g., due to threshold effects in dynamics or rewards). The bound then may not hold, and the penalty could be miscalibrated. Furthermore, `d_min` is unknown and the paper's λ choice is heuristic, so the guarantee is not operational in practice without knowing these constants.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Offline Reinforcement Learning (Offline RL) & Distributional Shift**
- **Why needed here:** The entire paper addresses the core offline RL problem: learning a policy from a fixed dataset without environment interaction. Distributional shift—where the learned policy considers actions not in the dataset—causes Q-value overestimation and failure. Understanding this failure mode is prerequisite to grasping why Geometric Pessimism is needed.
- **Quick check question:** Why can't an offline RL agent correct a mistakenly high Q-value estimate for an out-of-distribution action through experience?

**Concept: Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) and Expectile Regression**
- **Why needed here:** Geo-IQL is explicitly an augmentation of IQL. IQL's key feature is avoiding explicit OOD action sampling by using expectile regression on the Bellman backup, making it computationally efficient. However, its lack of explicit OOD pessimism is its weakness that Geo-IQL addresses. One must understand IQL's in-sample nature and its failure on "fractured" datasets.
- **Quick check question:** How does IQL's use of expectile regression differ from a standard Bellman backup, and what is the computational implication of this difference?

**Concept: Lipschitz Continuity in Function Approximation**
- **Why needed here:** The theoretical guarantee (Mechanism 3) rests on Lipschitz continuity of the Q-function and its approximator. This mathematical property bounds how fast the function can change, linking function error to input distance. It's the formalism that justifies penalizing based on distance.
- **Quick check question:** If a function is L-Lipschitz, what does that imply about the maximum change in its output if you change its input by a small amount `δ`?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**
Dataset & Embedder -> Geometric Penalty Pre-computer (k-NN index, U calculation, adaptive scaling) -> Penalty Lookup Table -> Modified IQL Training Loop (uses r_geo = r - p)

**Critical path:**
1. Pre-computation phase runs once before neural network training. It must process the entire offline dataset and produce a penalty lookup table of the same size.
2. The training loop samples minibatches, indexes into the pre-computed penalty table with exact dataset indices, and applies the penalty to the reward before any IQL loss calculation.
3. System stability hinges on pre-computed penalties being static and correctly aligned with dataset indices used during training. A mismatch breaks the mechanism.

**Design tradeoffs:**
*   **Pre-computation vs. Flexibility:** Pre-computation gives O(1) overhead but makes the penalty static. It cannot adapt if the policy's visited state-action distribution drifts during training (though this is less of a concern in offline RL). A dynamic penalty (like CQL) could be more responsive but is costly.
*   **Embedding Design:** Using raw action magnitude preserves physical meaning (as stated) but may dominate distance calculations if action scales are large. Normalizing actions might be necessary for some domains but was deliberately avoided for control tasks.
*   **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** `k` (neighbors), `α` (safe core percentile), and `λ_base` are critical. `k=10` and `α=0.3` were chosen via preliminary tuning. Changes require re-tuning. The adaptive scaling function (Eq 6) is a specific parameterized form; other forms (e.g., exponential) are possible.
*   **Computational Bottleneck:** k-NN search on large datasets can be expensive during pre-computation, but this is a one-time cost. Using approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) libraries is practical.

**Failure signatures:**
1.  **Pre-computation Index Mismatch:** Training indices do not correctly map to penalty table entries. Symptom: erratic, non-reproducible performance; penalties applied to wrong transitions.
2.  **Embedding Space Ill-posed:** Poor feature scaling (e.g., unnormalized states with vastly different ranges) causes Euclidean distance to be dominated by one feature dimension. Symptom: Penalties appear random or apply only to a subset of OOD behaviors. Check: Visualize 2D/3D PCA of embeddings; clusters should be meaningful.
3.  **`k` too small/large:** `k=1` makes penalties noisy (sensitive to single outliers). `k` too large (e.g., > dataset size/10) smooths penalties excessively, potentially failing to detect local OOD regions. Symptom: No performance gain on fractured tasks (Hopper) or performance drop on stable tasks.
4.  **`λ_base` mis-set:** Too low: no observable pessimism, IQL-like failure. Too high: over-pessimism, policy collapses to a tiny safe region, severe performance drop even on in-distribution data. Symptom: Very low normalized scores across all tasks.
5.  **Theoretical Assumption Violation:** If the true Q* is not Lipschitz (e.g., due to contact dynamics in robotics), the penalty may not guarantee `˜Q ≤ Q*`. Symptom: Policy may still overestimate and fail catastrophically on deployment, even with penalties applied. Hard to detect from offline metrics alone.

**First 3 experiments:**
1.  **Sanity Check: Penalty Distribution & Alignment.** After pre-computation, plot a histogram of `U(s,a)` and `p` values for the entire dataset. Verify: a mode at/near zero (the safe core), a long tail for OOD points. Randomly sample 100 transitions, manually check if their penalty seems reasonable given the `(s,a)` quality (e.g., a transition with a terminal fall in Hopper should have high penalty if it's a rare failure mode). **Pass:** Penalty distribution shows a clear peak at 0 and a tail; manual spot-check aligns with intuition.
2.  **Ablation on Fractured Task (Hopper-medium-replay).** Run three runs: (a) IQL, (b) Geo-IQL with `k=10`, (c) Geo-IQL with `k=50`. Compare normalized scores and standard deviation. **Pass:** Geo-IQL (`k=10`) substantially outperforms IQL in score and stability. `k=50` should show degraded or similar performance, confirming the local sensitivity of `k=10`.
3.  **Penalty Application Verification.** Modify the training loop to log the *raw* `r` and the penalized `r'` for a fixed batch at initialization and after 100k steps. Plot the difference `(r - r')` against `U(s,a)`. **Pass:** Strong positive correlation: higher `U` leads to larger reward subtraction. The relationship should be largely stable across training steps (since penalties are static). If correlation is weak or changes, indexing or computation is broken.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How should discrete actions be embedded in the state-action space for geometric distance computation, and does this affect Geo-IQL's performance on tasks like sepsis with discrete action spaces? The paper's method concatenates normalized state with raw action, excluding action normalization for continuous control, but it is unclear how discrete vasopressor/fluid doses (25 categories) are represented.
- **Open Question 2:** How robust is Geo-IQL to the choice of hyperparameters, especially the safe threshold percentile α and the base penalty λ_base, across datasets with varying density and fracturing? The paper uses α=0.30 and k=10 based on "preliminary tuning" without validation across tasks, and λ_base is tunable but its sensitivity is unstudied.
- **Open Question 3:** Does the adaptive penalty mechanism (λ_adapt varying with local density) preserve the theoretical guarantee that Geo-IQL's Q-estimates are pessimistically bounded by the true Q-function? The proof in Appendix A is for a fixed λ penalty, but the implemented Geo-IQL uses an adaptive λ_adapt; the proof does not account for this adaptivity.
- **Open Question 4:** What specific clinical decisions does Geo-IQL change compared to IQL in sepsis treatment, and are these deviations associated with improved patient outcomes as suggested by higher terminal agreement? The paper shows lower overall policy-clinician agreement but higher terminal agreement and positive Q-improvement, but does not analyze which actions differ or their clinical impact.

## Limitations
- The theoretical Lipschitz-based guarantee requires unknown constants (`bL`, `L*`, `ε`, `d_min`), and the chosen `λ_base` is heuristic, so the operational guarantee `Q̃ ≤ Q*` is not proven in practice.
- Critical hyperparameters (`λ_base`, exact `k`, `α` for `τ`) are not task-specifically reported, blocking exact replication; tuning details are absent.
- The representation of discrete actions in the MIMIC-III sepsis task is unspecified, a key design choice that could significantly impact the distance metric's validity and results.
- The penalty is static and pre-computed, so it cannot adapt if the policy's visited distribution drifts during training, though this is less likely in pure offline RL.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence:** Empirical results on D4RL MuJoCo. The metrics are clear, the Hopper ablation is convincing, and the improvement on a known difficult benchmark is substantial and stable.
- **Medium Confidence:** Theoretical mechanism. The proof structure is sound and connects to established Lipschitz continuity bounds, but its core assumptions (Lipschitz Q*, known `d_min`) are unvalidated and the operational λ choice is heuristic.
- **Low Confidence:** Sepsis results. The action embedding for discrete clinical interventions is not described, leaving a major ambiguity in how the geometric penalty is applied. The outcome may be highly sensitive to this design choice.
