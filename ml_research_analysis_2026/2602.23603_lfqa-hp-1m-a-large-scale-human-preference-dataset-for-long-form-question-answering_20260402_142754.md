# LFQA-HP-1M: A Large-Scale Human Preference Dataset for Long-Form Question Answering

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23603
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23603
- **Reference count:** 27
- **Primary result:** A logistic regression model using nine interpretable quality rubrics predicts human LFQA preferences with 68.3% accuracy, matching state-of-the-art LLM judges (GPT-4o: 69.4%) while resisting their systematic biases.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the unreliable evaluation of long-form question answering (LFQA) by introducing LFQA-HP-1M, a dataset of 1.3 million human pairwise preference annotations. It defines nine interpretable rubrics (e.g., coherence, completeness, factuality) to score answers. A simple logistic regression model using the differences in these rubric scores achieves performance comparable to LLM-as-a-judge systems like GPT-4o. Crucially, the rubric-based model is inherently resistant to positional bias, verbosity bias, and non-transitive judgments that plague LLM evaluators, demonstrating that transparent, feature-driven models can be both effective and robust for LFQA evaluation.

## Method Summary
The work constructs LFQA-HP-1M by aggregating and filtering existing pairwise preference data from sources like SHP-2 and Chatbot Arena using a rigorously defined, prompt-based LFQA classifier (operationalized with Llama-4). For each answer in a pair, nine quality rubrics are scored via a mix of tools: LLM-based prompts (G-EVAL) for coherence/fluency/relevance/completeness, Veriscore for factuality, a specificity model, spaCy for conciseness, LanguageTool for grammar, and a prompt for example usage. After normalization, the score differences between the two answers form the feature vector for training a binary logistic regression to predict the human-preferred answer. This framework is evaluated on a stratified 5,074-instance sample (LFQA-HP-Sample) and compared against zero-shot LLM judges, with additional tests for bias and adversarial robustness.

## Key Results
- LFQA-HP-1M contains 1.3M human pairwise preferences over 300K unique questions, created via a high-precision filtering pipeline.
- A logistic regression on nine rubric score differences achieves 68.3% accuracy/F1 in predicting human preferences, comparable to GPT-4o (69.4%).
- LLM-as-a-judge systems exhibit significant positional bias, verbosity bias, and non-transitive judgments, while the rubric-based linear model is inherently resistant to these flaws.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
A large-scale dataset of human pairwise preferences (LFQA-HP-1M) enables training models that predict human judgment. The dataset aggregates and filters multi-source data (SHP-2, Chatbot Arena, LFQA Eval) using a prompt-based LFQA classifier (Llama-4), yielding 1.3M (question, answer A, answer B, preference) tuples. A model trained on this data learns the mapping from quality features to the human preference signal. **Core assumption:** The filtered dataset provides a reliable, scalable LFQA preference signal. **Evidence:** [abstract] mentions the 1.3M scale; [section 3.4] details the aggregation, filtering (F1 ~0.75), and final construction. **Break condition:** High false negatives in filtering or noisy/imbalanced source annotations.

### Mechanism 2
Nine interpretable rubrics (completeness, coherence, etc.) serve as effective features. Each answer is scored on the rubrics using non-LLM tools (Veriscore, LanguageTool, spaCy) and LLM-based G-EVAL prompts. The score differences between two answers train a logistic regression; learned weights indicate rubric influence (e.g., coherence/completeness positive). **Core assumption:** The nine rubrics comprehensively capture human LFQA judgment dimensions, and automated scores are valid proxies. **Evidence:** [abstract] states the nine rubrics and comparable performance; [section 4.1] defines rubrics and extraction tools; [section 4.2] presents model weights. **Break condition:** Missing key quality dimensions or misaligned automated scorers (especially LLM-based ones).

### Mechanism 3
The linear model inherently resists biases (position, verbosity, transitivity) that affect LLM judges. It relies only on normalized rubric scores, not raw text order, so it cannot be swayed by length or position. LLM judges process full sequences and prompts, making them susceptible to spurious correlations. **Core assumption:** Rubric scores decouple true quality from superficial properties like length/position. **Evidence:** [abstract] notes LR's resistance; [section 5.1–5.3] shows LLM transitivity violations (4.7–15.3%), position swaps hurting accuracy, and verbosity bias; LR is stable by design. **Break condition:** If rubric scorers themselves are biased by length/position, the LR's immunity is illusory.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Pairwise Preference Modeling
  - **Why needed:** The task is predicting relative human preference (A vs. B), not absolute scores. This shapes the logistic regression on feature differences and LLM judge prompts.
  - **Quick check:** If the LR model gives a positive weight for "completeness" difference, what does that imply about human values for LFQA?

- **Concept:** Operationalization of a Fuzzy Task Definition
  - **Why needed:** "LFQA" is not a natural category; it must be precisely defined and iteratively validated (Gwet's AC1) to filter a noisy dataset. The prompt-based classifier operationalizes this definition.
  - **Quick check:** Why would a question like "How do you feel?" be filtered out, even if it elicits a long personal narrative?

- **Concept:** Multi-Dimensional Quality Assessment vs. Holistic Scoring
  - **Why needed:** The rubric approach decomposes quality into nine aspects, recognizing that preference is an aggregate of potentially conflicting dimensions (e.g., completeness vs. conciseness), unlike a single LLM "goodness" score.
  - **Quick check:** The LR model learned a negative weight for "relevance." Does this mean humans prefer irrelevant information, or what is a more likely explanation?

## Architecture Onboarding
1. Data Ingestion & Filtering Module -> 2. Rubric Feature Extraction Module -> 3. Pairwise Dataset Construction -> 4. Sampling & Splitting Module -> 5. Model Training & Evaluation -> 6. Bias & Robustness Test Harness

**Critical path:** Success depends on: (a) high-precision LFQA filter (§3.3) for valid task definition, (b) reliable rubric scorers (§4.1) for meaningful features, (c) balanced LFQA-HP-Sample splits to prevent domain skew.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Cost vs. Scale/Precision:** Used Llama-4 (not GPT-4o) for filtering with batching (size=10) and log_prob=0, saving ~60x cost for minor F1 trade-off.
- **Interpretability vs. Performance:** LR model (68.3%) vs. GPT-4o (69.4%); ~1% gap is exchanged for transparency, bias resistance, and low inference cost.
- **Feature Engineering vs. Learned Representations:** Fixed external tools (Veriscore, G-EVAL) provide causal explanations versus an end-to-end model (ModernBERT fine-tune) which may capture nuances but is a black box.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Data Level:** Low Gwet's AC1 scores (§3.2) indicate an unclear or inconsistently applied LFQA definition.
- **Feature Level:** Rubric scores show low variance across answer pairs or high inter-rubric correlation, weakening LR discriminative power.
- **Model Level:** LLM judges show large accuracy drops (>5%) on position-swapped data (§5.2); LR model shows high sensitivity to small adversarial perturbations if rubrics are brittle.
- **Evaluation Level:** Insufficient 3-way comparisons (same Q, three answers) to assess transitivity (§5.1).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Reproduce the LFQA filtering pipeline on a held-out set of 100 manually annotated questions; compute precision/recall/F1 against the human-defined LFQA label.
2. Manually score 100 random answer pairs on all nine rubrics; correlate with automated tool outputs (G-EVAL, Veriscore, etc.) to detect systematic tool biases.
3. Create a synthetic test set where answer pairs have identical rubric scores except one is longer (added irrelevant text); LR prediction should be ~50%, confirming no verbosity bias in features or weights.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** How can rubric representations be enriched (e.g., sub-scores, learned features) to better capture nuanced LFQA quality dimensions missed by the current linear model?
  - **Basis:** Future work states need for "richer rubric representations, such as sub-scores or learned features."
  - **Why unresolved:** Nine coarse rubrics linear combination may omit latent cognitive/semantic factors driving preferences.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Ablation with expanded rubric granularity or representation learning on LFQA-HP-1M to see if accuracy improves.

- **Question:** Does excluding dialogue-style questions in LFQA-HP-1M limit rubric generalizability to conversational/interactive LFQA?
  - **Basis:** Limitation: dataset "contains only question-answer-style content (excluding dialogue-style questions)."
  - **Why unresolved:** Filtering pipeline and definition explicitly exclude multi-turn prompts; transfer to dialogic contexts is unknown.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Apply rubric framework to dialogue-based LFQA datasets (e.g., multi-turn ChatBot Arena logs) and compare performance/weights.

- **Question:** What are the root causes of positional, verbosity, and transitivity biases in LLM-as-a-judge for LFQA, and how can they be mitigated without losing alignment?
  - **Basis:** Analysis documents biases but does not explain mechanisms or provide solutions beyond noting rubric model resistance.
  - **Why unresolved:** Biases are observed but not theoretically dissected; mitigation (e.g., prompt engineering, calibration) for LFQA is underexplored.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Controlled experiments perturbing order, length, content to isolate sources, then test debiasing techniques (randomization, length normalization) on LFQA-HP-Sample.

- **Question:** How do filtering choices (Llama-4 vs. GPT-4o, batch size, confidence threshold) affect LFQA-HP-1M's diversity, noise, and representativeness?
  - **Basis:** Filtering uses Llama-4 with batching/log_score for cost, but impact on final dataset composition is unanalyzed.
  - **Why unresolved:** Precision/recall trade-offs for cost may introduce selection biases; 300K questions may not reflect source distribution.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Compare subsets filtered with different models/parameters on domain coverage, question type distribution, and downstream model performance.

- **Question:** Can non-linear feature interactions or higher-order combinations of rubric scores improve LR's prediction, and what do they reveal about quality trade-offs?
  - **Basis:** Limitation notes "the LR model could be strengthened by considering latent cognitive features, structural hidden features."
  - **Why unresolved:** Human preference may involve complex trade-offs (e.g., coherence vs. conciseness) that linear models miss.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Train models with polynomial features or neural networks on rubric score pairs, evaluate on LFQA-HP-Sample, analyze importance of non-linear terms.

## Limitations
- Dataset curation relies on Llama-4 filtering without external human validation; potential high false negative rate may discard valid LFQA instances.
- Rubric scoring tools (G-EVAL, Veriscore, etc.) are assumed to align with human judgment on LFQA nuances, but their reliability is unverified.
- The claim of inherent bias resistance depends on rubric scores being invariant to length/position; if scorers themselves are biased, the LR's immunity is illusory.

## Confidence
- LFQA-HP-1M scale & diversity: Medium (evidence from internal filtering pipeline only)
- Rubric model performance (68.3%): Medium-High (replication possible but tool mismatches could shift results)
- Inherent bias resistance vs. LLMs: Low (theoretically plausible but not directly stress-tested in paper)
- Filtering pipeline precision/recall (~0.75 F1): Medium (based on automated validation against a definition)
- G-EVAL/Veriscore alignment with humans on LFQA: Medium-Low (assumed but not empirically validated for this task)
