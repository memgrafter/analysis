# Machine Unlearning under Retain-Forget Entanglement
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.26569
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26569
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: A two-phase unlearning framework that achieves near-zero forget accuracy while preserving high accuracy on both adjacent and remote retained subsets, outperforming state-of-the-art methods.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the challenge of retain-forget entanglement in machine unlearning, where forgetting a target subset inadvertently affects correlated retained samples. They propose a two-phase optimization framework: first, using an augmented Lagrangian method to increase loss on the forget set while preserving accuracy on less-correlated retained samples; second, applying gradient projection regularized by Wasserstein-2 distance to recover performance on semantically related retained samples. Experiments across multiple datasets, architectures, and tasks demonstrate effective forgetting with minimal impact on retention, surpassing existing baselines.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two sequential stages. Stage 1 formulates unlearning as constrained optimization: maximize loss on the forget set (i.e., minimize –L_f) subject to the remote retain loss staying within a small tolerance of its initial value. An augmented Lagrangian with adaptive multiplier and quadratic penalty ensures the constraint is satisfied without manual tuning of the penalty weight. Stage 2 focuses on the adjacent retain set, which is semantically close to the forget set. It employs gradient projection: updates are taken in the direction that improves adjacent retain loss while removing any component aligned with gradients from the modified forget loss (which includes a Wasserstein-2 distributional regularizer) or remote retain loss. The Wasserstein-2 term stabilizes the empirical loss distribution on the forget set, preventing recovery of accuracy on forgotten samples. This separation avoids conflicts between forgetting and retention objectives.

## Key Results
- Near-zero test forget accuracy (e.g., 0.00% on CIFAR-100).
- High retain accuracy on adjacent (98.17%) and remote (98.44%) subsets (CIFAR-100).
- Outperforms existing baselines in both removal fidelity and retention accuracy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The augmented Lagrangian in Stage 1 reliably increases loss on the forget set while preserving accuracy on less‑correlated retained samples.
- **Mechanism:** Solve `min_θ -L_f(θ)` subject to `L_rem_r(θ) = L_rem_r(θ0)`. The augmented Lagrangian is `Laug(θ;λ,μ) = -L_f + λ (L_rem_r - L_rem_r(θ0)) + μ/2 (L_rem_r - L_rem_r(θ0))^2`. Iterate gradient descent on `θ` and multiplier update `λ ← λ + μ (L_rem_r - L_rem_r(θ0))`. This adaptively balances the forgetting objective and the constraint.
- **Core assumption:** The remote retain set (`D_rem_r`) is sufficiently dissimilar from the forget set such that preserving its loss prevents forgetting from degrading it.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: “phase one uses an augmented Lagrangian method to increase loss on the forget set while preserving accuracy on less‑related retained samples.”; [section]: Section 4.1 describes the constrained problem (1) and augmented Lagrangian (2)-(4).
- **Break condition:** If `L_rem_r` deviates significantly from `L_rem_r(θ0)`, the constraint was violated—possibly due to `μ` too small or learning rate too high—and less‑correlated retain performance may drop.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** In Stage 2, gradient projection with a Wasserstein‑2 regularizer restores accuracy on adjacent retain samples without recovering accuracy on the forget set.
- **Mechanism:** Define modified forget loss `˜L_f = (1-α)L_f + α·W₂²(P_θ^forget, P_θ0^forget)`, where `P_θ^forget` is the empirical distribution of cross‑entropy losses over `D_f`. Update `θ` via `θ ← θ - η (∇θ L_adj_r - Proj_V ∇θ L_adj_r)`, with `V = span{∇θ ˜L_f, ∇θ L_rem_r}`. The projection removes gradient components that could improve `L_adj_r` by reducing `L_f` or `L_rem_r`. The `W₂` term keeps the loss distribution on `D_f` stable, preventing many forget samples from becoming correctly classified.
- **Core assumption:** The adjacent retain set (`D_adj_r`) is semantically close to the forget set; optimizing `L_adj_r` alone would also reduce `L_f` on overlapping samples. The projection and distributional regularization jointly prevent such cross‑set improvement.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: “phase two applies gradient projection regularized by Wasserstein‑2 distance to mitigate performance degradation on semantically related retained samples without compromising the unlearning objective.”; [section]: Section 4.2 introduces W‑PGD, the modified loss (9), and projection (10). Figure 1 shows standard PGD creates skewed loss distributions while W‑PGD maintains uniformity.
- **Break condition:** If forget accuracy rises during Stage 2, the `W₂` penalty is too weak (`α` too low) or the projection space `V` does not sufficiently capture conflicting gradients. If adjacent retain accuracy does not improve, the learning rate may be too low or the projection overly restrictive.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Splitting the retain set into `D_adj_r` (correlated) and `D_rem_r` (less‑correlated) and treating them in separate stages prevents gradient conflicts that would otherwise hinder effective forgetting.
- **Mechanism:** Stage 1 only enforces `L_rem_r(θ) ≈ L_rem_r(θ0)`, allowing `L_adj_r` to degrade. Stage 2 then restores `L_adj_r` using projection that protects `˜L_f` and `L_rem_r`. This temporal separation avoids simultaneous optimization of conflicting objectives.
- **Core assumption:** Gradients of `L_adj_r` and `L_f` are often aligned (positive dot product), while gradients of `L_rem_r` are less correlated. Joint optimization would require balancing competing updates; separation sidesteps this.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: “In the first phase… preserving accuracy on less‑related retained samples. The second phase… mitigate performance degradation on semantically related retained samples.”; [section]: Section 4.2.1 discusses the failure of standard PGD (which does not distinguish `D_adj_r`) and Appendix B.5 shows constraining `D_adj_r` in Stage 1 harms forgetting.
- **Break condition:** If the split between adjacent and remote is noisy (e.g., some `D_rem_r` samples are actually correlated), Stage 1 may inadvertently degrade those remote samples, violating the core assumption and reducing overall retain accuracy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Augmented Lagrangian method** – Why needed: Core optimizer for Stage 1 to enforce the constraint without manually tuning a fixed penalty weight. – Quick check: How does the multiplier `λ` adapt during training? (updated as `λ ← λ + μ (L_rem_r - L_rem_r(θ0))`).
- **Wasserstein‑2 distance** – Why needed: Regularizer in Stage 2 to preserve the full distribution of forget losses, not just the mean, thereby preventing accuracy recovery. – Quick check: Why is `W₂` computationally favorable for 1D loss distributions compared to KL divergence? (It reduces to sorting and averaging squared differences, avoiding density estimation or kernel approximations.)
- **Retain‑forget entanglement** – Why needed: Recognizing that retained subpopulations have different sensitivities to forgetting is fundamental to the two‑stage design. – Quick check: How would you automatically identify the adjacent retain set if no semantic label exists? (e.g., use feature‑space nearest neighbors to the forget set.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Data preparation → θ0 (original model) → Stage 1 (ALS optimizer) → Stage 2 (W‑PGD) → Evaluation

### Critical Path
1. Define D_f, D_adj_r, D_rem_r.
2. Load pretrained θ0.
3. Run Stage 1 for K epochs until L_rem_r ≈ L_rem_r(θ0); save θ_K.
4. Run Stage 2 for M epochs with gradient projection and W₂ regularization to restore D_adj_r accuracy.
5. Evaluate final model on test sets for D_f, D_adj_r, D_rem_r.

### Design Tradeoffs
- α: Higher α strengthens distribution regularization but may slow adjacent retain recovery. Empirically α=0.5 works well.
- μ: Larger μ tightens the constraint on L_rem_r but may impede forgetting if too high.
- Split quality: A more accurate D_adj_r reduces noise but the method tolerates moderate mislabeling (Appendix B.7).

### Failure Signatures
- Stage 1: Significant drop in remote retain accuracy indicates constraint violation—reduce learning rate or increase μ.
- Stage 2: Forget accuracy rises ⇒ α too low or projection not implemented correctly; check that gradients of ˜L_f and L_rem_r span V.
- Adjacent retain accuracy remains low after Stage 2 ⇒ learning rate too small or projection overly restrictive; consider increasing M.
- Remote retain accuracy degrades ⇒ verify ∇L_rem_r is computed from sufficiently large batch (use gradient accumulation).

### First 3 Experiments
1. CIFAR‑100 ResNet‑18: Forget subclass “aquarium fish” (superclass “fish”). Expected near-zero forget accuracy (≤2% test) and ≥98% retain accuracy on both adjacent and remote subsets.
2. ToxiGen RoBERTa‑base: After biased training (LGBTQ comments labeled normal), forget toxic LGBTQ comments. Expected forget test accuracy ≈11% and retain accuracy on normal LGBTQ comments ≥88%.
3. CelebA ViT‑B: Forget “female & not smiling” without glasses/young. Expected balanced forgetting (test forget ≤26%) and high retain on adjacent (≥75%) and remote (≥92%).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can the adjacent retain set be automatically determined without external labels?
**Basis in paper:** The method assumes a partition into adjacent (correlated with forget) and remote retain sets is provided; only a kNN heuristic is tested as a proof of concept.
**Why unresolved:** Automatic identification is crucial for real-world unlearning where such a partition is not known a priori.
**What evidence would resolve it:** An unsupervised or semi-supervised algorithm that reliably discovers correlated retained samples from the model's behavior, validated across multiple datasets.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** What is the precise theoretical trade‑off between forgetting and retention for the proposed two‑stage scheme?
**Basis in paper:** The paper gives a loose bound (Proposition 4.2) on forget accuracy but does not characterize the interaction with retention, especially the adjacent set.
**Why unresolved:** A tighter bound would guide hyperparameter choices and clarify the fundamental limits.
**What evidence would resolve it:** A finite‑sample, high‑probability bound linking forgetting/retention errors to the regularization parameters α and µ, empirically verified.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Can the Wasserstein‑2 regularization be efficiently approximated for large forget sets while preserving forgetting guarantees?
**Basis in paper:** The authors compute W₂ exactly by sorting, which is O(|Df| log|Df|), and mention mini‑batching without analysis.
**Why unresolved:** Exact computation may be prohibitive at scale; minibatch approximations could break the distributional constraint and inadvertently allow the model to recover forget‑set accuracy.
**What evidence would resolve it:** A study of approximation error versus batch size, or a streaming/sketching algorithm with provable guarantees showing that forgetting performance remains unchanged.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Does the framework extend to non‑classification tasks (e.g., generative models) and to black‑box model access settings?
**Basis in paper:** The experiments cover only classification (image and text). The method requires gradient access and cross‑entropy losses.
**Why unresolved:** Real‑world unlearning also targets generative models and often faces limited model access. Adaptation may be needed.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Successful application to unlearning concepts in diffusion models or under API‑only access, with appropriate metrics for forgetting and retention.

## Limitations
- Exact dataset splits and training schedules for TinyImageNet and ToxiGen are unspecified.
- Mini‑batch handling of the Wasserstein‑2 regularizer lacks full algorithmic detail (sorting and distribution matching).
- The method assumes accurate identification of adjacent vs. remote retain subsets; noisy splits may degrade performance.

## Confidence
- Stage 1 augmented Lagrangian preserves remote retain while increasing forget loss: **Medium**
- Stage 2 gradient projection with W₂ restores adjacent retain without reducing forgetting: **Medium**
- Two‑stage separation avoids gradient conflicts and enables effective unlearning: **High**

## Next Checks
1. Validate W₂ gradient: compute exact full‑batch W₂ on a subset, compare its gradient to the mini‑batch estimator, and measure impact on forgetting/retention.
2. Assess split robustness: randomly swap x% of remote retain samples into the adjacent set, vary x, and record retention accuracy to find noise tolerance threshold.
3. Ablate projection: remove the projection step in Stage 2 while keeping W₂; confirm that adjacent retain accuracy improves but forget accuracy also recovers, verifying projection’s necessity.
