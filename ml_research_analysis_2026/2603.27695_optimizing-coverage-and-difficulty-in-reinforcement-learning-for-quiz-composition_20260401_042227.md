# Optimizing Coverage and Difficulty in Reinforcement Learning for Quiz Composition
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.27695
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27695
- Reference count: 39
- Primary result: RL agents (DQN, SARSA, A2C/A3C) automate quiz composition, achieving target similarity 0.86–0.91 with fast convergence (~20–30 iterations) and ~100× speedup over exhaustive search.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of automatically assembling quizzes from multiple-choice questions to satisfy teacher-defined topic coverage and difficulty distributions. The authors formalize the task as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where states represent quizzes (sets of k questions) and actions replace a question based on topic or difficulty similarity/dissimilarity. Three reinforcement learning algorithms—DQN, SARSA, and A2C/A3C—are trained using a scalarized reward that combines topic and difficulty match. On real-world datasets (MeD, Math), the agents reach high target similarity (0.86–0.91) in about 20–30 iterations, run roughly 100 times faster than an exhaustive oracle, and transfer effectively across datasets and between uniform and biased target distributions. A user study with 28 teachers shows moderate demand but positive satisfaction, with 57% indicating willingness to reuse the system.

## Method Summary
The core method treats quiz composition as a sequential decision process. A quiz state encodes the topic distribution (vector across topics) and difficulty distribution (vector across levels). The action space consists of four swap operations: replace a question with another that is similar in topic (`SimTopic`), similar in difficulty (`SimLevel`), dissimilar in topic (`DissTopic`), or dissimilar in difficulty (`DissLevel`). The reward is the difference in a scalarized target match: α·cosine(topicMatch) + (1−α)·cosine(diffMatch), where α ∈ [0,1] balances teacher preferences. The agent interacts with an environment that executes the chosen swap by querying a pre-computed similarity index over the question pool. Training proceeds over many episodes (e.g., 5000) using DQN (with prioritized experience replay), SARSA, or A2C/A3C. The target distributions include a uniform mixture and a biased one favoring specific topics/difficulties. Results show that DQN with prioritized replay generally achieves the highest final similarity, while A2C converges faster per iteration but is more sensitive to hyperparameters.

## Key Results
- Agents achieve target similarity scores between 0.86 and 0.91 on real datasets (MeD, Math).
- Converges in approximately 20–30 iterations, with ~100× speed improvement over an exhaustive search oracle.
- Demonstrates strong transfer learning across datasets and between uniform/biased target distributions.
- A user study (N=28) reports positive satisfaction and 57% reuse intent, though demand is moderate.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The MDP formulation with quiz states and replacement actions enables efficient navigation toward a target distribution.
- **Mechanism:** Each state is a quiz (set of k MCQs). Actions (`SimTopic`, `SimLevel`, `DissTopic`, `DissLevel`) replace one MCQ with another that is similar or dissimilar in topic or difficulty, directly mimicking a teacher's iterative edit. The agent learns a policy that chooses actions to maximize the scalarized reward, effectively searching the combinatorial space of quizzes via small, meaningful steps.
- **Core assumption:** Teacher edits can be approximated by swapping questions based on topic/difficulty similarity, and a sequence of such swaps can reach a high-quality quiz.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We formalize a generic sequential decision-making problem... actions replace questions via topic/difficulty similarity or dissimilarity."
  - [section 2.1] Detailed definition of states (quizzes), actions (the four swap types), and the reward as the delta in `targetMatch`.
  - [corpus] Weak/absent. Neighboring papers focus on different applications (e.g., UAV path planning, code verification) and do not directly evidence this specific MDP design for quiz composition.
- **Break condition:** If the action space is insufficient to transform any starting quiz into a quiz matching the target (e.g., topic/difficulty labels are too coarse or the pool lacks necessary diversity), the sequential search will fail regardless of policy.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Scalarization of the bi-objective problem (topic coverage + difficulty match) into a single reward enables standard RL algorithms to learn a policy balancing pedagogical goals.
- **Mechanism:** The reward `targetMatch(Z, TC, TD) = α·topicMatch(Z, TC) + (1-α)·diffMatch(Z, TD)` converts two competing objectives into a scalar signal. The weight `α` allows teachers to express preference. The RL agent maximizes cumulative scalar reward, implicitly learning trade-offs.
- **Core assumption:** A linear weighted sum is a valid and learnable representation of a teacher's composite objective for quiz quality.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Three RL algorithms... trained with a scalarized reward combining topic and difficulty match."
  - [section 2.1] Full reward function definition and the role of `α ∈ [0,1]`.
  - [corpus] Weak/absent. While multi-objective RL is common, the corpus does not provide specific evidence for this scalarization approach in an educational context.
- **Break condition:** If teacher preferences are non-linear, conflicting, or context-dependent beyond a static weighting, the scalarized reward will misalign with true pedagogical goals, leading to suboptimal quizzes.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The learned action distribution (favoring similarity for uniform targets, exploring with dissimilarity for biased targets) reflects efficient adaptation to the landscape of the target distribution.
- **Mechanism:** For a uniform target (`Tuniform`), many quizzes already have moderate similarity; small `Sim`-actions yield positive rewards, leading to risk-averse, local optimization. For a biased target (`Tbias`), the agent must make larger changes to shift topic/difficulty proportions, so `Diss`-actions (which risk negative immediate reward) are necessary for exploration and are consequently used more.
- **Core assumption:** The shape of the reward landscape (smooth many local optima vs. few distant optima) determined by the target distribution dictates the optimal exploration-exploitation balance, and RL discovers this.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] "For `Tuniform`, agents favor similarity actions... for `Tbias`, they explore more using dissimilarity actions."
  - [section 3.3] Figures 3 & 4 visually show the divergent action distributions for uniform vs. biased targets during DQN training.
  - [corpus] Weak/absent. No neighboring paper analyzes action distribution shifts in response to target distribution geometry for a quiz task.
- **Break condition:** If the initial quiz pool is extremely homogeneous or the target is unreachable, the action distribution will not adapt meaningfully, and the agent will fail to find a high-similarity quiz.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
State Encoder -> Action Selector (Policy Network) -> Environment/Transition Model -> Reward Calculator -> RL Algorithm Core

**Critical path:**
1. Initialize agent with random policy. Start from a random quiz `Z1` (state `s1`).
2. Agent selects action `a1` based on policy `π(s1)`.
3. Environment applies `a1` to `Z1` to produce `Z2` (state `s2`).
4. Reward `r1 = targetMatch(Z2) - targetMatch(Z1)` is calculated and sent to agent.
5. Agent stores `(s1, a1, r1, s2)` in replay buffer (if off-policy) and performs a learning update.
6. Repeat from step 2 with `s2` until `targetMatch(Z) >= β` (threshold) or max iterations.
7. Over many episodes (`5000`), the policy `π` is optimized to maximize cumulative reward.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Action Space Granularity: The 4-action design is simple but may be limiting. A richer action space (e.g., "replace MCQ *i* with a MCQ from topic *X*, difficulty *Y*") could be more powerful but vastly increases complexity and may require more data.
- Reward Shaping Sensitivity: The `α` weight and `β` threshold are critical. Setting `β` too high (e.g., 0.925) makes convergence slow; too low yields mediocre quizzes. The `α` value must reflect true teacher priority.
- Algorithm Choice: DQN with PER is shown best for high final similarity but may be conservative. A3C is faster per iteration but sensitive to tuning. The tradeoff is between final solution quality (DQN) and sample efficiency during training (A3C).

**Failure signatures:**
- Action Distribution Lock-in: Agent almost exclusively uses `SimTopic`/`SimLevel` even for biased targets, indicating it is stuck in a local basin of moderate similarity and cannot escape.
- Q-value Divergence/Instability: During training (per Figure 2), max Q-values do not stabilize, suggesting learning rate is too high, reward scale is problematic, or the MDP is non-stationary due to the discrete action space over a fixed pool.
- Catastrophic Transfer Failure: Agent trained on `Tuniform` fails completely on `Tbias` (similarity remains near random) and vice versa. This suggests the learned policy overfits to the specific topology of one target distribution's reward landscape and cannot generalize its notion of "progress."

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Sanity Check: Action Execution.** For a manually chosen starting quiz and target, manually execute each of the 4 actions. Verify that `SimTopic` produces a quiz with a topic distribution *closer* (in cosine similarity) to `TC` than the original, and `DissTopic` produces one *further*. Do the same for `SimLevel`/`DissLevel` vs. `TD`. This validates the environment's transition logic.
2. **Reward Sensitivity Sweep.** Train a single algorithm (e.g., DQN) on `Tuniform` with `α` values in `{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1}`. Plot final `topicMatch` and `diffMatch` vs. `α`. Expect a trade-off: as `α` increases, `topicMatch` should increase and `diffMatch` decrease. This confirms the scalarization control.
3. **Convergence Diagnostics on Synthetic Data.** Train on the `Uniform` synthetic dataset for 5000 episodes. Plot the max Q-value (across all state-action pairs in the replay buffer) per 1000 steps (like Figure 2). Confirm stabilization within ~30k steps. If it does not stabilize, check reward scaling, learning rate, or discount factor `γ`.

## Limitations
- External validation is absent: the corpus shows low average similarity (~0.55) and zero citations to neighboring work, indicating the specific MDP design and scalarization approach lack corroboration.
- Teacher preference modeling is simplistic: the linear scalarized reward may not capture non-linear, contextual, or conflicting pedagogical objectives, risking misalignment.
- Real-world robustness is unclear: user study is small (N=28) and reports only moderate demand; no evidence on actual learning outcomes or generalization to unseen quiz pools.

## Next Checks
1. **Action Space Sufficiency Stress Test:** Construct a synthetic quiz pool where no sequence of the 4 defined swap actions can reach a target distribution (e.g., target requires 50% Topic X but pool contains only 10% Topic X). Run the agent from random starts and verify failure to converge, explicitly confirming the break condition.
2. **Non-Linear Preference Ablation:** Replace the linear `α·topicMatch + (1-α)·diffMatch` reward with a non-linear function (e.g., penalty only if both topic and difficulty mismatches exceed thresholds). Conduct a teacher preference elicitation study (N>50) to compare agent outputs under linear vs. non-linear scalarizations against teacher-ranked quiz quality.
3. **Zero-Shot Transfer on Unseen Corpus:** Train agents exclusively on the MeD dataset. Evaluate final similarity scores on a third, held-out dataset (e.g., a public MCQ bank like AI2 Reasoning Challenge) with both uniform and biased targets. Report degradation vs. in-dataset performance to quantify true generalization.
