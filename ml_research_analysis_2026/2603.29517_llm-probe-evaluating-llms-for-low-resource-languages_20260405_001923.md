# LLM Probe: Evaluating LLMs for Low-Resource Languages
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.29517
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29517
- **Reference Count**: 26
- **Primary Result**: A lexicon-based evaluation framework shows sequence-to-sequence models (mT5, ByT5) outperform causal models (Gemma, Falcon) on morphosyntactic probing and translation for Tigrinya, while causal models excel at lexical alignment, revealing architectural biases in handling morphologically complex, low-resource languages.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces LLM Probe, a standardized evaluation framework for low-resource, morphologically rich languages, addressing the gap in controllable benchmarks. The authors construct a manually annotated Tigrinya-English lexicon (7,234 phrase pairs) with high inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ 0.84–0.91) to test causal and sequence-to-sequence models across four tasks: lexical alignment, POS tagging, morphosyntactic probing, and translation. Findings indicate sequence-to-sequence architectures achieve superior performance on morphosyntax and translation (up to 77% accuracy, BLEU 26.5), while causal models show strength in lexical alignment but weaker translation fidelity (BLEU ~22). The work underscores the necessity of linguistically grounded evaluation and highlights how architectural design influences performance on complex linguistic structures.

## Method Summary
The study employs an inference-only evaluation using a manually curated bilingual benchmark. The dataset consists of 7,234 English–Tigrinya phrase pairs annotated with part-of-speech tags, grammatical gender, number, and semantic roles. Four tasks are defined: (1) lexical alignment (word-to-word mapping), (2) POS tagging, (3) morphosyntactic feature probing, and (4) translation fidelity. Models are selected from open-source causal (Falcon, Gemma, Mistral, Qwen) and sequence-to-sequence (mT5, ByT5) families. Standardized prompt templates guide each task, with greedy decoding and fixed maximum tokens. Performance is measured via accuracy for classification tasks and BLEU/token overlap for translation. The framework ensures tasks test unseen linguistic structures, minimizing data contamination risks.

## Key Results
- Sequence-to-sequence models (mT5, ByT5) achieve highest morphosyntactic probing accuracy (up to 77%) and translation BLEU (up to 26.5).
- Causal models (Gemma, Falcon) outperform in lexical alignment accuracy but show lower translation fidelity (BLEU ~22).
- The manually annotated benchmark demonstrates high reliability, with inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ) ranging from 0.84 to 0.91.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Lexicon-based probing provides a controlled, reproducible assessment of linguistic competence by testing on unseen grammatical rules and vocabulary, reducing data contamination risks.
- **Mechanism**: The framework uses a manually curated bilingual lexicon with morphosyntactic annotations as a gold-standard reference. Models are prompted to map or analyze specific phrase pairs, and outputs are compared against these fixed annotations. This isolates evaluation to the model's internal linguistic knowledge rather than its ability to recall or pattern-match from large pre-training corpora.
- **Core assumption**: Models have not seen the specific, manually verified Tigrinya-English phrase pairs or their detailed annotations during pre-training, and performance reflects generalization ability.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "By focusing on lexicon-level analysis, it provides interpretable insights into model behavior... By grounding evaluation in manually curated, linguistically annotated lexicons, we ensure that model performance is assessed on genuinely unseen linguistic structures rather than potentially memorized patterns from pretraining data."
  - [section 3]: "The framework is grounded in a manually curated benchmark dataset comprising bidirectional English–Tigrinya and Tigrinya–English lexicons annotated with part-of-speech tags, grammatical gender, morphosyntactic features, and semantic roles."
- **Break condition**: If the lexicon entries or their close paraphrases were present in the models' training data, the controlled evaluation premise is compromised, potentially inflating scores and invalidating the "unseen" claim.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Architectural differences (sequence-to-sequence vs. causal) lead to distinct performance profiles on morphologically complex, low-resource languages due to inherent modeling biases.
- **Mechanism**: Sequence-to-sequence models (mT5, ByT5) are trained with an encoder-decoder structure that explicitly models source-target alignment, which may benefit tasks requiring structural transformation (morphosyntactic probing, translation). Causal models (Gemma, Falcon) use decoder-only, left-to-right generation, which may excel at lexical prediction (alignment) but struggle with bidirectional structural analysis needed for accurate translation and morphological agreement.
- **Core assumption**: The observed performance gaps (e.g., mT5/ByT5 > causal models on morphosyntax/translation BLEU, causal models > seq2seq on lexical alignment format) are causally linked to architectural inductive biases, not just scale or training data differences.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "Results show sequence-to-sequence models (mT5, ByT5) achieve superior morphosyntactic probing (up to 77% accuracy) and translation fidelity (BLEU up to 26.5), while causal models (Gemma, Falcon) excel in lexical alignment but exhibit weaker translation accuracy (BLEU ~22)."
  - [section 5.1 & Table 5]: "ByT5 and mT5 variants show consistently strong performance... mT5 struggles to distinguish lexical correspondences reliably, whereas ByT5 attains notably higher accuracy on this specific task... Models such as Falcon-10B, Mistral-7B, and Qwen-7B produce valid predictions, but their performance is comparatively lower, particularly in morphosyntactic and BLEU scores."
- **Break condition**: If performance differences diminish or reverse with equivalent fine-tuning datasets or larger model scales for causal architectures, the architectural advantage claim weakens, suggesting training data/model size may be primary drivers.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: High inter-annotator agreement in the manually created benchmark validates the reliability of gold-standard labels, ensuring that performance differences reflect model limitations rather than annotation noise.
- **Mechanism**: The annotation process involved multiple trained linguists with standardized protocols, followed by adjudication. High Cohen's κ (0.84-0.91) and agreement percentages (90.7%-96.2%) indicate the labels are stable and linguistically sound. This provides a trustworthy target for model evaluation, so low model scores on tasks like morphosyntactic probing are attributable to model error, not ambiguous or inconsistent references.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.1 & Table 2]: "To ensure the reliability and consistency of linguistic annotations, we conducted an inter-annotator agreement (IAA) study... Agreement was measured using both percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa coefficient... These results indicate a high level of consistency across annotators, validating the reliability, linguistic accuracy, and robustness of the annotation protocol."
  - [section 6.2]: "The high inter-annotator agreement scores (Cohen’s κ ranging from 0.84 to 0.91) validate the quality and reliability of our annotation protocol, ensuring that the dataset serves as a trustworthy gold standard for model evaluation."
- **Break condition**: If a re-annotation by a different set of expert linguists yields substantially different labels or lower agreement, the gold standard's reliability is questionable, undermining all performance conclusions drawn from it.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Lexicon-Based Evaluation**
  - Why needed here: Moves evaluation beyond corpus-based metrics (like BLEU) to probe specific, controlled linguistic knowledge, crucial for low-resource languages where large clean corpora are scarce and contamination risks are high.
  - Quick check question: How does evaluating on a fixed, manually annotated lexicon reduce the risk of a model "cheating" by recalling pre-training data compared to evaluating on a news corpus?
- **Concept: Morphosyntactic Probing**
  - Why needed here: Tigrinya's rich morphology (gender, number, root patterns) is a key challenge for LLMs. Probing for these features explicitly tests if models understand grammatical rules, not just surface translation.
  - Quick check question: Why might a model achieve a decent BLEU score on Tigrinya translation but fail a morphosyntactic agreement probe?
- **Concept: Architectural Inductive Bias**
  - Why needed here: Explains *why* seq2seq and causal models show divergent strengths. The decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder design influences what linguistic patterns are learned most efficiently.
  - Quick check question: What inherent property of an encoder-decoder (seq2seq) architecture might make it better suited for tasks requiring consistent transformation of a source sequence into a target sequence with structural constraints?

- **Component map**:

- **Critical path**:
  1. Clone repository & install dependencies (Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.1, Transformers).
  2. Download/verify benchmark dataset JSON (7,234 pairs).
  3. For each task-model assignment from Table 4: load model via `transformers`, format prompt with placeholders, run inference with fixed parameters, parse output, compare to gold label, compute metric.
  4. Aggregate results, generate summary.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Manual annotation ROI vs. Scalability**: High IAA ensures label quality but limits dataset size/domain coverage.
  - **Prompt engineering vs. Model capability**: Fixed prompts ensure reproducibility but may not elicit optimal performance.
  - **Task-specific model assignment**: Prescribed assignments based on pilot tests; may overlook fine-tuned causal models for morphosyntax.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Lexical Alignment**: High semantic accuracy but low format accuracy (e.g., output phrasing mismatch) indicates prompt following/parsing issue.
  - **POS/Morphosyntax**: Confusion matrix shows noun-verb mix-ups; suggests failure on derivational morphology or context-dependent POS.
  - **Translation**: Low BLEU but high token overlap; model produces functionally correct but orthographically divergent outputs.
  - **Systemic**: Failures on multi-word expressions indicate lack of phrase-level compositionality.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Reproduce Table 5**: Run full pipeline for mT5-large and Gemma-7B; verify approximate scores (mT5: ~80% POS, ~77% morph, BLEU ~26.5; Gemma: ~75.5% POS, ~72% morph, BLEU ~22.7).
  2. **Lexical Alignment Edge Case**: Test 50 polysemous entries (e.g., "that" → multiple Tigrinya forms). Check if model picks consistent variant matching gold reference; diagnoses sensitivity to agreement.
  3. **Morphosyntactic Generalization**: Create 50 minimal pairs differing only in gender/number (e.g., masculine vs. feminine "applauder"). Probe if model predicts correct features; tests rule learning vs. memorization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do closed-source LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, Claude, Gemini) compare to open-source models in performing linguistically grounded tasks on Tigrinya? The paper explicitly notes "Limited Model Coverage" as a limitation.
2. Can automated NLP tools (e.g., tokenizers, morphological analyzers, POS taggers) be viably developed for Tigrinya to enable scalable, reproducible evaluation without manual annotation bottlenecks? Inferred from "Language-Specific Tooling Gaps".
3. To what extent are the reported performance differences between causal and sequence-to-sequence models sensitive to prompt engineering and task formulation? The paper identifies "Prompt Sensitivity and Evaluation Bias" as a limitation.
4. How do LLMs perform on Tigrinya dialects and informal registers (e.g., spoken varieties, social media text) compared to the standard, general-purpose language in the benchmark? Inferred from the dataset's focus on "general-purpose lexical items" and future work on dialectal variation.
5. Do high morphosyntactic probing scores (e.g., ~77% accuracy for mT5-large) reflect genuine rule-based understanding or the exploitation of shallower corpus-induced patterns? Inferred from confusion matrix analysis and discussion of "deeper deficiencies".

## Limitations
- Dataset and code release lack persistent URL/DOI, blocking exact replication.
- Prompt templates and output parsing logic are underspecified in the Appendix; brittle parsing may have impacted "format accuracy" metrics.
- Observed architectural performance differences are confounded by model scale, tokenization, and pretraining data mix; no controlled ablations isolate architecture as the sole causal factor.

## Confidence
- **Framework Novelty & IAA Reliability**: High. Well-documented manually annotated lexicon with high inter-annotator agreement provides a solid, controlled evaluation foundation.
- **Empirical Performance Results**: Medium. Results are clearly stated, but confidence is reduced by unknown parsing implementation and potential data contamination risks.
- **Architectural Inductive Bias Explanation**: Low-Medium. Plausible and consistent with findings, but lacks controlled ablations holding scale/data constant; scale or training regimen may be primary drivers.
