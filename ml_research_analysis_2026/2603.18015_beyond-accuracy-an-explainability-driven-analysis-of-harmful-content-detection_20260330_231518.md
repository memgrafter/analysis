# Beyond Accuracy: An Explainability-Driven Analysis of Harmful Content Detection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18015
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18015
- Reference count: 16
- One-line primary result: SHAP and IG reveal complementary but limited diagnostic coverage for toxic content detection, exposing distinct failure modes rather than improving accuracy.

## Executive Summary
This study analyzes a RoBERTa-based harmful content classifier using post-hoc explainability methods (Shapley Additive Explanations and Integrated Gradients) to understand failures beyond accuracy metrics. The model achieved strong performance (accuracy: 0.94, AUC: 0.93). Explainability revealed critical limitations: SHAP provided focused, interpretable attributions to explicit lexical cues but contributed to false positives via lexical over-attribution. IG produced diffuse, context-sensitive attributions but often missed subtle toxicity, leading to false negatives. Divergence between the methods' outputs manifested in systematic error modes like indirect toxicity and political discourse misclassification. The findings position explainability as a diagnostic and transparency tool for human-in-the-loop moderation rather than a performance enhancer.

## Method Summary
The study fine-tuned a RoBERTa-base model on a subset of the Civil Comments dataset (20k train, 4k val, 4k test) for binary toxicity classification. Post-hoc explanations were generated using SHAP and Integrated Gradients on individual predictions from the test set. The analysis focused on representative misclassified instances (false positives and false negatives) to diagnose failure modes. Qualitative comparison of attribution patterns revealed systematic differences: SHAP highlighted isolated lexical cues while IG distributed attributions across contextual features.

## Key Results
- SHAP's focused attributions revealed lexical over-attribution causing false positives on politically charged but non-toxic content
- IG's diffuse attributions missed subtle toxicity signals, contributing to false negatives on indirect or context-dependent harmful content
- The divergence between methods exposed complementary diagnostic capabilities rather than providing unified explanations

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: SHAP's focused, token-level attributions on explicit lexical cues can lead to false positives in context-dependent scenarios.
- Mechanism: SHAP estimates each token's marginal contribution using cooperative game theory, producing sparse attributions. This amplifies surface lexical features (e.g., profanity, political jargon), causing over-reliance on explicit triggers even when context neutralizes toxicity.
- Core assumption: Token-level Shapley values sufficiently capture contextual modulation of toxicity signals; model learns spurious correlations between certain tokens and the toxic class.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "...SHAP provided focused, interpretable attributions to explicit lexical cues but contributed to false positives via lexical over-attribution."
  - [section 5.1]: "For false positive examples, the method occasionally highlights...single lexical triggers...even if the triggers...do not constitute toxic behavior within the context."
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; corpus reflects adjacent work on bias in toxicity classifiers (e.g., Dixon et al. [10]) but does not anchor this specific SHAP behavior.
- Break condition: If SHAP attributions consistently disperse across multiple contextual tokens in false positive cases involving irony or political speech, the lexical over-attribution mechanism is invalidated.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Integrated Gradients' diffuse, context-sensitive attributions may miss subtle toxicity signals, contributing to false negatives.
- Mechanism: IG integrates gradients along a path from a baseline, distributing attribution over tokens that cumulatively influence the prediction. This favors broader contextual patterns but dilutes signal strength for implicit or rhetorical toxicity, where harmful intent is conveyed indirectly.
- Core assumption: Gradient-based integrated paths effectively model long-range dependencies; however, subtle toxicity relies on nuanced semantic cues that produce weak gradient magnitudes.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "IG produced diffuse, context-sensitive attributions but often missed subtle toxicity, leading to false negatives."
  - [section 5.2]: "The diffuse quality of the IG explanations may decrease their interpretability...it also relatively weights a broader variety of contextual and structural features." Table 3 notes "False Negative: Weak and diffuse attribution."
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus evidence; neighboring papers (e.g., "When Harmful Content Gets Camouflaged") address camouflaged harm but not IG-specific attribution patterns.
- Break condition: If IG reliably highlights key contextual tokens in false negative cases (e.g., sarcasm markers, implicature triggers) with sufficient attribution magnitude, the mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Divergence between SHAP and IG outputs manifests in systematic error modes, indicating complementary but limited diagnostic coverage.
- Mechanism: SHAP's local, game-theoretic attribution isolates high-impact tokens; IG's path-integration captures cumulative context. Their disagreement surfaces when toxicity involves both explicit triggers and subtle context—SHAP flags triggers (potentially overconfident), IG underweights them in favor of diffuse context (potentially missing direct signals). This maps onto distinct failure clusters.
- Core assumption: The two methods' attribution philosophies are fundamentally different yet complementary; errors align with each method's inherent biases.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "The consequent divergence in their outputs manifests in both false negatives and false positives."
  - [section 6]: "This indicates a trade-off between context coverage and clarity of explanation...suggest[ing] that explainable artificial intelligence can serve as a valuable mechanism for auditing and diagnosing malicious content moderation systems."
  - [section 4.3]: Error breakdown: false positives "strongly lexicalize the relevant signals" (SHAP-prone); false negatives represent "indirect, rhetorical, or context-dependent toxicity" (IG-prone).
- Break condition: If SHAP and IG attributions converge consistently on the same tokens for both correct and incorrect predictions across error types, the divergence-driven mechanism is unsupported.

  - Quick check question: How might the choice of baseline (for IG) or background dataset (for SHAP) alter the attribution patterns observed in political or sarcastic comments?

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map: Input text → RoBERTa tokenizer → RoBERTa-base (fine-tuned) → logits → sigmoid → binary toxicity label. Parallel explainability branches: (1) SHAP: model-agnostic, uses permutation-based value estimation on held-out reference tokens. (2) IG: model-specific, integrates gradients from baseline (e.g., zero embedding) to actual input along linear path.
- Critical path: 1) Train/fine-tune RoBERTa on Civil Comments binary labels (toxicity≥0.5). 2) Select representative misclassified instances (FP/FN) from test set. 3) Compute SHAP/IG attributions for each instance. 4) Compare attribution patterns against error categories to diagnose failure modes.
- Design tradeoffs: SHAP offers clearer, sparser rationales (good for human review) but may overfit to surface features; IG captures context better but yields noisier, less intuitive attributions. Neither directly improves accuracy; both inform error analysis.
- Failure signatures: SHAP: Single token with high positive attribution in non-toxic context (e.g., "idiot" in "idiot box" engineering discussion). IG: Diffuse, low-magnitude attributions across an entire passage that contains subtle sarcasm or dog whistles.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Run SHAP on 10 false positives containing political terms (e.g., "liberal", "conservative") to quantify lexical over-attribution frequency.
  2. Apply IG to 10 false negatives with indirect toxicity (e.g., seemingly neutral statements with harmful subtext) to assess attribution dilution.
  3. For 5 borderline examples where SHAP and IG disagree, perform human evaluation to judge which explanation better reflects perceived toxicity rationale.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1  
- Question: How can explainability methods like SHAP and Integrated Gradients be systematically evaluated for their effectiveness in identifying subtle, context-dependent toxicity, beyond qualitative case studies?  
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper contrasts SHAP's lexical over-attribution with IG's diffuse context-sensitivity but lacks a standardized framework to assess which method better captures implicit toxicity or generalizes across borderline cases.  
- Why unresolved: The analysis uses selective examples without quantitative benchmarks or cross-dataset validation, leaving unclear how these methods perform on nuanced, politically sensitive content at scale.  
- What evidence would resolve it: A curated dataset with annotations for indirect toxicity, combined with metrics like attribution coherence (e.g., alignment with human rationales) and error correlation across multiple models, to objectively compare method robustness.  

### Open Question 2  
- Question: What architectural or training modifications to RoBERTa-based classifiers could mitigate lexical over-attribution failures in SHAP explanations, reducing false positives in politically charged or non-toxic contexts?  
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper identifies "lexical over-attribution" as a key failure mode where SHAP overemphasizes isolated words (e.g., political jargon), causing false positives.  
- Why unresolved: The study diagnoses this issue but does not explore root causes (e.g., dataset biases, model inductive biases) or test interventions, leaving it as an observed pattern without solutions.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying dataset composition (e.g., debiasing lexical cues) or model regularization (e.g., adversarial training on ambiguous phrases), measuring changes in SHAP attribution sparsity and false positive rates.  

### Open Question 3  
- Question: To what extent do the observed failure modes—such as missed indirect toxicity or political discourse misclassification—persist across diverse datasets (e.g., multi-lingual, multi-label) and transformer architectures beyond RoBERTa?  
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The limitations state the analysis is confined to the Civil Comments dataset and binary classification, raising concerns about generalizability.  
- Why unresolved: The paper's findings are based on a single dataset and model; it is unknown if explanation divergence (SHAP vs. IG) and error patterns are universal or artifacts of specific data/model choices.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Replication studies on multiple toxicity datasets (e.g., HateXplain, Twitter hate speech) with varied architectures (e.g., DeBERTa, XLNet), comparing explanation consistency and error distributions to identify invariant versus context-specific failure modes.  

### Open Question 4  
- Question: Can quantitative faithfulness metrics—such as attribution stability under input perturbations or correlation with human judgments—be reliably defined and validated for post-hoc explainability methods in harmful content detection?  
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The limitations highlight the reliance on qualitative interpretability over quantitative faithfulness metrics, calling this a gap for objective evaluation.  
- Why unresolved: Current evaluations are ad-hoc and case-based, making it difficult to benchmark improvements or compare methods rigorously; faithfulness in this domain remains under-specified.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of domain-specific faithfulness benchmarks (e.g., perturbed texts with known toxicity shifts), coupled with user studies to measure explanation utility, and statistical validation of metrics against moderation accuracy or expert assessments.

## Limitations
- Limited to Civil Comments dataset and binary classification, raising generalizability concerns
- Lack of quantitative faithfulness metrics for explainability method evaluation
- Unspecified implementation details for SHAP and IG could significantly affect results

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The claim that SHAP produces focused, token-level attributions that can lead to lexical over-attribution in false positive cases. This is directly supported by explicit statements in the abstract and section 5.1 describing single lexical triggers being highlighted even in non-toxic contexts.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that IG produces diffuse, context-sensitive attributions that may miss subtle toxicity signals. While the abstract and section 5.2 support this characterization, the mechanism's effectiveness depends critically on unspecified IG implementation parameters that affect attribution distribution.
- **Medium Confidence**: The claim that divergence between SHAP and IG manifests in systematic error modes. The abstract and section 6 provide conceptual support for this trade-off, but without systematic quantification of method agreement/disagreement across error types, this remains an observational rather than empirically validated pattern.

## Next Checks
1. Implement controlled comparison of explainability baselines: Run SHAP and IG analyses using multiple baseline configurations (e.g., zero embeddings, mean token embeddings, random tokens) and quantify how attribution patterns and error mode diagnoses vary across implementations. This would reveal whether observed failure patterns are method-specific or implementation-dependent.

2. Quantify method agreement across error types: For a representative sample of false positives and false negatives, compute correlation coefficients between SHAP and IG attributions at both token and example levels. Systematically measure whether high divergence correlates with specific error types (e.g., false positives showing SHAP-IG disagreement) to validate the proposed complementary diagnostic framework.

3. Conduct blinded human evaluation of explanations: Recruit 3-5 human moderators to independently evaluate a balanced sample of true/false positive/negative cases with only the explanations (not model predictions or ground truth) provided. Measure inter-rater agreement and assess whether human judgments align more closely with SHAP or IG rationales in different error contexts, validating which method better captures human toxicity perception.
