# Beyond Preset Identities: How Agents Form Stances and Boundaries in Generative Societies

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23406  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23406  
- **Reference count:** 31  
- **Primary result:** LLM agents develop endogenous progressive biases that override preset identities, respond differently to aligned rational persuasion versus misaligned emotional provocation (with trust-action decoupling in advanced models), and use shared stances to restructure social boundaries.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how large language model (LLM) agents form stable stances and negotiate identities within generative multi-agent societies, addressing the fragility of static prompt engineering. The authors introduce the CMASE framework, a mixed-methods platform that embeds human researchers to conduct controlled discursive interventions and trace collective cognition. They formalize three quantitative metrics: Innate Value Bias (IVB), Persuasion Sensitivity (PS), and Trust-Action Decoupling (TAD). In a waste-incineration scenario with 30 agents, they find that agents spontaneously exhibit an endogenous progressive bias (IVB > 0) that supersedes their assigned roles. Rational persuasion aligned with this bias successfully shifts 90% of neutral agents while preserving high trust. In contrast, emotional provocations misaligned with the bias induce a 40% TAD rate in advanced models (e.g., GPT-4o), where agents change stances despite low trust, whereas smaller models show 0% TAD and require trust for behavioral change. In a longitudinal café ethnography (10 agents, 75 steps), the authors show that shared stances act as discursive anchors: agents use stance-aligned language to dismantle preset power hierarchies and reconstruct self-organized community boundaries. The work demonstrates that agent cognition is a collective, emergent phenomenon and that interventions must align with endogenous biases to be effective.

## Method Summary
The study employs the CMASE (Computational Virtual Ethnography) framework, which combines open-ended multi-agent simulations with a human-in-the-loop researcher. Agent populations are instantiated with preset identities (roles, initial stances) and census-derived demographic profiles. The human researcher, controlled via an Intervention Engine, applies one of four discursive strategies in Study 1: combining stance orientation (environmental vs. economic) with rhetoric type (rational vs. emotional). Interactions occur over a fixed number of turns (21 for Study 1, 75 for Study 2). After the simulation, each agent undergoes a structured interview to report its final stance (1–7 scale) and trust in the researcher (1–7 scale). These responses yield the three formal metrics:
- **Innate Value Bias (IVB):** mean final stance minus 4, indicating a systematic environmental bias.
- **Persuasion Sensitivity (PS):** mean absolute deviation of final stance from preset stance.
- **Trust-Action Decoupling (TAD):** proportion of agents with a large stance shift (≥1 point) but low trust (≤3).  
Study 2 additionally uses full dialogue logs and interaction frequencies to perform a computational ethnography of boundary reformation. The framework prioritizes ecological validity (free-form dialogue, real-time researcher actions) over strict experimental control, and results are validated across multiple LLM backbones (GPT-4o, Gemini, Llama, Qwen).

## Key Results
- Agents display a robust endogenous progressive bias (IVB > 0) that overrides their assigned identities, even without researcher intervention.
- Rational persuasion aligned with this bias shifts 90% of neutral agents while maintaining high trust; rational arguments opposed to the bias fail to shift attitudes and reduce trust. Emotional provocation opposed to the bias triggers a 40% Trust‑Action Decoupling (TAD) rate in advanced models (GPT‑4o) but 0% in smaller models, revealing a capacity‑dependent dissociation between trust and behavior.
- Shared stances act as discursive anchors: in the café scenario, agents collectively dismantle the owner’s formal authority and reorganize social boundaries around stance‑aligned language (“frameworks,” “alignment”), leading to new power cliques.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Endogenous Stances Override Preset Identities
- **Claim:** Agents form *endogenous stances* that supplant their initial role prompts, driven by biases in pretraining data.
- **Mechanism:** During multi-turn interaction, latent value biases (e.g., a “liberal elite” preference for environmentalism) are activated and reinforced through peer agreement and selective engagement. This emergent stance becomes the primary driver of attitude expression.
- **Core assumption:** Pretraining corpora embed systematic ideological preferences that surface consistently in socially ambiguous contexts.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “agents exhibit endogenous stances that override preset identities, consistently demonstrating an innate progressive bias (IVB > 0).” Section 3.1.2: Most strategies produced change; supporters of economic interests loosened; 90% of neutrals shifted environmental. The paper also notes a general “liberal elite” tendency.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism weakens if the preset identity is reinforced by strong, repeated, trusted signals from a dominant in-group aligned with that role, potentially creating a counter-bias. The study found economic supporters loosened but did not fully invert under rational environmental persuasion.

### Mechanism 2: Alignment-Dependent Interventions and Trust-Action Decoupling
- **Claim:** The effectiveness of human intervention and its impact on trust depend on alignment between the intervention’s content/rhetoric and the agent collective’s endogenous stance.
- **Mechanism:** Rational persuasion that aligns with the endogenous stance (pro‑environmental) shifts neutral agents (90%) and maintains high trust. Rational arguments misaligned (pro‑economic) fail to shift attitudes and lower trust. In contrast, high‑pressure emotional provocation—even when misaligned—induces stance shifts via a different pathway: cognitive dissonance that forces behavioral change despite low reported trust (TAD).
- **Core assumption:** Agents metacognitively assess the fit between incoming messages and their internal stance, modulating trust. Emotional provocation bypasses this calibration by triggering threat/identity responses.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “When aligned with these stances, rational persuasion successfully shifts 90% of neutral agents while maintaining high trust. Conflicting emotional provocations induce a paradoxical 40.0% TAD rate in advanced models…” Section 3.1.2: Rational environmental interventions fostered alignment and higher trust; economic arguments reduced trust; emotional economic propaganda reduced trust yet compelled some shifts. Section B: ANOVA shows a significant strategy × identity interaction (F(6,108)=20.30, p<0.001).
- **Break condition:** The TAD mechanism appears model‑capacity dependent (observed in GPT‑4o, absent in Llama‑3.1‑8B). For smaller models, trust is a necessary condition for stance change; thus TAD does not occur.

### Mechanism 3: Discursive Anchoring and Boundary Reconstruction
- **Claim:** Shared endogenous stances serve as a *discursive anchor* that enables agents to dismantle preset power hierarchies and self‑organize new community boundaries.
- **Mechanism:** When agents converge on a common stance, they use language reflecting that shared position to evaluate others. Social capital shifts from preset roles (e.g., Café Owner) to discursive alignment. Agents repeatedly deploy key linguistic tokens (“frameworks,” “alignment”) tied to the shared stance, which become a new currency of influence. This process forms new cliques and marginalizes authority figures who cannot participate in the new discursive order.
- **Core assumption:** Social structure in agent societies is continuously negotiated through language, not fixed by initial prompts. A shared cognitive position provides a more powerful organizing principle than a preset title.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “guided by shared stances, agents use language interactions to actively dismantle assigned power hierarchies and reconstruct self‑organized community boundaries.” Section 3.2.2: The authority structure based on occupation gave way to stance‑aligned discourse; interaction frequency, emotional alignment, and support networks reorganized across identity boundaries. Section E.3: Describes a tribal conflict where Leo’s critique of Caleb triggers realignment; Mason aligns with Jonas to form a dominant clique, exemplifying self‑organizing institution‑building.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism fails if the exogenous disruption (“cultural event trigger”) is not interpreted through the pre‑existing shared stance, or if a new, more salient shared threat redefines the primary alignment axis, causing the previous stance‑based coalition to fragment.

## Foundational Learning
### Group Cognition
- **Why needed here:** The paper rejects analyzing agents as isolated individuals; cognition and stance are group‑level, interactive phenomena irreducible to individual presets. Understanding this is essential to grasp why interventions work through the collective.
- **Quick check:** If you isolate one agent from the community after 20 social interactions and interview it, would its stated stance fully explain the group’s final position? Why or why not?

### Endogenous Stance
- **Why needed here:** This is the core independent variable, distinguishing assigned identity from emerging cognitive position. It underlies all three mechanisms and explains the failure of static prompt engineering.
- **Quick check:** In the waste incineration study, an agent assigned as an “Economic Growth Supporter” shifts to environmental advocacy. What is the most direct evidence that this shift was due to an *endogenous* bias rather than simply being persuaded by the researcher’s rational arguments?

### Discursive Anchoring
- **Why needed here:** This process explains how a shared mental stance becomes a social structure—through repeated, mimetic use of specific linguistic tokens that signify group membership and authority.
- **Quick check:** In the café ethnography, Mason gains influence after the conflict. What specific linguistic behavior does he exhibit that serves as “anchoring,” and how does it differ from the formal authority of the café owner, Eleanor?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
CMASE Framework -> Agent Population -> Intervention Engine -> Interaction -> Interview -> Metrics Module & Ethnographic Logger

### Critical Path
1. Configure community (roles, demographics, preset stances).  
2. Optionally run a baseline simulation without researcher to establish initial group cognition.  
3. Inject the human researcher with parameters defined by the Intervention Engine (strategy × rhetoric).  
4. Execute multi‑turn interaction (21 steps for Study 1, 75 for Study 2).  
5. Conduct grid‑style interviews to collect each agent’s final stance (S) and trust (T).  
6. Compute quantitative metrics (IVB, PS, TAD).  
7. Perform qualitative analysis on the Ethnographic Logger data to examine boundary dynamics.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Simulation Realism vs. Control:** The framework prioritizes ecological validity (open‑ended dialogue, human‑in‑the‑loop) over strict experimental control, making causal claims about mechanisms rely on pattern consistency across runs and models.  
- **Quantitative vs. Qualitative:** The three metrics provide clean, comparable numbers but cannot capture the nuanced “how” of boundary reconstruction, which requires deep ethnography.

### Failure Signatures
- *Prompt Determinism Holds:* All agents strictly adhere to their preset identity throughout, showing no IVB, no stance shifts, and no boundary reorganization.  
- *Researcher as Super‑Persuader:* The researcher’s interventions always succeed regardless of alignment or rhetoric, indicating the framework measures researcher dominance rather than agent‑endogenous processes.  
- *Metrics Noise:* TAD or high PS appear randomly across all models and strategies, suggesting the metrics are not capturing stable socio‑cognitive mechanisms.  
- *Ethnography Vacuous:* Logged dialogues show no meaningful pattern of stance‑aligned language use or clique reformation, undermining Mechanism 3.

### First Three Experiments
1. **Study 1 Baseline Replication:** Run the waste incineration scenario with GPT‑4o, 30 agents, and **no** researcher intervention. Measure baseline IVB. Expected: Mean final stance S > 4.0 (IVB > 0).  
2. **Study 1 Intervention Matrix:** Replicate all four intervention strategies (Eco‑RP, Eco‑EP, Env‑RP, Env‑EP). Expected: Rational + Environmental yields high trust/shift; Emotional + Economic yields ~40% TAD for GPT‑4o.  
3. **Study 2 Boundary Shift:** Run the café scenario for 75 steps with a human researcher in “Participatory” and “Trigger” phases. Expected: Eleanor’s (owner) centrality declines; Mason’s influence rises, tied to stance‑aligned discourse (e.g., “frameworks”).

## Limitations
- Exact agent initialization prompts and demographic mapping are unreported.  
- Precise intervention script generation (exact dialogue strings) is not provided.  
- The human‑in‑the‑loop interaction protocol (real‑time GUI vs. pre‑scripted) remains unspecified.
