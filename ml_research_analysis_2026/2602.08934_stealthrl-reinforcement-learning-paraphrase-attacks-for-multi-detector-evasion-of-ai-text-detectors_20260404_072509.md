# StealthRL: Reinforcement Learning Paraphrase Attacks for Multi-Detector Evasion of AI-Text Detectors

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.08934
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08934
*   **Reference Count:** 30
*   **Primary Result:** A reinforcement learning paraphrase attack achieves a 97.6% attack success rate against multiple AI-text detectors at a security-critical 1% false positive rate, reducing mean AUROC from 0.79 to 0.43.

## Executive Summary
StealthRL demonstrates that state-of-the-art AI-text detectors are highly vulnerable to adaptive adversarial paraphrasing. By training a policy network (Qwen3-4B) via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) against an ensemble of detectors, the attack learns to perturb surface-level statistical cues—such as token distributions and perplexity patterns—while preserving semantic meaning. Evaluated on a large benchmark (MAGE) at the stringent 1% false positive rate, the attack nearly evades all targeted detectors and transfers effectively to held-out architectures. The work reveals a fundamental brittleness in current detection methods and quantifies a clear trade-off: maximal evasion success comes at a measurable cost to output quality.

## Method Summary
StealthRL frames paraphrase-based evasion as a reinforcement learning problem. A policy network, based on Qwen3-4B-Instruct and fine-tuned with LoRA, generates paraphrases. The reward function aggregates two signals: (1) a **detector evasion reward** (`R_det = 1 - ensemble_score`) that incentivizes lowering the confidence of a multi-detector ensemble (RoBERTa and Fast-DetectGPT during training), and (2) a **semantic preservation reward** (`R_sem`) based on the cosine similarity of E5 embeddings between the original and paraphrased text. The policy is optimized using GRPO, which samples multiple paraphrase candidates per input, computes group-relative advantages from the composite reward, and updates the LoRA weights with a KL penalty (`λ=0.05`) to prevent excessive divergence from the reference model. Training occurs on 10k AI-generated texts from the MAGE benchmark. Evaluation is performed at a fixed 1% false positive rate threshold, calibrated on human text, using metrics like True Positive Rate (TPR), Attack Success Rate (ASR), and AUROC.

## Key Results
*   **Near-Perfect Evasion:** At the 1% FPR operating point, StealthRL achieves a mean TPR of **0.024** and an attack success rate (ASR) of **97.6%** across the in-training ensemble.
*   **Cross-Architecture Transfer:** The attack effectively evades two held-out detectors (Binoculars TPR=0.002, MAGE TPR=0.089), indicating shared vulnerabilities beyond the specific models seen during training.
*   **Quantifiable Trade-off:** High evasion success is achieved at the cost of semantic fidelity; paraphrases have lower E5 similarity (0.901) and LLM-judged quality scores compared to simpler paraphrasing baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Targeting Brittle Statistical Correlates
The attack succeeds because current detectors rely on shallow, surface-level statistical cues (e.g., token frequency, perplexity, embedding geometry) rather than robust semantic understanding. The RL policy learns to manipulate these cues to push detector confidence scores below the decision threshold (the 1% FPR cutoff), as evidenced by score distributions shifting leftwards. This brittleness is a core assumption validated by the catastrophic drop in TPR despite only moderate AUROC degradation. A break condition would be detectors retrained on features invariant to lexical/syntactic reformulation (e.g., discourse structures) or the use of embedded watermarks.

### Mechanism 2: Cross-Architecture Transfer via Shared Vulnerabilities
Training against a diverse detector ensemble (a fine-tuned classifier and a zero-shot statistical method) induces the policy to learn attack strategies that generalize to unseen detector architectures (Binoculars, MAGE). This transfer occurs because all detector families exploited here rely on overlapping, non-robust feature sets disrupted by paraphrasing. The composite reward's design—prioritizing evasion of one detector family (RoBERTa weight 0.6)—still yields transfer, suggesting a common underlying weakness. Transfer may fail if future detectors rely on fundamentally orthogonal signals (e.g., watermark detection plus deep semantic inconsistency).

### Mechanism 3: Explicit Evasion-Quality Trade-off
The composite reward (`R = α·R_det + β·R_sem`) explicitly balances evasion (`α=1.0`) against semantic fidelity (`β=0.1`), with a KL penalty (`λ=0.05`) to constrain degeneration. This creates a Pareto frontier; maximizing attack success (97.6% ASR) directly correlates with a quantifiable loss in quality (E5 similarity 0.901 vs. 0.974 for a simple paraphrase baseline). The trade-off hinges on the assumption that embedding cosine similarity sufficiently operationalizes semantic preservation. The trade-off may be mischaracterized if E5 is poorly correlated with human judgment or if `β` is set sub-optimally.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Reinforcement Learning from Environmental Feedback (GRPO)**
    *   *Why needed:* GRPO optimizes the paraphrase policy using reward signals from detector scores and semantic similarity, not human preferences. Understanding group-relative advantage normalization and KL penalties is key to interpreting policy convergence.
    *   *Quick check:* How does subtracting the group mean reward in GRPO affect exploration vs. exploitation compared to standard PPO, and why is this important for learning a robust evasion policy?
*   **Concept: Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (LoRA)**
    *   *Why needed:* The 4B-parameter policy is fine-tuned with low-rank adapters (rank 32), enabling efficient RL updates. This constraint influences the policy's capacity to learn novel syntactic structures.
    *   *Quick check:* If LoRA rank were increased to 256, what might change about the evasion-quality tradeoff, and what computational tradeoffs would emerge?
*   **Concept: Security Evaluation at Operating Points (TPR@1%FPR)**
    *   *Why needed:* Detector security is assessed at a strict 1% false positive rate—a realistic deployment constraint. AUROC can be misleading (e.g., high AUROC but near-zero TPR@1%FPR).
    *   *Quick check:* Why might a detector with AUROC 0.95 still be catastrophically insecure at the 1% FPR operating point when faced with an adaptive adversary?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Original AI Text -> Policy Network (π_θ: Qwen3-4B + LoRA) -> Paraphrase -> Detector Ensemble (d_k: RoBERTa, Fast-DetectGPT) & E5 Model -> Reward Calculator (R = α·R_det + β·R_sem + KL) -> GRPO Optimizer -> Updated LoRA Weights
*   **Critical path:** Training: For each batch of AI texts, sample `G=8` paraphrases per text from policy, query detectors, compute composite rewards, compute group-relative advantages, apply clipped policy gradient update with KL penalty on LoRA weights. Inference: Single-pass generation with temperature=1.0, top-p=0.9.
*   **Design tradeoffs:*
    *   *Evasion vs. Fidelity:* High `α`, low `β` maximizes ASR but degrades quality (Table 1). Increasing `β` may reduce ASR and potentially transfer.
    *   *Single-shot vs. Multi-candidate:* StealthRL generates one paraphrase at test time (realistic black-box attack) vs. reranking multiple candidates; may miss higher-quality but still evasive outputs.
    *   *Ensemble diversity vs. Complexity:* Two detectors reduce compute but may not capture the full spectrum of exploitable cues; a larger ensemble could improve transfer robustness.
*   **Failure signatures:*
    1.  **Low ASR (<0.8):** Policy failing to minimize detector scores—check reward scaling, learning rate, or KL coefficient.
    2.  **High Perplexity / Low Quality:** `β` too low or KL penalty insufficient—inspect output for grammar/meaning drift.
    3.  **Poor Transfer (<0.5 TPR on held-out):** Ensemble not diverse enough—consider adding a detector family with a different scoring mechanism.
    4.  **Training instability:** GRPO with small group size `G` or batch size may cause high variance—increase `G` or batch size, or inspect advantage normalization.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Reward Sanity Check:** With a fixed policy, manually perturb a sample (e.g., synonym swap). Verify `R_det` decreases while `R_sem` remains near 1.0.
    2.  **Baseline Evasion Upper Bound:** Run a simple paraphrasing baseline (M1) on a held-out subset. Confirm its ASR is moderate (~0.7), establishing the learning potential for RL.
    3.  **Single-Detector Overfit Control:** Train for 1 epoch *only* against RoBERTa. Evaluate on RoBERTa and held-out Binoculars. Expect high evasion on RoBERTa but low transfer to Binoculars, confirming the need for multi-detector training for transfer.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** Does adversarial training on StealthRL examples improve detector robustness at 1% FPR?
    *   *Basis in paper:* Future work: "Adversarial training: Incorporating adversarial examples into detector training to improve robustness against adaptive attacks."
    *   *Why unresolved:* The paper only evaluates attacks; no defensive adversarial training experiments are conducted.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Fine-tune detectors on a dataset containing StealthRL-generated paraphrases, then evaluate TPR@1%FPR on fresh attack samples, comparing to baseline detectors.
*   **Open Question 2:** What deeper linguistic features (beyond surface statistics) are robust to paraphrasing and can discriminate AI-generated text?
    *   *Basis in paper:* Future work: "Semantic-aware detectors: Developing detection methods that rely on deeper linguistic features rather than surface-level statistical cues."
    *   *Why unresolved:* Current detectors rely on token-level distributions and perplexity; deeper features (e.g., syntax, discourse) remain unexplored.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Extract linguistic features (syntactic parse trees, discourse markers) from human vs. AI text, test which features remain stable under paraphrasing, and train classifiers using these features.
*   **Open Question 3:** Can theoretical bounds be established for detection accuracy under bounded semantic-preserving transformations?
    *   *Basis in paper:* Future work: "Provable robustness: Establishing theoretical guarantees on detector robustness under bounded perturbations."
    *   *Why unresolved:* The paper provides empirical results but no formal analysis linking paraphrasing to fundamental detectability limits.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Develop a formal model of semantic-preserving attacks as a transformation class, then prove upper or lower bounds on detection accuracy as a function of language model capability.
*   **Open Question 4:** Can constrained reinforcement learning (e.g., CPO) improve StealthRL's quality without sacrificing attack success?
    *   *Basis in paper:* Future work: "Multi-objective optimization: Improving StealthRL’s quality preservation through constrained RL or Pareto-optimal training."
    *   *Why unresolved:* StealthRL uses a linear weighted sum reward; constrained methods might better balance semantic preservation and evasion.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Re-train StealthRL using a constraint policy optimization (CPO) approach with a minimum similarity threshold (e.g., E5 > 0.95), then compare attack success rate and quality metrics to the original.
*   **Open Question 5:** How robust are watermark-based detection methods to adaptive paraphrasing attacks that preserve semantics?
    *   *Basis in paper:* Limitation: "We do not evaluate against watermark-based detectors... Evaluating StealthRL against watermarked text is an important direction."
    *   *Why unresolved:* Watermark detection is orthogonal to post-hoc detectors; it is unclear whether paraphrasing can remove or alter embedded watermarks while preserving meaning.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Apply StealthRL to AI-generated watermarked text (using a standard watermarking scheme), measure both the watermark detection rate and the semantic fidelity of the paraphrases.

## Limitations
*   The exact GRPO implementation and precise detector score normalization/scaling are unspecified, requiring the official codebase for faithful replication.
*   The correlation between the E5 embedding similarity metric and true human-judged semantic fidelity is assumed but not directly validated in the paper.
*   The claim of "shared fundamental vulnerability" is based on transfer to a limited set of detector families; generalization to all possible detector architectures is not proven.

## Confidence
*   **Evasion Effectiveness:** High. Results are numerically precise on a large, standard benchmark with a clear security operating point (1% FPR).
*   **Cross-Architecture Transfer:** Medium. Transfer to two held-out detectors is demonstrated, but the claim is based on a limited set of detector families.
*   **Evasion-Quality Trade-off:** Medium. The trade-off is quantified via E5 similarity and an LLM judge, but the judge's model is not a standard benchmark and E5's correlation with human judgment remains an assumption.
