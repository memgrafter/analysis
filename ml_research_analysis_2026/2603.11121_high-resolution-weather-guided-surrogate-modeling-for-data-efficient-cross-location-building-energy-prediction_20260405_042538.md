# High-resolution weather-guided surrogate modeling for data-efficient cross-location building energy prediction

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.11121
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11121
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: A weekly weather-informed surrogate model trained on a single location generalizes to other locations within the same climate zone and shows only minimal degradation across different climate zones, outperforming traditional annual-level surrogates.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the lack of reusability in building energy surrogate models, which are typically location-specific and require extensive multi-location simulation data to generalize. The authors propose a high-resolution, weekly weather-informed surrogate modeling approach that encodes hourly weather sequences using temporal deep learning models (TCN, Transformer, or a pre-trained autoencoder) and combines them with building design variables to predict weekly energy consumption. When trained on data from just one or two strategically selected locations, the model maintains high predictive accuracy across diverse climate zones, demonstrating that shared short-term weather patterns enable data-efficient, scalable building energy prediction for design optimization.

## Method Summary
The study generates simulation data for a medium office prototype across ten Canadian cities (five climate zones, two cities per zone) using Latin Hypercube Sampling over 13 design parameters. EnergyPlus simulations produce hourly energy data aggregated to weekly totals. The surrogate model takes two inputs: a 168-hour weekly weather sequence (temperature, humidity, solar, etc.) and a 13-dimensional building design vector. Three encoder variants process the weather: a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN), a Transformer, or a pre-trained convolutional autoencoder (decoupled). The encoder output (embedding) is concatenated with the design vector and passed through a feedforward neural network to predict weekly energy. Models are trained with mean squared error loss and evaluated using weekly SMAPE, RMSE, and Pearson correlation. Key innovations include using high-resolution weekly weather instead of annual aggregates and strategic multi-location training to improve cross-zone generalization.

## Key Results
- The Transformer-based joint model achieves low weekly SMAPE (6–10%) when trained on a single location and tested on other cities within the same climate zone, with only moderate increases across zones.
- Training on two strategically selected locations (e.g., Toronto + Edmonton) significantly improves generalization to distant zones compared to single-location training.
- The weekly-resolution surrogate consistently outperforms traditional annual-level surrogates in cross-location scenarios, confirming that short-term weather patterns are more transferable than annual climate summaries.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: High-Resolution Weather Representation
Using weekly-averaged weather sequences (168 hours) instead of annual aggregates exposes the model to higher variance (5×) and overlapping distributions across climates (cosine similarity 0.64 vs. 0.52). This captures recurring short-term weather-energy patterns (e.g., heatwaves, cold spells) that are common across regions, while annual averages are too low-variance and location-distinct. Thus, the model learns transferable dynamics rather than location-specific summaries.  
*Evidence*: Variance and cosine similarity comparisons across ten Canadian cities (section 3.1); cross-location heatmaps show clearer intra-zone diagonals for weekly models (Figs. 5–7).  
*Break condition*: Fails if weekly patterns are not shared (e.g., tropics vs. arctic); degradation observed when training on extreme zones and testing on dissimilar ones.

### Mechanism 2: Temporal Encoding for Weather Embeddings
Deep temporal encoders (TCN, Transformer) trained on hourly sequences learn compact embeddings that distill climate-invariant features from high-variance weekly data. Joint training aligns the embedding with the energy prediction task, forcing the encoder to extract predictive weather features while ignoring location-specific noise. The Transformer’s self-attention captures complex multi-scale dependencies (e.g., seasonal transitions) better than TCNs, leading to superior cross-zone accuracy.  
*Evidence*: Transformer consistently yields lowest SMAPE and highest Pearson *r* in single-location tests (section 4.3); heatmaps (Fig. 6) show stronger intra-zone diagonals than TCN (Fig. 5) or Autoencoder (Fig. 7).  
*Break condition*: Insufficient encoder capacity or training data lacking key weather regimes (e.g., only heating-dominated climates) leads to embeddings that fail under unseen conditions.

### Mechanism 3: Strategic Training Location Selection
Training on two or three climatically diverse locations provides a balanced distribution of weather-energy interactions (heating, cooling, transition), mitigating the "regime mismatch" problem. For example, a model trained only on a subarctic city sees only heating-dominated profiles; adding a moderate climate like Toronto exposes it to mixed dynamics, improving robustness to unseen climates.  
*Evidence*: Paired-location training (e.g., Toronto+Edmonton) yields greener (lower error) heatmap regions across more test locations than single-location training (Figs. 9 vs. 5–7). Three-location mixes (Zone 1+3+5) achieve strong cross-zone performance (Fig. 11).  
*Break condition*: Omission of critical weather regimes (e.g., humid subtropical) or limited model capacity to integrate diverse patterns without overfitting.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Time-series encoding and sequence-to-embedding mapping**  
*Why needed*: The model ingests 168-hour weather sequences; raw sequences are high-dimensional and noisy. Encoders must compress them into low-dimensional embeddings that retain predictive features while discarding irrelevance.  
*Quick check*: If embedding dimension is too high, what happens? → May overfit to training location specifics; increases compute.

**Concept: Transferability and domain shift in surrogate modeling**  
*Why needed*: The goal is a surrogate that works for unseen weather files (domains) without retraining, requiring learning invariant weather-energy relationships rather than spurious correlations.  
*Quick check*: Would a model trained on Vancouver (Zone 1, mild) generalize to Winnipeg (Zone 4, extreme continental)? → Unlikely; training lacks exposure to extreme cold, so embeddings may not capture heating-dominated dynamics adequately.

**Concept: Surrogate model evaluation for design optimization**  
*Why needed*: Surrogates replace EnergyPlus in iterative design loops; errors must not distort design ranking. Weekly SMAPE is primary; annual aggregation may hide weekly failure modes critical for optimization.  
*Quick check*: Why report Pearson correlation alongside SMAPE? → SMAPE measures magnitude error; Pearson *r* assesses if the model captures the temporal pattern (seasonal shape) of energy use, which is vital for optimization even if magnitude is slightly off.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Weekly weather sequence *W* (168×*d<sub>w</sub>*) and building design vector *B* (*d<sub>b</sub>*) → Weather Encoder *φ<sub>θ</sub>* (TCN/Transformer/Autoencoder) → Encoding *H* (embedding) → Concatenation with *B* → Feedforward Neural Network *f<sub>θ</sub>* → Predicted weekly energy *Ẽ*.

### Critical path
1. Generate simulation data: Latin Hypercube Sampling over 13 design parameters → EnergyPlus runs → 52 weeks × 50 designs per location.
2. Preprocess: Min-max normalize *B* using predefined ranges; min-max normalize *W* using scaler from training location only (no clipping).
3. Train encoder (if autoencoder approach) on concatenated weekly *W* from multiple locations (unsupervised reconstruction loss).
4. Train full model (joint) or just FFNN (decoupled) on (*W*, *B*) → *E* pairs from training location(s).
5. Evaluate on test locations: feed their *W* (unseen) + *B* → *Ẽ*; compute SMAPE/RMSE/Pearson *r* vs. simulation *E*.

### Design tradeoffs
- Joint training (TCN/Transformer) yields higher accuracy but requires retraining encoder per training set; decoupled autoencoder allows encoder reuse but sacrifices task-specificity (higher SMAPE).
- Transformer > TCN > Autoencoder in cross-zone accuracy due to better long-range dependency capture, but Transformers are more compute-intensive.
- Single-location training is cheapest but performs poorly on climatically distant zones; paired/mixed-zone training improves generalization but multiplies simulation cost.

### Failure signatures
- High weekly SMAPE (>15–20%) on intra-zone test: indicates model cannot capture inter-annual variability; likely underfitting or poor hyperparameters.
- Gradual SMAPE increase with climatic distance expected, but sharp jumps suggest regime mismatch (e.g., training on Zone 1, testing on Zone 4).
- Low Pearson *r* (<0.7) despite moderate SMAPE: model predicts wrong seasonal shape (e.g., fails to capture summer peak), fatal for optimization.
- Autoencoder approach: consistently higher SMAPE than joint models; acceptable only if compute for joint training is prohibitive.

### First 3 experiments
1. Baseline replication: Train TCN joint model on Toronto only; evaluate on London (same Zone 2). Expect SMAPE ~6–10%. If higher, check data preprocessing (normalization) and TCN depth.
2. Cross-zone stress test: Train Transformer on Vancouver (Zone 1); test on Winnipeg (Zone 4). Expect higher SMAPE (~15–25%). If lower, verify weather scaling didn't clip extremes.
3. Diversity benefit: Train Transformer on Toronto+Edmonton; test on Whitehorse (Zone 5). Compare SMAPE to single-location Toronto training. Expect improvement (e.g., ~9% vs. ~15%). If none, training pair may be too climatically similar.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalizability across building typologies**: The study uses only a medium office prototype; it is unknown whether the learned weather-energy relationships transfer to residential, high-rise, or industrial buildings.
2. **Adaptation to climate change**: The model is trained on historical TMY data; it may degrade under future climate projections without an adaptation strategy.
3. **Multi-objective prediction**: The surrogate predicts only total weekly energy; adding other objectives (peak demand, thermal comfort) may affect cross-location generalization.
4. **Systematic training location selection**: The paper shows benefits of two or three locations but provides no generalizable method to choose a minimal set that maximizes performance across all target zones.

## Limitations
- Geographic scope limited to 10 Canadian cities; transferability to other continents (tropics, arid) is untested.
- Evaluation based on a single building prototype and 13 design variables; may not generalize to complex building types.
- Hyperparameter details incomplete; results may be sensitive to tuning.

## Confidence
- **Weekly weather generalization**: Medium-High (in-domain empirical support, but out-of-distribution untested).
- **Temporal encoder efficacy**: Medium-High (Transformer consistently best, yet architecture trade-offs not fully explored).
- **Strategic location selection**: Medium (two-location gains shown, but scalability uncertain).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-continental test**: Train on Canadian cities; test on tropical (Singapore) and arid (Phoenix) locations.
2. **Optimization impact**: Run a design optimization loop with the surrogate; compare identified optimal design to EnergyPlus ground truth.
3. **Embedding dimension sweep**: Vary encoder output size (e.g., 16–256 dims) and measure cross-location SMAPE and training time to identify capacity thresholds.
