# Identification of Bivariate Causal Directionality Based on Anticipated Asymmetric Geometries

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26024
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26024
- **Reference count:** 4
- **Primary result:** Anticipated Asymmetric Geometries (AAG) with Pearson correlation achieves 76.5% accuracy on 102 real-world pairs and 77.9% on the standard 95-pair benchmark, outperforming additive noise models and a Monotonicity Index method.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Anticipated Asymmetric Geometries (AAG) to infer bivariate causal direction. The method fits a joint density to normalized data, then for each candidate cause-effect direction, encodes the conditional distribution of the hypothesized effect into a dual response (mean and standard deviation) and reconstructs an "anticipated" joint distribution assuming normality. The direction whose anticipated distribution deviates least from the original joint—measured by a chosen metric—is selected as the true causal direction. Through extensive design-of-experiment tuning on 102 real-world pairs, AAG with Pearson correlation or cosine similarity achieves 76.5% accuracy, and 77.9% on the canonical 95-pair subset. A screening decision tree further boosts accuracy to 80% for the remaining pairs by filtering indecisive cases. The work provides both a novel geometric approach and a robust benchmark for bivariate causal discovery.

## Method Summary
AAG operates on normalized bivariate data. First, a 2D kernel density estimate (KDE) of the joint distribution φ(X,Y) is fitted on a k×k grid. For a hypothesized direction (e.g., X→Y), the conditional distribution φ(Y|X) is extracted from the joint grid. This conditional is encoded into a dual response: the mean μ_Y(X_i) and standard deviation σ_Y(X_i) for each X-bin. An anticipated joint distribution φ̃(X,Y) is then reconstructed by placing a normal distribution N(μ_Y(X_i), σ_Y(X_i)) at each X-bin and re-normalizing. A deviation metric M (e.g., Pearson correlation, K-L divergence) between the original φ and anticipated φ̃ quantifies information loss. Steps are repeated for the opposite direction (Y→X). The direction with the smaller deviation (or larger correlation) is chosen as the true cause. Hyperparameters (grid size k, KDE bandwidth bw_par, contour level m, regularization thresholds) are tuned via full factorial design of experiments on the evaluation set. The paper also proposes a Monotonicity Index method that counts gradient sign changes along axes of the joint density, with lower index indicating the cause→effect direction.

## Key Results
- AAG with Pearson correlation achieves 76.5% accuracy on 102 real-world cause-effect pairs and 77.9% on the standard 95-pair benchmark (ids <100), with AUC ≈ 0.787.
- Pearson and cosine similarity metrics consistently rank highest across evaluated metrics; mutual information and entropy perform worst (~62% accuracy).
- A screening decision tree applied to indecisive cases boosts accuracy to 80% on the remaining 99 pairs by identifying low mutual information (threshold < 0.125) pairs.
- AAG significantly outperforms Additive Noise Models (ANMs, 63±10%) and the Monotonicity Index (best 70.6%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Information Loss via Dual-Response Encoding
- **Claim:** Causal direction can be inferred by measuring information loss when encoding the conditional distribution of a candidate effect into a dual-response (mean, standard deviation) under an assumed direction, then decoding it back to a joint distribution.
- **Mechanism:** For each direction hypothesis, the method fits a 2D KDE joint φ(X,Y), extracts the conditional (e.g., φ(Y|X)), and encodes it into mean μ_Y(X_i) and std σ_Y(X_i) per X-bin. An "anticipated" joint φ̃(X,Y) is reconstructed by assuming the conditional is normal around this dual response. The deviation metric M between φ and φ̃ quantifies misalignment; the direction with smaller M is selected.
- **Core assumption:** The true effect’s conditional φ(Y|X) is unimodal and well-approximated by a normal distribution due to additive noise. If X→Y, encoding φ(Y|X) loses little information; forcing the opposite direction Y→X yields φ(X|Y) that is less unimodal, incurring greater loss.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3 details the encoding/decoding logic; abstract states "anticipates distributions as normal based on dual response"; neighboring papers (ids 46534, 75081) support unimodality in causal discovery.
- **Break condition:** Fails if the true cause’s conditional is multimodal/non-normal, or if the effect’s conditional is accidentally unimodal under the wrong direction.

### Mechanism 2: Metric Selection Impacts Robustness
- **Claim:** The choice of deviation metric significantly impacts accuracy, with correlation-based metrics (Pearson, cosine) performing best.
- **Mechanism:** The metric M[φ, φ̃] compares two 2D probability grids. Eight metrics (K-L, K-S, cosine, entropy, mutual information, Jaccard, correlation) are evaluated. Empirical tuning via full factorial DOE on 102 pairs identifies Pearson correlation and cosine similarity as top performers (~76.5% accuracy) due to normalization and sensitivity to geometric alignment.
- **Core assumption:** The optimal metric aligns with statistical properties of the KDE-approximated data. Correlation on normalized grids captures structural similarities effectively.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 6 lists all metrics; abstract notes AAG with Pearson achieves 76.5% and consistent top performance; Figure 6 shows robust accuracy surfaces for Pearson.
- **Break condition:** Metrics fail if insensitive to the specific geometric asymmetry induced by causality; choice is dataset-dependent with no theoretical guarantee.

### Mechanism 3: Monotonicity Index via Gradient Sign Changes
- **Claim:** The Monotonicity Index evaluates unimodality directly by counting gradient sign changes along axes of the joint density φ(X,Y); fewer changes along Y for fixed X suggest X→Y.
- **Mechanism:** Compute gradients ∇X, ∇Y of φ. Apply weights ω based on marginal densities φ_X, φ_Y and gradient amplitudes γ to down-weight noisy tails. Sum sign changes to produce MIX (along X) and MIY (along Y). The direction with the *lower* index is inferred as cause→effect, reflecting that φ(Y|X) should be more unimodal.
- **Core assumption:** If X→Y, slices φ(Y|X=x) are unimodal, yielding few gradient sign changes along Y (low MIY), while φ(X|Y=y) may be more complex (higher MIX).
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4 derives the index and weights; abstract describes "counts of gradient sign changes"; neighboring gradient-based work (id 53705) provides analogous context.
- **Break condition:** Sensitive to KDE smoothing and noise; fails if true conditional is unimodal but KDE introduces spurious wiggles, or if wrong direction’s conditional is accidentally smooth.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Conditional Distributions & Unimodality Assumption  
  **Why needed here:** AAG and Monotonicity Index assume the effect’s conditional (e.g., P(Y|X)) given the cause is unimodal due to additive noise. The cause’s conditional given the effect is unconstrained, creating asymmetry.  
  **Quick check:** Given Y = sin(X) + ε (ε ~ normal), is P(Y|X) unimodal for each X? Is P(X|Y) unimodal for a fixed Y?
  
- **Concept:** Divergence Metrics for Probability Distributions  
  **Why needed here:** AAG requires a metric to compare the original joint φ with the anticipated φ̃. Different metrics (K-L, correlation, cosine) capture different aspects (shape vs. magnitude); correlation excels on normalized KDE grids.  
  **Quick check:** Why might Pearson correlation be more robust than K-L divergence when comparing two normalized KDE density grids with different absolute peak heights?
  
- **Concept:** Hyperparameter Sensitivity & Design of Experiments (DOE)  
  **Why needed here:** AAG and Monotonicity Index have sensitive hyperparameters (grid size k, bandwidth bw_par, contour level m, regularization γ*). Full factorial DOE systematically explores the hyperparameter space to find robust settings.  
  **Quick check:** If increasing grid size k improves resolution but also increases noise and computation, what trade-off is being made? How does DOE help navigate this?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Data → KDE Fitting → Joint Grid φ → (AAG Path: Conditional Extraction → Dual Response → Anticipated φ̃ → Deviation Metric) OR (Monotonicity Path: Gradient Calc → Weighted Sign Change Counts) → Decision Logic → (Optional Screening Module → Final Prediction)
- **Critical path:** Raw bivariate data → normalize → fit 2D KDE → generate joint density grid → for each direction, either (encode conditionals to dual response → reconstruct anticipated joint → compute deviation) or (compute gradients → apply weights → sum sign changes) → compare → causal decision.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Grid size k: Larger k captures finer details but increases noise, computation, and overfitting risk; smaller k oversmooths.  
  - Bandwidth bw_par: Larger oversmooths, potentially erasing asymmetries; smaller yields noisy conditionals breaking unimodality.  
  - Metric choice: Correlation/cosine are simple/normalized but may miss tail differences; K-L is information-theoretic but sensitive to zero bins. No universal best.  
  - Complexity vs. interpretability: Dual-response (mean/std) is simple but assumes normality; higher moments (skewness) overfitted.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High mutual information pairs (e.g., MI < 0.125) flagged by screening indicate weak/complex dependencies violating unimodality.  
  - Extreme bw_par sensitivity: accuracy drops sharply at edges of hyperparameter planes (Figure 6).  
  - Contradictory metrics (e.g., K-L vs. Pearson) give opposing decisions → low confidence.  
  - Monotonicity index instability without proper weighting (ω) due to spurious tail gradient wiggles.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline replication:** Run AAG+Pearson (k=25, bw_par=0.175) on 95-pair benchmark; confirm ~77.9% accuracy, AUC ~0.787.  
  2. **Metric ablation:** Fix hyperparameters; compute all eight deviation metrics on 102-pair set; verify Pearson/cosine top (~76.5%), mutual info/entropy bottom (~62%).  
  3. **Screening validation:** Apply mutual info threshold <0.125; ensure flagged pairs are 17,87,107 and remaining 99 pairs yield ~80% accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can we determine whether a bivariate pair exhibits a causal relationship at all before attempting to infer direction?**  
   *Basis:* Future research may address preceding classification between cases with and without cause-effect relationships.  
   *Why unresolved:* Current methods assume a causal link exists; they do not handle non-causal associations common in real-world data.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* Benchmark classifiers on datasets containing both causal and non-causal pairs to assess precision/recall for detecting causality.

2. **Can using continuous values of the deviation metrics (instead of binary directional decisions) improve accuracy?**  
   *Basis:* Switching from binary distinguishing (3) to more informative metric values may provide lift.  
   *Why unresolved:* The sign-based decision rule discards magnitude information that may be predictive.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* Train a regression/probabilistic model on raw metric differences Δ and evaluate against sign-based rule on benchmark.

3. **How can AAG be effectively integrated with ANMs via ensemble methods to boost performance?**  
   *Basis:* It is reasonable to explore ensemble methods, such as AAG with different metrics or incorporating AAG with other approaches like ANMs.  
   *Why unresolved:* AAG (conditional unimodality) and ANMs (additive independent noise) rely on different assumptions; complementary strengths are untested.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* Implement ensemble schemes (weighted voting, stacking) using AAG (multiple metrics) and ANM outputs on the 95-pair benchmark.

4. **Can alternative anticipated distribution geometries (e.g., triangular, or using higher moments) improve robustness?**  
   *Basis:* Future studies may include different geometries of anticipated distributions in addition to normal dual response; triple-response (skewness) was briefly tested without success.  
   *Why unresolved:* Systematic exploration of distribution families (normal, uniform, triangular, Johnson) and moment counts (2,3,4) is lacking.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* Full factorial experiment varying family and moments across all 102 pairs, measuring accuracy stability.

5. **Under what conditions does the unimodality assumption for the effect’s conditional hold in real-world pairs, and how does violation affect accuracy?**  
   *Basis:* Both methods rely on "anticipated unimodality of conditional distributions of the effect," but violation frequency and impact are not analyzed.  
   *Why unresolved:* No diagnostic is provided to assess unimodality per pair; if many pairs have multimodal effects, generalizability is compromised.  
   *What evidence would resolve:* Quantify unimodality (e.g., Hartigan’s dip test) for φ(Y|X) across all pairs, correlate with correct/incorrect classifications, identify degradation thresholds.

## Limitations
- **Data cleaning ambiguity:** Exact rules for excluding "filled" observations in pairs 81–83 are unspecified, impairing dataset reproducibility.
- **Undisclosed screening model:** The decision tree for indecisive-case screening (splits, thresholds, feature engineering like "Kendall R2") is not released; the 80% boost claim cannot be verified.
- **Overfitting risk in tuning:** Hyperparameter selection (via full factorial DOE) used the same 102-pair evaluation set, potentially inflating reported performance and limiting generalizability.
- **Unimodality assumption untested:** The paper does not quantify how often the effect’s conditional is unimodal in the benchmark data or analyze degradation from violations.

## Confidence
- **High:** AAG accuracy (77.9% on 95-pair benchmark) – fully specified hyperparameters (k=25, bw_par=0.175) and metric.
- **Medium:** Pearson correlation as optimal metric – strong empirical results but dataset-dependent; may not generalize.
- **Low:** Screening decision tree boost to 80% – model details undisclosed; reproducibility is not possible without the tree structure or feature thresholds.
