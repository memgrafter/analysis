# Learning Evolving Preferences: A Federated Continual Framework for User-Centric Recommendation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17315
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17315
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** FCUCR achieves up to 60.1% relative improvement in HR@10 and 95.3% in NDCG@10 over state-of-the-art baselines on the XING dataset.

## Executive Summary
Federated recommender systems struggle with temporal forgetting—loss of performance on earlier user sessions as new data arrives—and weakened collaborative personalization due to data silos. This paper introduces FCUCR, a federated continual framework that addresses both challenges. FCUCR employs a time-aware self-distillation strategy to implicitly preserve historical user preferences during local model updates, and an inter-user prototype transfer mechanism to share knowledge across users via a server-maintained knowledge base while keeping individual decision logic private. Extensive experiments on four benchmark datasets (XING, RetailRocket, LastFM, Tmall) show consistent superiority over state-of-the-art federated, session-based, and sequential recommenders, with substantial relative gains on XING (e.g., HR@10 up by 60.1%, NDCG@10 up by 95.3%). Ablation studies confirm the complementary roles of the two components, and the framework demonstrates robustness under local differential privacy noise.

## Method Summary
FCUCR decouples the recommendation model into a globally shared representation module \(f_\phi\) and client-private prediction modules \(p_{\psi_u}\). During each communication round, clients compute session-level prototypes \(h_u^{(t)} = f_\phi(R_u^{(t)})\) and upload them to the server. The server maintains a knowledge base (KB) of these prototypes, and for each client retrieves the top-\(k\) most similar prototypes (by cosine similarity), fuses them into a consolidated prototype \(\rho_u^{(t)}\), and sends it back. The client then concatenates its local prototype \(h_u^{(t)}\) with \(\rho_u^{(t)}\) and feeds the result to its private prediction module to generate recommendations. Locally, each client optimizes both the shared \(\phi\) and its private \(\psi_u\) using a combination of the standard recommendation loss \(L_{rec}\) and a time-aware self-distillation loss \(L_{dist} = \text{MSE}(f_\phi^{(t)}(R_u^{(t)}), f_\phi^{(t-1)}(R_u^{(t)}))\) that regularizes the representation module to retain past knowledge. The updated \(\phi\) is sent to the server for federated averaging, producing the global model for the next round. This loop enables continuous adaptation to evolving user preferences while mitigating forgetting and leveraging cross-user knowledge without exposing private decision logic.

## Key Results
- On XING with SASRec backbone, FCUCR achieves HR@10 of 0.2882 (60.1% relative improvement over the best baseline 0.1800) and NDCG@10 of 0.1863 (95.3% improvement over 0.0954).
- FCUCR consistently outperforms state-of-the-art federated, session-based, and sequential recommendation methods across all four benchmark datasets.
- Ablation studies verify that both time-aware self-distillation and inter-user prototype transfer contribute significantly to performance, with their combination yielding the best results.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Time-aware Self-Distillation
This component combats temporal forgetting by imposing a consistency constraint on the representation module. At each time step \(t\), the current model \(f_\phi^{(t)}\) is regularized to match the output of its previous state \(f_\phi^{(t-1)}\) on the current session’s data via a mean squared error loss. The underlying assumption is that a user’s fundamental semantic understanding of items remains stable even as specific interaction behaviors evolve. Evidence includes the formal definition of \(L_{dist}\) in Section 4.1 and ablation results in Table 3 showing that removing self-distillation leads to a performance drop. A break condition could arise if a user undergoes a non‑gradual, significant shift in item perceptions, as the regularization might unduly constrain adaptation to new stable preferences.

### Mechanism 2: Inter-User Prototype Transfer
To enhance collaborative personalization, the server maintains a dynamic knowledge base of session-level prototypes uploaded by clients. For each client, it retrieves the top-\(k\) most similar prototypes (by cosine similarity), fuses them into a consolidated prototype \(\rho_u^{(t)}\), and sends it back. The client then concatenates \(\rho_u^{(t)}\) with its local prototype \(h_u^{(t)}\) before prediction. This mechanism assumes that users with semantically similar intent patterns at a given time have complementary or reinforcing preferences, and that cross-user knowledge can improve local modeling despite data heterogeneity. Evidence is provided in Section 4.2 and the positive contribution shown in ablation studies (Table 3). However, under extreme heterogeneity, the transferred prototypes may introduce noise, especially if \(k\) is very large, as indicated by non‑monotonic performance in hyperparameter sensitivity (Fig. 3).

### Mechanism 3: Modular Decoupling
FCUCR separates the recommendation model into a shared representation module \(f_\phi\) and client-private prediction modules \(p_{\psi_u}\). Only \(f_\phi\) is aggregated across clients via federated averaging; each client’s \(p_{\psi_u}\) remains local. This design enables effective knowledge sharing (via enriched representations) without exposing user-specific decision logic. The assumption is that item semantics are generalizable across users, while user-specific choice patterns are inherently private. Evidence includes the optimization objective in Section 4.3 and the fact that the prototype transfer workflow relies on this separation. A potential failure occurs if the shared representation inadvertently captures user-specific biases, thereby compromising the privacy benefit or harming personalization.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:**  
Client: \(R_u^{(t)} \rightarrow f_\phi \rightarrow h_u^{(t)}\) (upload) ; receive \(\rho_u^{(t)}\) ; \([h_u^{(t)}; \rho_u^{(t)}] \rightarrow p_{\psi_u} \rightarrow\) recommendations.  
Server: KB \(\leftarrow\) all \(h_u^{(t)}\) ; for each client: retrieve top-\(k\) similar prototypes, fuse \(\rightarrow \rho_u^{(t)}\) ; aggregate \(\phi_u^{(t)}\) via FedAvg \(\rightarrow \phi^{(t+1)}\).

**Critical Path:**  
1. Client computes and uploads \(h_u^{(t)}\).  
2. Server updates KB, retrieves/fuses prototypes, sends \(\rho_u^{(t)}\) to each client.  
3. Client trains locally with enriched representations and self-distillation.  
4. Client uploads updated \(\phi\).  
5. Server aggregates to global \(\phi^{(t+1)}\).

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Knowledge Base Size vs. Performance/Compute:* Larger KB increases relevant prototype hit rate but raises retrieval cost and may add noise; optimal \(k\) is non‑monotonic.  
- *Distillation Strength (\(\lambda\)) vs. Plasticity/Stability:* High \(\lambda\) preserves old knowledge but may hinder adaptation; low \(\lambda\) risks forgetting.  
- *Transfer Frequency vs. Efficiency:* Infrequent prototype transfer reduces communication overhead but may slow knowledge propagation.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Temporal forgetting: Sharp decline in HR/NDCG on the user’s earliest sessions as training progresses.  
- Prototype transfer noise: Performance drops when \(k\) is very large (e.g., 1000) or when KB is unfiltered.  
- Privacy‑utility tension: Significant performance degradation under LDP noise scale \(\text{std} \ge 0.6\).

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. Ablation study (Table 3): Remove TASD (w/o TASD) and IUPR (w/o IUPR) to isolate contributions.  
2. Hyperparameter sensitivity (Fig. 3, Appendix C): Sweep \(k\) and \(\lambda\) to determine robust ranges and dataset-specific optima.  
3. Privacy robustness (Section 5.6, Appendix D.2): Apply LDP noise to client uploads and evaluate performance across noise levels.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper identifies several open questions that arise from its design and evaluation:
- How does the time‑aware self‑distillation mechanism handle users with abrupt, non‑gradual preference shifts, where the assumption of stable item semantics may not hold?
- Under extreme heterogeneity or very large \(k\), why does inter‑user prototype transfer introduce noise, and how can the prototype selection be adaptively tuned to mitigate this?
- Can FCUCR scale effectively to larger, more diverse datasets and domains beyond the four evaluated benchmarks, and will the performance gains remain consistent?

## Limitations
- The self-distillation assumption of stable item semantics may not hold for users with abrupt preference shifts, potentially hindering adaptation.
- Inter-user prototype transfer can introduce noise under high user heterogeneity, and performance is non‑monotonic with respect to the number of retrieved prototypes \(k\).
- Evaluation is limited to four datasets, leaving scalability and domain generalizability unverified.

## Confidence
- Overall SOTA gains: **High** (consistent across datasets)
- Self-distillation forgetting mitigation: **Medium** (ablation supportive but direct assumption test missing)
- Prototype transfer benefit: **Medium** (ablation positive, extreme heterogeneity untested)
- Modular decoupling preserves privacy: **Medium** (LDP experiments show trade‑off, formal guarantees lacking)

## Next Checks
1. *Representation drift:* Track embedding stability for users with known preference shifts to test the self-distillation premise.
2. *Heterogeneous groups:* Construct low-similarity user clusters to identify the breakpoint where prototype transfer becomes detrimental.
3. *Privacy audit:* Perform membership inference attacks on the shared representation under varying LDP budgets to quantify leakage risks.
