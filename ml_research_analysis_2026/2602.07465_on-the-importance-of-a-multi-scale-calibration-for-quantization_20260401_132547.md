# On the Importance of a Multi-Scale Calibration for Quantization

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.07465
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07465
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: MaCa consistently improves accuracy under low-bit quantization (e.g., +5.5 points at 3-bit and +8.7 at 2-bit/g128 on Qwen3-8B) for both GPTQ and GPTAQ.

## Executive Summary
Post-training quantization (PTQ) for large language models (LLMs) often suffers from inaccurate Hessian estimation because conventional calibration uses fixed-length sequences, whereas activation distributions and weight importance strongly depend on input length. This paper introduces MaCa (Matryoshka Calibration), which addresses these issues by (i) incorporating multi-scale sequence lengths into Hessian estimation and (ii) treating each calibration sequence as an independent sample with length-agnostic normalization. Experiments on Qwen3, Gemma3, and LLaMA3 demonstrate consistent accuracy gains under low-bit quantization (e.g., up to +8.7 points at 2-bit) and on the LongBench long-context benchmark. Ablations confirm the necessity of both multi-scale aggregation and per-sequence normalization. MaCa is a lightweight, drop-in enhancement that incurs no extra calibration cost.

## Method Summary
MaCa modifies the calibration procedure of Hessian-based PTQ methods like GPTQ. Instead of using a fixed sequence length, calibration data consists of sequences whose lengths are drawn uniformly from a predefined set (e.g., 256, 512, 1024, 2048, 4096). For each sequence of length L, the activation matrix X (shape D×L) is captured and its contribution to the input-side Hessian H_in is computed as (X X^T)/L, thereby normalizing by sequence length. These per-sample Hessians are aggregated across M sequences using sample-count weighting (e.g., running average H = ((m-1)/m)H + (1/m)H_sample). The final H_in is then fed into the standard GPTQ or GPTAQ weight optimization step, which minimizes quantization error. This approach ensures that every sequence contributes equally regardless of token count, avoiding bias toward longer inputs, and captures a diverse range of activation patterns across lengths.

## Key Results
- At 3-bit quantization, MaCa yields up to +5.5 accuracy points on Qwen3-8B; at 2-bit with group size 128, gains reach +8.7 points.
- Consistent improvements are observed across multiple model families (Qwen3, Gemma3, LLaMA3) and PTQ methods (GPTQ, GPTAQ).
- MaCa also enhances performance on the LongBench long-context benchmark, demonstrating robustness to varying context lengths.
- Ablation studies show that both multi-scale length aggregation and per-sequence normalization are essential; removing either eliminates the gains.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
We outline three mechanisms underlying MaCa’s effectiveness.

### Mechanism 1: Multi‑scale length captures diverse activation distributions
- **Claim**: Incorporating multiple sequence lengths during calibration produces a Hessian that better captures the variable activation distributions of LLMs.
- **Mechanism**: MaCa draws sequence lengths uniformly from a predefined set (e.g., [256,512,1024,2048,4096]) and aggregates the per‑sample Hessians after normalizing each by its own length. This mixes patterns from short and long contexts, avoiding the narrow view of fixed‑length calibration.
- **Core assumption**: Activation distributions (and thus the input‑side Hessian H_in) depend strongly on input length; excluding length diversity leads to a biased weight‑importance estimate.
- **Evidence anchors**: The paper explicitly states that MaCa incorporates multi‑scale sequence length information into Hessian estimation; the algorithm description shows how lengths are drawn; the corpus acknowledges that calibration data characteristics (including length diversity) are critical.
- **Break condition**: If all calibration sequences share the same length, the Hessian remains biased and the gains from MaCa disappear.

### Mechanism 2: Per‑sequence normalization prevents bias toward long inputs
- **Claim**: Treating each calibration sequence as an independent sample and normalizing by its length prevents longer sequences from dominating the Hessian estimate.
- **Mechanism**: Instead of token‑weighted averaging (which gives more weight to longer sequences), MaCa updates the Hessian with sample‑count weights and first scales the outer product by 1/L. This makes every sequence contribute equally regardless of token count.
- **Core assumption**: The true Hessian should reflect the distribution over distinct input sequences, not over individual tokens; token‑level weighting skews the estimate toward longer inputs.
- **Evidence anchors**: The paper says MaCa regularizes each sequence as an independent sample; the algorithm replaces token‑level moving average with a sample‑level formulation; the idea of sample‑level weighting is novel compared to prior calibration works.
- **Break condition**: Omitting the length normalization (or using token‑based averaging) reintroduces the bias and degrades quantization accuracy.

### Mechanism 3: A robust Hessian reduces reconstruction error and improves downstream accuracy
- **Claim**: A more robust, length‑aware Hessian directly reduces reconstruction error and improves downstream task accuracy, especially at low bit‑widths.
- **Mechanism**: The refined Hessian yields a more precise second‑order approximation of the loss landscape, so the GPTQ/GPTAQ update perturbs weights along directions that better preserve full‑precision output, lowering the reconstruction error ||ΔW X||_F^2.
- **Core assumption**: The accuracy of Hessian‑based PTQ is tightly coupled to the quality of the Hessian estimate; a better Hessian leads to smaller quantization‑induced output error.
- **Evidence anchors**: Experimental results show substantial accuracy improvements at low bit‑widths; Figure 2 (reconstruction error histogram) indicates MaCa achieves lower error for most layers; other works (e.g., HeRo‑Q) also emphasize Hessian conditioning.
- **Break condition**: If the calibration set lacks length diversity or the normalization is flawed, the Hessian does not improve, and reconstruction error may exceed baseline GPTQ.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Modified DataLoader (var‑length sequences) → Calibration Loop (compute per‑sample H and aggregate) → GPTQ Integration (standard weight update with final H).

- **Critical path**:
  1. Define a length set L_1 … L_K covering expected deployment lengths (e.g., 256‑4096).
  2. In the calibration loop, randomly pick a length for each sample and crop/pad the input accordingly.
  3. After forward, capture the activation matrix X of the linear layer, compute H_sample = (X X^T)/L, and update the global H (e.g., H = ((m-1)/m)H + (1/m)H_sample).
  4. After all M samples, run the standard GPTQ/GPTAQ update with the accumulated H.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Length set vs token budget*: Total calibration tokens are fixed. Using many very short sequences reduces the number of distinct samples; uniform sampling is recommended.
  - *Normalization*: Division by L prevents long sequences from dominating; the overall Hessian magnitude scales with sample count M. Avoid double‑scaling.
  - *Compatibility*: Works with any Hessian‑based PTQ (GPTQ, GPTAQ, AWQ) without additional cost.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - *Bias check*: Using only a single length (e.g., 2048) yields results identical to plain GPTQ; any deviation suggests a bug in normalization or update.
  - *Length diversity missing*: Poor performance on long‑context benchmarks indicates the length set does not cover sufficiently long inputs.
  - *Numerical instability*: Extremely short lengths (<128) produce noisy H_sample; avoid them.
  - *Incorrect token budget*: If total tokens exceed the nominal budget, verify sample count M and that updates use sample‑based weights.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. *Hessian visualization*: For a small model (e.g., Qwen3‑4B), plot the diagonal of H_in for fixed‑length vs. MaCa. Expect a richer pattern across input channels.
  2. *Quantization accuracy sanity*: With a fixed token budget (524 288), run 4‑bit GPTQ+MaCa on Qwen3‑4B and compare to plain GPTQ. Target improvement ~ +1.07% on the average of 8 tasks.
  3. *Equivalence under uniform length*: Set the length set to only 2048. MaCa should match GPTQ results exactly within statistical noise. Significant differences indicate implementation errors.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Multi‑scale calibration design** – The paper explicitly asks: Can we design a calibration strategy that makes the Hessian robust to varying sequence lengths? This is the core open question motivating MaCa.
- **Isolating the Hin contribution** – To what extent does improving input‑side Hessian (H_in) estimation via multi‑scale calibration contribute to accuracy gains compared to refining output‑side Hessian (H_out) in low‑bit quantization? The paper notes that MaCa mainly addresses Hin but the separate impact remains unquantified.
- **Domain generalization** – How does domain mismatch between calibration data (e.g., C4) and deployment scenarios affect the robustness of MaCa relative to fixed‑length calibration? The experiments use only C4, so transfer to specialized domains is unresolved.
- **Optimal length selection** – What is the optimal strategy for selecting sequence lengths in multi‑scale calibration (e.g., uniform vs. adaptive sampling) across different model context capacities and usage patterns? The paper uses an ad‑hoc uniform set without exploration.

## Limitations
- The exact calibration configuration (length set, sampling distribution) is not fully specified; minor variations could affect reproducibility.
- Evaluation protocol details (prompt templating, few‑shot settings) are not fully described, which may lead to score mismatches.
- Implementation subtleties of GPTQ/GPTAQ (layer quantization order, group size, outlier handling) are not enumerated; small deviations could alter absolute gains.
