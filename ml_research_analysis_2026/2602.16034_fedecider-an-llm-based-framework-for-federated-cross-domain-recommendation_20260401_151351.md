# FeDecider: An LLM-Based Framework for Federated Cross-Domain Recommendation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16034
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16034
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** FeDecider achieves superior federated cross-domain recommendation by sharing directional LoRA components and using client-specific learnable weights, outperforming baselines on Goodreads and Amazon with higher Hit@5 and NDCG@5.

## Executive Summary
Federated cross-domain recommendation (CDR) with large language models (LLMs) faces two major challenges: overfitting due to scale-varying local adapters during federated aggregation, and the difficulty of implicitly measuring similarity across domains when sharing knowledge. Existing federated methods either ignore these issues or rely on simplistic averaging, which can degrade performance under heterogeneity. FeDecider addresses these problems by introducing two key mechanisms: (1) directional LoRA component sharing, where the server normalizes each client's LoRA update to a unit-norm direction, discarding client-specific scaling factors that cause overfitting; (2) client-specific learnable weights that automatically integrate the received directions in a data-aware manner, optimizing for beneficial gradient alignment. This iterative process stabilizes training, preserves privacy by removing raw magnitude information, and produces personalized models for each domain. Extensive experiments on Goodreads and Amazon datasets demonstrate consistent improvements in Hit@5 and NDCG@5 over strong baselines like FedAvg and PFedAvg. Ablation studies confirm the indispensable contribution of each component, and analysis of the learned weights reveals a strong correlation with user-level domain similarities, validating the personalization mechanism.

## Method Summary
FeDecider builds on the IDGenRec backbone, a pre-trained LLM for sequential recommendation. First, the backbone is pre-trained on a small shared dataset and then distilled into a more compact model to reduce computational overhead. In the federated stage, each client (representing a domain) freezes the base model and trains low-rank adaptation (LoRA) matrices A_i and B_i locally. The core innovation lies in the communication protocol: clients upload their raw LoRA updates (A_i, B_i) to the server, which normalizes each update to extract a unit-norm directional component (˜A_i, ˜B_i) by scaling with s = (1/∥B_iA_i∥_F)^{1/2}. These normalized directions are broadcast to all clients. Each client then learns a set of personalization weights α_i_j, one per client j, to form its own update as a weighted sum of received directions: ΔW_i = Σ_j α_i_j (˜B_j ˜A_j). The local training objective jointly optimizes the LoRA parameters (˜A_i, ˜B_i) and the weights α_i_j over multiple communication rounds (e.g., 5 local epochs per round). The final client model combines the frozen base Θ with its integrated LoRA: W_i = Θ + Σ_j α_i_j (˜B_j ˜A_j). This design reduces communication by sharing only low-rank directions, mitigates scale heterogeneity via normalization, and enables personalized knowledge fusion through learned α weights.

## Key Results
- FeDecider consistently outperforms federated baselines (FedAvg, PFedAvg, FFA-LoRA, RoLoRA) across both Goodreads and Amazon datasets, achieving higher Hit@5 and NDCG@5 scores, with particularly strong gains in NDCG@10.
- Comprehensive ablation studies verify that removing any of the three core components—directional decomposition (w/o Decomp), learnable weights (w/o Per), or separate A/B aggregation (w/o Sep)—leads to measurable performance drops, confirming their essential contributions.
- Analysis of the learned client-specific weights α_i_j shows a clear alignment with user-level domain similarities (as measured by user embedding overlaps), but not with item-level similarities, demonstrating that the personalization mechanism effectively captures cross-domain user preference relationships.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Separating the directional component from the magnitude of LoRA updates mitigates overfitting caused by scale-varying local adapters during federated aggregation.
- **Mechanism:** Each client's raw LoRA update ΔW_raw_j = s_j * D_j (with ||D_j||_F=1) is normalized server-side to extract D_j. Only these unit-norm directions are shared, removing the client-specific scale factor s_j that would otherwise dominate aggregation.
- **Core assumption:** (1) The direction D_j encodes domain-specific knowledge patterns less affected by local data scale. (2) The loss landscape's first-order effect is dominated by direction alignment, not magnitude scaling.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "mitigating overfitting from scale-varying local adapters by... sharing only their directional components."
  - [section 3.1] Server normalization: `˜𝐴 𝑗 ←𝑠·𝐴 𝑗, ˜𝐵 𝑗 ←𝑠·𝐵 𝑗, where 𝑠:= (1/∥𝐵 𝑗𝐴 𝑗 ∥𝐹)^1/2` ensures `∥ ˜𝐵 𝑗 ˜𝐴 𝑗 ∥𝐹 = 1`.
  - [section A.2] Theoretical analysis shows baseline aggregation's influence is `(β_j s_j) g_i_j`, where scale s_j causes domination; FeDecider's influence is `α_i_j * g_i_j`, decoupling scale.
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighbor papers (e.g., FedHCDR, FedGCDR) address scale/heterogeneity but not via directional LoRA decomposition.
- **Break condition:** If domain-specific knowledge is primarily encoded in the magnitude (scale) of updates rather than direction, removing scale information would discard critical signal. Ablation "w/o Decomp" shows slight performance drop, suggesting direction is primary but magnitude has marginal utility.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Client-specific learnable weights for directional component integration enable data-aware, personalized knowledge fusion, addressing implicit similarity measurement challenges in LLM-based models.
- **Mechanism:** Each client i learns weights α_i_j to combine received directions D_j: ΔW_i = Σ_j α_i_j * D_j. Weights are optimized via local training, automatically up-weighting beneficial directions (negative gradient alignment) and down-weighting harmful ones.
- **Core assumption:** (1) Beneficial/harmful directions for client i can be identified via gradient alignment g_i_j = ⟨∇F_i(W0), D_j⟩_F. (2) Personalization weights can converge to meaningful domain similarities.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "client-specific learnable weights for data-aware integration."
  - [section 3.1] Local objective: `min_{α_i_j, ˜𝐴_i, ˜𝐵_i} E_{(x,y)~D_i} [L(...)]` with `ΔW_i = Σ α_i_j * ˜𝐵_j ˜𝐴_j`.
  - [section A.3] Proposition A.6 proves gradient descent on α_i_j increases α for beneficial directions (g_i_j < 0) and decreases for harmful (g_i_j > 0).
  - [section 4.2.3] Learned weights align with user-level domain cosine distances (Figure 4), not item-level, supporting that weights capture user-preference similarity.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like FedPCL-CDR use contrastive learning for alignment; FeDecider's adaptive weighting is a distinct, learned mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If gradient alignment g_i_j is a poor proxy for long-term benefit (e.g., due to non-convexity or myopic optimization), learned weights may misalign with true domain utility. Ablation "w/o Per" (uniform weights) degrades performance, confirming personalization necessity.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The iterative federated process with directional sharing and client-side integration stabilizes training under heterogeneity while preserving privacy via scale obfuscation.
- **Mechanism:** Clients upload raw LoRA; server normalizes to directions; clients integrate with learned weights. This repeats. Normalization acts as a privacy filter (removes scale), and client-side integration avoids single-point biased aggregation.
- **Core assumption:** (1) Normalized directions carry transferable knowledge without exposing raw update magnitudes that could leak data distribution specifics. (2) Client-side integration allows adaptation to local distributions that server-side aggregation cannot capture.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "directional LoRA component sharing and client-specific... integration."
  - [section 3.1] "This step also prevents other clients from recovering the exact original updates."
  - [section 4.3.1] Discusses LDP compatibility and that normalization removes scale information as inherent obfuscation.
  - [section 4.2.1] FeDecider outperforms server-aggregation baselines (FedAvg, PFedAvg) and other LoRA-federated methods (FFA-LoRA, RoLoRA), indicating the process stability.
  - [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., FedLora, FDLoRA) also manipulate LoRA for federated learning; FeDecider's unique directional+client-weight loop is not directly mirrored.
- **Break condition:** If normalized directions are insufficient for knowledge transfer (e.g., critical information lies in magnitude patterns), the mechanism loses efficacy. Privacy guarantees may weaken if directions themselves can be inverted to infer raw updates, though paper argues this is difficult.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)**
  - Why needed here: Enables parameter-efficient fine-tuning of LLMs by freezing base weights and learning low-rank matrices (A, B), reducing communication and storage costs in federated settings.
  - Quick check question: Given a weight matrix W ∈ R^{d×d}, how does LoRA represent the update ΔW, and why does this reduce the number of trainable parameters?
- **Concept: Federated Learning with Personalization**
  - Why needed here: Federated CDR requires learning personalized models per domain (client) under data heterogeneity, not a single global model. Techniques like client-specific weights or dual models (Ditto, FDLoRA) are baseline references.
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between FedAvg and personalized FL methods like Ditto in terms of final model per client?
- **Concept: Cross-Domain Recommendation (CDR)**
  - Why needed here: Aims to transfer knowledge from data-rich source domains to sparse target domains. In federated CDR, domains are siloed; FeDecider transfers via LLM-generated semantic knowledge rather than ID embeddings.
  - Quick check question: Why are traditional ID-based CDR methods (e.g., embedding mapping) less suitable for LLM-based recommenders compared to FeDecider's approach?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - **Client:** Holds frozen base LLM (Θ), local LoRA adapter (A_i, B_i), and learnable personalization weights {α_i_j}. Steps: (1) Local training on D_i. (2) Upload raw (A_i, B_i). (3) Receive all normalized directions ˜A_j, ˜B_j. (4) Integrate: ΔW_i = Σ α_i_j * (˜B_j ˜A_j). (5) Update α_i_j and own (˜A_i, ˜B_i) during local training.
  - **Server:** Receives raw LoRA (A_j, B_j) from all clients. Normalizes each to unit-norm direction (˜A_j, ˜B_j). Broadcasts all directions to all clients. No model storage.
- **Critical path:** (1) Initial local training on raw LoRA per client. (2) First communication: upload -> server normalization -> broadcast directions. (3) Clients start integrating directions and learning α_i_j during local training. (4) Repeat steps 2-3 for T rounds. (5) Final model: W_i = Θ + Σ α_i_j * (˜B_j ˜A_j) using converged α.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Communication cost:** FeDecider downloads K*r*d per round (K clients) vs. O(r*d) for PFedAvg. Tradeoff: higher download for fine-grained personalization.
  - **Computation:** Client must compute weighted sum of K directions and train α_i_j (size K). Slight overhead vs. only training LoRA.
  - **Storage:** Client stores K*r*d for directions + local LoRA + α vector (size K). Acceptable for small K (typical CDR: few domains).
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Ablation "w/o Decomp": performance drop indicates scale noise is significant.
  - Ablation "w/o Per": uniform aggregation fails, showing personalization is critical.
  - Ablation "w/o Sep": separate aggregation of A and B matrices degrades, proving structural alignment of (A,B) pairs from same client matters.
  - If learned α_i_j do not converge stably (Figure 5 shows convergence) or misalign with domain similarity (Figure 4), integration may be ineffective.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Main results (RQ1):** Compare FeDecider vs. baselines (FedAvg, PFedAvg, FFA-LoRA, etc.) on Goodreads/Amazon using Hit@5, NDCG@5. Expect consistent outperformance, especially in NDCG@10.
  2. **Ablation study (RQ2):** Validate components: w/o Decomp, w/o Per, w/o Sep. Expect each removal to degrade performance, confirming necessity.
  3. **Personalization weight analysis (RQ3):** Visualize α_i_j heatmap vs. user/item cosine distances. Expect alignment with user-level similarity, not item-level. Also track α convergence over rounds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can extending personalized weights to be layer-wise improve FeDecider's adaptability and performance?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion identifies "extending the personalized weights to a layer-wise design" as future work.
- **Why unresolved:** The current model uses a single set of client-specific weights {𝛼𝑖𝑗} across all layers, which may not capture layer-specific relevance of shared directions for knowledge integration.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement layer-wise {𝛼𝑖𝑗𝑙} for each layer l, evaluate on Goodreads/Amazon datasets, and analyze the performance-complexity trade-off compared to the original FeDecider.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Under realistic non-convex training dynamics, do the learned weights {𝛼𝑖𝑗} converge to a stable optimum that reliably down-weights harmful directions?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Proposition A.6 shows gradient descent suppresses harmful directions under a sign-consistency assumption; Figure 5 shows empirical convergence but provides no theoretical guarantees for general non-convex losses.
- **Why unresolved:** Federated local updates involve stochastic gradients and heterogeneous data, which may violate assumptions and cause oscillations or suboptimal convergence of weights.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive convergence guarantees under non-convex objectives or conduct extensive experiments tracking weight trajectories across varying degrees of data heterogeneity and noise.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does FeDecider's communication cost and recommendation performance scale when the number of clients K is large (e.g., dozens of domains)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 4.3.2 notes communication cost is O(Krd) and states that "K is small" to avoid negative transfer, but no empirical study with large K is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** The client-side integration requires downloading directional components from all other clients; overhead grows linearly with K, but the breaking point where utility degrades due to noise from weakly-related domains is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Simulate federated CDR with varying K (e.g., 5, 10, 20, 50 synthetic domains), measure NDCG/Hit and communication volume, and explore sparsity or clustering to mitigate cost.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the normalization of LoRA updates into directional components offer meaningful privacy protection against reconstruction attacks in practice?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 4.3.1 claims normalization "removes scale information, acting as an inherent obfuscation mechanism" but lacks formal privacy analysis or empirical validation.
- **Why unresolved:** The privacy budget of revealing normalized directions is unclear; an adversary might still infer local data distributions from directional patterns, especially with multiple rounds of observation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform membership inference attacks comparing directional sharing vs. raw LoRA sharing, or quantify privacy loss under local differential privacy with controlled perturbations.

## Limitations
1. Pre‑training data sampling (shared dataset composition) unspecified → replication risk.
2. Distillation hyperparameters (layer mapping, loss weights) omitted → backbone variability.
3. Normalization details (e.g., numerical epsilon, storage) unclear → could affect direction consistency and privacy.
4. Evaluation limited to two datasets; generalization unknown.
5. Privacy claim rests on difficulty of inverting directions; not formally proven.

## Confidence
- *Mechanism 1 (directional decomposition)*: Medium. Theory and ablation show benefit, but magnitude may carry marginal signal; break condition possible if scale encodes domain knowledge.
- *Mechanism 2 (learnable weights)*: High. Strong theoretical backing (Prop A.6) and empirical alignment with user similarity; ablation confirms necessity.
- *Mechanism 3 (stability/privacy)*: Medium. Outperformance vs. baselines suggests stability, but privacy relies on scale removal which may be reversible; limited analysis.

## Next Checks
1. **Direction norm check:** After server normalization, compute ∥˜B_j ˜A_j∥_F for all clients; should be ≈1 with low variance across rounds.
2. **Magnitude contribution test:** Introduce a variant that adds scaled magnitude back into aggregation (e.g., weighted sum of raw updates) and compare against w/o Decomp ablation to quantify magnitude’s utility.
3. **Privacy leakage assessment:** Simulate an attacker with auxiliary knowledge (e.g., multiple directions from same client) to attempt reconstruction of raw LoRA; measure reconstruction error to bound information leakage.
