# No More DeLuLu: Physics-Inspired Kernel Networks for Geometrically-Grounded Neural Computation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.12276
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12276
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Yat-product kernel unifies alignment and proximity; replaces activations and normalization; NMNs achieve 92.38% MNIST with bounded prototypes; Aether-GPT2 lowers validation loss (4.5747 vs 4.6417) and reduces memory by 15–25%.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the yat-product kernel, a Mercer kernel combining squared inner product with inverse squared Euclidean distance, creating a self-regulating potential well. This kernel simultaneously performs geometric alignment and non-linear transformation, eliminating separate activation and normalization layers. Neural Matter Networks (NMNs) instantiate the kernel with learned prototypes, achieving linear‐level MNIST accuracy while maintaining bounded weight evolution and robust superposition handling. Aether-GPT2, a Transformer variant, swaps standard attention and MLPs for yat‑based equivalents, matching GPT-2 performance with lower validation loss and significant memory savings.

## Method Summary
The core operator is ⵟ(w, x) = (w⊤x)²/(‖w−x‖²+ε). In Neural Matter Networks, this kernel is applied to trainable prototypes, with outputs aggregated and scaled by s = (n/log(1+n))^α (α initialized to 1). Aether‑GPT2 replaces scaled dot‑product attention with ⵟsimilarity (qᵢ⊤kⱼ)²/(‖qᵢ−kⱾ‖²+ε) and swaps MLP blocks for an NMN layer followed by a bias‑free linear projection. All LayerNorm and activation functions are removed. Training uses AdamW with BF16 mixed precision; ε must be set relative to data scale. A minimal reproduction involves (1) implementing the kernel and verifying properties, (2) training a 10‑prototype MNIST classifier (≈5 epochs, Adam lr=0.001) to achieve ~92.38% test accuracy and bounded prototype changes, and (3) adapting a GPT‑2 (124M) to Aether‑GPT2 on FineWeb (2.5B tokens) to validate loss reduction and memory savings. Unknowns remain around exact weight initialization, detailed training schedule (warmup, clipping, weight decay), and the update scheme for α.

## Key Results
- NMNs achieve 92.38% accuracy on MNIST (vs 92.08% linear), with prototype magnitude changes bounded within ±4.5%.
- Under prototype inversion, NMNs retain 87.87% accuracy versus 0.01% for a linear baseline.
- Aether‑GPT2 (124M) lowers validation loss compared to GPT‑2 (4.5747 vs 4.6417) and reduces memory consumption by 15–25%, with LayerNorm entirely eliminated.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The yat‑product’s denominator provides intrinsic normalization, eliminating the need for separate normalization layers.
- **Mechanism:** The kernel output K(w, x) = (w⊤x)²/(‖w–x‖²+ε) is bounded: for large ‖x‖, K → ‖w‖² cos²θ ≤ ‖w‖². This “self‑regulation” prevents unbounded activations and implicitly adapts to input scale.
- **Core assumption:** ε > 0 is finite; if ε is too small, the denominator can become near‑zero for inputs close to w, risking numerical instability. Remark 2 suggests scaling ε as O(dσ²) for noisy data.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “self‑regularizing potential well”, “shifting normalization into the kernel itself”.
  - Section: Proposition 1 (self‑regulation limit), Corollary 2 (dimensional O(1) scaling), and the LayerNorm ablation (Table 4) showing incompatibility.
  - Corpus: The related work *Deep Learning 2.0: Artificial Neurons That Matter* (arXiv:2411.08085) also reports bounded prototype evolution and removal of normalization layers.
- **Break condition:** If ε is set significantly below the typical squared distance scale, K(w, w) = ‖w‖⁴/ε grows extremely large, causing exploding activations and loss of gradient stability.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The yat‑product’s combination of squared alignment and inverse‑square proximity yields a non‑linear kernel that can solve linearly inseparable problems (e.g., XOR) with a single unit.
- **Mechanism:** K(w, x) peaks only when inputs are both aligned (large |w⊤x|) **and** close (small ‖w – x‖). For XOR, choosing w = [1, –1]⊤ makes the negative class orthogonal to w (K=0), while the positive class yields positive values – non‑linearity emerges from geometry rather than a hard threshold.
- **Core assumption:** The decision boundary between classes can be expressed in terms of orthogonality or distance; otherwise, sufficient width is needed to approximate arbitrary boundaries (Theorem 4).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “unifies alignment and proximity in a single computation”.
  - Section: Section 3.1 (single‑unit XOR solution), Theorem 2 (zero response on orthogonality), and Figure 3 (potential well shape).
  - Corpus: *Fast and Geometrically Grounded Lorentz Neural Networks* (arXiv:2601.21529) exemplifies a broader trend of using geometric structure as the primary non‑linearity.
- **Break condition:** In a highly over‑parameterized setting, if many units are placed far from data points, they contribute little (gradients vanish, Proposition 2), effectively reducing capacity. Also, sign‑sensitive tasks may suffer because (w⊤x)² discards polarity.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) of a yat‑network decays for orthogonal inputs, providing an implicit bias that encourages class separation without explicit contrastive losses.
- **Mechanism:** In the infinite‑width limit, the NTK is K_NTK(x, x′) = E[ⵟ(w, x)ⵟ(w, x′)] + E[∇ⵟ(w, x)ᵀ∇ⵟ(w, x′)]. Since ⵟ contains (w⊤x)², cross‑terms vanish under spherical initialization when x⊤x′ ≈ 0, making K_NTK(x, x′) → 0. Training dynamics (gradient descent) then update weights based mainly on examples with large NTK values, i.e., same‑class (aligned) inputs.
- **Core assumption:** Training operates in the lazy (NTK) regime – wide networks, small learning rate – so the kernel stays approximately constant. Weight initialization is isotropic (e.g., Gaussian).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “unifies kernel learning, gradient stability, and information geometry”.
  - Section: Theorem 17 (orthogonality preservation), Corollary 4 (implicit class separation), Figure 8 (NTK eigenvalue spectrum and calibration improvement).
  - Corpus: *A Gap Between the Gaussian RKHS and Neural Networks* (arXiv:2502.16331) discusses functional differences between kernel and neural inductive biases, contextualizing the NTK analysis.
- **Break condition:** If the network is not wide enough or learning is too fast, the kernel evolves significantly (non‑lazy regime), and the orthogonality bias may not materialize. Additionally, if classes are not approximately orthogonal in the input space, the NTK decay may not provide useful separation.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Mercer Kernel Theory**  
  **Why needed:** The yat‑product’s status as a Mercer kernel (Theorem 1) guarantees a unique RKHS, universal approximation (Theorem 4), and valid kernel‑based learning algorithms. Without this, the theoretical foundations are shaky.  
  **Quick check:** *What three properties must a symmetric kernel satisfy on a compact domain to be a Mercer kernel, and how does the yat‑product meet them?*

- **Concept: Self‑Regulation and Lipschitz Continuity**  
  **Why needed:** These ensure numerical stability, bounded activations, and robust gradients, which together enable the removal of LayerNorm and safe mixed‑precision training (Section 3.5, Proposition 3, Proposition 2).  
  **Quick check:** *State Proposition 1 (self‑regulation) and Proposition 3 (Lipschitz constant). What do they imply about the gradient magnitude as ‖x‖ → ∞?*

- **Concept: Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) Theory**  
  **Why needed:** NTK analysis connects the kernel’s geometry to training dynamics and generalization, explaining why the yat‑network exhibits smoother function spaces and implicit class separation (Section C.20, Figure 8).  
  **Quick check:** *How does the NTK of a yat‑network differ from that of a ReLU network for orthogonal inputs? Relate to Theorem 17.*

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  - **Embedding** (optional): optional learned token/pos embeddings.  
  - **Aether block** (repeated L times):  
    - ⵟ‑Multi‑Head Attention: Q, K, V projections; similarity = (qᵢ⊤kⱼ)²/(‖qᵢ – k⪼‖² + ε); softmax → weighted sum.  
    - **NMN layer:** n units with learnable prototypes {wᵢ} and biases {bᵢ}; output = s · Σᵢ (wᵢ⊤x + bᵢ)²/(‖wᵢ – x‖² + ε). Scaling factor s = (n/log(1+n))^α, α learnable (init = 1).  
    - **Linear projection** after attention and NMN (no bias per GPT‑2 design).  
  - **Output head** (e.g., language modeling: vocabulary linear layer).  
  - **Absent components:** All LayerNorm, activation functions (GeLU/ReLU), and explicit normalization.

- **Critical path:** Forward pass hinges on computing the squared inner product (reused for denominator via ‖w – x‖² = ‖w‖² + ‖x‖² – 2w⊤x). Backward pass uses closed‑form gradients (Theorem 5). The scaling factor α adjusts overall magnitude; the denominator ε must be chosen relative to data scale (Remark 2).

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Memory*: Eliminating activation storage reduces memory by 15–25% (Section 3.5).  
  - *Compute*: Per‑neuron FLOPs ~2× Linear+ReLU (Table 6), but overall Θ(Bn d) complexity matches linear layers.  
  - *Expressivity*: Universal approximation holds (Theorem 4), but practical capacity may require wider layers than ReLU nets.  
  - *Stability*: Bounded outputs and Lipschitz gradients favor BF16/FP16; however, too small ε risks spikes, while too large ε flattens responses (Figure 7a).

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Divergence within first 1k steps if any LayerNorm is reintroduced (Table 4).  
  - Exploding loss or NaN when ε is orders of magnitude below typical ‖w – x‖² (e.g., ε < 1e–6).  
  - Very slow convergence if scaling factor α remains near 1 and responses are too weak (but α is learnable and typically increases).  
  - Poor performance on tasks requiring explicit sign information (since numerator is squared); XOR works because orthogonality suffices.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **XOR sanity check** – train a single yat‑unit on XOR; expect 100% accuracy and a localized decision boundary (Section 3.1).  
  2. **MNIST linear probe** – 10‑neuron classifier on flattened images; compare test accuracy to a linear baseline, track prototype norm change, and evaluate inversion robustness (Table 1, Figure 6).  
  3. **Aether‑GPT2 pretraining** – train on a held‑out 2.5 B token subset of FineWeb; compare validation loss (BF16) to GPT‑2, monitor memory usage, and check calibration curves (Table 3, Figure 8).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What is the precise relationship between the ⵟ‑kernel's RKHS norm and that of the ReLU (arc‑cosine) kernel? The paper sketches a bound in Corollary 3 but does not derive the exact constant \( C_{\varepsilon,R} \) or prove tightness.
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Corollary 3 states: "kf k_Hⵟ ≤ C_{ε,R} kf k_{H_ReLU}" but the proof is a sketch and the constant is left unspecified.
- **Why unresolved**: Without a tight, computable constant, it is unclear whether the ⵟ‑kernel's implicit regularization (via self‑regulation) yields provably better generalization than ReLU networks in practice. The sketch relies on smoothness comparisons but does not account for the geometric structure of the ⵟ‑product.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A rigorous derivation of the feature maps for both kernels on a compact domain, followed by an explicit calculation of the norm constants. Alternatively, empirical evaluation of the effective RKHS norms (e.g., via minimum‑norm interpolants) on benchmark datasets could quantify the gap.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: For noisy inputs with covariance \( \sigma^2 I \), what is the theoretically optimal choice of \( \varepsilon \) that maximizes the gradient signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) during training? The paper suggests \( \varepsilon^* \propto d\sigma^2 \) in Remark 2 but provides no formal optimization.
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Remark 2: "For noisy inputs with \( n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I) \), the stability constant should scale as \( \varepsilon^* \propto d\sigma^2 \) to maximize gradient signal‑to‑noise ratio." This is presented as a heuristic without a theorem.
- **Why unresolved**: The scaling heuristic is not derived from first principles. The exact trade‑off between gradient magnitude (which decays with \( \varepsilon \)) and noise suppression (which increases with \( \varepsilon \)) is not quantified. This leaves practitioners without principled guidance for tuning \( \varepsilon \) in noisy or high‑dimensional settings.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: An analytic expression for the gradient SNR as a function of \( \varepsilon \), \( d \), and \( \sigma^2 \), followed by optimization to find \( \varepsilon^* \). Validation could come from training curves showing optimal loss/accuracy vs. \( \varepsilon \) under controlled noise levels.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Why does adding LayerNorm cause training collapse in Aether architectures, and is this phenomenon universal across tasks and architectures? The paper shows experimental divergence (Table 4) but offers only an intuitive explanation.
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section 3.6 states: "Reintroducing LayerNorm to Aether‑GPT2 causes training collapse, with validation loss diverging within the first 1000 steps." The explanation points to redundancy with the denominator's intrinsic normalization.
- **Why unresolved**: The argument is qualitative. A formal analysis of how LayerNorm transforms the input distribution (e.g., forcing fixed norm) and thereby breaks the denominator's geometric interpretation is missing. It is also unclear whether this holds for all ⵟ‑based architectures (e.g., CNNs, GNNs) or only Transformers.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A theoretical study of the gradient flow with and without LayerNorm, showing how the denominator's dependency on input norms is altered. Empirical tests across diverse architectures (MLPs, ResNets, Transformers) and datasets (vision, language, tabular) would establish scope.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: How does the ⵟ‑kernel's "potential well" geometry affect feature learning and representation quality in deep networks? The paper shows improved calibration and bounded prototype evolution but does not analyze learned representations (e.g., via linear probes or similarity metrics).
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper discusses decision boundaries (Section E) and prototype evolution (Section 3.3) but does not evaluate representation quality (e.g., feature alignment with semantic classes, disentanglement). The NTK analysis (Section C.20) suggests implicit orthogonality, but this is not verified empirically.
- **Why unresolved**: Without measuring representation quality, it is unclear whether the geometric grounding translates to meaningful features. For example, do ⵟ‑networks learn more linearly separable representations? Are features more robust to adversarial perturbations?
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Standard representation analysis: train linear probes on frozen ⵟ‑vs‑ReLU features, evaluate on downstream tasks; measure feature similarity (e.g., CKA) between layers; conduct adversarial robustness tests based on the bounded output property.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: Can the ⵟ‑product be generalized beyond Euclidean space to non‑Euclidean geometries (e.g., spherical, hyperbolic, or graph‑structured data) while preserving Mercer kernel properties and self‑regulation?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper explicitly grounds the kernel in Euclidean distance (Equation 1) and tests only on grid‑structured (MNIST) and sequence (language) data. Related work mentions geometric deep learning but the ⵟ‑product is not extended to manifolds or graphs.
- **Why unresolved**: Many real‑world data lie on non‑Euclidean manifolds. The inverse‑square denominator relies on Euclidean distance; replacing it with geodesic distance may break the Mercer proof (which uses Gaussian kernel mixtures). This limits applicability to broader geometric deep learning settings.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Derive a generalized ⵟ‑product using appropriate distance metrics (e.g., graph Laplacian, hyperbolic distance) and prove Mercer properties on compact manifolds. Empirical validation on graph classification or spherical image datasets would demonstrate utility.

## Limitations

- Exact initialization and scaling of yat‑layers (ε, α, weight variance) are underspecified, affecting stability and performance.
- The claim of sign‑agnostic operation may limit applicability to tasks where polarity matters; no ablation on this is reported.
- Theoretical proofs (e.g., Mercer property) are provided but not independently verified; potential edge cases on non‑compact domains.
- Reproducibility relies on correct implementation of the ⵟ‑multi‑head attention and adaptive scaling factor, which are novel and prone to coding errors.

## Confidence

- **Kernel theory (Mercer, universal approx)**: Medium (proofs exist but unverified).
- **Empirical results (MNIST, GPT‑2)**: Medium (numbers are specific but lacking raw logs; risk of overfitting/tuning).
- **Mechanistic explanations (self‑regulation, NTK bias)**: Medium (support from theory and limited experiments; causal link not fully isolated).
