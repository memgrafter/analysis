# Med-V1: Small Language Models for Zero-shot and Scalable Biomedical Evidence Attribution

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.05308
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05308
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** A 3B-parameter model family achieves frontier LLM-level accuracy (~0.73) on biomedical evidence attribution at a fraction of the cost, enabling scalable analysis of tens of thousands of documents.

## Executive Summary
Automated attribution of biomedical claims to evidence sources is essential for detecting hallucinations and verifying assertions, but frontier models like GPT-5 are prohibitively expensive for large-scale deployment. This work introduces Med-V1, a family of 3B-parameter models fine-tuned on a novel synthetic dataset, MedFact-Synth. Med-V1 performs zero-shot attribution by producing structured verdicts (e.g., supported, refuted) and rationales, matching frontier model accuracy on benchmark datasets while being significantly more cost-effective. Two applied use cases demonstrate its scalability: analyzing citation patterns in GPT model outputs and auditing 57,000+ statements from clinical guidelines, where it identified a notable rate of potential misattributions.

## Method Summary
The core innovation is MedFact-Synth, a large-scale synthetic training dataset of 1.5M claim-article pairs. It is constructed by sampling PubMed articles, using GPT-4o-mini to generate both supported and refuted claims, retrieving relevant context with the MedCPT retriever, and deriving consensus verdicts and rationales from a panel of frontier LLMs. Med-V1 models (based on Llama and Qwen) are then fine-tuned on this data using a combination of supervised learning (for verdict and rationale generation) and reinforcement learning (to optimize for attribution accuracy).

## Key Results
*   On the MedFact-Bench suite (five datasets), Med-V1 variants outperform their base 3B models by 27-71% and achieve accuracy on par with frontier LLMs (~0.73).
*   Analysis of 57,000+ citation statements from 6,152 clinical guidelines using Med-V1 flagged numerous contradictions; manual review of 100 samples found 28% were genuine misattributions, often concerning treatment effectiveness.
*   The approach provides a scalable, low-cost alternative to frontier LLMs for large-scale biomedical evidence verification tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by creating a high-quality, diverse training signal (MedFact-Synth) that teaches a small model the specific task of evidence attribution. The synthetic pipeline ensures broad coverage of verdict types and retrieval contexts. Reinforcement learning fine-tuning further aligns the model's outputs with a multi-LLM consensus on what constitutes a correct attribution, effectively compressing the "knowledge" of larger, more expensive models into a compact, specialized form.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Synthetic Data Generation:** Why needed: To create a massive, labeled dataset for supervised fine-tuning without prohibitive human annotation cost. Quick check: Verify generated claims are factually anchored to the source article text.
*   **Retrieval-Augmented Attribution:** Why needed: To provide the model with the specific evidence context required for a verdict, reducing hallucination. Quick check: Ensure retrieved passages are highly relevant to the claim's key entities.
*   **Consensus Labeling:** Why needed: To establish a more robust "gold standard" than a single LLM, mitigating individual model biases. Quick check: Measure inter-LLM agreement rates on a held-out sample.
*   **Reinforcement Learning from Verdict Feedback:** Why needed: To optimize the model for the final attribution accuracy metric, not just likelihood of plausible text. Quick check: Monitor for reward hacking, where the model learns to game the reward model without improving true attribution.

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component Map:** Article Sampler -> Claim Generator (GPT-4o-mini) -> Retriever (MedCPT) -> Verdict Panel (Frontier LLMs) -> Dataset Aggregator -> Med-V1 (SFT + RL)
*   **Critical Path:** PubMed article -> synthetic claim/retrieval -> consensus label -> Med-V1 training -> zero-shot claim attribution.
*   **Design Tradeoffs:** Cost vs. Scale (synthetic data reduces cost but risks artifacts), Specificity vs. Generality (focused on PubMed articles may limit application to other text types), Accuracy vs. Explainability (verdicts come with rationales, but rationale fidelity is not guaranteed).
*   **Failure Signatures:** High confidence in incorrect verdicts, rationales that cite non-existent or irrelevant text snippets, performance collapse on non-PubMed source types (e.g., clinical trial reports, EHRs).
*   **First 3 Experiments:**
    1.  Evaluate Med-V1 on a held-out set of *real human-generated* claim-article pairs (not synthetic) to benchmark against potential synthetic data artifacts.
    2.  Ablate the reinforcement learning stage to quantify its specific contribution to final accuracy vs. supervised fine-tuning alone.
    3.  Test attribution latency and cost per claim for Med-V1 vs. GPT-4o/5 on a fixed workload of 10,000 diverse biomedical claims.

None explicitly stated by the authors.

## Limitations
*   **Synthetic data bias:** MedFact-Synth may contain GPT-4o-mini's stylistic artifacts or hallucination patterns not present in real-world attribution.
*   **Benchmark dependence:** Evaluation is on MedFact-Bench and clinical guidelines; generalizability to other biomedical domains (e.g., patents, EHRs) is unproven.
*   **Consensus label unreliability:** Frontier LLM panels may share systemic biases, propagating them into the training labels instead of providing a true gold standard.

## Confidence
*   Med-V1 achieves performance parity with frontier LLMs on provided benchmarks: **Medium** (Strong results on curated test sets, but unknown cross-dataset robustness).
*   The synthetic data pipeline produces valid training signal: **Medium** (Process is transparent, but lacks human validation of source article-claim fidelity).
*   The 28% misattribution rate in clinical guidelines is representative: **Low** (Based on a small, n=100 manual sample; may not scale to the full 57k+ corpus).

## Next Checks
1.  Conduct an out-of-domain evaluation by testing Med-V1 on evidence attribution tasks involving non-PubMed sources, such as FDA drug labels or clinical trial study reports.
2.  Perform a human annotation study where domain experts re-annotate 500+ guidance statements; compare expert verdicts against both Med-V1's outputs and the original frontier LLM consensus labels.
3.  Execute a direct cost-latency trade-off study, running Med-V1 and GPT-5 in parallel on a large, random sample of claims to measure actual runtime, monetary cost, and accuracy degradation under load.
