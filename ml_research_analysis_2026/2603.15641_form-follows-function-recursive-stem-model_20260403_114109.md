# Form Follows Function: Recursive Stem Model
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.15641  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15641  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: RSM achieves 97.5% exact accuracy on Sudoku-Extreme and ~80% on Maze-Hard (30×30) within one hour on a single A100, with >20× faster training and ~5× error reduction versus TRM.

## Executive Summary
The Recursive Stem Model (RSM) introduces a highly efficient training technique for recursive reasoning models. By detaching hidden-state history during training and applying loss only at the final step, RSM learns a depth‑agnostic transition operator that can be arbitrarily scaled at test time—enabling refinements of up to ~20,000 steps, far beyond the ~20 steps seen during training. This yields >20× faster training and ~5× lower error compared to prior models like TRM, achieving state‑of‑the‑art results on challenging puzzle domains (97.5% exact accuracy on Sudoku‑Extreme, ~80% on Maze‑Hard) within just one hour on a single A100. Moreover, RSM’s convergence behavior provides a native reliability signal: non‑settling trajectories flag potential failures, while stable fixed points can be paired with verifiers to guarantee correctness.

## Method Summary
RSM’s core mechanism revolves around two latent states: a fast state z_L that performs local computations and a slow state z_H that aggregates and stabilizes. The model iterates for a configurable number of outer steps H. In each outer step, it first applies the shared transition operator F_θ for L inner steps: z_L ← F_θ(z_L; z_H + input_emb). After the inner refinement, z_H is updated: z_H ← F_θ(z_H; z_L). The final z_H is projected to output logits via a tied weight matrix (W_out = W_emb).

Training employs three innovations to enable stable scaling. First, during forward training passes, all but the last outer step are detached from the computational graph, so gradients are applied only to the final step’s predictions—this avoids overfitting to a specific depth and encourages a convergent operator. Second, a stochastic outer‑transition scheme randomly applies gradients to the penultimate outer step with small probability (e.g., p_detach=0.99 means mostly detaching), smoothing optimization as depth grows. Third, H and L are grown independently via milestone‑based curriculum schedules, allowing the model to start with shallow recursions and later handle much larger test‑time depths (e.g., H_test=20000 vs H_train=20).

## Key Results
- RSM achieves 97.5% exact accuracy on Sudoku‑Extreme and ~80% on Maze‑Hard (30×30) within one hour on a single A100.  
- Training is >20× faster and reduces error by ~5× compared to the previous TRM model.  
- Test‑time scaling to arbitrarily long refinements (e.g., ~20,000 steps vs ~20 trained) yields stable convergence, and non‑settling trajectories provide a native reliability signal.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: RSM learns a stable, depth‑agnostic transition operator by detaching hidden‑state history during training and applying loss only at the final step.  
- **Mechanism**: During training, RSM executes multiple outer recursion steps but stops gradient flow from all but the last step (warm‑up iterations). This prevents the model from overfitting to a specific unroll depth and biases it toward convergence rather than greedy intermediate correctness.  
- **Core assumption**: A transition operator trained with terminal‑only supervision will be stable under repeated application, enabling arbitrary test‑time scaling.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "RSM fully detaches the hidden‑state history during training, treats early iterations as detached ‘warm‑up’ steps, and applies loss only at the final step."  
  - [section] II.C.1–C.2 explicitly contrasts with BPTT/deep supervision and defines terminal loss with detached warm‑up.  
  - [corpus] Corpus contains related iterative computation work (e.g., "From Growing to Looping"), but none evaluate detached training; evidence is indirect.  
- **Break condition**: If the operator lacks contractive properties or tasks require precise intermediate states that verifiers cannot catch, the model may converge to wrong fixed points or fail to settle.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: RSM decouples training stability from inference compute by independently growing outer (H) and inner (L) recursion depths during training, allowing test‑time scaling to depths far beyond training.  
- **Mechanism**: RSM starts with shallow H and L, then gradually increases them via milestone‑based schedules. This keeps optimization stable early while still enabling H_test ≫ H_train and L_test ≫ L_train at inference without retraining.  
- **Core assumption**: The learned transition operator remains effective when applied for many more steps than seen during training.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "We further grow the outer recursion depth H and inner compute depth L independently... enabling test‑time scaling where inference can run for arbitrarily many refinement steps (~20,000 H_test ≫ 20 H_train)."  
  - [section] II.D.1–D.2 describes independent curricula and milestone schedules.  
  - [corpus] "ParaFormer: Shallow Parallel Transformers with Progressive Approximation" discusses depth growth but not independent H/L scaling; evidence is weak.  
- **Break condition**: If the operator’s dynamics change qualitatively beyond training depth (e.g., divergence or new attractors), test‑time scaling may degrade performance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Stochastic outer‑transition training mitigates instability when increasing recursion depth by randomly deciding whether gradients flow through the penultimate outer step (H−1).  
- **Mechanism**: At each training step, with high probability p_detach (e.g., 0.99) RSM detaches before the final outer update, yielding a 1‑step gradient. Occasionally it includes the transition step, yielding a 2‑step gradient. This smooths optimization as depth grows.  
- **Core assumption**: Exposing the optimizer to a distribution of gradient horizons prevents brittle adaptation to a fixed depth and improves generalization across unroll lengths.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "use a stochastic outer‑transition scheme (stochastic depth over H) to mitigate instability when increasing depth."  
  - [section] II.C.5 and Figure 7 detail the stochastic transition logic and its stabilizing effect.  
  - [corpus] "Relaxed Recursive Transformers" explores parameter sharing but not stochastic depth for recursion; evidence is indirect.  
- **Break condition**: If p_detach is too high, the model may not learn long‑range outer dependencies; if too low, instability returns.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input → Embedding → init(z_H, z_L) → Outer loop (H): Inner loop (L): z_L ← F_θ(z_L, z_H+Embedding); z_H ← F_θ(z_H, z_L) → Final z_H → Tied Predictor → Logits.

**Critical path**:  
1. Initialize z_H and z_L from fixed buffers.  
2. For each outer step h = 1 to H:  
   a. Repeat L times: compute z_L ← F_θ(z_L; z_H + input_emb).  
   b. Update z_H ← F_θ(z_H; z_L).  
3. Decode logits from the final z_H using the tied projection.  
4. Compute cross‑entropy loss against targets (only at the final step during training).

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Detachment vs. gradient flow: Detaching most steps reduces memory and avoids vanishing gradients but may slow learning of long‑range dependencies; mitigated by stochastic transition.  
- Independent H/L scaling: Allows flexible allocation of compute between inner and outer loops, but introduces additional curriculum hyperparameters.  
- Terminal loss only: Simpler training with less bias, but requires sufficient test‑time steps to refine and may lack intermediate guidance.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Sudden loss spikes when increasing H, indicating instability.  
- Non‑settling trajectories where decoded outputs keep changing without converging.  
- Accuracy drop when test H significantly exceeds train H, suggesting operator instability beyond trained depths.  
- Divergence or oscillation in long rollouts.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Train RSM on Sudoku‑Extreme with base H=5, L=5 and a milestone‑based growth schedule; track solve rate versus total refinement steps.  
2. Ablate the stochastic transition mechanism (compare p_detach=0 vs p_detach=0.99); measure training loss stability and final validation accuracy.  
3. Perform test‑time scaling: after training with H_train=20, run inference with H_test=100, 1 000, and 20 000; plot accuracy versus H_test.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question:** How can RSM be modified to guarantee convergence to correct solutions and avoid spurious attractors or oscillations?  
   - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Convergence is not guaranteed. RSM is trained to behave like a stable operator, but without explicit contraction constraints it may still oscillate or settle to spurious attractors. DEQ‑style techniques—explicit equilibrium solving and implicit differentiation—offer one direction..."  
   - **Why unresolved:** Current training relies on empirical stability without theoretical convergence guarantees, risking failure on complex instances where the learned operator does not contract.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Integrating DEQ‑style equilibrium solving or adding explicit contraction regularizations into RSM, then measuring reduction in non‑convergent trajectories and spurious fixed points on hard puzzles like Maze‑Hard.

2. **Question:** What general principles govern step‑aligned supervision in iterative models (e.g., recursive, diffusion, masked‑refinement) to ensure training‑inference alignment across varying compute budgets?  
   - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Training–inference alignment remains a deep theme... A longer‑term goal is to develop general principles for step‑aligned supervision in iterative models—recursive, masked‑refinement, or diffusion."  
   - **Why unresolved:** The paper notes that misalignment between training and inference step structures can degrade performance, but no unified framework exists to design supervision that generalizes across depths.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic cross‑architecture experiments where supervision granularity (e.g., terminal vs. deep) and step semantics are varied, evaluating the train‑test depth generalization gap across puzzle and generative tasks.

3. **Question:** How can the trade‑off between inference compute (number of steps) and training memory be optimally managed in RSM‑like models for different hardware constraints?  
   - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "The compute–memory frontier is still open. Modern diffusion and masked‑refinement models show many ways to trade ‘number of steps’ against ‘tokens per step’... RSM contributes a complementary approach: keep memory cheap during training via detachment, then spend inference compute where it matters."  
   - **Why unresolved:** While RSM’s detachment reduces training memory, the optimal balance of H/L vs. per‑step complexity (e.g., attention vs. MLP) for maximal accuracy‑per‑flop is unknown.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Profiling memory usage and accuracy across varied L, H, and model widths/attentions on fixed hardware, identifying Pareto frontiers and scaling laws.

4. **Question:** What is the optimal curriculum for growing recursion depths (H and L) during training to maximize stability and sample efficiency?  
   - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "What’s the optimal rate of growth? Can we determine a priority condition for stability and conditions of growth based on information theoretic and/or thermodynamics frameworks? Can we determine the timing of growth based on the behavior of gradients and/or the internal activations?"  
   - **Why unresolved:** The paper uses heuristic milestone‑based schedules without theoretical guidance; growth timing and rate may critically affect convergence and final performance.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparing adaptive curricula (e.g., triggered by gradient variance, activation entropy, or fixed‑point error) against fixed schedules, measuring training stability and final accuracy on Sudoku/Maze.

5. **Question:** How can RSM be extended to open‑ended generation tasks where multiple valid outputs exist and correctness is not easily verifiable, preventing convergence to consistent but undesired fixed points?  
   - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Extending RSM‑style recursion to open‑ended generation (where multiple valid outputs exist) will likely require integrating explicit verification, constraint satisfaction, or preference‑based signals—otherwise fixed points may encode ‘consistent’ but undesired solutions."  
   - **Why unresolved:** Current success hinges on verifier‑rich domains; without such verifiers, RSM may converge to plausible but incorrect or low‑quality outputs.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Applying RSM to text or code generation with learned reward models or human feedback as verifiers, evaluating output quality and diversity relative to standard autoregressive baselines.

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameters (model dimensions, layer counts, batch size, training steps, optimizer settings) are unspecified.  
- Curriculum schedules for H and L growth are described conceptually but lack quantitative details (milestone triggers, increments).  
- Dataset generation parameters (e.g., Sudoku givens count, Maze‑Hard construction) and train/validation/test splits are not provided, hindering exact replication and score validation.
