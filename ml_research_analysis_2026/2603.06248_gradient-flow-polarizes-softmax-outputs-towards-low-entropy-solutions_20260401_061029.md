# Gradient Flow Polarizes Softmax Outputs towards Low-Entropy Solutions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06248
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06248
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Gradient flow on the value-softmax model inherently drives softmax outputs toward one-hot vectors (low entropy) due to the softmax-specific Jacobian, explaining attention sinks in transformers.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why transformer attention often becomes sparse, focusing on the phenomenon of attention sinks. Through analysis of a simplified value-softmax model, the authors prove that gradient flow—without any task-specific inductive bias—polarizes softmax outputs toward one-hot vectors. This polarization arises from the softmax Jacobian, which induces repulsive dynamics between coordinates during training. The effect is strong for classification losses like logistic loss (converging to one-hot) and weaker for regression losses. Empirical comparisons on induction and classification tasks confirm that softmax attention yields significantly more sink formation than alternatives like sigmoid or linear attention. The work provides a theoretical mechanism linking optimization dynamics to observed sparse attention and massive activation phenomena.

## Method Summary
The study combines continuous-time gradient flow analysis with controlled experiments on small transformers. Theoretically, the authors consider the model β = Vσ(a) and derive the coupled dynamics for u = V⊤β* and a under gradient flow. Key insights come from the Jacobian diag(s)−ss⊤, which creates mean-centering and repulsion among softmax scores. They prove order preservation, derive decay rates for non-maximal scores, and show that for logistic loss the integral of the scaling factor diverges, driving the output to a one-hot vector; for regression, convergence is faster and the integral finite, yielding partial sparsity. Experimentally, they train two-layer transformers on an induction task and a one-layer transformer on a classification task, comparing softmax to sigmoid and linear attention variants. They quantify sink-head prevalence, attention entropy, and robustness to token swaps, demonstrating that softmax uniquely promotes low-entropy attention patterns.

## Key Results
- Gradient flow on a value‑softmax model with logistic loss provably drives the softmax output to a one‑hot vector (low entropy) even when dense solutions exist.
- The polarization effect extends to regression losses but is partial; the achieved sparsity depends on the integral of the gradient magnitude, which converges quickly for well‑conditioned targets.
- Multi‑head attention with softmax exhibits far more sink formation and sensitivity to token perturbations compared to alternative attention mechanisms, linking the theory to empirical transformer behavior.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The polarization mechanism originates from the softmax-specific Jacobian \( \text{diag}(s) - ss^\top \), which introduces cross-coordinate interactions absent in element‑wise nonlinearities. Under gradient flow for the value‑softmax model \( \beta = V\sigma(a) \), the dynamics for \( a \) can be written as \( da_i/dt \propto s_i (u_i - \langle u, s \rangle) \). This creates a mean‑centering effect: coordinates with \( u_i \) above the weighted average are amplified relative to those below, inducing repulsion between \( u \)-values and eventual dominance of the largest. For logistic loss, the scaling factor \( \gamma \) grows logarithmically, causing the integral \( \int \gamma dt \) to diverge and forcing the softmax to a one‑hot limit. For regression, \( \gamma \) decays exponentially, so the integral converges and the limit retains some mass on non‑maximal coordinates. In multi‑head self‑attention, each row’s softmax operates independently but under the same polarization mechanism; if all rows share the same target \( \beta^* \), the coordinate with maximal \( u_i(0) \) becomes an attention sink across all positions. This explains the empirical prevalence of sinks and massive activations.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Gradient Flow as Continuous‑Time GD**  
*Why needed:* The analysis studies the continuous‑time limit of gradient descent to isolate the principal directional behavior of first‑order optimization, avoiding discrete‑step artifacts.  
*Quick check:* For \( \beta = V\sigma(a) \), what is \( \nabla_a L \) in terms of \( r = -\nabla_\beta \ell \) and the softmax Jacobian?  
*Answer:* \( (\text{diag}(s) - ss^\top) V^\top r \).

**Concept: Replicator Dynamics Analogy**  
*Why needed:* The polarization mechanism mirrors evolutionary replicator dynamics, where fitness above the mean grows proportionally. This analogy provides intuition for coordinate divergence.  
*Quick check:* In replicator dynamics \( dx_i/dt \propto x_i(f_i(x) - \langle f, x \rangle) \), what does \( \langle f, x \rangle \) represent?  
*Answer:* The weighted average fitness across the population.

**Concept: Softmax Parameterization Invariance**  
*Why needed:* The loss depends only on \( \beta = V\sigma(a) \), not separately on \( V \) and \( a \). The softmax Jacobian is key to polarization; understanding this parameterization reveals why shifts in \( a \) by a constant leave the loss unchanged.  
*Quick check:* Show that shifting \( a \) by a constant does not change \( \sigma(a) \) or \( L \). How does this invariance relate to \( \langle \nabla_a L, 1 \rangle = 0 \)?  
*Answer:* Invariance implies the gradient is orthogonal to the all‑ones direction; indeed \( \nabla_a L \) sums to zero.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Self‑attention output \( X^+ = \sigma(XQK^\top X^\top)XV^\top \) decomposes per token as \( z^+ = \sigma(\bar{a})\bar{V}^\top \), where \( \bar{a} = XKQ^\top z \) and \( \bar{V} = XV^\top \). The core unit studied is the value‑softmax model \( \beta = V\sigma(a) \).

**Critical path:**  
1. Compute gradients: \( \nabla_V L = -r s^\top \), \( \nabla_a L = -(\text{diag}(s)-ss^\top)V^\top r \) with \( r = -\nabla_\beta \ell \).  
2. Introduce \( u = V^\top \beta^* \) (for fixed target \( \beta^* \)) and derive reduced system: \( du/dt = \gamma s\|\beta^*\|^2 \), \( da/dt = \gamma (\text{diag}(s)-ss^\top)u \) with scalar \( \gamma \).  
3. Mean‑centering: \( da_i \propto s_i (u_i - \langle u, s \rangle) \).  
4. Use Lyapunov potential \( \Phi_{ij} = (u_i - u_j)(- (e^{-a_i} - e^{-a_j})) \) to prove order preservation and repulsion (\( d(u_i - u_j) > 0 \) for \( i<j \)).  
5. Track ratios \( q_i = s_i/s_0 \); derive \( dq_i/dt \leq -(\delta/p)\gamma q_i^2 \) yielding \( q_i(t) \leq 1/(1+(\delta/p)\int_0^t \gamma dt) \).  
6. For logistic loss, \( \int_0^t \gamma dt \) diverges (since \( u_0 \) grows logarithmically) → \( s \to e_0 \). For regression, \( \int \gamma dt \) converges → \( q_i(t) \) bounded away from zero.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Sparsity vs. flexibility:** Softmax‑induced polarization yields low‑entropy attention, which can enable efficient KV‑cache compression but may over‑concentrate information and increase vulnerability to single‑token perturbations.  
- **Normalization choice:** Normalized positive functions preserving the mean‑centering structure (e.g., softmax, normalized sigmoid) still induce polarization; unnormalized or linear alternatives remove it.  
- **Loss function:** Classification losses (logistic) drive stronger polarization than regression; the task objective indirectly controls sparsity.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Exploding \( \|u\| \) (especially \( u_0 \)) while other \( u_i \) decay, indicating rank‑one collapse of \( V \).  
- Attention entropy collapse: convergence of softmax to a one‑hot vector when dense solutions exist.  
- High flip rate: predictions that change dramatically when a non‑sink token is swapped with the sink token.  
- Training instability if polarization is extreme (e.g., loss spikes, NaNs).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Logistic loss simulation:** Solve gradient flow for \( V\sigma(a) \) with logistic loss. Verify loss → 0, softmax → one‑hot, \( u_0 \to \infty \) (Fig. 2).  
2. **Regression condition number sweep:** Train on regression problem with varying target condition number; measure integral \( \int \gamma dt \) and resulting sparsity; expect stronger sparsity for higher condition number (Fig. 12).  
3. **Induction task comparison:** Train 2‑layer transformer (4 heads) on induction task using softmax, sigmoid, and linear attention (normalized/unnormalized). Compute proportion of sink heads (≥90% attention to BOS on random positions). Softmax should yield significantly higher sink rate (Fig. 4).

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes strictly ordered initial \( u_i(0) \), uniform \( s_i(0) \), and (for regression) alignment of \( \nabla_\beta \ell \) with \( \beta^* \); these may not hold in practice.
- The continuous‑time gradient flow approximation may differ from discrete optimizers like Adam.
- Experiments are limited to small synthetic models; scalability to large language models remains to be fully validated.

## Confidence
- **Logistic loss → one‑hot polarization:** High (rigorous proof, simulation matches).
- **Regression partial polarization:** Medium (proof relies on gradient alignment; empirical evidence limited).
- **Attention sinks as a consequence of polarization:** Medium (empirical support but simplified model may miss full self‑attention complexities).
- **Softmax‑specific mechanism vs. alternatives:** High (theoretical Jacobian argument and experimental comparisons strongly support).

## Next Checks
1. **Robustness to initialization:** Simulate value‑softmax gradient flow with random (non‑uniform) \( s(0) \) and unordered \( u(0) \) to test if polarization still occurs and whether a one‑hot limit is attained.
2. **Regression sparsity vs. condition number:** In a controlled regression task, vary the condition number of the target \( \beta^* \), measure \( \int_0^t \gamma dt \), and correlate with observed softmax sparsity.
3. **Sink prevalence in larger models:** Train medium‑sized transformers (e.g., 6 layers, 8 heads) on a standard language modeling corpus, quantify per‑layer/head sink occurrence for softmax versus sigmoid/linear attention, and assess how sink frequency scales with model size.
