# VLGOR: Visual-Language Knowledge Guided Offline Reinforcement Learning for Generalizable Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22892  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22892  
- **Reference Count:** 20  
- **Primary Result:** VLGOR enhances offline RL for unseen robotic tasks by generating counterfactual rollouts via a fine-tuned vision-language model, achieving >24% higher success rates (e.g., CLEVR-Robot counterfactual: 15%→39% with BC; Meta-World: +10–14 pp over KALM).

## Executive Summary
VLGOR addresses the key limitation of large language models in reinforcement learning: they lack direct environmental perception. By integrating a vision-language model (BAGEL), VLGOR generates imaginary rollouts from an initial visual observation and a language instruction, using counterfactual prompts to diversify the data. The synthetic rollouts are combined with a limited offline dataset to train a goal-conditioned policy via offline RL algorithms (BC or CQL). This approach yields substantial improvements on CLEVR-Robot and Meta-World benchmarks, particularly on unseen tasks requiring generalization beyond rephrasing. The results demonstrate that vision-language knowledge can effectively augment offline RL data, enabling agents to acquire skills for novel instructions.

## Method Summary
The proposed framework operates in two stages. In Stage 1, a pre-trained BAGEL model is fine‑tuned on a subset of the offline dataset `D` (which pairs language goals `G_k` with trajectories of state–action pairs). Given an initial image `s_0` and a goal `G_k`, the model predicts future states and actions `(s_h, a_h)` for multiple steps, using an MSE loss. After fine‑tuning, counterfactual goals `G'_k` are constructed by modifying the target object or behavior pattern of an original instruction (factorized as `<t, m, b>`). Feeding these new goals and suitable initial states into BAGEL produces a set of synthetic rollouts `R'_k`. Stage 2 merges the real trajectories `D` and synthetic rollouts `R'_k` into an augmented dataset `D_t`. A goal‑conditioned policy with separate BERT text and CNN visual encoders is trained on `D_t` using either behavior cloning or conservative Q‑learning. The policy is then evaluated on held‑out unseen tasks, including instruction rephrasings, counterfactual goals, and hard compositions.

## Key Results
- On CLEVR‑Robot counterfactual tasks, success rate rises from 15% (BC) to 39% (BC+VLGOR).  
- On CLEVR‑Robot hard tasks, success rate leaps from 1% (BC) to 19% (BC+VLGOR).  
- On Meta‑World counterfactual tasks, VLGOR gains 10–14 percentage points over the KALM baseline.  
- Overall, VLGOR yields more than 24% higher success rates compared to baseline methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
VLGOR leverages the rich visual‑language grounding of BAGEL to imagine physically plausible future trajectories from a single starting image and a language goal. The fine‑tuning step adapts BAGEL to the specific task distribution and state‑action dynamics of the target environment, while the counterfactual prompt mechanism systematically explores variations along semantic axes (target object, behavior) to produce diverse synthetic experience. These rollouts fill gaps in the original offline dataset, covering instructions and outcomes that were not observed during data collection. When mixed with real data, the synthetic trajectories expose the offline RL algorithm to a broader distribution, enabling the learned policy to generalize to unseen language specifications. The success of both BC and CQL on the augmented dataset confirms that the generated rollouts are of sufficient quality to be useful for imitation and value‑based learning.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map:**  
  `Initial Image (s0) + Language Goal (G)` → `BAGEL (fine‑tuned)` → `Predicted Trajectory (s_h, a_h)`  
  `Real Trajectories (D) + Synthetic Trajectories (R')` → `Combined Dataset (D_t)` → `Offline RL (BC or CQL)` → `Policy π(a|s, G)`

- **Critical Path:**  
  1. Fine‑tune BAGEL on a subset of `D`.  
  2. Generate counterfactual rollouts for unseen goals.  
  3. Augment dataset with synthetic rollouts.  
  4. Train goal‑conditioned policy on `D_t` using offline RL.  
  5. Evaluate on held‑out unseen tasks.

- **Design Tradeoffs:**  
  *Rollout quality vs. diversity:* Maximizing diversity through aggressive counterfactual modifications can introduce physical implausibility; the paper balances this by using GPT‑generated prompts grounded in the same scene.  
  *Synthetic data ratio:* Too many synthetic rollouts may cause overfitting; the authors find that using 10,000 fine‑tuning trajectories saturates performance.  
  *Joint state‑action prediction:* Simpler than decoupled approaches but risks error accumulation; a progressive (state‑first) strategy is suggested for future work.

- **Failure Signatures:**  
  - Low PSNR/SSIM and high mean Euclidean distance (MED) on a validation set of fine‑tuning data indicate poor rollout fidelity, likely leading to degraded policy performance.  
  - No improvement or performance drop on counterfactual tasks compared to a BC baseline suggests the synthetic rollouts are harmful or misaligned.  
  - Significant gap between training and evaluation success rates during offline RL points to overfitting on synthetic patterns.

- **First Three Experiments:**  
  1. **Rollout Fidelity Check:** Compute PSNR, SSIM, and MED on a held‑out subset of `D` to verify that fine‑tuned BAGEL generates plausible trajectories.  
  2. **Single‑Task Validation:** Train BC with and without VLGOR augmentation on one counterfactual task and compare success rates to confirm the benefit of augmentation.  
  3. **Counterfactual Ablation:** Compare performance when using only rephrasing rollouts versus full counterfactual rollouts to assess the contribution of diverse prompts, especially on Hard Tasks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Can VLGOR generalize effectively to complex, real‑world robotic control scenarios beyond simple simulations? *Basis:* The authors explicitly state that experiments are confined to relatively simple simulations and identify extension to more complex scenarios as a key limitation. *What would resolve:* Test VLGOR on high‑fidelity simulators or physical robots across a wider range of tasks, measuring robustness.  
- **Open Question 2:** How can VLGOR decouple state and action prediction to improve physical consistency and reduce computational workload? *Basis:* The paper notes that joint prediction requires simultaneously learning environment modeling and imitation policies, proposing a progressive strategy as a potential solution. *What would resolve:* Implement and evaluate a two‑stage version where state prediction precedes action conditioning, comparing trajectory quality and training efficiency.  
- **Open Question 3:** To what extent does the choice of vision‑language model affect the quality of generated rollouts and downstream RL performance? *Basis:* The study uses only BAGEL without comparing alternatives, implying an unstated assumption about its sufficiency. *What would resolve:* Replace BAGEL with other VLMs (e.g., BLIP‑2, GATO) and evaluate rollout fidelity (PSNR, SSIM) and success rates on unseen tasks.  
- **Open Question 4:** What counterfactual prompt generation strategies (beyond template‑based swaps) maximize rollout diversity and utility? *Basis:* The paper uses fixed templates but does not explore automated methods or optimize prompt design. *What would resolve:* Develop and test diverse prompt generation methods (e.g., LLM‑generated paraphrases, latent space perturbations), measure trajectory clustering diversity, and assess impact on Hard Tasks.  
- **Open Question 5:** How robust is VLGOR to imperfections in vision‑language model rollouts (e.g., physical inconsistencies, semantic errors)? *Basis:* The paper assumes generated rollouts are “physically plausible” and uses them directly; tolerance of offline RL algorithms to such noise is unexamined. *What would resolve:* Inject controlled noise (e.g., object penetration, action drift) into rollouts, evaluate VLGOR’s success rates under varying noise levels, and determine error thresholds for effective learning.

## Limitations
- BAGEL model code/weights unavailable; high‑level description may not suffice for exact replication.  
- KALM dataset `D` not released; format, splits, and preprocessing details are unclear.  
- Counterfactual prompt generation algorithm (template selection, component modification rules) is underspecified.

## Confidence
- **Core improvement:** Medium – proof‑of‑concept evident but independent verification is hindered by missing artifacts.  
- **Rollout quality metrics:** Low – no ability to recompute PSNR/SSIM/MED without the original implementation.  
- **Generalization over baselines:** Medium – benchmarks are standard, but baseline reimplementation is uncertain.
