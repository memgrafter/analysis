# SciDef: Automating Definition Extraction from Academic Literature with Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.05413  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05413  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Multi-step and DSPy-optimized prompting with an NLI-based metric significantly improves definition extraction, achieving up to 69.7% recall while reducing over-generation compared to one-step baselines.

## Executive Summary
SciDef tackles the growing challenge of extracting definitions from scientific literature using large language models. It introduces two benchmarks: DefExtra (268 definitions from 75 papers) and DefSim (60 definition pairs for similarity). Evaluating 16 language models, the study finds that multi-step extraction pipelines and automated prompt optimization (via DSPy) outperform one-step baselines. An NLI-based similarity metric is validated as the most reliable for evaluation. The best configuration—DSPy with Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 on section chunking—scores 0.397 on the test set and extracts 69.7% of ground-truth definitions. However, models still over-generate, highlighting the need to identify *relevant* definitions rather than merely extracting all definition-like statements.

## Method Summary
The SciDef pipeline begins with PDF ingestion using GROBID to convert documents into structured text. Text is chunked using various strategies (section, paragraph, sentence, sliding windows). An LLM is prompted to extract definition text and a type label (explicit/implicit). Two benchmark datasets are constructed: DefExtra contains human-annotated definitions with context, and DefSim provides definition pairs for similarity assessment. The core evaluation metric is an NLI-based set-level score: predictions are matched to ground truth via bidirectional entailment, with bonuses for type agreement and (during DSPy training) context similarity. Prompting approaches range from one-step to multi-step (with a pre-screening classifier) and are further optimized automatically using DSPy (BootstrapFewShot, MIPROv2) to maximize the NLI score on a development set. Sixteen open-weight and proprietary models are evaluated across these configurations.

## Key Results
- Multi-step and DSPy-optimized prompting significantly improve extraction quality over one-step baselines.
- An NLI-based similarity metric outperforms embedding-based and LLM-as-judge metrics for evaluating definitional equivalence.
- The top model (DSPy + Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507, section chunking) achieves a test NLI score of 0.397 and extracts 69.7% of ground-truth definitions.
- One-step few-shot models can extract up to 86.4% of definitions but exhibit severe over-generation, underscoring the need to filter for relevance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: NLI-based evaluation reliability
- **Claim**: An NLI-based similarity metric provides the most reliable evaluation for definition extraction.
- **Mechanism**: The system computes bidirectional entailment scores between predicted and ground-truth definitions, aggregating them with type-label agreement and (for DSPy) context similarity to produce a set-level score that balances recall-like coverage and precision-like over-generation penalties.
- **Core assumption**: Definitional equivalence is better captured by directional entailment relationships than by symmetric embedding similarity or LLM-as-judge scoring.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "we show that an NLI-based method yields the most reliable results."
  - Section 3.2: Detailed comparison across STS-B, STS3k, SICK, MSRP, QQP datasets shows NLI achieves best F1 in 5 of 7 benchmarks; manual validation on DefSim confirms correlation with human judgment.
- **Break condition**: If target domain definitions exhibit high lexical overlap but divergent semantic scope (e.g., interdisciplinary term drift), NLI may under-penalize mismatched scopes compared to context-aware metrics.

### Mechanism 2: DSPy-optimized prompting
- **Claim**: DSPy-optimized prompting autonomously improves extraction quality over manually designed prompts.
- **Mechanism**: DSPy compiles the pipeline by treating prompting steps as modular programs; its optimizers (MIPROv2, BootstrapFewShot) search for prompt instructions and few-shot samples that maximize the chosen NLI-based metric on dev data, implicitly adapting to model-specific behaviors and domain nuances.
- **Core assumption**: The NLI-based set score is a differentiable proxy for real-world definition relevance, allowing automated prompt search to surpass static human prompts.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "multi-step and DSPy-optimized prompting significantly improve extraction over one-step baselines."
  - Section 4.2: Top-performing configurations use DSPy (e.g., Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 with section chunking, test NLI 0.397); Figure 2 shows DSPy-based prompts generally outperform manual ones.
- **Break condition**: If the dev set distribution diverges significantly from test papers (e.g., different scientific subdomains or writing styles), DSPy’s optimized prompts may overfit and underperform on unseen paper types.

### Mechanism 3: Multi-step extraction pipeline
- **Claim**: Multi-step extraction pipelines (with a pre-screening step) reduce over-generation compared to one-step extraction.
- **Mechanism**: The pipeline first classifies whether a text chunk contains a definition before attempting extraction, filtering out non-definitional content early; this contrasts with one-step models that generate candidate definitions from every chunk, increasing false positives.
- **Core assumption**: A binary "contains definition" classifier can be learned or prompted effectively and will prune irrelevant extractions without sacrificing recall of true definitions.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "multi-step and DSPy-optimized prompting improve extraction performance" and "models tend to over-generate them."
  - Section 3.3: Describes MultiStep design where model first judges presence before extraction; Table 4 shows MultiStep strategies average higher NLI scores than OneStep.
  - Section 4.2 & Figure 6: DSPy (which includes multi-step logic) averages lower predicted definitions per paper than OneStep-FS (531.6 vs. 10.1), indicating reduced over-generation.
- **Break condition**: If definitions are highly implicit or distributed across non-adjacent sentences, the pre-screening step may discard chunks containing partial definitional content, harming recall.

## Foundational Learning
- **Natural Language Inference (NLI) for semantic equivalence**  
  *Why needed*: The evaluation metric relies on bidirectional entailment to judge if two definitions express the same concept, going beyond word overlap.  
  *Quick check*: Why would symmetric cosine similarity fail to identify that a verbose definition and a concise paraphrase are semantically equivalent?

- **Prompt engineering for information extraction**  
  *Why needed*: The pipeline’s performance hinges on effective prompts for classification and extraction; multi-step designs require coordinating separate prompts.  
  *Quick check*: What is a key risk of using one-step "extract all definitions" prompts versus a two-step "filter then extract" design?

- **Definition extraction vs. term extraction**  
  *Why needed*: The task targets author-stated definitions (explicit/implicit), not just term mentions; this distinction affects dataset construction and evaluation.  
  *Quick check*: How might an extractor that pulls all noun phrases containing a target term differ from SciDef’s output on a media bias paper?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: PDF ingestion -> Chunking -> LLM extractor -> Scoring module -> Optimization loop (DSPy)
- **Critical path**: PDF → Chunk → LLM extraction → Scoring → (DSPy) Prompt optimization. Any break in chunking or scoring invalidates results.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Chunk size*: Larger chunks preserve context but risk LLM attention overflow; smaller chunks may split definitions. Section-level chosen for DSPy to match context span.
  - *Metric strictness*: NLI threshold (τ=0.25) filters spurious matches but may penalize valid paraphrases; threshold tuned qualitatively.
  - *DSPy cost*: Optimization requires many LLM calls on dev set; manual prompts cheaper but less adaptive.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - *Over-generation*: Many predicted definitions, low precision-like match_P; common in OneStep-FS.
  - *Context mismatch*: DSPy extractor predicts context that doesn’t align with gold, hurting score.
  - *Domain shift*: High scores on media-bias papers but collapse on added non-media papers.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Validate NLI metric: Run all metrics (NLI, embeddings, LLM-judge) on STS-B/STS3k; confirm NLI best F1 at GT threshold 0.90.
  2. Compare chunking strategies: On a fixed model (e.g., Qwen3-30B), test section vs. paragraph vs. three-sentence window; measure NLI score and average definitions per paper.
  3. Test DSPy vs. manual: For a model like Magistral-Small, run MultiStep-FS (manual) and DSPyPaperExtractor with MIPROv2; compare test scores and over-generation rates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question**: How can we effectively filter LLM-extracted definitions to identify only the *relevant* ones, given that models tend to over-generate definitional statements that are not included in the human-annotated ground truth?  
  *Basis*: Abstract and Section 5 state that future work should focus on identifying relevant definitions, as models over-generate them.  
  *Why unresolved*: The current pipeline optimizes for extraction coverage and similarity to gold, but lacks a principled method to judge conceptual relevance to the paper’s core themes or user queries.

- **Question**: What is the performance ceiling for definition extraction when using the most capable frontier LLMs (e.g., GPT-5, Gemini 3 Pro), and do they fundamentally alter the relevance vs. recall trade-off?  
  *Basis*: Section 5 notes that frontier models were excluded due to cost, representing a key limitation.  
  *Why unresolved*: It is unknown whether superior reasoning and instruction-following capabilities would reduce over-generation, improve contextual understanding, or yield higher NLI scores.

- **Question**: To what extent are the findings on optimal prompting strategies (multi-step, DSPy) and the NLI evaluation metric generalizable beyond the media bias domain and the modest DefExtra dataset?  
  *Basis*: Section 3.1 and 5 acknowledge the dataset’s modest size and domain concentration (primarily media bias), limiting generalizability.  
  *Why unresolved*: The dataset covers only 268 definitions from 75 papers in a single domain; optimal configurations may differ for other scientific fields with different writing conventions.

## Limitations
- Exact train/validation/test splits for DefExtra are unreported, risking split-dependent performance variability.
- Full prompt templates for manual baselines (OneStep, MultiStep) are unspecified, hindering precise replication.
- The dataset is small and domain-concentrated (media bias), so generalizability to other scientific fields is unvalidated.
- Over-generation persists; the core challenge of identifying *relevant* definitions remains unsolved.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-domain NLI test**: Evaluate the NLI metric on definition pairs from a disparate field (e.g., computational mathematics) to quantify performance drop under lexical overlap/semantic scope mismatches.
2. **DSPy split stability**: Re-run DSPy optimization on 5 random data splits; report variance in test NLI scores to assess overfitting sensitivity.
3. **Multi-step recall ablation**: For the top DSPy model, ablate the pre-screening step and measure recall change on definitions marked as *implicit* in DefExtra to directly test the recall-risk break condition.
