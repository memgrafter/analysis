# Opportunities and Challenges of Natural Language Processing for Low-Resource Senegalese Languages in Social Science Research

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.09716
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09716
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** First extensive overview identifying severe data scarcity beyond Wolof, a living open-access repository to foster collaboration and reproducibility, and a roadmap emphasizing ethical data governance, open resources, and interdisciplinary partnerships for sustainable NLP ecosystems in social science contexts.

## Executive Summary
Senegalese national languages, aside from Wolof, remain severely underrepresented in natural language processing (NLP), hindering social science research that relies on multilingual qualitative data. This paper surveys the state of NLP for six low-resource Senegalese languages (Wolof, Pulaar, Sereer, Joola, Mandingue, Soninké), compiles available datasets and tools into a centralized GitHub repository, and proposes a roadmap for sustainable, community-centered development. The work highlights data scarcity, ethical and governance challenges, and the potential of NLP to significantly reduce transcription costs and broaden participation in social research. It calls for interdisciplinary collaboration, ethical data practices, and open resource sharing to build robust NLP pipelines that respect local contexts.

## Method Summary
The study combines a comprehensive literature survey with resource aggregation. Researchers identified existing corpora, tools, and benchmarks for the target languages through systematic collection and verification, resulting in a living GitHub repository that tracks publicly accessible resources across NLP tasks. The paper synthesizes linguistic characteristics, infrastructural gaps, and sociotechnical factors, and illustrates potential impact through a case study on multilingual transcription, translation, and analysis in social science. The roadmap emphasizes ethical data governance, open resources, and interdisciplinary partnerships as key pillars for sustainable ecosystem development.

## Key Results
- Centralized open-access repository (https://github.com/DerXter/State-of-NLP-Research-in-Senegal) compiling datasets, tools, and benchmarks for multiple NLP tasks across six Senegalese languages.
- Identification of severe data scarcity for Pulaar, Sereer, Joola, Mandingue, and Soninké, with Wolof being the only language with moderate resources.
- Roadmap outlining ethical, legal, and governance considerations, and stressing the need for community involvement, data sovereignty, and regional collaboration to enable NLP-augmented social science workflows.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Centralized Resource Aggregation
*Claim:* Compiling datasets, tools, and benchmarks into a single GitHub repository reduces fragmentation and enables collaborative progress on low-resource Senegalese languages.  
*Mechanism:* The repository acts as a living, open-access catalog that tracks existing resources and gaps, making it easier for researchers to find, reuse, and contribute data and tools, thereby lowering entry barriers.  
*Core assumption:* Open sharing and sustained community contributions will accelerate resource development and prevent duplicated efforts.  
*Evidence anchors:* The abstract and Section 3 describe the repository’s role in tracking datasets and its design as a living resource. The analogous approach in the Ethiopian languages survey supports its effectiveness.  
*Break condition:* If the repository becomes unmaintained or community contributions cease, fragmentation re-emerges and the coordination benefit dissipates.

### Mechanism 2: Ethical and Community-Centered Governance
*Claim:* Embedding ethical data governance and interdisciplinary partnerships ensures sustainable, culturally appropriate NLP development that respects local contexts.  
*Mechanism:* The roadmap emphasizes ethical frameworks (e.g., CDP/SODAV compliance), data sovereignty, and collaboration between linguists, computer scientists, policymakers, and community groups to guide data collection, model building, and deployment.  
*Core assumption:* Top-down technical solutions without genuine community involvement risk bias, misuse, and rejection by the populations they aim to serve.  
*Evidence anchors:* The abstract and Sections 6.1–6.2 stress ethical, legal, and governance challenges and opportunities for regional collaboration. The Ethiopian languages survey similarly highlights community involvement.  
*Break condition:* If ethical guidelines are ignored or partnerships remain superficial, systems may embed cultural biases, violate privacy, or fail to meet user needs, leading to distrust and abandonment.

### Mechanism 3: NLP-Augmented Social Science Workflows
*Claim:* Integrating NLP pipelines (ASR, MT, QA) into social science research can drastically reduce transcription/translation costs and enable larger-scale qualitative analysis of multilingual field data.  
*Mechanism:* Automated processing of interview and focus-group recordings in national languages allows researchers to skip manual transcription, accelerate thematic analysis, and include non-French-speaking participants more equitably.  
*Core assumption:* Current NLP tools, even if imperfect, can provide useful approximations when combined with human validation, thereby expanding the scope of social science research.  
*Evidence anchors:* The abstract and Section 5 detail how NLP can transform qualitative methodologies, citing examples like sentiment analysis of social media and chatbot-based data collection. Neighboring work on generative AI for NER in Nepali reinforces the applicability.  
*Break condition:* If NLP errors propagate unchecked into research findings, they may distort analysis; therefore, rigorous validation against human-coded ground truth is essential.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data acquisition & curation -> Preprocessing & normalization -> Modeling layer -> Evaluation & benchmarking -> Deployment & monitoring -> Governance & compliance  
**Critical path:** Resource assessment -> Foundational corpus building (minimum viable speech/text data) -> Basic tokenizer/normalizer development -> Fine-tune a multilingual model -> Language-specific evaluation -> Iterative improvement with community feedback -> Deployment  
**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Data quantity vs. quality:* Rapid crowdsourcing yields volume but may introduce noise; balanced annotation yields reliability but is slower.  
- *Model size vs. accessibility:* Larger models (e.g., LLMs) perform better but demand high compute; consider distillation or smaller architectures for local deployment.  
- *Open data vs. privacy:* Maximizing reuse requires open licenses, but social science data often includes identifiable information; anonymization may reduce utility.  
**Failure signatures:**  
- Poor coverage of non‑Latin scripts (Ajami) or minority dialects, leading to biased performance.  
- High WER/BLEU gaps between test and real‑world domains (e.g., social media vs. formal text).  
- Lack of community engagement: no contributions to the GitHub repo or feedback on deployed tools.  
**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Wolof ASR** – Fine-tune Whisper‑small on combined ALFFA/Dvoice data; report WER on a held‑out set; test speed perturbation for robustness.  
2. **Cross‑lingual NER transfer** – Use Pulaar annotations to improve Wolof NER via adapter fusion or multilingual training; measure F1 gain over monolingual baseline.  
3. **Ethical audit simulation** – Remove PII from a social‑science interview corpus; compare model performance before/after removal to quantify trade‑off between privacy and utility.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What participatory and sustainable data collection strategies can effectively build large, high-quality corpora for the under-resourced Senegalese languages (Pulaar, Sereer, Joola, Mandingue, Soninké) while ensuring community involvement and long-term maintenance?  
- **Basis:** Inferred from the identified severe data scarcity beyond Wolof in the key outcome and Table 1 showing a stark imbalance in available resources.  
- **Why unresolved:** Current efforts are fragmented, underfunded, and often exclude native speaker communities from design and ownership; there is no scalable model for ongoing data curation that aligns with local needs and ethical standards.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Documented pilot projects demonstrating community-led data collection with clear consent protocols, quality assessment metrics for corpora, and sustainable governance models (e.g., data cooperatives) that ensure long-term accessibility and benefit-sharing.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can NLP systems be made robust to the digraphic nature (Latin and Ajami) and orthographic inconsistencies of Senegalese languages, particularly for tasks like text normalization and spell checking?  
- **Basis:** Inferred from Sections 2.1 and 4.4, which highlight challenges in transliteration, script diversity, and the lack of standardized resources for Ajami.  
- **Why unresolved:** Existing approaches rely on small, low-coverage parallel corpora and rule-based methods that fail to generalize across dialects and code-switching; no unified benchmark exists for evaluating script-agnostic processing.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Large, balanced Latin-Ajami parallel datasets for multiple languages; standardized evaluation benchmarks for transliteration and normalization; and models that jointly leverage both scripts, showing significant improvement on downstream tasks like machine translation or sentiment analysis.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What governance frameworks can effectively address issues of consent, cultural ownership, and data sovereignty for language resources derived from oral traditions and social research in Senegal?  
- **Basis:** Inferred from Section 6.1 on ethical, legal, and governance challenges, and the reference to the UNESCO AI Readiness Assessment Methodology's call for a centralized governance body.  
- **Why unresolved:** National policies are underdeveloped; community voices are often absent from data stewardship decisions; and tensions exist between open-science ideals and indigenous rights, risking extractive practices.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implemented data-sharing agreements co-created with local communities, legally validated frameworks for benefit-sharing, and audits of existing repositories against principles like the Senegal CDP and SODAV, with measurable outcomes in community trust and data control.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What end-to-end pipelines and validation protocols can responsibly integrate NLP tools (ASR, MT, sentiment analysis) into multilingual social science research in Senegal, ensuring accuracy, privacy, and cultural sensitivity?  
- **Basis:** Inferred from Section 5's case study, which identifies potential gains but highlights ethical pitfalls, data sensitivity, and lack of validated workflows for low-resource contexts.  
- **Why unresolved:** Current tools have high error rates for non-Wolof languages; privacy-preserving methods for sensitive qualitative data are untested; and there are no standards for interdisciplinary validation between NLP engineers and social scientists.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Deployed, documented pipelines in real social research projects with error analyses disaggregated by language and domain, integration of privacy-enhancing technologies (e.g., differential privacy), and co-evaluation frameworks where social scientists assess NLP outputs for cultural relevance and research utility.

## Limitations
- Severe data scarcity for five of six languages, with unknown corpus sizes and splits.
- No code, hyperparameters, or dataset splits provided for key models, hindering exact replication.
- Repository sustainability is uncertain; volunteer-run without dedicated funding or governance may lead to abandonment.

## Confidence
- Centralized resource aggregation: Medium (repository exists but maintenance uncertain).
- Ethical and community-centered governance: Low (roadmap conceptual, no evidence of implementation).
- NLP-augmented social science workflows: Medium (theoretical benefits, limited validation).
- Overall feasibility for low-resource languages: Low (severe data scarcity and reproducibility gaps).
