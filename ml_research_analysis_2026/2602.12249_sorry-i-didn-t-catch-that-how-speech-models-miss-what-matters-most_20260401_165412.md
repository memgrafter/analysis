# "Sorry, I Didn't Catch That": How Speech Models Miss What Matters Most

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.12249
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12249
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: Fine-tuning with fewer than 1,000 synthetic samples improves street name transcription accuracy by nearly 60% relative to base models for non-English primary speakers.

## Executive Summary
State-of-the-art speech recognition systems struggle with short, high-stakes utterances such as street names, showing a 44% average error rate across 15 commercial models. Errors are markedly worse for non-English primary speakers, who experience nearly double the routing distance error compared to English-only speakers, highlighting a critical reliability and equity gap. To address this, the authors generate synthetic speech by using multilingual text-to-speech (XTTS) to produce accented pronunciations of English street names embedded in foreign-language prompts. This synthetic data—less than 1,000 examples—enables fine-tuning of Whisper models, yielding a ~60% relative accuracy gain for non-English speakers and a scalable path to robust real-world deployment.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates ASR performance on two dedicated street-name transcription datasets (SF Streets and US Streets), revealing high error rates and disparities linked to speaker language background. The proposed mitigation involves a synthetic data pipeline: select non-English languages supported by XTTS, clone voices from Common Voice speakers, prompt XTTS to generate sentences in those languages containing English street names (e.g., “Estoy en Washington”), then programmatically extract the audio segment for the street name. The resulting <1,000 samples are used to fine-tune Whisper-Base with a low learning rate (1e-5) and early stopping. Fine-tuned models are evaluated on held-out real speech, measuring transcription error via phonetic matching and downstream routing error via Google Maps. The approach improves accuracy significantly, especially for non-English primary speakers, with minimal data and compute.

## Key Results
- Overall transcription error rate on street names averages 44% across 15 commercial ASR systems.
- Non-English primary speakers face nearly 2× higher routing distance error than English-only speakers due to mis-transcriptions.
- Fine-tuning Whisper with <1,000 synthetic samples yields a 60% relative accuracy improvement for non-English speakers and reduces routing error.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Synthetic accent generation via foreign‑language TTS
- **Claim:** XTTS, when prompted in a foreign language with an embedded English street name, transfers the phonetic patterns of the source language onto the English word, generating plausible non‑native pronunciations.
- **Mechanism:** A voice is cloned from a monolingual speaker in a supported language (e.g., Spanish). The TTS model then synthesizes a foreign‑language phrase containing the English street name. Because the model is operating in a monolingual mode, its acoustic characteristics (vowel quality, consonant realization, prosody) are imposed on the out‑of‑vocabulary English segment.
- **Core assumption:** Voice‑cloning TTS models encode language‑specific phonetic styles that extend to foreign words embedded in the prompt.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 6.1 describes the breakthrough; abstract notes “synthetic speech data… diverse pronunciations.”
- **Break condition:** TTS fails to preserve the source language’s phonetic envelope or produces unintelligible audio.

### Mechanism 2: Fine‑tuning adapts ASR to accented variations
- **Claim:** Fine‑tuning a pre‑trained ASR (Whisper‑base) on the synthetic data updates its acoustic and language models to recognize the induced accent patterns, reducing errors on real non‑native speech.
- **Mechanism:** The small synthetic dataset (<1,000 examples) trains the model to map accented acoustic patterns to correct orthography, increasing robustness to pronunciation diversity without catastrophic forgetting.
- **Core assumption:** The distribution of synthetic accented pronunciations overlaps sufficiently with real non‑native speech acoustics.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports ~60% relative gain; section 6.2 details fine‑tuning hyperparameters (batch 16, lr 1e‑5) and Figure 7 shows improvement.
- **Break condition:** Overfitting to synthetic artifacts (unnatural prosody) or inability to generalize beyond the synthetic accent set.

### Mechanism 3: Disproportionate benefit for non‑English speakers
- **Claim:** Because the synthetic pronunciations echo L1 interference patterns, non‑English primary speakers—whose English is shaped by similar phonetic transfer—experience larger gains.
- **Mechanism:** Training on 16 diverse foreign languages exposes the model to a wide range of accent features that align with those of non‑native speakers in the test set, even if the exact L1 is not covered.
- **Core assumption:** The accent transfer from foreign‑language TTS approximates the L1‑interference patterns of the study’s non‑English‑primary participants.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5 shows 18% baseline gap; section 6 reports 116% gain for non‑English and 60% for multilingual speakers after fine‑tuning.
- **Break condition:** If a participant’s L1 is not represented among the 16 TTS languages, alignment may be weak (e.g., Vietnamese speakers in SF Streets).

## Foundational Learning
**Phonetic Variation in ASR:**  
*Why needed:* Street‑name errors stem from models failing on out‑of‑distribution pronunciations, especially for non‑native speakers. Understanding how L1 influences English phonetics (vowel reduction, consonant substitution) is key to diagnosing the failure.  
*Quick check:* How might a Spanish speaker’s pronunciation of “Washington” differ from a native speaker’s, and why would an ASR model trained on mainstream English data struggle?

**Synthetic Data Generation for Low‑Resource Speech:**  
*Why needed:* Collecting thousands of real accented street‑name utterances is expensive. The paper’s solution uses TTS to cheaply generate diverse, targeted training data. Understanding trade‑offs (accent authenticity, coverage) is crucial for replication.  
*Quick check:* What are two risks of using TTS‑generated speech to train ASR models for real accent adaptation?

**Fine‑tuning Large Pre‑trained ASR Models:**  
*Why needed:* Whisper models are pre‑trained on massive datasets but fail on this narrow, high‑stakes task. Fine‑tuning adapts their parameters to the new distribution without full retraining.  
*Quick check:* Why might a small learning rate (1e‑5) and early stopping be appropriate when fine‑tuning Whisper on <1k samples?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Common Voice (speaker clips) → XTTS (voice cloning + prompt with embedded street name) → generated full‑sentence audio → Data extraction (isolate street name audio) → Synthetic dataset → Fine‑tune Whisper → Evaluation (SF Streets / US Streets / routing).

**Critical path:**  
Select TTS source languages → pick Common Voice speaker clips → generate foreign‑language utterances with embedded street names → extract audio → manually validate → fine‑tune Whisper → evaluate on held‑out real speakers.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Synthetic data diversity vs. quality:* Adding more languages/speakers increases accent coverage but risks lower fidelity if TTS degrades with mixed‑language prompts.
- *Model size vs. deployability:* Larger Whisper variants give higher baseline accuracy but fine‑tuning them is costlier; the paper shows gains across sizes.
- *Data quantity:* <1k samples is lightweight yet may not cover all street‑name/accents combinations; out‑of‑distribution generalization is limited.

**Failure signatures:**
- Poor audio extraction: TTS may not cleanly separate the embedded English word from foreign context.
- Accent mismatch: The 16 TTS languages may omit some participants’ L1s (e.g., Vietnamese), relying on generalization.
- Evaluation leakage: Synthetic data accidentally including phrases from the test set would invalidate gains.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Baseline replication: Evaluate Whisper‑base on SF Streets to confirm ~44% overall error and the ~18% gap between English‑only and non‑English speakers.
2. Single‑language pilot: Implement the synthetic pipeline for one TTS‑supported language (e.g., Spanish), generate 100 samples, validate quality and intelligibility, and identify extraction issues.
3. Fine‑tuning sanity check: Fine‑tune Whisper‑base on the 100 Spanish samples and evaluate on held‑out synthetic data to confirm the model learns the accent pattern before testing on real human speech.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Question 1:** Why do context‑providing prompts fail to significantly improve transcription accuracy for short, high‑stakes named entities like street names, even when the full set of possible street names is supplied?  
*Basis:* The authors tested promptable models with situational framing and an “oracle” prompt listing all target street names, yet accuracy only rose to 76%, indicating recognition and selection errors—not lack of context—are the primary bottleneck.  
*Why unresolved:* The paper identifies phonetic ambiguity and mishearing as culprits but does not dissect the specific model behaviors or acoustic‑phonetic features that cause these errors to persist despite strong contextual cues.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Fine‑grained error analysis comparing model internal representations (e.g., attention patterns, phoneme‑level confusions) when processing street names with and without context, especially for similar‑sounding entities.

**Question 2:** What specific phonetic or prosodic variations introduced by the synthetic multilingual training data lead to improved recognition for non‑English primary speakers, and why does aggregated training across many languages outperform training on any single language?  
*Basis:* Training on synthetic data from all 16 languages jointly yields a 28% accuracy gain, while any single language yields only 12–18%. The paper notes no direct transfer from a trained language to speakers of that language, suggesting a generalizability effect.  
*Why unresolved:* The mechanism behind why exposure to diverse, accent‑mimicking synthetic pronunciations helps models handle real, unseen accented speech is not explained. It is unclear whether models learn robust phoneme representations, accent‑invariant features, or something else.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Ablation studies controlling for phonetic diversity (e.g., measuring phoneme distribution shifts), speaker variation, and linguistic family similarity in the synthetic data. Per‑language analysis of which phonetic traits correlate with improved performance on specific language backgrounds.

**Question 3:** To what extent do the synthetic pronunciations generated via foreign‑language TTS voice cloning accurately represent the true acoustic‑phonetic characteristics of non‑English‑primary speakers’ accented speech, and what are the limitations of this approach for covering the full spectrum of real‑world pronunciation variability?  
*Basis:* The method relies on XTTS imposing a “canonical” speaking style of the target foreign language onto English words. The authors acknowledge synthetic data “is unlikely to fully reflect the nuances of real human speech” and that XTTS may “systematically normalize or suppress foreign accents.” Success in improving real‑speech performance suggests some transfer, but ecological validity is unquantified.  
*Why unresolved:* The paper demonstrates utility but does not measure how well the synthetic accent patterns align with actual pronunciation errors made by non‑English‑primary speakers in the dataset. This limits understanding of which aspects of accented speech are being effectively modeled.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Acoustic phonetic comparison (e.g., formant analysis, vowel space, duration patterns) between synthetic samples, real utterances from non‑English‑primary speakers, and a baseline of native English speech. A study measuring correlation between synthetic data characteristics and performance gains on specific speaker subgroups.

**Question 4:** How do transcription errors and their downstream routing impact scale to other high‑stakes domains beyond ride‑hailing (e.g., emergency dispatch, delivery services, public transit), and are the observed disparities for non‑English‑primary speakers exacerbated in these contexts?  
*Basis:* The financial and delay analysis is confined to San Francisco taxi trips, using conservative estimates that assume all riders are English‑primary. The authors state this is a lower bound and that non‑English‑primary speakers experience nearly double the routing distance error. They also mention deployment in emergency call centers but do not analyze impact there.  
*Why unresolved:* Extrapolation to other domains is speculative. Different domains may have different tolerance for errors, query structures (e.g., multi‑entity addresses), and demographic usage patterns, which could alter both error rate and equity implications.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Collection and evaluation of similar street‑name/address transcription datasets from other high‑stakes domains (e.g., 911 call transcripts, delivery app voice commands). Simulation of routing errors using map APIs for those domains, stratified by speaker language background. Qualitative studies with domain experts (dispatchers, emergency responders) on error consequences.

**Question 5:** Why does fine‑tuning with synthetic data lead to larger relative improvements for users who had the worst baseline transcription accuracy, and what does this imply about the type of data deficiency causing high‑stakes failures?  
*Basis:* In the out‑of‑distribution street name experiment, the change in accuracy after fine‑tuning is strongly predicted by baseline accuracy (R² = 0.185, p<0.001): the worse the original transcription, the larger the gain. The authors suggest synthetic training can help users “whose voices suffered the worst error rates.”  
*Why unresolved:* The paper does not investigate whether the worst baseline errors correlate with specific phonetic, prosodic, or language‑background characteristics. It is unknown if the synthetic data patches a general robustness issue or specifically targets rare or difficult pronunciation patterns that were underrepresented in the original model training.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Error typology analysis on the worst‑performing subset (e.g., confusion matrices for street names, analysis of phoneme error rates). Controlled experiments where synthetic data is crafted to target specific error types (e.g., fricative confusion, stress shifts) to see which yield the biggest gains for worst‑case users.

## Limitations
- Synthetic data authenticity: Accent transfer via XTTS may not fully capture the acoustic‑phonetic details of real non‑native speech, risking a mismatch between synthetic and real distributions.
- Language coverage gap: The 16 TTS languages may not align with all L1 backgrounds of test participants (e.g., Vietnamese), so gains for uncovered languages rely on uncertain generalization.
- Real‑world deployment blind spots: Evaluation uses read speech templates; performance on spontaneous, noisy, or heavily mispronounced street names in actual navigation contexts remains unknown.

## Confidence
- **High error rates on short, high‑stakes utterances (44% avg):** High
- **Disproportionate errors for non‑English primary speakers (routing):** Medium‑High
- **Synthetic data fine‑tuning yields ~60% relative gain:** Medium

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the end‑to‑end synthetic pipeline for 3–5 TTS languages, then perform forced alignment and acoustic analysis (e.g., formant, duration) to validate that extracted street‑name audio exhibits intended accent characteristics and aligns with real non‑native speech.
2. Evaluate fine‑tuned models separately on participants whose L1 is covered by the TTS languages (e.g., Spanish, Mandarin) versus uncovered (e.g., Vietnamese). A significant performance drop for uncovered L1s would confirm the language‑coverage limitation.
3. Test generalization by constructing two held‑out sets: (a) novel pronunciations of training street names by real non‑native speakers, and (b) entirely new street names never seen in synthetic data. Compare error reduction on both sets to detect overfitting or lack of OOD robustness.
