# Influence Malleability in Linearized Attention: Dual Implications of Non-Convergent NTK Dynamics

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.13085
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13085
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Linearized attention's NTK does not converge at practical widths due to spectral amplification (condition number cubed), leading to high influence malleability (6–9× higher than ReLU).

## Executive Summary
Attention mechanisms are known to deviate from the lazy-training regime predicted by Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory. This work investigates why, using a parameter-free linearized attention model that exactly corresponds to a data-dependent Gram-induced kernel. They show empirically that, unlike a two-layer ReLU network whose NTK distance to the infinite-width limit decreases with width, MLP-Attn’s distance remains high or even increases on MNIST and CIFAR-10. Theoretically, they prove that the attention transformation cubes the Gram matrix’s condition number, requiring impractical width \(m = \Omega(\kappa^6)\) for convergence. This non-convergence is quantified via *influence malleability*—the rate at which influential training examples flip their helpful/harmful status under adversarial perturbation. MLP-Attn exhibits 6–9× higher flip rates than ReLU. The findings reveal a trade-off: the data-dependent kernel can reduce approximation error by aligning with task structure, but the same sensitivity increases vulnerability to data manipulation, linking attention’s power and vulnerability to its departure from the kernel regime.

## Method Summary
The authors compare two architectures: a two-layer ReLU network (2L-ReLU) and an MLP with a linearized attention preprocessing layer (MLP-Attn). The attention layer computes \(f_{\text{att}}(X) = X X^\top X\), a parameter-free operation applied to the entire training set \(X\), yielding a transductive representation. Both architectures share the same subsequent MLP head (width \(m\)) with fixed first-layer signs and Gaussian weights. Models are trained on binary classification tasks using MNIST and CIFAR-10 (e.g., digits 3 vs 8, cars vs planes). Training uses Adam for 500 epochs with L2 regularization. Key evaluation metrics are: NTK distance, measuring L2 deviation between finite-width predictions \(f_m\) and infinite-width NTK predictor \(f_{\text{NTK}}\); and influence malleability, defined as the flip rate of sign for the top 10% positively influential training points after adversarial PGD perturbation (\(\varepsilon=0.3\)). Influence scores are computed via the NTK-based leave-one-out formula \((K_m + \lambda I)^{-1} y\) without retraining. Theoretical analysis derives the condition number amplification: for data Gram matrix \(G = X X^\top\), the transformed Gram \(\tilde{G}\) used in the attention kernel is \(G^3\), so \(\kappa(\tilde{G}) = \kappa(G)^3\), implying required width \(m = \Omega(\kappa(G)^6 n \log n / \varepsilon^2)\) for NTK convergence.

## Key Results
- MLP-Attn’s NTK distance does not decrease monotonically with width and can increase (CIFAR-10) or plateau (MNIST), contrasting with 2L-ReLU which shows clear convergence.
- Theoretical bound: attention cubing condition number forces \(m\) to be impractically large (e.g., if \(\kappa(G) \sim 10^3\), then \(m > 10^{18}\)) to achieve standard NTK convergence.
- Across datasets and attacks (FGSM, PGD, MIM), MLP-Attn’s influence flip rates are 6–9 times higher than those of 2L-ReLU, demonstrating high influence malleability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1** (Spectral amplification): The linearized attention transformation \(f_{\text{att}}(X) = X X^\top X\) applies a cubic spectral transformation to the data Gram matrix \(G = X X^\top\). If \(G\) has eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\), the effective Gram becomes \(\tilde{G}\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_i^3\), amplifying the condition number \(\kappa(\tilde{G}) = (\lambda_1/\lambda_n)^3 = \kappa(G)^3\). This inflation of the finite-width NTK’s spectral gap requires width \(m = \Omega(\kappa(G)^6 n \log n / \varepsilon^2)\) for convergence. For natural datasets, \(\kappa(G)\) can be \(\sim10^3\), making the required width astronomically large and practically unattainable.

**Mechanism 2** (Influence malleability): The data-dependent kernel \(K_{\text{LinAttn}} = G^3\) creates global coupling: a perturbation to one example influences others through a cubic chain of inner products. Consequently, influence scores (computed via \((K_m+\lambda I)^{-1}y\)) become highly sensitive. When adversarial perturbations are applied to influential examples, the sign of their influence flips at a rate 6–9× higher than in ReLU networks. This high flip rate, termed influence malleability, stems from the transitive amplification of perturbations across the cubic structure.

**Mechanism 3** (Dual implications): The same data-dependent cubic kernel yields a trade-off. Because \(G^3\) shares eigenvectors with \(G\), it can represent target functions with spectral energy aligned with \(G\)’s top modes more efficiently than a polynomial kernel that mixes eigenbases, reducing approximation error (bias). However, this alignment makes influence scores extremely sensitive; the Lipschitz constant of influence with respect to inputs scales as \(O(n \|G\|^2)\) for attention versus \(O(1)\) for ReLU. Thus, attention’s expressive power and its vulnerability to data manipulation both originate from its departure from the lazy-training kernel regime.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Data X -> Linearized attention \(f_{\text{att}}(X) = X X^\top X\) (parameter-free, transductive) -> Row-normalized outputs -> MLP head (two-layer ReLU, width \(m\)) -> Predictions

Critical path: The Gram matrix \(G = X X^\top\) is central. Its condition number \(\kappa(G)\) directly determines the amplification factor \(\kappa(G)^3\) and thus the impractically large width required for NTK convergence. Any preprocessing (e.g., normalization) that affects \(G\) changes the behavior.

Design tradeoffs:
- Expressivity vs Stability: The cubic kernel aligned with data reduces bias but makes influence scores highly sensitive.
- Transductivity vs Scalability: \(f_{\text{att}}\) requires the entire training set, leading to \(O(n^2 d)\) memory/computation, limiting large \(n\).
- Parameter-free vs Parameterized: Using identity Q/K/V isolates pure quadratic interaction; adding learnable projections may further complicate dynamics but is not studied.

Failure signatures:
1. NTK distance does not decrease with increasing width (or increases) for MLP-Attn.
2. Condition number \(\kappa(G) \gg 1\) (e.g., \(>10^3\)) on dataset, predicting \(m \gg 10^{18}\).
3. Influence flip rate under PGD exceeds 20% for MLP-Attn, while ReLU remains ~3%.

First 3 experiments:
1. Replicate NTK convergence test: Train MLP-Attn and 2L-ReLU at widths \(m \in \{4,8,16,\dots,4096\}\) on MNIST/CIFAR-10. Compute NTK distance \(\|f_m - f_{\text{NTK}}\|\). Expect non-monotonic/increasing for MLP-Attn vs monotonic decrease for 2L-ReLU.
2. Measure influence malleability: For a trained model, compute influence scores for top-10% positively influential points on 100 test samples. Apply PGD (\(\varepsilon=0.3\), 40 steps) to those training points and recompute scores. Calculate flip rate. Compare architectures.
3. Estimate Gram condition number: Compute \(G = X X^\top\) on normalized training data. Report \(\kappa(G)\) and derived \(m_{\text{required}} \approx \kappa(G)^6 \cdot n \log n / \varepsilon^2\). Compare \(m_{\text{required}}\) to tested widths (e.g., 4096). If \(\kappa(G)^6 \gg m\), consistent with non-convergence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does NTK non-convergence extend to full softmax attention? The authors note this as a natural next step; softmax normalization introduces token competition that current NTK analysis cannot handle.
- How do trainable QKV projections and multi-head attention affect NTK convergence? The study assumes identity QKV; analyzing parameterized and multi-head variants remains open.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes identity Q/K/V projections and binary classification; extension to standard multi-head softmax attention is nontrivial.
- Experiments use small subsets of MNIST/CIFAR-10 (\(\sim1000\) samples); scalability to larger \(n\) is limited by transductive nature of \(f_{\text{att}}\).
- The derived width requirement \(m = \Omega(\kappa^6)\) is based on a bounded incoherence assumption (Assumption 4.4) that may not hold for all datasets, but \(\kappa(G)\) is typically large in practice.

## Confidence
- Spectral amplification (\(\kappa(G)^3\)) and width bound: **High** (rigorous proof under assumptions).
- Empirical NTK non-convergence: **High** (consistent across datasets and widths, replicated experiment).
- Influence malleability results: **High** (multiple attacks, large effect sizes 6–9×).
- Bias-variance trade-off argument: **Medium** (theoretical proposition C.7, but limited empirical verification).

## Next Checks
1. Verify condition number amplification: Compute \(\kappa(G)\) and \(\kappa(G^3)\) for the training Gram matrices and confirm \(\kappa(G^3) \approx \kappa(G)^3\).
2. Extend experiments to larger widths (e.g., up to 16384) and additional datasets to test the monotonicity prognosis of NTK distance.
3. Test influence malleability under varying perturbation strengths (\(\varepsilon\)) and with different top-influence fractions to assess robustness of the 6–9× flip rate difference.
