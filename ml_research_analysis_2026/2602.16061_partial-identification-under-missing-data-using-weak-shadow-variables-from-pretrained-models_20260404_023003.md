# Partial Identification under Missing Data Using Weak Shadow Variables from Pretrained Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.16061
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16061
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Develops a partial identification framework where predictions from pretrained models act as weak shadow variables to tighten sharp bounds on MNAR population means, shrinking intervals by 75–83% with >98% coverage in simulations and semi-synthetic experiments.

## Executive Summary
Missing-not-at-random (MNAR) outcomes hinder unbiased estimation of population means. This paper introduces a partial identification approach that yields sharp bounds via linear programming without strong parametric assumptions. By leveraging predictions from pretrained models (e.g., LLMs) as weak shadow variables—requiring only conditional independence between the prediction and missingness given true outcome and covariates—the feasible set is tightened, often achieving point identification. A set-expansion estimator provides valid finite-sample inference. In simulations and real customer-service dialogue data, LLM-based shadow variables reduce interval widths by 75–83% while maintaining coverage above 98%. The method bridges robust statistics with modern AI, enabling practical handling of MNAR in settings where auxiliary data are scarce.

## Method Summary
The core method constructs a partial identification region for the population mean θ under MNAR by solving two linear programs that encode the observed data structure, specifically the joint distribution of outcome Y and missingness indicator R. Without auxiliary variables, the bounds are derived from the observed frequencies of Y among respondents and the overall missing rate. To tighten these bounds, predictions from a pretrained model (F) are incorporated as a weak shadow variable, requiring the key assumption that F is conditionally independent of R given Y and covariates X. This assumption yields additional linear constraints on the unknown response probabilities, shrinking the feasible polytope. For finite samples, a set-expansion estimator relaxes the constraints by adding a data-driven slack term κ/√n to maintain asymptotic coverage. The approach accommodates stratification by covariates and handles both discrete outcomes and discrete predictions. It is implemented via per-stratum linear programming, followed by aggregation.

## Key Results
- Sharp bounds on MNAR means are obtained via linear programs; the feasible region is a polytope whose extreme points give the tightest possible intervals without further assumptions.
- Weak shadow variables from LLMs shrink identification intervals by 75–83% relative to bounds without predictions, while maintaining coverage above 98% under realistic MNAR mechanisms in semi-synthetic experiments.
- Theoretical analysis shows √n convergence rate under point identification (when shadow variable is sufficiently informative), but a slower rate under general partial identification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The sharp identification region for the population mean under MNAR is exactly an interval obtained by solving two linear programs.
- **Mechanism**: Decompose θ = Σ_y y P(Y=y) = Σ_y y α(y)/π(y), where α(y) = P(Y=y, R=1) is identifiable, but π(y) = P(R=1|Y=y) is not. The only constraint from the observed data is Σ_y α(y)/π(y) = 1 (probabilities sum to one). Let w(y) = 1/π(y) - 1 ≥ 0. The feasible set is {w : Σ_y α(y)(w(y)+1)=1, w≥0}. The objective Σ_y y α(y)(w(y)+1) is linear in w, so the minimum and maximum occur at extreme points: for θ_min, set w(y)=0 for y>lowest outcome and allocate remaining mass to the smallest y; for θ_max, allocate to the largest outcome.
- **Core assumption**: The observed data structure (α(y)) is correctly specified; no further assumptions about the missingness mechanism are needed.
- **Evidence anchors**: Proposition 1 provides closed-form bounds and proves optimality; the LP formulation encodes the observed data.
- **Break condition**: If P(R=0)=1, bounds collapse to the full support [1, M], which is uninformative.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Predictions from pretrained models, when satisfying conditional independence given the true outcome and covariates, act as weak shadow variables that add linear constraints and shrink the identification region without requiring completeness.
- **Mechanism**: For each covariate stratum x and each predicted value f, define β_x(f)=P(R=0,F=f|X=x). Under F ⟂ R | Y,X, we have β_x(f) = Σ_y α_x(f,y)(1/π_x(y)-1), where α_x(f,y)=P(Y=y,R=1,F=f|X=x). This yields |F| linear constraints per stratum on w_x(y)=1/π_x(y)-1, tightening the feasible set compared to the single aggregated constraint without F. The matrices A_x = [α_x(f,y)] encode the association; tighter bounds occur when A_x has higher rank. If rank(A_x)=M, the feasible set becomes a singleton (point identification).
- **Core assumption**: Assumption 1: F ⟂ R | Y, X. Also, F and Y have finite support (or are discretized).
- **Evidence anchors**: Definition 1 (weak shadow variable) and Proposition 4 (identification region with shadow variables).
- **Break condition**: If F is conditionally independent of Y given X and R=1 (no predictive power), A_x may be rank-deficient and bounds may not tighten much. Violation of F ⟂ R | Y,X invalidates constraints.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The set-expansion estimator ensures valid finite-sample coverage of the identified set by solving a relaxed linear program that accounts for estimation error in the probability constraints.
- **Mechanism**: True probabilities α_x, β_x are unknown; plug in empirical estimates ˆA_x, ˆβ_x. Direct substitution may yield infeasible LP due to sampling error. The set-expansion method first computes minimal constraint violation ˆm_x = min_{0≤w≤C} ||ˆA_x w - ˆβ_x||_∞. Then it solves the LP with constraints ||ˆA_x w - ˆβ_x||_∞ ≤ ˆm_x + κ_n/√n and 0≤w≤C. The term κ_n/√n (with κ_n → ∞) provides asymptotic coverage. Under general partial identification, convergence rate is κ_n/√n; if A_x has full column rank, a constant κ_n suffices for √n rate.
- **Core assumption**: Assumption 2: estimators converge at √n rate; Assumption 3: there exists known C > 0 such that true w_x(y) ≤ C (i.e., π_x(y) ≥ 1/(C+1)).
- **Evidence anchors**: Equation (3), Theorems 2 and 3; builds on Chernozhukov et al. (2007).
- **Break condition**: If estimation errors are larger than anticipated (e.g., small cells), slack may be too small, causing undercoverage; overly large κ_n yields conservative intervals. Positivity condition (Assumption 3) must hold.

## Foundational Learning
### Partial Identification
- **Why needed**: Under MNAR, the mean outcome is not point-identified without strong, often untestable assumptions. Partial characterization yields robust bounds that cover the true value regardless of the specific missingness mechanism, as long as the observed data structure is accurate.
- **Quick check**: In the base case without auxiliary variables, the width of the identification interval equals (M-1)·P(R=0). This implies that the information loss due to missingness is proportional to the missing rate and the outcome support.

### Weak Shadow Variables
- **Why needed**: Classical shadow variables require completeness (full rank of the conditional distribution matrix), which is often violated by predictions from generic pretrained models. Weak shadow variables relax completeness, allowing imperfect predictions to still tighten bounds.
- **Quick check**: How does the weak shadow variable assumption differ from the classical shadow variable? Classical requires that the shadow variable fully predicts Y (completeness), while weak shadow only requires conditional independence between F and R given Y and X. What happens if the shadow variable is conditionally independent of Y given X and R=1? Then F provides no information about Y, so A_x may be rank-deficient and bounds may not tighten.

### Linear Programming for Set Identification
- **Why needed**: The identification region is defined by linear equalities and inequalities derived from probability relations. The extreme points of the resulting polytope give sharp bounds. LP provides a systematic, tractable way to compute these bounds, especially with additional constraints from shadow variables.
- **Quick check**: What are the decision variables in the LP formulation? The w_x(y) ≥ 0 (or equivalently the inverse response odds). Why are they restricted to be nonnegative? Because w(y)=1/π(y)-1 ≥ 0 since π(y) ≤ 1.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Preprocessor (stratify by X) → Probability Estimator (compute ˆA_x, ˆβ_x) → LP Solver (per stratum: compute minimal violation, then solve relaxed LP) → Aggregator (combine stratum-specific bounds).

### Critical path
- Input must have finite support for X and F. Continuous X must be discretized.
- For each stratum, n_x must be large enough to estimate α_x and β_x reliably; zero counts are permissible but may affect feasibility. Set-expansion slack mitigates small-sample inconsistencies.
- Bound C (on w) must be set based on a plausible lower bound for π(y); C = 1/π_min - 1.
- Choice of κ_n (e.g., log n_x) affects coverage; should grow slowly with n.

### Design tradeoffs
- Discretization granularity: More strata capture heterogeneity but reduce n_x; fewer strata reduce noise but may miss covariate effects.
- Bound C: Too small risks infeasibility; too large yields wider intervals. Requires domain knowledge about minimum plausible response rates.
- Slack parameter κ_n: Larger improves coverage at cost of wider intervals; smaller tightens but risks undercoverage.
- Numerical stability: Near rank-deficient A_x may cause unreliable LP results; consider regularization or high-precision solvers.

### Failure signatures
- LP infeasibility (even with set-expansion): indicates violation of conditional independence or C too tight; check for cells where β_x(f)>0 but α_x(f,y)=0 for all y.
- Excessively wide intervals: high missingness or shadow variable provides little information (A_x nearly rank-deficient); compute condition number of A_x.
- Coverage below nominal level: slack too small or estimation errors larger than √n; increase κ_n or verify positivity assumptions.
- Numerical instability (solver warnings): ill-conditioned A_x; rescale or use robust solver.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Synthetic simulation**: Generate MNAR data with discrete outcomes (M=5) and known mean. Compare set-expansion with/without weak shadow variable against baselines (CCA, NI, PPI, Heckman, PM) across sample sizes. Check coverage and interval width.
2. **Semi-synthetic dialogues**: Use USS dataset with human ratings as truth. Generate LLM predictions via GPT-4o-mini under different prompts (zero-shot, few-shot, CoT, fine-tuned). Simulate MNAR by sampling π(y) from Uniform(0.1,0.9) and predefined patterns. Evaluate coverage and width reduction.
3. **Assumption stress test**: Violate F ⟂ R | Y,X by letting F directly influence R (e.g., users with certain predictions are more likely to respond). Verify coverage degrades. Also, vary informativeness of F (by adding noise) to see interval width changes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How do multiple LLM prompts and ensembles affect the tightness of identification bounds?
- **Basis in paper**: The conclusion explicitly suggests leveraging richer model outputs (e.g., multiple prompts, ensembles) to tighten identification regions.
- **Why unresolved**: The current framework uses a single prediction per unit; aggregating diverse predictions could extract more information about Y and further shrink the bounds.
- **What evidence would resolve**: Run experiments comparing different aggregation methods (averaging, majority vote, weighted ensembles) across prompting strategies; measure reductions in bound width and coverage rates on semi-synthetic data with known MNAR mechanisms.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How should platforms design data collection and incentive mechanisms to minimize identification bound width under budget constraints and strategic user behavior?
- **Basis in paper**: The conclusion calls for studying data collection and incentive design to minimize bound width.
- **Why unresolved**: Practical deployment requires understanding the trade-off between the cost of obtaining auxiliary predictions or responses and the resulting precision of bounds; strategic users may respond differently to incentives.
- **What evidence would resolve**: Build an economic model linking platform effort (e.g., rewards for responding, quality of LLM predictions) to the joint distribution of Y, R, and F; optimize allocation of a fixed budget to minimize expected bound width; validate via simulations with utility-maximizing users.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How sensitive are the identification bounds to violations of the conditional independence assumption (F ⟂ R | Y, X)?
- **Basis in paper**: The entire identification strategy hinges on Assumption 1, which is untestable from the observed data because Y is missing for non-respondents.
- **Why unresolved**: In real applications, LLM outputs might be shown to users or otherwise influence their decision to respond, creating dependence; if violated, the bounds may no longer cover the true θ.
- **What evidence would resolve**: Conduct sensitivity analysis by simulating plausible violations (e.g., adding a direct effect of F on R) and checking coverage; alternatively, conduct a field experiment where response is forced for a random subset (so R is always 1) to estimate the conditional distribution of F given Y, X and test fit.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Can the linear programming partial identification framework be extended to continuous outcomes without discretization, and what identification properties result?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper assumes discrete Y and finite support for F; continuous outcomes would require infinite-dimensional constraints.
- **Why unresolved**: Many outcomes of interest (e.g., income, spending) are continuous; forcing discretization loses information and may widen bounds unnecessarily.
- **What evidence would resolve**: Reformulate as an infinite-dimensional linear program (e.g., using measure constraints) or a moment-based approach; derive conditions for sharp bounds; implement numerically by approximating with fine discretization and show convergence; validate on simulated continuous outcomes under MNAR.

## Limitations
- Assumes discrete outcomes and finite support, forcing discretization and potential information loss.
- Conditional independence (F ⟂ R | Y, X) is untestable and likely violated when LLM predictions influence user behavior.
- Bound C and slack κₙ require strong prior knowledge about response probabilities; misspecification causes infeasibility or undercoverage.

## Confidence
- LP sharp bounds: High
- Weak shadow variables tighten bounds without completeness: Medium
- Set-expansion estimator coverage: Medium
