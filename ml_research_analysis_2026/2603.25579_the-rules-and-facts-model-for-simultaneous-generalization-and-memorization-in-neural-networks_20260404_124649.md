# The Rules-and-Facts Model for Simultaneous Generalization and Memorization in Neural Networks

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID**: 2603.25579  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25579  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: The RAF model shows that overparameterized neural networks can learn a structured rule and memorize random exceptions, with performance governed by kernel geometry; however, for any non-zero exception fraction, generalization decays as α⁻¹/² at large sample complexity, slower than Bayes-optimal α⁻¹.

## Executive Summary

The paper tackles how neural networks simultaneously generalize underlying rules and memorize unstructured exceptions. It introduces the Rules-and-Facts (RAF) model, where a fraction 1−ε of labels follow a structured teacher rule and a fraction ε are random facts. Using high-dimensional statistical physics, the authors characterize generalization and memorization errors for linear models, random features, and kernel regression. Overparameterization is key: it decouples rule learning (via the leading eigenspace of the kernel) from fact storage (using excess capacity). The kernel’s geometry, summarized by the angle γ = arctan(μ₁/μ*), dictates the trade-off, and for kernel ridge regression with square loss an optimal γ yields perfect memorization and best generalization. Yet for any ε>0, generalization error decays as α⁻¹/²—slower than the Bayes-optimal α⁻¹—raising the question of whether faster rates can coexist with memorization. The RAF model offers a minimal, solvable framework for studying this interplay.

## Method Summary

The authors analyze synthetic data: inputs x∼N(0,I_d), teacher w*∼N(0,I_d), labels y = sign(w*·x/√d) with probability 1−ε, else random ±1. Learner architectures include a linear perceptron, random features (→ kernel in the wide limit), and kernel ridge/SVM with dot-product kernels. The core tool is the replica method from spin glass theory, applied to the high-dimensional limit where n,d,p→∞ with ratios α=n/d, κ=p/d fixed. This yields state equations for order parameters (m,q,V) that determine the overlap with the teacher, norm, and a conjugate variable. Generalization error is E_gen = arccos(m/√q)/π; memorization error follows from a proximal operator (square loss: E_mem = ½ erfc(√(V/(2q)))). The kernel’s Hermite expansion provides two coefficients: μ₁² = K'(0) and μ*² = K(1)−K(0)−K'(0). Asymptotically, only the ratio μ₁/μ* (angle γ = arctan(μ₁/μ*)) matters due to a rescaling symmetry. The theory is validated on Gaussian data and on a CIFAR10-RAF variant.

## Key Results

- Overparameterization separates the rule-aligned subspace (μ₁) from fact-storing components (μ*), enabling simultaneous generalization and memorization.
- The trade-off is fully captured by kernel angle γ; for square loss, an optimal γ achieves perfect memorization and minimal generalization error.
- For any ε>0, generalization decays as α⁻¹/² at large α—slower than Bayes-optimal α⁻¹—leaving open whether faster rates can coexist with memorization.
- Linear models (μ*=0) cannot achieve both objectives; overparameterization is essential.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Overparameterization decouples rule learning from fact storage.
Overparameterization creates a high-dimensional representation where the teacher-aligned subspace (leading eigenspace of the kernel) is preserved while perpendicular components interpolate random labels. The kernel geometry separates μ₁ (rule) from μ* (facts). This relies on the infinite-width limit; if μ*=0 (linear kernel), memorization vanishes in the ridgeless limit for ε>0 at α<1, lacking a high-dimensional subspace for facts.

### Mechanism 2: Kernel angle γ = arctan(μ₁/μ*) determines allocation.
The state equations are invariant under rescaling (μ*, μ₁, λ) → (rμ*, rμ₁, r²λ). Thus all performance curves for convex losses depend only on the ratio μ₁/μ*, not absolute magnitudes. The effective kernel geometry collapses to γ, which governs the rule-fact balance. This symmetry holds asymptotically for dot-product kernels; for non-dot-product or finite-n, additional spectral aspects matter (e.g., CIFAR10-RAF).

### Mechanism 3: Regularization λ orchestrates the trade-off.
For fixed γ, varying λ traces a curve in (E_gen, E_mem). Decreasing λ drives memorization to zero for any μ*>0, while generalization first improves then worsens, yielding λ_opt. Square loss admits analytic λ_opt and a phase transition in (μ*, μ₁) at γ_opt. If μ*=0, the curve becomes vertical: memorization cannot reach zero for ε>0 unless α is below an interpolation threshold.

## Foundational Learning

- **High-dimensional random matrix theory / Marchenko-Pastur law**  
  *Why needed*: The replica analysis and Gaussian equivalence rely on the spectral distribution of feature matrices. In random features, (1/p)FF⊤ follows the Marchenko-Pastur law; its Stieltjes transform enters the entropic term of the free energy, linking overparameterization ratio κ to capacity.  
  *Quick check*: In the random features model, as p,d→∞ with p/d=κ fixed, what is the limiting spectral distribution of (1/p)FF⊤ and how does its Stieltjes transform appear in the state equations?

- **Replica method from spin glass theory**  
  *Why needed*: To compute the average free energy density over random features and data in the high-dimensional limit, the replica trick reduces the problem to a saddle-point equations for order parameters (m, q, V).  
  *Quick check*: What is the replica-symmetric ansatz for overlaps (m_a, q_{ab}) and how does it simplify the free energy?

- **Kernel methods and dot-product kernels**  
  *Why needed*: The results apply to kernel regression with dot-product kernels (e.g., from single-layer networks). The Hermite expansion coefficients a_ℓ of K(ρ)=∑ a_ℓ ρ^ℓ give μ₁² = a₁ and μ*² = ∑_{ℓ≥2} a_ℓ, the two key geometric quantities controlling errors.  
  *Quick check*: Given K(ρ), how are μ₁ and μ* computed from the Hermite coefficients, and why do only these combinations matter asymptotically?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Data generator (x∼N(0,I_d), teacher w*∼N(0,I_d), mixed labels) → Learner (linear perceptron / random features / kernel ridge or SVM) → Optimization (ERM with ridge λ) → Evaluation (E_gen, E_mem). Theoretical pipeline: asymptotic high-dimensional limit (n,d,p→∞, α,κ fixed) → replica method → state equations → performance predictions.

**Critical path**:  
1. Define RAF data model (ε, teacher rule).  
2. Choose learner and loss (square or hinge).  
3. Compute μ₁, μ* from kernel/activation.  
4. Solve state equations for (m,q,V) given α,ε,λ,μ₁,μ*.  
5. Compute E_gen = arccos(m/√q)/π and E_mem (Eq. 22; square loss: ½ erfc(√(V/(2q)))).  
6. Explore λ ↦ (E_gen, E_mem) curves; find λ_opt or λ→0 limit.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Kernel choice*: Sets γ; square loss has analytic optimal γ_opt(ε) for perfect memorization + best generalization; hinge loss similar but no closed form.  
- *Regularization*: λ=0 gives perfect memorization for μ*>0 but may hurt generalization; λ>0 can improve generalization at E_mem cost.  
- *Architecture*: Linear models (μ*=0) cannot jointly achieve low errors; overparameterization (wide RF/kernel) is necessary.  
- *Loss*: Square loss yields simpler equations, often λ_opt=0; hinge loss may need cross-validation.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Memorization fails (E_mem>0 as λ→0) if μ*=0 or α exceeds interpolation threshold α_c(ε) (square: α_c=1; hinge: α_c(ε)).  
- Generalization fails (E_gen≈1/2) if γ is too small (μ* large vs μ₁) or α too small.  
- Theory breakdown: for non-Gaussian data (e.g., CIFAR10-RAF) performance depends on more than μ₁,μ*; matching these does not guarantee agreement.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Reproduce Fig. 1: For linear perceptron and kernels (erf, ReLU) at fixed α,ε, sweep λ. Verify overparameterized models attain lower E_gen at low E_mem vs linear.  
2. γ-sweep: Fix α,ε, vary γ (e.g., RBF bandwidth η). Plot E_gen at λ→0 and λ_opt vs γ. Check square loss optimum matches Eq. (39); hinge loss optimum nearby.  
3. Validate state equations vs finite-n: Generate Gaussian RAF with d=1000, n=αd; train kernel ridge/SVM; compare errors to asymptotic predictions as d grows. Test kernel matching: two kernels with identical μ₁,μ* but different higher Hermite spectra should give same curve asymptotically; deviations at finite d indicate limitations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Bayes-optimal generalization rate**: Can a learning algorithm achieve Bayes-optimal decay (∝ α⁻¹) while simultaneously memorizing the unstructured facts in the RAF model? Existing solvable models (linear, kernel, random features with fixed first layer) all decay as α⁻¹/² for ε>0. It is unknown whether faster rates can coexist with memorization.
2. **Two-layer neural networks with trainable first layer**: Does a wide two-layer neural network with a trainable first layer achieve both memorization and a generalization rate faster than α⁻¹/²? Analysis of such models on RAF data remains technically open due to data-dependent feature Gram matrices.
3. **Architectural specialization**: Do different submodules (e.g., attention heads vs. feedforward layers in transformers) specialize for rule learning versus factual memorization? The paper suggests this as an intriguing question; current theory treats the network monolithically.
4. **Extension to structured non-Gaussian data**: Can the RAF theory be extended to datasets like CIFAR10-RAF where performance depends on more than μ₁, μ*? The current high-dimensional analysis assumes Gaussian inputs; real data have manifold structure and correlations.

## Limitations

- Asymptotic theory assumes Gaussian inputs and dot-product kernels; finite-n or real data may deviate (e.g., CIFAR10-RAF mismatches).
- Generalization rate α⁻¹/² for ε>0 is suboptimal vs Bayes-optimal α⁻¹; it is not known whether faster rates can coexist with memorization.
- Linear kernels (μ*=0) cannot simultaneously achieve zero memorization and good generalization for ε>0 beyond α<1 in the ridgeless limit.
- The γ-only dependence is an asymptotic symmetry; for non-dot-product kernels or finite dimensions, the full spectrum matters.

## Confidence

- **High**: Core asymptotic derivations (state equations, square-loss closed form), overparameterization decoupling mechanism, rescaling invariance for dot-product kernels.
- **Medium**: Kernel angle γ as sole trade-off parameter for square loss; validation on synthetic Gaussian data; existence of optimal γ for square loss.
- **Low**: Generalization to non-Gaussian data (CIFAR10-RAF); predictions for kernels beyond matching μ₁,μ*; hinge-loss trade-off specifics; possibility of faster generalization rates with memorization.
