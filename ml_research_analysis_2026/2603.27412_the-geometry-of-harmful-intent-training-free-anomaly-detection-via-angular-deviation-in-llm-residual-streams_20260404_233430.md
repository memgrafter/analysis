# The Geometry of Harmful Intent: Training-Free Anomaly Detection via Angular Deviation in LLM Residual Streams

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.27412  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27412  
- **Reference count**: 6  
- **Primary result**: LatentBiopsy detects harmful prompts with AUROC ≥0.937 across six Qwen model variants and AUROC =1.000 on XSTest, using only angular deviation in residual streams with sub‑millisecond overhead.

## Executive Summary
LatentBiopsis is a novel, training‑free method for identifying harmful prompts in large language models by analyzing the geometry of residual‑stream activations. The approach fits a principal component from a small set of safe prompts and scores new inputs by the angular deviation of their last‑token activation from this reference direction. Across six Qwen models (base, instruction‑tuned, and abliterated), the method achieves strong detection performance (AUROC ≥0.937) and perfect separation on the XSTest benchmark, all with negligible latency. Key findings reveal that harmful intents occupy a tightly clustered angular region that persists even after surgically removing the refusal direction, and that the relative orientation of this cluster (inner vs. outer ring) varies across model families, motivating a direction‑agnostic scoring rule.

## Method Summary
LatentBiopsy operates on residual‑stream vectors at a single transformer layer. Given a normative set of safe prompts (e.g., 200 from Alpaca‑Cleaned), it extracts the last‑token activation \(f_\ell(x)\) for each prompt. Using centered PCA, it computes the leading principal component (PC1), which defines a reference direction. For each safe prompt, the angular deviation \(\theta = \arccos(f \cdot c / \|f\|)\) is calculated, and a Gaussian distribution \(N(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\) is fitted to these angles. To score a test prompt, its activation is extracted at the same layer, its \(\theta\) relative to PC1 is computed, and the negative log‑likelihood under the Gaussian is returned as an anomaly score. The method is symmetric (high scores for both large and small deviations) and does not assume a fixed side for harmful prompts. Layer selection is performed by maximizing AUROC on a held‑out set; performance typically plateaus across many late layers. The entire pipeline requires no model retraining and incurs only sub‑millisecond overhead per query.

## Key Results
- **High detection accuracy**: AUROC ≥0.937 for harmful vs. normative prompts across six Qwen variants; AUROC =1.000 on XSTest (harmful vs. benign‑aggressive).
- **Angular compactness of harm**: Harmful prompts form a near‑degenerate angular cluster with standard deviation \(\sigma_{\theta}^{\text{harm}} \approx 0.03\) rad, an order of magnitude tighter than normative prompts (\(\sigma_{\theta}^{\text{norm}} \approx 0.27\) rad).
- **Dissociation from refusal & ring orientation**: The harmful‑intent geometry persists after abliteration (AUROC difference ≤0.015 from instruction‑tuned models), and the orientation of the harmful ring (inner vs. outer relative to PC1) differs between model families (e.g., Qwen3.5‑0.8B outer, Qwen2.5‑0.5B inner), necessitating a direction‑agnostic score.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Harmful prompts occupy a near‑degenerate angular cluster distinct from normative prompts in the residual stream, enabling detection via angular deviation from a normative reference direction.
- **Mechanism**: LatentBiopsy computes PC1 from a small set of safe prompts. New prompts are scored by the angular deviation \(\theta\) of their last‑token activation from PC1; the anomaly score is the negative log‑likelihood of \(\theta\) under a Gaussian fit to the normative \(\theta\) distribution. The symmetric score flags both unusually large and small deviations.
- **Core assumption**: The leading principal component captures the dominant variance among safe prompts; harmful intents are concentrated on a thin angular shell distinct from the normative cloud.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "harmful prompts form a near‑degenerate angular cluster with \(\sigma_{\theta}^{\text{harm}} \approx 0.03\) rad, an order of magnitude tighter than normative (\(\sigma_{\theta}^{\text{norm}} \approx 0.27\) rad)"  
  - [section 6.3, table 2] Reports \(\sigma_{\text{harm}}^\theta = 0.030–0.052\) rad vs. \(\sigma_{\text{norm}}^\theta = 0.183–0.272\) rad across all six models.  
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers focus on anomaly detection in vision/medical domains; no direct evidence for LLM angular harm geometry. **Assumption**: The angular separation pattern generalizes beyond Qwen and AdvBench.
- **Break condition**: If harmful prompts are not angularly compact (e.g., diverse jailbreaks populate a broad angular region) or if normative PC1 is unstable with small \(N\) (<30), detection fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The geometric representation of harmful intent is dissociated from the refusal mechanism; it persists even after surgically removing the refusal direction (abliteration).
- **Mechanism**: Abliteration orthogonalizes the refusal direction from all model weights, preventing refusal generation. LatentBiopsis's detection performance (AUROC h/n) on abliterated models remains within 0.015 of instruction‑tuned counterparts, showing the harmful‑intent geometry is encoded independently of the refusal circuit.
- **Core assumption**: The refusal direction is a linear artifact separate from the manifold encoding harmful semantics; ablating it does not collapse the harmful‑normative angular separation.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "geometry survives refusal ablation: both abliterated variants achieve AUROC at most 0.015 below their instruction‑tuned counterparts"  
  - [section 5.2, table 1] AUROC h/n for abliterated models: Qwen3.5‑0.8B (0.9517) vs. Chat (0.9497); Qwen2.5‑0.5B (0.9374) vs. Instruct (0.9420).  
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers do not address refusal ablation or geometric dissociation. **Assumption**: The dissociation holds across architectures beyond Qwen.
- **Break condition**: If harmful geometry is entailed by the refusal direction itself, abliteration would collapse the angular separation, causing AUROC to drop near random.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The orientation of the harmful ring (inner vs. outer relative to normative PC1) is architecture‑ and layer‑dependent, necessitating a direction‑agnostic scoring rule.
- **Mechanism**: At the same layer (L20), Qwen3.5‑0.8B places harmful prompts at larger \(\theta\) than normative (outer ring, \(\Delta\theta \approx +0.62\) rad), while Qwen2.5‑0.5B places them at smaller \(\theta\) (inner ring, \(\Delta\theta \approx -0.45\) rad). A fixed directional threshold (e.g., \(\theta >\) threshold) would fail on one family. The symmetric Gaussian NLL score flags both high and low deviations equally.
- **Core assumption**: Ring orientation is not predictable a priori; thus, detection must not assume harmful prompts lie on a specific side of the normative mean.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "the two families exhibit opposite ring orientations… harmful prompts occupy the outer ring in Qwen3.5‑0.8B but the inner ring in Qwen2.5‑0.5B"  
  - [section 6.1, table 2] \(\Delta\theta\) positive for Qwen3.5‑0.8B (outer), negative for Qwen2.5‑0.5B (inner).  
  - [corpus] No related evidence; ring orientation is a novel empirical finding specific to this work.
- **Break condition**: If ring orientation were consistent across architectures and layers, a simpler directional threshold would suffice; the symmetric score would still work but is not strictly necessary.

## Foundational Learning
### Residual stream activations in transformer LMs
- **Why needed**: LatentBiopsy operates on the final‑token residual‑stream vector \(f_\ell(x)\) at layer \(\ell\). Understanding that this space accumulates mixed representations is key to interpreting angular geometry.
- **Quick check**: Why does using angle rather than Euclidean distance help when norms grow monotonically with depth in Pre‑LN transformers?

### Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
- **Why needed**: PC1 defines the reference direction from safe prompts; the method assumes leading variance captures "normative" semantics.
- **Quick check**: What would happen if the first PC captured dataset artifacts (e.g., prompt length) instead of safe semantics?

### Angular distance in high‑dimensional spaces
- **Why needed**: The score \(s(x)\) depends only on \(\theta\), isolating semantic orientation from magnitude effects (concept intensity, norm growth).
- **Quick check**: If two prompts have nearly antiparallel directions (\(\theta \approx \pi\)), what does that imply about their semantic relationship under the Linear Representation Hypothesis?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Activation Extractor -> Normative PCA Fitter -> Angular Calculator -> Gaussian Fitter -> Scorer

### Critical path
1. Per‑query activation extraction at the chosen layer.  
2. Offline: Fit PC1 from 200 safe prompts and Gaussian to their \(\theta\) values.  
3. Compute \(\theta\) for a test prompt relative to PC1.  
4. Return the negative log‑likelihood under the Gaussian as the anomaly score.  
Layer selection is performed separately using held‑out safe/harmful data to maximize AUROC.

### Design tradeoffs
- **K=1 vs. K>1**: Using only the top PC (K=1) is simpler, more robust, and uniformly outperforms multi‑dimensional baselines; K>1 adds complexity with no gain.
- **Layer selection**: Performance plateaus across many late layers; the exact layer choice is not critical, though the best layer varies slightly by model.
- **Normative set size N**: AUROC saturates by \(N \approx 100\); thus \(N=200\) is safe, while smaller \(N\) risks unstable PC1.

### Failure signatures
- **Unstable PC1**: If \(N < 30\), PC1 direction may be noise‑sensitive, leading to inconsistent \(\theta\) across runs. Diagnose by forward/reverse ordering checks.
- **Angular overlap**: If \(\sigma_{\text{harm}}^\theta\) is comparable to \(\sigma_{\text{norm}}^\theta\), the Gaussian fit will not separate classes. Plot \(\theta\) distributions to verify.
- **Ring orientation flip**: Harmful prompts normally switch between inner/outer across layers, but if at the chosen layer \(\Delta\theta \approx 0\), separation fails.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate two‑ring structure**: Use the provided code on Qwen3.5‑0.8B‑Base and Qwen2.5‑0.5B‑Base at layer 20. Plot \(\theta\)–\(\phi\) (Figure 4) and verify that harmful prompts form a tight ring opposite the normative cloud.
2. **Test abliteration invariance**: Load abliterated checkpoints. Compute AUROC h/n and compare to the instruction‑tuned counterpart; expect a difference < 0.015 (Table 1).
3. **Validate N‑stability**: Run LatentBiopsy with normative fit sizes \(N = 10, 20, \dots, 200\). AUROC h/n should stabilize by \(N \approx 100\) (Figures 7–8). If not, the normative set may be non‑iid or too small.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper highlights several open questions for future investigation:
- **Generalization across architectures**: Does the angular clustering of harmful intents generalize to model families beyond Qwen (e.g., Llama, Mistral)?
- **Theoretical basis for ring orientation**: What mechanism determines whether harmful prompts occupy the inner or outer ring relative to the normative PC1?
- **Normative set sensitivity**: How does the size, diversity, and selection bias of the safe prompt set influence the stability of PC1 and detection performance?
- **Adversarial robustness**: Can adversarial jailbreak techniques adjust the angular geometry to bypass detection, and how can the method be hardened?

## Limitations
- Findings are restricted to six Qwen variants; generalization to other architectures is unproven.
- Prompt formatting (e.g., chat templates) is unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Dataset splits and versions of AdvBench/XSTest are not detailed; reliance on PC1 from 200 safe prompts may be unstable with smaller or biased sets.

## Confidence
- **Harmful prompts form a narrow angular cluster**: **Medium**. The evidence is strong across six Qwen variants (σ_harm^θ ≈ 0.03 rad vs. σ_norm^θ ≈ 0.27 rad), but generalization to other architectures remains unproven; the cluster compactness might not hold for all harmful intents or models.
- **Geometry persists after refusal ablation**: **High**. Abliteration experiments show AUROC differences ≤0.015, indicating robust dissociation from the refusal mechanism; however, tests are limited to two Qwen models.
- **Ring orientation is architecture‑dependent**: **Low**. The observed opposite orientation between Qwen3.5‑0.8B and Qwen2.5‑0.5B is based on a single layer (L20) and may not hold uniformly across layers or new architectures; more systematic study is needed.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑architectural replication**: Test LatentBiopsy on Llama 2/3, Mistral, and Phi families; reproduce PC1‑based AUROC and ring orientation patterns.
2. **Normative set robustness**: Vary safe prompt size (50–500) and source (Alpaca, HH‑RLHF); measure AUROC and PC1 stability.
3. **Jailbreak diversity evaluation**: Evaluate on GCG, AutoDAN, and other jailbreak suites; compute \(\sigma_{\text{harm}}^\theta\) to verify compactness; expect AUROC drop if angular spread increases.
