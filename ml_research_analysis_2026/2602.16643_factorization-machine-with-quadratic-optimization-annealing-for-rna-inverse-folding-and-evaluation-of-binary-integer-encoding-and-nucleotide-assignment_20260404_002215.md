# Factorization Machine with Quadratic-Optimization Annealing for RNA Inverse Folding and Evaluation of Binary-Integer Encoding and Nucleotide Assignment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16643
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16643
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** FMQA achieves lower normalized ensemble defect with far fewer evaluations (~1000) than Bayesian optimization and genetic algorithms; one-hot and domain-wall encodings are superior, with domain-wall benefiting from GC assignment to boundary integers.

## Executive Summary
Designing RNA sequences that fold into a target secondary structure—RNA inverse folding—is computationally challenging and experimentally expensive. This paper introduces FMQA, an iterative framework that combines a factorization machine (FM) surrogate model with quadratic-optimization annealing on an Ising machine to propose new sequence candidates. FMQA encodes sequences as binary variables via four schemes (one-hot, domain-wall, binary, unary) and explores all 24 nucleotide-to-integer assignments. The model is trained on evaluated sequences to predict normalized ensemble defect (NED) and then optimized via a QUBO formulation solved on a specialized annealer, drastically reducing the number of required evaluations.

The study finds that one-hot and domain-wall encodings significantly outperform binary and unary ones. For domain-wall, the specific assignment of guanine and cytosine to the boundary integers (0 and 3) yields even lower NED and improved minimum free energy, attributed to enrichment of stable GC base pairs in stem regions. Across multiple Eterna100 benchmarks, FMQA attains high-quality solutions with only about 1000 evaluations, demonstrating superior sample efficiency compared to Bayesian optimization and genetic algorithms. The results underscore the critical impact of encoding choices and the promise of FMQA for RNA design.

## Method Summary
FMQA operates in an iterative loop. Starting from 10 random sequences, each iteration consists of three steps: (1) train a factorization machine (FM) with embedding dimension K=12 on the accumulated binary-encoded sequences and their NED values, using AdamW (learning rate 0.01, 1000 epochs, MSE loss); (2) convert the trained FM into a quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problem, adding penalty terms (coefficient µ=2) to enforce encoding-specific constraints (e.g., one-hot sum-to-one); solve the QUBO using a simulated-annealing-based Ising machine (Fixstars Amplify) with a 2000 ms time limit to obtain a new binary candidate; (3) decode the binary vector to an integer array according to the chosen encoding and assignment, map to a nucleotide sequence, compute its NED using ViennaRNA (v2.7.2, -dangles=2, 37°C), and add the result to the dataset. This process repeats for up to 1000 iterations. The method is evaluated on target structures from the Eterna100 benchmark, comparing NED, success rate (MFE structure matches target), and MFE against baselines like random search, TPE, and GA.

## Key Results
- One-hot and domain-wall encodings achieve significantly lower NED than binary and unary across tested RNA structures.
- For domain-wall, assignments placing G and C on the boundary integers (0,3) yield lower NED and MFE than other assignments, including one-hot, due to GC enrichment in stem regions.
- FMQA reaches low NED with only ~1000 evaluations, outperforming Bayesian optimization and genetic algorithms in sample efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
FMQA's effectiveness stems from two synergistic components. First, the factorization machine surrogate model captures nonlinear interactions among binary-encoded nucleotides with relatively few parameters (embedding dimension K=12), enabling accurate prediction of NED from limited evaluations. Second, casting the acquisition problem as a QUBO and solving it on an Ising machine yields high-quality candidates globally rather than relying on local search. Moreover, the choice of binary encoding crucially shapes the optimization landscape: one-hot maintains equal Hamming distances between all nucleotides, preventing bias; domain-wall introduces an asymmetry where boundary integers (0,3) are separated by one bit from their neighbor but two bits from the opposite boundary, making transitions away from them less likely. This inherent bias, when combined with assignments that place G and C at the boundaries, leads to enrichment of stable GC pairs in stem regions, directly lowering NED and MFE. Consequently, FMQA achieves superior sample efficiency.

## Foundational Learning
- **Factorization Machine (FM)**: *Why needed:* Provides a compact, expressive surrogate model for predicting NED from binary-encoded sequences, capturing pairwise interactions via factorized parameters. *Quick check:* Verify that FM training loss decreases on a held-out set and predictions correlate with actual NED.
- **QUBO (Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization)**: *Why needed:* Allows the acquisition problem to be expressed in a standard form that can be efficiently solved by Ising machines or simulated annealing. *Quick check:* Ensure converting the FM to QUBO yields a symmetric Q matrix and that constraint penalties are correctly added.
- **Ising Machine (e.g., Fixstars Amplify)**: *Why needed:* Performs global optimization of the QUBO, avoiding local minima that plague gradient-based methods. *Quick check:* Run the solver on a known small QUBO and confirm it finds the optimal binary vector within the time limit.
- **RNA Secondary Structure Prediction (ViennaRNA)**: *Why needed:* Computes NED and MFE to evaluate candidate sequences, linking design to biophysical reality. *Quick check:* Compute NED for a simple hairpin and verify that perfectly matched sequences have low NED.
- **Binary-Integer Encodings (One-hot, Domain-wall, Binary, Unary)**: *Why needed:* Convert nucleotides into binary variables for QUBO formulation; different encodings impose different constraint structures and Hamming distances that affect search. *Quick check:* Validate that each encoding/decoding pair is bijective and that constraint penalties (e.g., sum-to-one for one-hot) are enforced.
- **Ensemble Defect (ED) and Normalized ED**: *Why needed:* Primary objective quantifies the deviation between the target structure and the ensemble of low-energy structures of a candidate sequence; allows assessment of design quality. *Quick check:* For a candidate, compute NED and ensure it lies in [0,1] and decreases as sequence design improves.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Data → FM Trainer → QUBO Formulator → Ising Solver → Decoder → Evaluator → Data (loop)

**Critical Path:** The iterative loop: (1) FM training (computationally heavy if dataset large), (2) QUBO solving (time-limited but may vary), (3) decoding and NED evaluation (fast). Total per-iteration time dominated by FM training or QUBO solving.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- Encoding choice: One-hot preserves uniform distances but requires penalty; domain-wall introduces bias beneficial for GC stems but may hinder exploration of other assignments.
- Hyperparameter K: Larger K increases FM capacity but risks overfitting; smaller K may underfit.
- Penalty coefficient µ: Too low yields infeasible solutions; too high may overshadow acquisition objective.
- Ising machine time limit: Longer solves improve candidate quality but increase runtime; tradeoff with iteration count.

**Failure Signatures:**
- Infeasible binary outputs after Ising solve (e.g., one-hot vectors not summing to 1, domain-wall lacking exactly one transition) indicates missing/incorrect penalty.
- Stagnant NED after many iterations suggests FM underfitting or poor QUBO formulation.
- High variance in NED across runs with same encoding/assignment indicates instability in Ising solver or random seed sensitivity.
- Discrepancy between reported and computed NED for known sequences suggests ViennaRNA misconfiguration.

**First Experiments:**
1. **Encoding-Decoding Validation:** For each encoding and all 24 assignments, generate random binary vectors, decode to nucleotides, re-encode, and verify bijectivity and constraint satisfaction (e.g., one-hot sums to 1). Log failure rates.
2. **FM-to-QUBO Conversion Check:** With a small synthetic dataset (e.g., 50 points), train FM, convert to QUBO, and solve with a deterministic solver (e.g., exhaustive search for small L). Compare obtained binary vector's FM value to global optimum to ensure correct QUBO formation.
3. **Baseline Comparison on a Simple Structure:** Pick a short RNA target (e.g., a 12-nt hairpin). Run FMQA for a few iterations (e.g., 20) and compare average NED improvement against random search and a simple GA to confirm sample efficiency signal early.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do specific nucleotide-to-integer assignments interact with different binary encodings to influence thermodynamic stability and ensemble defect in FMQA for RNA inverse folding?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper shows that for domain-wall encoding, assignments placing G and C on boundary integers (0,3) yield lower NED and MFE due to GC enrichment in stems (Abstract, Results, Discussion). It also notes one-hot encoding is performance-invariant to assignment.
- **Why unresolved:** The mechanism linking assignment, encoding geometry (like Hamming distances), and the resulting nucleotide frequency bias (especially for boundary integers) is not formally modeled or experimentally isolated. The causal chain from assignment choice → search landscape bias → GC content in stems → stability is inferred but not proven.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments where only the assignment is varied while keeping encoding fixed, measuring transition frequencies between integer states during Ising machine optimization to correlate Hamming distance asymmetry with nucleotide deposition rates, and thermodynamic analysis tying final GC content in stems directly to observed MFE changes.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does domain-wall encoding exhibit a consistent bias toward higher frequency of nucleotides assigned to boundary integers (0 and 3), and how does this bias differ from one-hot encoding's uniform distance property?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper observes that in domain-wall encoding, nucleotides assigned to integers 0 and 3 appear more frequently overall (Fig. 9c). It speculates this is due to asymmetric Hamming distances (adjacent integers differ by 1 bit, non-adjacent by 2+), making transitions away from 0 and 3 less frequent (Discussion).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper presents the frequency observation and a plausible Hamming-distance explanation but does not quantify the transition probabilities in the Ising machine's search dynamics or simulate the encoding's effect on the QUBO landscape's connectivity. The claim remains correlative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis of the Ising machine's proposal distribution or simulated annealing trajectories under different encodings, measuring actual bit-flip transition rates between integer states. Constructing synthetic optimization problems where the only variable is the encoding's Hamming distance graph and measuring solution quality bias.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can FMQA's performance on RNA structures with inherently unstable motifs (e.g., very short stems) be systematically improved by incorporating explicit base-pairing constraints or motif-level decomposition, and how do encoding/assignment choices affect this?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper states that FMQA struggles with structures like *Shortie 4* and *Small and Easy 6* which contain very short, thermodynamically unstable stems (Discussion). It suggests future work could introduce explicit base-pairing constraints or motif decomposition (Discussion).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies the failure mode (short stems) but does not test any remedies. It is unknown whether constraints (e.g., enforcing Watson-Crick pairs in stems) would help, or whether the encoding/assignment that favors GC enrichment in stems (domain-wall with G/C on boundaries) can partially compensate for short stem instability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-running FMQA on the difficult benchmarks with modified acquisition functions that penalize deviations from target base pairs, or with a hierarchical design approach that optimizes stems and loops separately. Comparing success rates across encodings/assignments specifically on short-stem motifs.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the complexity of the target secondary structure (e.g., stem length distribution, loop types, total length) quantitatively affect FMQA's sample efficiency and success rate, and is there a threshold where performance degrades?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper notes that longer structures (36 nt) and those with short stems had lower success rates (Results, Discussion). It attributes this to expanded search space and local thermodynamic instability but does not provide a systematic analysis across a broader range of structural features.
- **Why unresolved:** The evaluation used only eight Eterna100 structures of varying length/motif. There is no controlled study isolating specific structural features (e.g., average stem length, number of bulges, loop sizes) as predictors of FMQA performance. The relationship between combinatorial complexity (search space size ~4^L) and empirical success rate is not modeled.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A large-scale benchmark spanning a designed set of target structures with systematically varied features (e.g., fixed length but varying stem length distributions, or fixed motif density but varying total length). Measuring NED convergence curves and success rates for each encoding/assignment combination to build a predictive model of difficulty.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What is the optimal relationship between the factorization machine hyperparameter K (embedding dimension) and the binary-integer encoding method, and does this interaction depend on the RNA target structure's complexity?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper chose K=12 for all main experiments after a preliminary test on *stickshift* with one fixed assignment (G,A,U,C) (Methods, Fig. 10). It notes that performance order (one-hot ≈ domain-wall > binary > unary) was consistent across K=4,8,12,16 but did not explore interactions with different assignments or structure difficulties.
- **Why unresolved:** The hyperparameter selection was based on a single target structure and assignment. The paper does not investigate whether K=12 remains optimal for different encodings when the assignment changes (which alters the effective feature space) or for more complex structures where higher-order interactions may require different K.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A full factorial experiment varying K (e.g., 4 to 32) across all 24 assignments and a diverse set of target structures (from simple to complex). Analyzing the interaction effect of encoding×assignment×K on NED and success rate, using metrics like model validation loss to check for under/overfitting relative to the surrogate's capacity.

## Limitations
- Fixstars Amplify engine details (temperature schedule, coefficient scaling) are undisclosed, preventing exact replication of QUBO solving.
- FM hyperparameter K=12 is used universally, but optimality across targets is unverified.
- Penalty coefficient µ=2 may require per‑target tuning; not explored.
