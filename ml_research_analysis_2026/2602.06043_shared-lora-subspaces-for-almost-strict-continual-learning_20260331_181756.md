# Shared LoRA Subspaces for almost Strict Continual Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06043  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06043  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Share achieves up to 100× parameter reduction and 281× memory savings compared to task-specific LoRA adapters while matching or exceeding non-continual LoRA performance across benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Continual learning for large pretrained models suffers from catastrophic forgetting and high retraining costs. Existing parameter-efficient methods like LoRA do not strictly integrate knowledge across tasks. This paper introduces Share, a method that learns and dynamically updates a single shared low-rank subspace across tasks. By projecting each task’s LoRA adapter onto this subspace and analytically merging new knowledge, Share replaces hundreds of task-specific adapters with one continually updated model. Experiments on GLUE, image classification, 3D pose estimation, and text-to-image generation show that Share attains performance comparable to jointly trained upper bounds while reducing trainable parameters by up to 100× and memory by 281×. The approach enables efficient, nearly strict continual learning without replay.

## Method Summary
Share operates in three phases: initialization, continual adaptation, and merging. Initialization constructs the shared subspace by stacking column vectors of LoRA adapters (B or A matrices) across tasks, centering, and applying SVD to obtain the top‑k singular vectors (principal basis β, α). For a new task, Share temporarily expands the subspace with φ new basis vectors initialized from the top φ of the current basis, learns task‑specific coefficients alongside random temporary basis vectors, and optimizes on the new data. After adaptation, the temporary components are merged analytically: all adapters (including the new one) are reconstructed using the current basis, stacked, and SVD is performed to obtain an updated basis; task coefficients are recomputed by projecting the reconstructed adapters onto the new basis. Optionally, coefficients can be fine‑tuned further on available data. This process avoids gradient‑based rehearsal and keeps the parameter footprint constant, enabling continual integration of knowledge.

## Key Results
- On GLUE, Share reaches an average score of 83.44% using only 0.012M trainable parameters, compared to 83.43% with full non‑continual LoRA using 7.2M parameters.
- Across vision and NLP benchmarks, Share achieves up to 100× fewer trainable parameters and 281× less memory than task‑specific LoRA adapters while matching joint‑training upper bounds.
- The method successfully replaces hundreds of adapters with a single model that integrates knowledge from all tasks without forgetting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: LoRA adapters for different tasks lie near a common low‑rank subspace; Share identifies and reuses this subspace as a foundational basis.
- **Mechanism**: Stack column vectors of B (or A) matrices across tasks, center them, and apply SVD to extract the top‑k singular vectors (β, α). These are frozen and serve as the shared basis for all tasks.
- **Evidence**: Abstract and Section 3.2.1 describe initialization via SVD; neighboring work shows LoRA subspaces overlap.
- **Break condition**: Highly dissimilar tasks may yield a poor shared subspace, causing high reconstruction error.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: After learning a new task’s adapter, Share merges it into the shared subspace without gradient replay, preserving all prior knowledge.

- **Break condition**: If a new task introduces many novel directions beyond the current top‑k, important information may be discarded.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: When streaming data for a new task arrives, Share efficiently adapts by temporarily expanding the subspace with a few φ new basis vectors, learns them and coefficients, then merges.
- **Mechanism**: Select the top φ principal vectors as “temporary” basis, initialize random extra basis vectors (βₜ→ₜ₊₁, αₜ→ₜ₊₁) and coefficients (ϵₜ→ₜ₊₁). Optimize on new data; treat the temporary components as a new adapter and merge via Mechanism 2.

- **Break condition**: φ too small limits capacity; φ too large reduces efficiency gains.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Pretrained weights (W₀) → SVD module → Shared basis (α, β) → Task coefficients (ϵ) → Reconstructed adapter (ΔW)  
During adaptation, temporary basis (α_tmp, β_tmp) is inserted and later merged.

**Critical path**
1. **Initialization**: Stack existing adapters (or train a LoRA on first task), center, apply SVD per layer to get α₀, β₀.
2. **Continual adaptation for new task**: Select top‑φ vectors from current basis as temporary basis; initialize random extra basis and coefficients; train on new task data.
3. **Merging**: Reconstruct all task adapters (including new temporary) using current basis; stack; SVD to obtain updated α, β; analytically recalculate all task coefficients via projection; optionally fine‑tune coefficients.
4. Repeat steps 2–3 for each subsequent task.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Smaller *k* yields greater compression but risks underfitting if shared subspace is insufficient.
- φ balances adaptation capacity vs. efficiency; too small limits learning of new directions, too large increases overhead.
- Adapter‑only initialization may introduce bias if early adapters are low quality; data‑based LoRA initialization improves subspace at extra compute cost.
- Gradient‑free merging avoids replay but may accumulate reconstruction errors when many tasks are added.

**Failure signatures**
- Sharp increase in reconstruction error of past adapters during merging.
- Significant drops in performance on earlier tasks after adding a new task (catastrophic forgetting).
- Low explained variance (<60%) from SVD at merge step indicates poor shared subspace.
- Training instability during temporary expansion, e.g., high variance loss when φ is inadequate.

**First 3 experiments**
1. Continual Natural Language Understanding on GLUE benchmark using RoBERTa‑base (Section 4.1).
2. Continual Image Classification across CIFAR‑100, Food‑101, Caltech‑101, and Flowers‑102 using ViT‑B/16 (Section 4.2).
3. Continual 3D Object Pose Estimation on Pascal3D+ and its occluded variants using ResNet‑50 (Section 4.3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Extension to learning from scratch**: Can Share operate without a pretrained model? The conclusion suggests future work on this.
- **Cross‑domain transfer in multi‑modal models**: How to adapt Share for large multi‑modal models (e.g., CLIP) to enable cross‑domain knowledge transfer?
- **Impact of adapter quality on Share’s performance**: When only adapters are available, performance depends heavily on adapter quality; no systematic analysis exists.
- **Effect of task similarity on subspace convergence**: The theoretical δ‑similarity notion is not empirically linked to forgetting or accuracy; measuring this could guide task ordering.
- **Understanding backward knowledge transfer**: Share occasionally shows backward transfer (e.g., CoLA improvement), but the cause (subspace merging, coefficient recalculation) is not isolated.

## Limitations
- SVD centering procedure is ambiguous (per‑layer vs. cross‑task), potentially affecting orthonormality and reconstruction.
- Hyperparameter selection (*k*, φ) relies on heuristics like explained variance thresholds; no guarantee of generalization.
- Adapter‑only initialization can be brittle if initial adapters are of low quality.
- The core assumption of a shared low‑rank subspace may fail for highly dissimilar tasks, leading to forgetting.

## Confidence
- Shared subspace initialization (Mechanism 1): Medium – supported by neighboring subspace studies but not directly validated.
- Merging without replay (Mechanism 2): Medium – analytical projection avoids rehearsal but reconstruction error may accumulate.
- Temporary expansion for streaming data (Mechanism 3): Low – φ selection lacks systematic justification; scalability unclear.
- Main empirical result (parameter reduction while matching upper bound): High – strong numbers on multiple benchmarks, but reproducibility hampered by undisclosed details.

## Next Checks
1. **SVD centering**: Determine whether centering should be applied per‑layer or across stacked adapter vectors; evaluate impact on reconstruction error and task accuracy.
2. **Adaptive φ selection**: Explore a dynamic rule (e.g., based on projection residual of new adapter) versus fixed φ; test performance on heterogeneous task sequences.
3. **Similarity breakpoint**: Compute pairwise task similarity metrics (e.g., CKA, gradient overlap) and correlate with forgetting rates and final accuracy to identify when the shared‑subspace assumption breaks.
