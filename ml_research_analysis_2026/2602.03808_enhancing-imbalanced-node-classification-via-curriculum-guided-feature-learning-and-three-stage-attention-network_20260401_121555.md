# Enhancing Imbalanced Node Classification via Curriculum-Guided Feature Learning and Three-Stage Attention Network
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03808
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03808
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** CL3AN-GNN achieves +3.9% accuracy and +4.7% AUC-ROC on OGBN-Arxiv, and +1.1% accuracy and +4.1% AUC-ROC on Amazon Photo compared to state-of-the-art methods.

## Executive Summary
Imbalanced node classification remains a challenging problem in graph neural networks, where minority classes are often poorly learned due to label skew. This paper introduces CL3AN-GNN, a novel framework that integrates curriculum learning with a three-stage attention mechanism (Engage, Enact, Embed) to progressively refine representations from simple to complex graph features. By stabilizing early learning with localized patterns and dynamically adjusting attention to capture multi-hop relationships, CL3AN-GNN effectively mitigates neighborhood bias and class imbalance. Evaluated on eight benchmark datasets, it consistently outperforms existing methods, delivering significant gains in accuracy, AUC-ROC, and F1-score, while also demonstrating faster convergence and greater robustness across varying imbalance ratios.

## Method Summary
CL3AN-GNN consists of four modules. (1) **Feature Extraction** employs parallel GCN and GAT branches to produce combined node/edge embeddings from local neighborhoods. (2) **Embedding Layer** applies an initial projection followed by multi-head attention refinement to obtain a base representation. (3) **Curriculum Feature Learning** is a three-stage module: **Engage** (focus on simple 1‑hop, low‑degree, class‑separable patterns), **Enact** (dynamically adjust attention for multi‑hop connections and minority‑class boundaries), and **Embed** (final consolidation via iterative message passing). (4) **Classifier & Curriculum Loss** uses a final attention layer and a composite loss that adaptively re‑weights class contributions and includes entropy/diversity regularization. Training follows a fixed schedule: Engage (λ_c=1.0, λ_e=0.0), Enact (λ_c=0.7, λ_e=0.3), Embed (λ_c=0.3, λ_e=0.7), shifting focus toward minority classes over time.

## Key Results
- CL3AN-GNN consistently outperforms state-of-the-art across eight benchmark datasets, with +3.9% ACC and +4.7% AUC-ROC on OGBN-Arxiv, and +1.1% ACC and +4.1% AUC-ROC on Amazon Photo.
- Ablation studies confirm each stage’s contribution; the full model (W/3EW) significantly surpasses partial variants.
- The method shows superior robustness across varying imbalance ratios and achieves faster convergence than baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The Engage stage stabilizes early learning under label skew by focusing on structurally simple, locally discriminative graph features.
- **Mechanism:** It uses a combined GCN (for fixed neighborhood aggregation) and GAT (for adaptive neighbor importance) to generate initial node and edge embeddings from 1‑hop neighborhoods, low‑degree attributes, and class‑separable node pairs identified by initial GNN embeddings.
- **Core assumption:** Minority‑class nodes possess locally discriminative patterns that can be learned without interference from complex, long‑range dependencies dominated by majority‑class statistics.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  – [abstract] “The model begins by engaging with structurally simpler features, defined as (1) local neighbourhood patterns (1‑hop), (2) low‑degree node attributes, and (3) class‑separable node pairs identified via initial graph convolutional networks and graph attention networks (GCN and GAT) embeddings.”  
  – [section IV.A] “The GCN‑based Aggregation captures local graph structure… The GAT‑based Attention learns dynamic neighbour importance weights… The Combined Update Rule integrates both fixed (GCN) and adaptive (GAT) neighbourhood aggregation for balanced structural and semantic learning.”  
  – [corpus] Prior works (e.g., “Imbalanced Node Classification Beyond Homophilic Assumption”, “BuffGraph”) highlight neighborhood composition bias; Engage directly addresses this via localized balanced aggregation.
- **Break condition:** If the graph is extremely heterophilous (high HR, e.g., >0.8) where 1‑hop neighborhoods provide no class‑discriminative signal, Engage’s simple features become uninformative, leading to poor initialization that cascades.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The Enact stage progressively introduces complexity by dynamically adjusting attention to capture multi‑hop relationships and the topological boundaries of minority classes.
- **Mechanism:** Node and edge attention scores are refined using current embeddings and neighborhood context, allowing the model to weight connections based on their relevance to the current learning stage. This includes focusing on nodes at the “edges” of minority classes.
- **Core assumption:** After a stable representation of simple patterns is formed (Engage), the model can safely attend to more complex topological features (multi‑hop paths, cross‑class edges) without being overwhelmed by majority‑class noise.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  – [abstract] “The Enact stage then addresses complicated aspects: (1) connections that require multiple steps, (2) edges that connect different types of nodes, and (3) nodes at the edges of minority classes by using adjustable attention weights.”  
  – [section IV.C.2] “We update node attention scores using the current embeddings… The Enact block represents the intermediate phase in curriculum learning where the model starts to handle more complex nodes and edges connectivity.”  
  – [corpus] Surveys on attention in imbalanced GNNs note static attention amplifies majority bias; Enact’s dynamic, stage‑specific adjustment is a direct response.
- **Break condition:** If the curriculum pacing is too aggressive (Engage stage too short), representations may not be robust enough to interpret complex features in Enact, causing attention divergence or reinforcement of spurious correlations.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The Embed stage consolidates all learned features and mitigates residual imbalance bias through iterative message passing and curriculum‑aligned loss weighting.
- **Mechanism:** Final attention‑weighted aggregation over refined node/edge embeddings from Enact. The composite loss function dynamically re‑weights class contributions (`w_c(y_v, t)`) and adds entropy/diversity regularization, shifting focus toward minority classes as training progresses.
- **Core assumption:** A consolidated representation built from progressively refined features is more amenable to adaptive loss weighting to correct imbalance than a monolithic representation.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  – [abstract] “Finally, Embed consolidates these features via iterative message passing and curriculum‑aligned loss weighting.”  
  – [section IV.D] “The composite loss function is defined as: L_total = λ_c(t)[...] + λ_e(t)[...] where w_c(c, t) the adaptive class weights… Training progresses through three distinct phases: the Engage phase… the Enact phase… and the Embed phase…”  
  – [corpus] Ablation results (Table VI) show “W/3EW” (all blocks) significantly outperforms “W/EL” (embedding layer only), confirming the necessity of consolidation and loss weighting.
- **Break condition:** If adaptive loss weighting parameters (`α(t)` in `w_c`) are not calibrated (e.g., minority weighting introduced too early or too late), training may destabilize or over‑correct.

## Foundational Learning
- **Curriculum Learning**  
  – *Why needed here:* Standard training on imbalanced graph data causes the model to converge on easy, majority‑class patterns first, starving minority‑class gradients. CL structures learning from easy to hard, ensuring minority‑class features are encountered when the model is capable.  
  – *Quick check question:* How would you design a difficulty metric for graph nodes that is robust to the very class imbalance you are trying to solve?
- **Dynamic Graph Attention**  
  – *Why needed here:* Static attention weights in standard GATs can be dominated by high‑degree majority‑class nodes. CL3AN‑GNN requires attention that can evolve across curriculum stages (from simple to complex) and be modulated by the current learning objective.  
  – *Quick check question:* What is the risk of using a single, monolithic attention mechanism versus the proposed stage‑specific attention gates?
- **Graph Imbalance & Neighborhood Bias**  
  – *Why needed here:* Imbalance in node labels is compounded by structural imbalance: minority‑class nodes often have sparser or noisier local neighborhoods. A successful method must disentangle class information from this topological bias.  
  – *Quick check question:* Why is a simple oversampling of minority nodes (like SMOTE) insufficient for graph data, and how does CL3AN‑GNN’s approach differ fundamentally?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Graph → Feature Extraction (GCN+GAT) → Embedding Layer (projection + multi‑head attention) → Engage Block → Enact Block → Embed Block → Classifier → Predictions & Loss.
- **Critical path:** The chain ensures progressive refinement: initial simple aggregation, then dynamic attention adjustments, culminating in curriculum‑aligned loss weighting.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  – *Modularity vs. Complexity:* The three‑stage design increases parameter count and training steps but allows independent analysis and targeted mitigation of imbalance at each level.  
  – *Attention Stability:* Stage‑specific attention gates prevent early over‑fitting to noisy complex patterns but require careful hyperparameter tuning (`Λ₁, Λ₂, Λ₃`).
- **Failure signatures:**  
  1. Gradient explosion/vanishing in early stages, especially on heterophilous graphs, due to overly simplistic Engage features.  
  2. Attention weight collapse in Enact/Embed, where attention overly concentrates on majority‑class hub nodes.  
  3. Curriculum pacing mismatch: if the difficulty progression (Engage→Enact→Embed) is too aggressive or misaligned with data complexity, minority class performance suffers.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Ablation Study (RQ3):** Remove components (e.g., without curriculum, without embedding layer) to quantify each stage’s contribution. Expect full model to achieve the best performance and Engage removal to cause the largest drop.  
  2. **Imbalance Ratio Robustness (RQ2,RQ7):** Evaluate against baselines across a wide range of imbalance ratios (Λ = 0.1 to 0.8). CL3AN‑GNN’s advantage should grow as imbalance worsens.  
  3. **Gradient Stability & Attention Correlation (RQ3,RQ5):** Monitor gradient norms/variances and attention weight distributions across training epochs for the three stages. Expect progressive stabilization and increasing correlation between attention and gradients in later stages.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can graph‑specific data augmentation techniques (e.g., GraphSMOTE, GraphMixup) be seamlessly integrated into the CL3AN‑GNN’s three‑stage curriculum pipeline to enhance minority class representations without causing overfitting or disrupting progressive learning?  
**Basis in paper:** Future work mentions “integrating data augmentation techniques to further mitigate class imbalance” (Section VI).  
**Why unresolved:** CL3AN‑GNN does not incorporate explicit data augmentation; the interaction between augmentation and curriculum stages is unexplored, risking noise introduction or stage desynchronization.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implement augmentation at each curriculum stage, evaluate on imbalanced benchmarks (e.g., OGBN‑Arxiv with Λ=0.2) using minority‑class F1‑score and convergence stability, compare against vanilla CL3AN‑GNN and augmentation‑only baselines.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Does the curriculum‑guided three‑stage attention mechanism maintain its effectiveness when applied to non‑GCN/GAT architectures (e.g., Graph Transformers, GiG) that employ different message‑passing or positional encoding strategies?  
**Basis in paper:** Future work states “extending the curriculum approach to other neural architectures” (Section VI).  
**Why unresolved:** CL3AN‑GNN is only validated with GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT; other architectures may have different inductive biases that could break the assumed difficulty progression.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Adapt the Engage‑Enact‑Embed stages to GraphTransformer and GiG backbones, run experiments on heterophilous and imbalanced datasets (e.g., Chameleon, Amazon Photo), measure accuracy, AUC‑ROC, and training epoch efficiency against architecture‑specific baselines.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Can a dynamic curriculum scheduler that adjusts stage transitions based on real‑time model performance (e.g., per‑class validation F1, gradient norm variance) improve CL3AN‑GNN’s robustness across graphs with unseen imbalance ratios and structural properties?  
**Basis in paper:** Future work proposes “developing adaptive strategies that dynamically adjust learning based on real‑time performance metrics” (Section VI).  
**Why unresolved:** The current curriculum uses fixed stage durations (uniform thirds of training epochs); this static pacing may not align with varying graph complexities, potentially undertraining or overtraining certain stages for minority classes.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Design a feedback controller that monitors minority‑class recall or attention entropy to trigger stage shifts, test on extreme imbalance cases (Λ=0.1) and high‑heterophily graphs, compare fixed‑schedule CL3AN using F1‑score disparity and convergence speed.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** What automated hyperparameter optimization method (e.g., meta‑learning, Bayesian optimization) can reliably set curriculum loss weights (Λ₁, Λ₂, Λ₃) for CL3AN‑GNN across diverse graph domains without manual per‑dataset tuning?  
**Basis in paper:** Sensitivity analysis (Section M) reveals dataset‑specific optimal ranges for Λ parameters, suggesting manual tuning is needed for each new graph.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper identifies optimal Λ values empirically for a few datasets but provides no generalizable strategy; this limits deployment on novel graphs and increases practical overhead.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a meta‑learning model that predicts Λ values from graph meta‑features (imbalance ratio, heterophily index, average degree), validate on held‑out datasets (e.g., Coauthor CS, PubMed), assess predictive accuracy and resulting model performance compared to grid‑searched baselines.

## Limitations
- Exact difficulty function `D_v` and `D_{u,v}` for curriculum pacing (Definition 1) is unspecified, hindering replication of node/edge selection thresholds.
- Stage transition triggers (fixed epoch counts vs. adaptive) and decay of `α(t)` in loss weighting remain unclear, which may lead to suboptimal curriculum scheduling.
- Critical configuration details (attention head sharing, combination method, edge feature initialization) are not provided; also, the approach may fail on extremely heterophilous graphs (HR > 0.8) where 1‑hop neighborhoods lack class‑discriminative signal.
