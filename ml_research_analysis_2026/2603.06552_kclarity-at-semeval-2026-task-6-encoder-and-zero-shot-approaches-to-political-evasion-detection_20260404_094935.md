# KCLarity at SemEval-2026 Task 6: Encoder and Zero-Shot Approaches to Political Evasion Detection

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06552  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06552  
- **Reference count:** 39  
- **Primary result:** Encoder fine-tuning yields top in‑domain performance (clarity macro‑F1 0.661), while zero‑shot GPT‑5.2 demonstrates superior robustness to distribution shift (hidden test clarity F1 0.74).

## Executive Summary

This paper tackles the detection of ambiguity and evasion in political discourse (SemEval‑2026 Task 6 / CLARITY). The authors compare two modeling formulations: directly predicting clarity labels versus predicting evasion techniques and deriving clarity via a fixed hierarchical taxonomy. They fine‑tune transformer encoder models (RoBERTa, DeBERTa) with variations in input representation (segmented vs. marked), loss weighting, and person‑name masking, and evaluate zero‑shot large language models (GPT‑5.2, Llama, Qwen, Gemma) using a structured chain‑of‑thought prompt. On the public test set, the best encoder (RoBERTa‑large with evasion formulation and marked input) achieves clarity macro‑F1 0.661 and evasion average macro‑F1 0.371. Zero‑shot GPT‑5.2 scores slightly lower (clarity 0.626, evasion 0.358) but on the hidden evaluation set, GPT‑5.2 generalises better (clarity 0.74, evasion 0.50), surpassing a RoBERTa‑large ensemble (clarity 0.72, evasion 0.45). Auxiliary strategies (loss re‑weighting, masking, cross‑domain transfer) did not yield consistent gains, highlighting a trade‑off between in‑domain encoder performance and zero‑shot robustness.

## Method Summary

The study uses the QEvasion dataset of US presidential interview question‑answer pairs. Each instance has a clarity label (3 classes: Clear Reply, Ambivalent Reply, Clear Non‑Reply) and up to three independent evasion annotations across nine evasion techniques. A fixed hierarchical mapping links evasion techniques to clarity levels. Train‑validation splits are dual‑stratified on both evasion and mapped clarity to preserve distributions. For encoders, RoBERTa (base/large) and DeBERTa‑v3 (base/large) are fine‑tuned with a linear classification head, either for direct clarity or for evasion (then deterministically mapped to clarity). Input formats are compared: segmented (`[CLS] answer [SEP] question [SEP]`) vs. marked (`[QUESTION] question [ANSWER] answer`). Training uses AdamW, bfloat16, early stopping on validation macro‑F1, and three random seeds. Optional preprocessing includes person‑name masking and class‑balanced loss re‑weighting (inverse‑frequency, sqrt). Evaluation metrics: clarity macro‑F1; evasion per‑annotator macro‑F1 averaged (F1avg) and ACCmatch (prediction matches at least one annotator). Zero‑shot LLMs are prompted with a structured decision ladder (Figure 5) to output evasion labels, which are then mapped to clarity. Models tested: GPT‑5.2 (API), Llama 3.1/3.3, Qwen3, Gemma 3. The best encoder configuration (RoBERTa‑large, marked input, un‑weighted loss) is identified on the public test set.

## Key Results

- **Encoder best:** RoBERTa‑large (evasion‑based, marked input) achieves clarity macro‑F1 0.661 and evasion F1avg 0.371 on the public test set.  
- **Zero‑shot vs hidden test:** GPT‑5.2 scores clarity 0.626/evasion 0.358 on public test but on the hidden test reaches clarity 0.74 (rank 22/44) and evasion 0.50 (rank 13/33), outperforming a RoBERTa‑large ensemble (clarity 0.72, evasion 0.45).  
- **Auxiliary strategies:** Loss re‑weighting, person‑name masking, and cross‑domain transfer did not produce statistically significant or consistent improvements.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

Three key mechanisms explain the observed performance differences:

### Mechanism 1: Evasion‑first formulation simplifies learning

- **Claim:** Predicting the finer‑grained evasion label and deriving clarity via the task’s hierarchical taxonomy yields performance comparable to directly predicting the clarity label, while reducing modeling complexity.  
- **Mechanism:** The taxonomy maps each of the nine evasion techniques to one of three clarity levels. By training a single multi‑class classifier for evasion, the model learns more discriminative subcategories; clarity is then inferred deterministically, leveraging the assumption that the evasion hierarchy fully specifies the clarity boundary.  
- **Core assumption:** The evasion label space is more semantically coherent and better supervised than the clarity space, where *Ambivalent Reply* is overrepresented and ambiguous (IAA κ=0.65–0.71).  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “two formulations yield comparable performance”  
  - Section 3.4, Table 2: small, statistically overlapping F1 differences between direct and evasion‑based clarity for both RoBERTa‑base and ‑large.  
- **Break condition:** If hierarchical mapping introduces systematic label noise (e.g., multiple evasion techniques plausibly map to different clarity levels for the same instance) or if annotator disagreement in evasion exceeds that in clarity, the evasion‑first approach could degrade clarity performance.

### Mechanism 2: Marked input representation enhances alignment

- **Claim:** Using a marked input representation (`[QUESTION] q [ANSWER] a`) with explicit boundary tokens and question‑first ordering improves model performance over a segmented format.  
- **Mechanism:** The question provides essential grounding for evaluating answer clarity/evasion. Placing the question first and marking boundaries creates a stronger inductive bias for the encoder to align answer content with the question’s demands, potentially mitigating truncation issues where the question might be cut off in long inputs.  
- **Core assumption:** The question is the primary context; answer‑only or answer‑first representations provide weaker supervision.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section 3.3, Tables 8 and 9: consistent improvements in both evasion‑based and direct clarity F1 with the marked format for RoBERTa‑base.  
  - Authors speculate that question‑first ordering reduces likelihood of question truncation.  
- **Break condition:** If answer content is highly truncated and the question always fits, the benefit may vanish. Also, if the special tokens `[QUESTION]`/`[ANSWER]` introduce training instability or the model fails to learn their distinct roles, performance could drop.

### Mechanism 3: Zero‑shot LLMs generalize better to distribution shift

- **Claim:** Zero‑shot large language models (LLMs), particularly GPT‑5.2, exhibit greater robustness to distribution shift than fine‑tuned encoders, as evidenced by better relative performance on the hidden test set.  
- **Mechanism:** Fine‑tuned encoders overfit to idiosyncrasies of the training distribution (speaker‑specific phrasing, topic distribution). LLMs, prompted zero‑shot, rely on broadly acquired linguistic and world knowledge from pre‑training, which may generalise better to unseen interview conditions or slight domain shifts.  
- **Core assumption:** The hidden test set differs systematically from the public test/training sets (e.g., different presidents, time periods, question styles). GPT‑5.2’s broader pre‑training provides a more robust prior.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Section 5.3: On public test, RoBERTa‑large (F1 0.661) beats GPT‑5.2 (0.626); on hidden test, GPT‑5.2 (0.74) beats RoBERTa‑large ensemble (0.72).  
  - Authors hypothesise that the gap reflects distributional differences and that zero‑shot GPT‑5.2 generalises better.  
- **Break condition:** If the distribution shift is minimal or if the LLM’s zero‑shot prompt is poorly matched to the task structure, the fine‑tuned encoder’s in‑domain advantage may persist even on hidden data. Also, if the hidden test set is easier for the fine‑tuned model’s learned heuristics, the observed reversal might not generalise.

## Foundational Learning

- **Hierarchical Multi‑Label Mapping**  
  *Why needed here:* The CLARITY task defines two subtasks with a fixed many‑to‑one mapping (evasion technique → clarity level). Understanding how to exploit hierarchical label relationships is core to the evasion‑first formulation.  
  *Quick check:* If an instance is labeled *Implicit* at the evasion level, what is its guaranteed clarity level? What if multiple evasion labels are plausible (per annotator disagreement)?

- **Class Imbalance and Loss Reweighting**  
  *Why needed here:* The clarity training distribution is highly skewed (59.2% *Ambivalent*). Properly handling imbalance via inverse‑frequency or sqrt weighting is necessary to prevent the model from ignoring minority classes (*Clear Non‑Reply* at 10.3%).  
  *Quick check:* Why might inverse‑frequency weighting increase variance (Table 7)? What is a potential downside of up‑weighting a rare class with only 23 test instances?

- **Zero‑Shot Prompt Engineering for Structured Tasks**  
  *Why needed here:* Evaluating LLMs without fine‑tuning requires a prompt that encodes the task’s decision logic (taxonomy, tie‑breakers). The prompt in Figure 5 is a complex, rule‑based chain‑of‑thought.  
  *Quick check:* What part of the prompt is designed to handle multi‑part questions? What mechanism ensures exactly one label is output?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
- **Encoders:** RoBERTa (base/large), DeBERTa‑v3 (base/large). Input: tokenised question‑answer pairs (max 512 tokens). Heads: linear classifiers for 3‑way clarity or 9‑way evasion.  
- **Decoders (zero‑shot):** GPT‑5.2 (commercial API), Llama 3.1/3.3, Qwen3, Gemma 3 (open‑weight APIs). Prompt: fixed system prompt (Fig. 5) with dynamic QA insertion.  
- **Preprocessing:** Optional person‑name masking (spaCy), input format selection (segmented vs. marked).  
- **Training loop:** AdamW, bfloat16, early stopping on validation macro‑F1, 3 random seeds.  
- **Evaluation:** Clarity macro‑F1 (single gold label). Evasion: per‑annotator macro‑F1 averaged (F1avg) and ACCmatch.

**Critical path:**  
1. Data loading → apply input format → optional masking.  
2. Model forward pass → loss computation (weighted/unweighted CE) → backward pass.  
3. Validation metric selection (macro‑F1) → checkpoint saving.  
4. Test‑time: for evasion‑based clarity, map predicted evasion label to clarity via Table 1.  
5. For LLMs: format prompt → call API → parse JSON → map evasion→clarity.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Encoder fine‑tuning vs. zero‑shot LLM:** Encoders give higher in‑domain performance but require labelled data and risk overfitting; LLMs are more robust and require no training but have API costs and prompt sensitivity.  
- **Input representation:** Marked format (`[QUESTION]…[ANSWER]`) improves performance but needs adding special tokens to tokenizer; segmented format is standard but weaker (Tables 8, 9).  
- **Loss weighting:** Un‑weighted loss generally best (Table 7); weighting increased variance and underperformed, possibly due to mismatch between weighting scheme and validation distribution.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Model stuck near 0.5 macro‑F1 on clarity → likely majority‑class prediction; check class distribution of predictions.  
- Evasion model confuses *General*, *Deflection*, *Implicit* (Table 12) → overlapping semantics and low support; consider multi‑label or probabilistic output.  
- Masking experiments hurt performance → person names may carry useful cues; spaCy NER errors could introduce noise.  
- Cross‑domain transfer fails → severe domain mismatch; requires adaptation like fine‑tuning on target domain.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Train RoBERTa‑large with marked input, un‑weighted loss, stratified split on evasion labels. Compute evasion‑based clarity F1 and evasion F1avg on public test. Verify numbers ~0.66/0.37 (Table 3).  
2. **Ablation – Input format:** Compare segmented vs. marked on RoBERTa‑base (Table 8). Expect marked to win by ~0.08 F1 on clarity.  
3. **Zero‑shot evaluation:** Run GPT‑5.2 with prompt in Figure 5 on public test. Expect clarity F1 ~0.626 (Table 4). If lower, check prompt formatting and JSON parsing.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions, but the mechanism analysis highlights several research directions based on the break conditions:

- How sensitive is the evasion-first formulation to noise in the hierarchical mapping or annotator disagreement in evasion labels?  
- What factors determine the efficacy of marked input representation, and when might it introduce training instability?  
- What specific characteristics of the hidden test set distribution differ from the public set, and how do they affect model generalisation?

## Limitations

- **Dataset & code access:** Exact file formats, preprocessing scripts, and dual‑stratified split implementation are unspecified, hindering direct replication without inspecting the GitHub repository.  
- **Zero‑shot setup:** Critical API parameters (temperature, batch size, rate limits) for GPT‑5.2 are unreported; commercial access may be restricted, making exact replication uncertain.  
- **Generalisation claim:** The observed reversal (LLMs outperforming on hidden test) is based on a single distribution shift; the nature of the shift is not analysed, limiting confidence in robustness conclusions.

## Confidence

- Encoder vs. LLM performance on public test: **High** confidence.  
- Evasion‑first formulation parity: **Medium** confidence.  
- Zero‑shot robustness to shift: **Low** confidence.
