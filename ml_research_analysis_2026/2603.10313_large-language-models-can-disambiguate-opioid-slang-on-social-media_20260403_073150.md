# Large language models can disambiguate opioid slang on social media

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.10313
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10313
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LLMs significantly outperform lexicon baselines across three tasks, demonstrating scalable open-vocabulary detection of low-prevalence opioid content on social media.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the use of large language models (LLMs) to overcome the limitations of lexicon-based monitoring of opioid-related content on social media. Ambiguous slang terms (e.g., “fenty”, “smack”, “lean”) hinder traditional filters, leading to low precision and recall. The authors evaluate four state-of-the-art LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 2.5 Pro) using an iterative prompting scheme across three tasks: (1) disambiguating specific slang terms, (2) lexicon-free identification of opioid posts, and (3) detection of posts containing simulated emergent slang. Manual labels serve as ground truth.

Results show LLMs far exceed lexicon performance: for “fenty” F1 scores range 0.824–0.972 vs. lexicon 0.126; for “smack” 0.540–0.862 vs. 0.009. In the lexicon-free task, LLM F1 scores are 0.544–0.769 vs. lexicon 0.080–0.540, with uniformly higher recall. For emergent slang, LLMs average accuracy 0.784, F1 0.712, precision 0.981, and recall 0.587. The study concludes that LLMs provide a scalable, open-vocabulary solution for identifying low-prevalence opioid content, potentially enhancing public health surveillance.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates four large language models (GPT-4, GPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 2.5 Pro) on three tasks related to opioid detection on social media. Task 1 tests disambiguation of ambiguous slang terms (“fenty”, “smack”, “lean”) using manually labeled tweets. Task 2 assesses lexicon-free identification on a geolocated dataset of ~3 million tweets, comparing against the RedMed lexicon. Task 3 simulates emergent slang by substituting known opioid terms with “Pokemon” names in a balanced set of 160 tweets. All tasks employ an iterative prompting scheme: the model first reasons step-by-step about the tweet content, then outputs a constrained label (“yes”, “no”, “unsure”). Models are queried with temperature 0, and performance is measured against manual ground truth using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1.

## Key Results
- LLMs achieved F1 scores of 0.824–0.972 for “fenty” and 0.540–0.862 for “smack”, far exceeding lexicon F1 scores of 0.126 and 0.009 respectively.
- In lexicon-free detection, LLMs attained F1 scores of 0.544–0.769 compared to lexicon 0.080–0.540, with uniformly higher recall.
- For simulated emergent slang, LLMs averaged accuracy 0.784, F1 0.712, precision 0.981, and recall 0.587, again surpassing lexicon performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** LLMs can accurately disambiguate whether an ambiguous slang term (e.g., “fenty”, “smack”) refers to opioids in a specific social media post.
- **Mechanism:** The pipeline uses iterative prompting: first, the model reasons step-by-step about the tweet’s content; second, it outputs a constrained label (“yes”, “no”, “unsure”). This chain-of-thought approach helps the model weigh contextual cues (e.g., co-occurring drug names, paraphernalia references) against common non-opioid meanings.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM’s training on vast internet corpora has equipped it with sufficient knowledge of both opioid slang and its alternative meanings, and it can apply that knowledge when given a clear reasoning task.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Abstract]: “advanced textual reasoning capability of large language models (LLMs) presents an opportunity to disambiguate these slang terms at scale.”
  - [Section 3.2]: Describes the iterative prompting scheme, temperature set to 0, and context definition.
  - [Corpus]: Corpus neighbors include work on slang knowledge in LLMs, but direct evidence for this mechanism comes from the paper’s own experiments.
- **Break condition:** If the slang term is extremely novel or the surrounding context is too sparse/ambiguous even for human interpretation, the LLM may produce false negatives/positives or “unsure” at high rates.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** LLMs can identify opioid-related posts without any predefined lexicon, capturing diverse linguistic expressions beyond known slang.
- **Mechanism:** The model receives a batch of tweets with no keyword filtering and is prompted to classify each as opioid-related based purely on overall semantics. The open-vocabulary capability allows it to recognize explicit mentions (“fentanyl”, “Norcos”) as well as implicit references.
- **Core assumption:** Opioid-related discussions contain distinctive lexical and thematic patterns that the LLM learned during pre‑training, enabling retrospective identification.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Abstract]: “lexicon-free task… LLM F1 scores (0.544–0.769) surpassed those of lexicons (0.080–0.540).”
  - [Section 3.5]: Details the lexicon-free task using the geolocated dataset of ~3 million tweets.
  - [Corpus]: Related work on informal language processing supports that LLMs can handle non‑standard text, though the specific opioid context is novel.
- **Break condition:** When opioid content is extremely subtle or relies on cultural subtext absent from the LLM’s training, recall may degrade significantly.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** LLMs can still flag opioid relevance when a novel (emergent) slang term is used, by leveraging contextual clues around the unknown token.
- **Mechanism:** Known opioid slang terms are replaced with fictional “Pokemon” names while preserving the rest of the tweet. The LLM must infer that the fake term likely stands for an opioid based on surrounding language (e.g., mentions of “purple”, “syrup”, “high”).
- **Core assumption:** Contextual information near a novel slang term is sufficiently predictive of opioid reference; the LLM’s reasoning can generalize to out‑of‑vocabulary terms.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Abstract]: “emergent slang… LLMs averaged accuracy 0.784, F1 0.712, precision 0.981, recall 0.587, again surpassing lexicon performance.”
  - [Section 3.6]: Explains the Pokemon substitution methodology and balanced dataset of 160 tweets.
  - [Corpus]: Neighbors include “Toward Informal Language Processing: Knowledge of Slang in Large Language Models”, suggesting LLMs have some capacity for novel slang.
- **Break condition:** If the emergent term is completely opaque and lacks supporting context, the LLM may classify the tweet as non‑opioid, leading to missed signals.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Ambiguous slang in social media text.
  - **Why needed here:** Lexicon filters fail because many words (e.g., “smack”, “lean”) have common non-drug meanings; understanding this ambiguity is key to appreciating why LLMs are needed.
  - **Quick check question:** “Give an example of a word that is both an opioid slang and a common non‑opioid term.”

- **Concept:** Iterative prompting (chain‑of‑thought).
  - **Why needed here:** The paper’s pipeline hinges on a two‑step prompt (reason then label). Engineers must grasp how this structure improves classification accuracy.
  - **Quick check question:** “What is the purpose of asking the LLM to reason step‑by‑step before producing a final label?”

- **Concept:** Evaluation metrics for imbalanced binary classification.
  - **Why needed here:** Opioid‑related posts are <3% of the data; raw accuracy is misleading. Precision, recall, and F1 are essential to judge utility.
  - **Quick check question:** “When the positive class is very rare, which metric best reflects a model’s ability to find relevant posts?”

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input ingestion → Pre‑processor → Prompt builder → LLM service → Response parser → Error handler → Evaluation module

### Critical path
1. Load dataset and (if applicable) filter by ambiguous terms.
2. For each batch, construct the iterative prompt (context + `<tweet>` tags).
3. Send to LLM; capture raw response.
4. Parse labels and handle errors.
5. Store predictions for downstream analysis or manual review.

### Design tradeoffs
- *Coverage vs. cost*: Using the LLM on all tweets maximizes recall but is expensive; pre‑filtering with a lexicon reduces cost but may miss opioid posts without known terms.
- *Precision vs. recall*: The “unsure” label provides a middle ground—posts can be sent to human review, balancing automated throughput with accuracy.
- *Model choice*: Newer models (GPT‑5) may offer higher recall/F1 but at higher API cost; open‑source alternatives require GPU resources and may lag in performance.

### Failure signatures
- High volume of *content restriction errors*: likely due to violent/sexual content unrelated to opioids; indicates dataset needs cleaning or the LLM’s safety filters are over‑triggering.
- Many *API errors* (non‑Unicode, mojibake): input text requires sanitization.
- Low recall despite correct prompt: could be missing obvious opioid terms due to tokenization or outdated slang in the LLM’s training.
- Low precision: model over‑generalizing; might need prompt refinement or a more conservative label mapping.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Prompt validation**: Using the 100‑tweet engineering set from the paper, replicate the prompt comparison (5 prompts × 2 temperatures) to confirm the best configuration (iterative, `temperature=0`, specified context).
2. **Lexicon‑based disambiguation**: Take all tweets containing “smack” from the September 2022 Spritzer sample, run the best prompt, and compute F1 against the manually labeled subset (as in Table 5). Expect F1 >0.5.
3. **Lexicon‑free recall test**: Randomly sample 3,000 geolocated tweets (CA/NY), obtain LLM labels, and compare recall to the RedMed lexicon. Confirm LLM recall >0.7.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does model-specific prompt engineering significantly improve LLM disambiguation performance compared to a unified prompting scheme?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors conducted prompt engineering only with GPT-4 and applied the same iterative prompting setup to all four LLMs, noting that model-specific optimization might be necessary for deployment.
- **Why unresolved:** The study assumes prompt transferability across models without validation, possibly overlooking model-specific strengths and weaknesses.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled experiment where each LLM is optimized with model-specific prompts on the same three tasks, with performance differences statistically quantified.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How accurately can LLMs detect opioid-related content containing naturally emergent slang terms that post-date their training data?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The emergent slang task used Pokémon names as simulated new slang, but real emergent slang may involve different linguistic, cultural, and evasive patterns.
- **Why unresolved:** Artificial substitution may not capture the semantic opacity, intentional misspellings, or community-specific coinages of actual emergent internet slang.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A prospective study collecting newly emerged opioid slang terms from social media after model training cutoffs, evaluating LLM detection on real-world posts containing these terms.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can smaller, open-source LLMs achieve comparable performance to commercial models at a fraction of the cost for opioid slang disambiguation?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors highlight the prohibitive cost of commercial LLMs and mention free alternatives (e.g., Llama 3) but did not evaluate them.
- **Why unresolved:** Unknown whether models with fewer parameters retain the contextual reasoning needed for ambiguous slang without excessive false positives/negatives.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmarking leading open-source LLMs on the same three tasks, reporting performance metrics and estimated cost-per-inference compared to commercial APIs.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent do errors or subjectivity in manual labeling affect the measured performance of LLMs in this domain?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors acknowledge potential human error in ground truth labels, citing cases where LLM labels may have corrected manual mistakes, and report only moderate inter-rater agreement.
- **Why unresolved:** Performance metrics rely on single-annotator labels for most data; noisy ground truth may inflate or deflate LLM accuracy estimates.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Independent multi-expert re-annotation of a large, stratified sample of tweets to establish a consensus ground truth, followed by re-evaluation of LLM performance.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What technical and governance safeguards are necessary to ensure LLM-based social media monitoring serves public health goals without enabling individual surveillance or censorship?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly oppose individual-level applications but describe a pipeline without built-in privacy or ethical constraints.
- **Why unresolved:** The proposed workflow could be repurposed for harmful uses; no technical measures (e.g., aggregation, differential privacy) or usage policies are detailed to prevent misuse.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and empirical testing of privacy-preserving architectures (e.g., only outputting aggregated trends) and binding ethical use agreements, evaluated for effectiveness in preventing re-identification or targeting.

## Limitations
- Small manual ground-truth samples with no inter-annotator agreement, leading to potential noise in performance estimates.
- Emergent slang evaluation used artificial “Pokemon” substitutions that may not capture the complexity of real-world slang evolution.
- No comparison with supervised machine learning baselines beyond simple lexicons, leaving open whether fine-tuned models could achieve similar gains.

## Confidence
- LLMs outperform lexicon baselines: High
- LLMs disambiguate ambiguous slang: Medium
- LLMs detect emergent slang: Medium-Low
