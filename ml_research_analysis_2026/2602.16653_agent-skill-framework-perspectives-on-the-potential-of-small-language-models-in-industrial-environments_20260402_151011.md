# Agent Skill Framework: Perspectives on the Potential of Small Language Models in Industrial Environments

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.16653
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16653
- **Reference count**: 19
- **Primary result**: Agent Skill framework improves performance for moderately sized SLMs (12B–30B) on industrial tasks, while tiny models (<4B) fail at reliable skill selection; code-specialized 80B models achieve comparable accuracy to closed-source baselines with better GPU efficiency.

## Executive Summary
The paper explores the Agent Skill framework, which formalizes skill selection as a progressive-disclosure partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP), as a method to enable small language models (SLMs) to perform industrial tasks without relying on external APIs. Evaluating models from 270M to 80B parameters on IMDB, FiNER, and the proprietary InsurBench dataset, the study compares three context-engineering strategies: Direct Instruction (DI), Full-Skill Instruction (FSI), and Agent Skill Instruction (ASI). Results indicate that models below 4B parameters cannot reliably select the correct skill even with a small set, whereas models in the 12B–30B range gain substantial improvements on complex tasks. Furthermore, code-specialized 80B models match closed-source baselines in accuracy while reducing GPU memory-time. The work concludes that progressive disclosure is beneficial for models above 12B, and that chat history helps very small models at a high efficiency cost.

## Method Summary
The experimental methodology centers on the Agent Skill framework, which treats skill selection as a POMDP with an explicit `reveal(ρk)` action to dynamically load skill context. The authors implement three prompt construction strategies: (1) DI, providing only the task instruction; (2) FSI, asynchronously loading all skill descriptors into the prompt; and (3) ASI, which first elicits a skill selection from the model and then reveals the chosen skill's full context before execution. Evaluation uses three datasets: IMDB (sentiment classification), FiNER (financial entity tagging), and InsurBench (insurance claim decision-making). Models range from Gemma-3 270M to Qwen3-80B variants, including both instruction-tuned and code-specialized versions. Primary metrics are task accuracy (classification F1), skill selection accuracy, and efficiency measured as average GPU VRAM-time (GB·min). The skill repository is constructed from `SKILL.md` files containing descriptors, execution policies, and reference mechanisms. Experiments also vary the number of skills (5–100) and incorporate chat history (ASIH) to assess robustness and cost trade-offs.

## Key Results
- Tiny models (<4B parameters) consistently fail at reliable skill selection, even when presented with only 4–6 skills.
- Moderately sized SLMs (12B–30B) achieve significant performance gains on complex tasks using the ASI strategy, maintaining high skill accuracy as the skill count scales to 100.
- Code-specialized 80B models (e.g., Qwen3-80B-Coder) match or exceed closed-source baseline accuracy while reducing GPU memory-time (e.g., 0.657 F1 on FiNER at 6.359 GB·min vs. 0.647 F1 at 5.242 GB·min for the instruct variant).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Progressive disclosure optimizes context by revealing only necessary skill information
- **Claim**: Progressive disclosure reduces hallucinations and computational load by loading full skill context only when uncertainty is high.
- **Mechanism**: The framework models skill selection as a POMDP where the agent maintains a belief over the latent task state and can take `reveal(ρk)` actions to acquire additional information. This trades off the cost of revealing against improved execution accuracy.
- **Core assumption**: Models have limited effective context windows; overwhelming them with all skills upfront degrades performance.
- **Evidence anchors**: The abstract notes that progressive-disclosure design maintains a bounded context length and increases success rates; Section 3.1.2 argues that revealing when uncertain is worthwhile; related work identifies context rot and lost-in-the-middle phenomena.
- **Break condition**: If a model can process full context without accuracy loss (e.g., very large models with perfect retrieval), progressive disclosure adds unnecessary latency.

### Mechanism 2: Reliable skill selection scales with model size
- **Claim**: Models below 4B parameters cannot consistently route to the correct skill, while 12B–30B models maintain accuracy even as skill count grows to 100.
- **Mechanism**: Skill selection is a multi-class classification task requiring fine-grained discrimination between skill descriptors. Larger models possess emergent in-context learning abilities that support this routing.
- **Core assumption**: Skill selection is non-trivial reasoning, not mere pattern completion.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states tiny models fail even with 4–6 skills; Section 4.1 and Figure 2 show Gemma-3-270M/4B have near-zero or declining skill accuracy as skill count increases, while 12B+ models stay stable.
- **Break condition**: With extremely small skill repositories (≤2 highly distinct skills), even tiny models might achieve acceptable routing.

### Mechanism 3: Code-specialized models achieve higher GPU efficiency under Agent Skill
- **Claim**: Code-oriented models reduce GPU VRAM-time while matching or exceeding instruct model accuracy because their training induces structured reasoning patterns that minimize missteps and re-generation.
- **Mechanism**: Code models are trained on corpora emphasizing syntactic precision, control flow, and constrained output formats, leading to better adherence to skill workflows and fewer failed executions.
- **Core assumption**: Skill execution involves stepwise instructions; code-oriented training improves procedural compliance.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract reports Qwen3-80B-Coder achieves 0.657 F1 on FiNER at 6.359 GB·min vs. Qwen3-80B-Instruct's 0.647 F1 at 5.242 GB·min; Section 4.1 shows code models consistently outperform instruct counterparts in accuracy and VRAM-time.
- **Break condition**: For tasks without structured outputs (e.g., creative writing), code specialization may not yield efficiency gains and could hinder performance.

## Foundational Learning
**POMDP with Information Actions**  
*Why needed*: The paper formalizes Agent Skill as a constrained POMDP where `reveal(ρk)` actions trade cost for reduced uncertainty. Understanding this explains why progressive disclosure is optimal.  
*Quick check*: How does the belief state `bt = P(st | o≤t, a<t)` inform the decision to `reveal` versus `execute`?

**Skill Selection Accuracy (Skill ACC)**  
*Why needed*: This metric captures the quality of the routing decision; low Skill ACC dooms the entire pipeline regardless of execution capability. The paper shows it scales with model size.  
*Quick check*: Why does Skill ACC drop sharply for <4B models as skill count increases beyond 10–20?

**VRAM-time (GB·min) as Industrial Cost Proxy**  
*Why needed*: In production, both memory footprint and latency determine cost and throughput. This metric captures the true resource burden better than FLOPS or pure accuracy.  
*Quick check*: Why might a model with slightly lower accuracy but significantly lower VRAM-time be preferable in a real deployment?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Skill Repository (stores SKILL.md descriptors) → Agent Controller (POMDP loop, belief update) → Context Manager (builds DI/FSI/ASI prompts, handles skill revelation) → Model Backend (generates JSON for skill selection and execution) → Evaluation Harness (computes metrics). For ASI, the loop may iterate: after initial skill selection, Context Manager retrieves full skill context from Skill Repository and re-prompts Model Backend.

**Critical path**:  
User input → Context Manager constructs prompt based on strategy (DI/FSI/ASI) → Model Backend returns JSON with `"Skills"` list → If ASI and skills listed, Context Manager retrieves corresponding full skill context → Model Backend executes with skill instructions → final output. Belief update occurs implicitly via conversation history (prompt engineering), not explicit Bayesian state.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- DI vs. FSI vs. ASI: DI is cheapest but worst for complex tasks; FSI gives all context but may cause overload; ASI is optimal but requires high Skill ACC.  
- Model size vs. cost: Larger models (80B) give higher accuracy but increase VRAM-time; 12B–30B may offer best ROI for skill selection; tiny models only viable with DI and simple tasks.  
- Skill descriptor crafting: The design of `ρ_k` (reference mechanism) affects reveal efficiency; poor cross-skill references break ASI for all but largest models.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Skill ACC near 0% (e.g., Gemma-270M): agent ignores skills, reverts to DI behavior.  
- VRAM-time spikes with chat history (e.g., Qwen-80B ASIH doubles cost): avoid history for large models.  
- Accuracy collapse as skill count exceeds ~20 for <12B models: cannot scale skill repos.  
- Code models showing no VRAM improvement: task may not benefit from code-style reasoning.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Baseline comparison**: Run DI, FSI, ASI on IMDB, FiNER, InsurBench for all models. Confirm ASI gains for 12B+ and that FSI often underperforms ASI due to context overload.  
2. **Skill scalability test**: For a fixed model (e.g., Qwen-30B), increase skill count from 5 to 100. Measure Skill ACC decay curve. Expect steep drop for ≤4B models, stable for 12B+.  
3. **Chat history cost-benefit**: Compare ASI vs. ASIH (with trimmed history) on InsurBench. Quantify accuracy delta vs. VRAM-time increase. Expect tiny models benefit more, large models degrade cost-efficiency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What are the underlying cognitive or architectural reasons that prevent tiny models (<4B parameters) from reliably performing skill selection?** The paper identifies the empirical failure but does not investigate whether this stems from insufficient in-context learning capacity, lack of emergent reasoning, poor parsing of structured descriptors, or an inability to maintain the belief state in the POMDP formulation. Ablation studies, probing internal representations, and fine-tuning on synthetic skill-selection tasks could provide evidence.

2. **Why do code-specialized LLMs achieve superior GPU efficiency while matching accuracy under Agent Skill?** The paper notes the phenomenon but provides no mechanistic explanation. It is unclear if this stems from more efficient token generation, different attention patterns, training objectives favoring structured output, or better compression of the skill repository. Comparative analysis of activation patterns, attention head utilization, and token generation speed could resolve it.

3. **How should the structure and representation of Skill.md be optimally designed to support progressive disclosure and intra-skill referencing for smaller models?** The paper used a fixed, simple skill format and found SLMs fail at nested dependencies. Unknown modifications (e.g., hierarchical formatting, explicit tagging, simplified referencing) could make progressive disclosure feasible for <12B models. Controlled experiments varying syntax, length, and cross-reference structure are needed.

4. **To what extent does the choice of keyword (e.g., "Skill" vs. "Expertise" vs. "Know-how") in the skill repository title systematically affect routing accuracy and efficiency?** Table 5 shows "Expertise" outperformed "Skill" in classification accuracy, and "Know-how" improved GPU memory efficiency, but the study was limited to one model and dataset. The underlying reason—whether related to pre-training corpus frequency, concreteness, or alignment with the system prompt—is not investigated. A larger-scale study across models, tasks, and synonyms, coupled with analysis of token representations, could resolve it.

5. **For very small models, does incorporating chat history (ASI+H) truly improve task performance by providing contextual grounding, or does it primarily induce overfitting to superficial dialogue patterns?** The paper reports accuracy jumps but does not analyze the nature of the improvement; it could be due to the model extracting keywords from recent turns rather than deeper reasoning. Error case studies comparing ASI vs. ASIH outputs, checking for spurious pattern reliance, and evaluating on perturbed history data would provide evidence.

## Limitations
- The proprietary InsurBench dataset is not publicly released, preventing external validation and replication on that task.
- Details on constructing the temporary skill repository (e.g., source of distractor skills, sampling method, pairing with ground-truth skill) are vague, affecting reproducibility of skill selection tests.
- The exact model identifiers (mid‑2025 Qwen3 variants) may be inaccessible under the same names on Hugging Face today, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- Progressive disclosure benefit: Medium
- Skill selection scaling with size: High
- Code-specialized efficiency: Medium

## Next Checks

2. Test Agent Skill on three additional industrial datasets (e.g., manufacturing logs, clinical notes, legal contracts) to evaluate cross-domain robustness.
