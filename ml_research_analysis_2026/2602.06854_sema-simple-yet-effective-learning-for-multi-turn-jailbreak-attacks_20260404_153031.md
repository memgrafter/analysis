# SEMA: Simple yet Effective Learning for Multi-Turn Jailbreak Attacks

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** `2602.06854`
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06854
- **Reference count:** 40
- **One-line primary result:** SEMA achieves state-of-the-art attack success rates, averaging 80.1% ASR@1 across three victim models—33.9 percentage points above prior best.

## Executive Summary

Multi-turn jailbreak attacks present a realistic threat to safety-aligned chatbots, yet existing methods struggle with exploration complexity and intent drift across turns. SEMA addresses these challenges with a simple yet effective two-stage framework. The first stage employs prefilling self-tuning to stabilize the generation of multi-turn adversarial rollouts by fine-tuning the attacker on its own non-refusal, correctly formatted plans, using a minimal prefix such as "1." The second stage applies reinforcement learning with an intent-drift-aware reward—computed by an LLM judge—to preserve harmful intent while generating open-loop, response-agnostic attack sequences. This design eliminates the need for victim feedback during planning and dramatically reduces the search space.

Across standard benchmarks (AdvBench and HarmBench) and three diverse victim models, SEMA attains an average attack success rate of 80.1% at the first turn, improving upon the previous best by 33.9 percentage points. It consistently outperforms single-turn, manually scripted, and template-driven multi-turn baselines, as well as variants trained via supervised fine-tuning or direct preference optimization. The method is compact, reproducible, and transfers across targets, marking a significant advance in automated jailbreak attack generation.

## Method Summary

SEMA consists of a two-stage training pipeline. Stage one, prefilling self-tuning, generates multi-turn rollouts by prefixing the attacker model with "1." and sampling continuations; non-refusal and parseable rollouts (consecutive numbered turns) are collected and used for supervised fine-tuning, thereby teaching the model to emit valid attack plans without external data. Stage two, reinforcement learning, uses the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm in an open-loop regime: the attacker produces a fixed-length adversarial sequence (Qadv) without conditioning on intermediate victim responses. Each Qadv is executed on a training-time victim model (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B); the final response is scored by an LLM judge (GPT-4.1-mini) on intent alignment, compliance risk, and level of detail. These scores are combined into an intent-drift-aware reward `R_ida = ½·Alignment·(Risk+Detail)` and added to a format reward. Group-relative advantages computed over sampled rollouts guide the policy update, encouraging plans that preserve harmful intent and elicit detailed, risky outputs.

## Key Results

- Average ASR@1 of **80.1%** across three victim models on AdvBench and HarmBench.
- Improves over the prior state-of-the-art by **33.9 percentage points**.
- Outperforms all single-turn, manually scripted, and template-driven multi-turn baselines, as well as SFT and DPO variants.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Prefilling Self‑Tuning Stabilizes Rollouts

**Claim:** Prefilling self‑tuning stabilizes multi‑turn rollout generation by de‑refusing the attacker model and ensuring parseable, format‑consistent adversarial plans.

**Mechanism:** A minimal prefix (e.g., "1.") is added to the start of the attacker’s generation. The model continues in a list format ("2.", "3.", …), producing a numbered sequence of adversarial prompts. These self‑generated, non‑refusal rollouts are then used for supervised fine‑tuning, teaching the attacker to emit valid multi‑turn plans without external data.

**Core assumption:** Safety‑aligned LLMs refuse harmful requests but will continue generating if provided a benign structural prefix that anchors the output format.

**Break condition:** If the attacker refuses even with prefilling, the rollout pool empties, halting training.

### Mechanism 2: Intent‑Drift‑Aware Reward Preserves Harmful Intent

**Claim:** An intent‑drift‑aware reward, computed via an LLM judge, guides reinforcement learning to preserve the original harmful intent across multiple turns while eliciting detailed, risky responses.

**Mechanism:** The reward combines three components scored by an LLM judge: (i) *Intent Alignment* measures topical relevance to the original harmful query; (ii) *Compliance Risk* rates the illegality/policy violation in the victim’s final response; (iii) *Level of Detail* favors concrete, actionable outputs. The aggregate reward penalizes drift and rewards successful, detailed jailbreaks. This reward, plus a format reward, is used with GRPO to update the attacker policy.

**Core assumption:** Intent drift is a primary failure mode in multi‑turn attacks and can be quantified and penalized by an LLM‑based evaluator.

**Break condition:** If the reward model mis‑scores alignment or risk (e.g., due to judge brittleness), the attacker may optimize for spurious cues, leading to brittle attacks or false positives.

### Mechanism 3: Open‑Loop Generation Reduces Exploration Complexity

**Claim:** Open‑loop, response‑agnostic attack generation reduces exploration complexity by factorizing the search space from the joint prompt‑response product to the prompt sequence alone, enabling efficient, batched RL training.

**Mechanism:** The attacker produces a complete, fixed‑length adversarial plan (Qadv) in one shot, independent of the victim’s intermediate replies. The plan is executed on the victim only after generation to compute the reward. This decoupling avoids the combinatorial explosion of closed‑loop, response‑conditioned search.

**Core assumption:** An effective multi‑turn jailbreak can be pre‑planned; the victim’s exact mid‑session replies are not needed to design a successful sequence.

**Break condition:** If the victim’s mid‑turn replies are highly unpredictable and strongly influence the final success, open‑loop plans may frequently fail due to distribution shift at test time.

## Foundational Learning

### Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is an online reinforcement learning algorithm that computes advantages relative to a group of sampled rollouts, reducing variance and stabilizing training in sparse-reward settings.

**Why needed here:** SEMA uses online RL to explore a high-dimensional, sparse-reward space (multi-turn plans). GRPO's group-based advantage calculation stabilizes learning by comparing rollouts within a query group, making it suitable for outcome-supervised learning without a separate reward model.

**Quick check question:** How does GRPO's use of relative advantages within a group reduce the variance that would arise from using absolute rewards in open-loop multi-turn generation?

### Prefill Rollout for Alignment Hacking

Prefill rollout is a technique to elicit continued generation from safety-aligned models by providing a non-semantic structural prefix (e.g., "1.") that anchors the output format, bypassing the initial refusal.

**Why needed here:** Standard safety-aligned models refuse to generate adversarial prompts. Prefill rollout injects a non-semantic structural cue ("1.") to bypass the initial refusal, providing a starting point for self-generated rollouts.

**Quick check question:** Why does adding a simple prefix like "1." cause the model to continue in a list format rather than re-asserting its refusal?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map (plain text):**

Attacker Model (πθ) → Prefilling Controller (prefix "1.") → generate multi‑turn plan Qadv → execute on Training‑time Victim (V_train) → final response r_T → Reward Judge (J_eval) → compute R_ida and format reward → GRPO update → updated Attacker Model (πθ)

**Critical path:**

1. For a batch of harmful queries, run prefilling rollout with the current attacker to generate Qadv.
2. Execute each Qadv on V_train to get final response r_T.
3. Evaluate r_T with J_eval to get R_IDA and R_format; form total reward R.
4. Compute group advantages and update πθ via GRPO objective.
5. Repeat from step 1.

**Design tradeoffs:**

- *Open‑loop vs. Closed‑loop:* Open‑loop reduces search cost and enables batched RL but may produce plans brittle to victim response variance. Closed‑loop could adapt but explodes branching factor and requires interactive victim APIs.
- *Reward design:* Using an LLM judge is flexible but introduces judge brittleness and cost. Simpler proxies (e.g., no‑refusal phrase check) fail to capture intent drift.
- *Training‑time victim strength:* A stronger V_train yields more robust attackers but may produce fewer exploitable rollouts initially, slowing early learning.

**Failure signatures:**

- High refusal rate in rollouts: attacker rejects generating multi‑turn prompts even with prefilling → prefilling trick ineffective; need less‑aligned base or stronger prefix.
- Low parseability (format reward ≈ 0): rollouts don’t follow numbered list format → prefilling self‑tuning failed; increase SFT iterations or filter low‑quality self‑rollouts.
- High no‑refusal ASR but low LLM‑classifier ASR: indicates severe intent drift (victim replies safe but non‑refusal) → intent‑drift‑aware reward components under‑weighted; adjust reward scaling or improve judge prompts.
- Poor transfer to stronger victims: attacker only works on V_train → overfitting to V_train’s response patterns; increase victim diversity during RL or use a more capable V_train.

**First 3 experiments:**

1. **Ablate prefilling self‑tuning:** Train the RL attacker without the initial SFT stage. Expected: high refusal rates, unstable training, near‑zero ASR due to unusable rollouts.
2. **Ablate intent alignment in reward:** Set Alignment(r_T, q) = 1 always. Expected: ASR drop on strict judges (LLM‑classifier) despite similar no‑refusal rates, confirming drift mitigation.
3. **Vary T_max during training:** Train with T_max = 1, 3, 5, 7, 10. Expected: ASR increases with more turns up to a capacity limit (e.g., T_max=7 for 3B/7B models), then degrades due to low‑quality long rollouts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1: Cost‑Aware Rewards for Turn Optimization

- **Question:** How can cost‑aware rewards (e.g., per‑turn penalties) be integrated into SEMA to minimize unnecessary turns in multi‑turn jailbreak attacks without compromising success rates?
- **Basis:** Section E.1 identifies turn redundancy as a key limitation and proposes "cost‑aware rewards that penalize unnecessary turns" for future work.
- **Why unresolved:** The current SEMA uses a fixed maximum turn budget without any penalty for extra turns, leading to overlong dialogues that may increase detectability and reduce practicality.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Introduce a turn penalty term (e.g., subtract λ·T) into the reward function and experimentally demonstrate a significant reduction in average turns per successful attack while keeping ASR within a small margin of the original SEMA across multiple datasets and victims.

### Open Question 2: Extension to Multimodal Inputs

- **Question:** How can SEMA be extended to multimodal inputs (e.g., images, audio) to conduct jailbreak attacks beyond pure text, and what modality‑aware rewards and safety judges are required?
- **Basis:** Section E.2 states that the framework is currently text‑only and that extending to vision and audio "requires modality‑aware rewards and safety judges."
- **Why unresolved:** SEMA's generation, reward computation, and evaluation are all designed for text; no existing design addresses harmful intent when adversarial prompts include visual or auditory elements or how to plan across modalities.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A multimodal variant of SEMA that produces adversarial prompts combining text with images/audio and employs a multimodal safety judge, validated by successful jailbreaks on systems like GPT‑4o or LLaVA using non‑text inputs.

### Open Question 3: Diversity of Attack Strategies

- **Question:** What reinforcement learning modifications can enable a single trained SEMA attacker to produce a diverse range of attack strategies at inference time, rather than converging to a narrow prompting pattern?
- **Basis:** Section E.3 notes that a single SEMA run "tends to exhibit a narrow prompting paradigm" and suggests exploring "diversity rewards or diversity‑enhanced online reinforcement learning" to broaden the tactic space.
- **Why unresolved:** The standard GRPO objective optimizes only for high scalar reward, which typically converges to one dominant strategy; without explicit diversification, intra‑model variance is limited.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Add a diversity‑promoting term (e.g., entropy bonus or similarity regularization) to the GRPO loss and show increased diversity of generated attacks (e.g., higher embedding‑based pairwise distances) with minimal degradation in ASR.
