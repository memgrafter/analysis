# Hierarchical Zero-Order Optimization for Deep Neural Networks
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10607
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10607
- Reference count: 32
- Primary result: HZO reduces query complexity from O(ML²) to O(ML log L) while achieving competitive accuracy with backpropagation on CIFAR-10 (74.2%) and a 10-class ImageNet subset (65.0%).

## Executive Summary
Hierarchical Zeroth-Order (HZO) optimization tackles the prohibitive query complexity of standard zeroth-order methods for deep neural networks. By recursively bisecting the network along depth and estimating Jacobians via finite differences, HZO reduces the number of forward passes per gradient from O(ML²) to O(ML log L). Experiments on CIFAR-10 and a 10-class ImageNet subset show that HZO attains competitive accuracy (74.2% and 65.0% respectively) and maintains high gradient cosine similarity (>0.95) with backpropagation. The method's stability is shown to depend on the network's effective Lipschitz constant; residual connections and smooth activations like GELU are crucial for deep models. HZO enables gradient-based training without backpropagation, opening avenues for neuromorphic hardware and alternative optimization paradigms. Its divide-and-conquer structure also offers natural parallelism across depth, making it a scalable solution for very deep networks.

## Method Summary
HZO implements a hierarchical gradient estimation scheme. Starting from the full network, it recursively selects a midpoint layer. The right sub-network (from midpoint to output) is treated as a function whose Jacobian with respect to the midpoint activation is estimated using bidirectional finite differences: for each column, perturbations are applied to the midpoint and the resulting change in output is measured. The target signal from the loss is then backpropagated through this Jacobian to obtain the gradient signal for the left sub-network. The process recurses on both left and right sub-networks until single layers are reached, where a local delta rule update is performed. This binary recursion yields a total of O(ML log L) forward passes per gradient. For convolutional networks, Spatial Parallel Perturbation (SPP) reduces cost further by grouping independent spatial patches based on receptive fields. The method is mathematically equivalent to standard backpropagation when Jacobian estimates are exact, and practical stability requires the network's Lipschitz constant near unity—achieved via residual connections and smooth activations.

## Key Results
- Reduces query complexity from O(ML²) to O(ML log L).
- Achieves 74.2% accuracy on CIFAR-10 and 65.0% on a 10-class ImageNet subset.
- Maintains gradient cosine similarity >0.95 with backpropagation, indicating stable gradient estimates.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Hierarchical decomposition along the network depth reduces the query complexity of zeroth-order optimization from O(ML²) to O(ML log L).
- **Mechanism:** The network is recursively bisected into sub-networks along the depth dimension. At each recursion level, Jacobians for a midpoint layer are estimated via finite differences on the right sub-network, and target signals are propagated backward. This replaces a single, full-network perturbation with many small, local perturbations organized in a binary tree structure.
- **Core assumption:** The computational cost of a forward pass through a sub-network scales linearly with its depth, and Jacobian estimation at a split point costs O(M * depth_of_right_subnetwork).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] States the reduction from O(ML²) to O(ML log L).
  - [section 4.2] Provides the recurrence T(L) = 2·T(L/2) + C·Jacobian(L/2) and solves it to T(L) = O(ML log L) via level-wise summation.
  - [corpus] The related corpus focuses on other ZO efficiency improvements (e.g., low-rank, neuron-wise) but does not contain work analyzing hierarchical depth decomposition; evidence for this specific mechanism is thus primarily from the paper itself.
- **Break condition:** If the recursive splitting creates sub-networks of depth 1 too early (e.g., for non-power-of-2 depths), the theoretical O(log L) factor may degrade slightly, but the asymptotic improvement remains.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The recursive Jacobian-target product in HZO is mathematically equivalent to the chain rule in backpropagation, ensuring the estimated gradient direction matches the true gradient.
- **Mechanism:** At each bisection, the target signal T_k for the midpoint is computed as T_k = J^T T_j, where J is the Jacobian of the right sub-network. This directly implements the chain rule ∂L/∂a_k = (∂a_j/∂a_k)^T ∂L/∂a_j. When recursion reaches a single layer, the local update ∆W_i ∝ T_i a_{i-1}^T is the delta rule, which is equivalent to the BP gradient for a linear layer.
- **Core assumption:** Each layer's transformation is locally differentiable (or can be approximated), and the Jacobian estimation via finite differences is sufficiently accurate for the product.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.1] Theorem 1 formally states gradient equivalence and sketches the proof linking the recursive product to the chain rule.
  - [section 3.2.3] Shows the local update rule matches the BP gradient expression.
  - [corpus] The corpus contains papers on ZO optimization and biologically plausible learning, but none prove equivalence for a hierarchical recursive scheme; this is a novel theoretical contribution of the paper.
- **Break condition:** If the finite-difference Jacobian estimate has an error that is not orthogonal to the true gradient direction, the product J^T T_j could become misaligned, though the paper's error analysis bounds this.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** HZO maintains numerical stability (high gradient cosine similarity) only when the network operates near the "unitary limit" (L_lip ≈ 1), where L_lip is the product of layer-wise Lipschitz constants.
- **Mechanism:** The error in a zeroth-order gradient estimate scales as O(β δ² L L_lip), where β is smoothness, δ is perturbation size, and L is depth. HZO's recursive bisection does not change this worst-case exponential dependence on L_lip. For L_lip > 1, errors explode exponentially with depth; for L_lip ≈ 1, error scales linearly with L (O(L β δ²)), making deep networks tractable.
- **Core assumption:** Each layer function f_l is β-smooth and L_lip-Lipschitz, and the network's effective Lipschitz constant is the product of per-layer constants.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.3.3] Derives the error bound and explicitly states the stability condition L_lip ≈ 1, linking it to residual connections.
  - [section 5.4.1] Empirically shows GELU (smoother than ReLU) yields higher cosine similarity, supporting the smoothness assumption.
  - [corpus] Corpus papers on ZO optimization discuss error scaling with dimension, but not specifically the hierarchical case or the unitary limit; evidence is paper-specific.
- **Break condition:** If L_lip significantly exceeds 1 (e.g., deep plain CNNs with ReLU and poor initialization), error accumulation will degrade gradient estimates, as seen in the lower cosine similarity for Plain CNN in Figure 1.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Zeroth-Order (Derivative-Free) Optimization**
  - Why needed here: HZO is fundamentally a ZO method; it estimates gradients using only function evaluations (forward passes) without relying on backward differentiation. Understanding the basic finite-difference estimator (e.g., Gaussian or coordinate perturbations) is prerequisite to grasping HZO's Jacobian estimation.
  - Quick check question: How does the bidirectional difference formula (Equation 3) approximate the Jacobian-vector product for a sub-network, and what is the role of the perturbation ϵ?
- **Concept: Chain Rule and Jacobian Matrices**
  - Why needed here: The core of HZO is recursively applying the chain rule via Jacobian-transpose times vector products (T_k = J^T T_j). Comprehending how gradients propagate through a composite function is essential to see the equivalence to backpropagation.
  - Quick check question: Given a composite function F(x) = f₂(f₁(x)), express the gradient ∂L/∂x using Jacobians J₁ and J₂. How does HZO compute this recursively?
- **Concept: Lipschitz Constants and Error Propagation in Deep Networks**
  - Why needed here: The theoretical error bound and stability condition hinge on understanding how the Lipschitz constant of a deep network (product of per-layer constants) amplifies finite-difference errors. This explains why HZO works better with residual connections.
  - Quick check question: If each layer has Lipschitz constant L_lip = 1.1, what happens to the worst-case error bound for a 100-layer network compared to a 10-layer network? What architectural trick can help keep L_lip ≈ 1?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - Network Depth Decomposition: Recursive bisection function `HZO(N_{i:j}, a_{i-1}, T_j)`.
  - Jacobian Estimator: For a midpoint k, estimates each column of ∂a_j/∂a_k using `(N_right(a_k + ϵe_m) - N_right(a_k - ϵe_m)) / (2ϵ)`.
  - Target Propagation: Computes T_k = J^T T_j.
  - Local Weight Update: At leaf layers (i=j), applies ∆W_i = -η T_i a_{i-1}^T (or uses autograd for exact delta rule).
  - Spatial Parallel Perturbation (SPP): For CNNs, groups spatial locations with non-overlapping receptive fields to reduce query cost.
- **Critical path:** Input x → Forward through left sub-network to midpoint a_k → Perturb a_k to estimate Jacobian of right sub-network → Compute T_k → Recurse on right (from a_k to T_j) and left (from a_{i-1} to T_k) → At leaves, update weights. The total number of forward passes per gradient is O(M L log L).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Depth vs. Width Complexity: HZO reduces the depth dependence from O(L²) to O(L log L), but the width M dependence remains O(M). This makes it suitable for deep but not extremely wide networks without additional width-compression strategies.
  - Stability vs. Activation Choice: Using smooth activations (GELU) improves stability but may affect model capacity; ReLU is cheaper but risks higher ZO error. Residual connections are practically necessary to maintain L_lip ≈ 1.
  - Precision vs. Efficiency: Float32 is the minimum for deep HZO; float16 causes error explosion (Section 5.4.2). Higher precision (float64) is stable but slower.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Cosine similarity << 1** for deep plain CNNs (L ≥ 32) with ReLU, indicating gradient misalignment due to L_lip > 1.
  - **Training divergence or oscillating loss** when using float16 for networks deeper than ~16 layers.
  - **No improvement over standard ZO** when network depth is small (L ≤ 8), where the O(L log L) overhead recursion may not yet pay off.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Gradient Cosine Similarity vs. Depth:** Train a plain CNN and ResNet with HZO, compute average cosine similarity between HZO and BP gradients per layer for L=16,32,64. Expect ResNet to maintain >0.95; plain CNN should drop significantly.
  2. **Wall-clock Time per Gradient vs. Depth:** On MLPs of varying L, measure time for one gradient estimation using HZO vs. ZO-SGD (full perturbation) vs. DeepZero (neuron-wise). HZO should show O(L log L) scaling, crossing ZO-SGD's O(L²) around L≈32.
  3. **Activation Function Ablation:** Compare CIFAR-10 accuracy after fixed training steps for networks using ReLU vs. GELU under HZO. Also measure gradient cosine similarity. Expect GELU to yield higher similarity and better final accuracy, especially for deeper nets.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** For architectures lacking residual connections or orthogonal initialization, can HZO's gradient estimation remain numerically stable at extreme depths beyond the unitary limit (Llip ≈ 1)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The error analysis shows exponential error growth if Llip > 1, and stability is only demonstrated with ResNet. The paper states that residual connections anchor Llip near unity.
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical bound (Equation 12) suggests error explodes without Llip ≈ 1, but practical techniques to enforce this constraint for non-residual architectures are not explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train very deep plain CNNs (NoRes) with HZO, measuring gradient cosine similarity and test accuracy as depth increases; compare against ResNet baselines.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does HZO interact with modern adaptive optimizers (e.g., Adam, Lion) beyond the observed fluctuations on ImageNet, and can optimizer-specific noise accumulation be mitigated?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper attributes test set fluctuations on ImageNet to "internal state dynamics of the Adam optimizer" rather than HZO gradient errors.
- **Why unresolved:** The interplay between zeroth-order noise and optimizer state (e.g., momentum, variance) is not analyzed; different optimizers may amplify or dampen HZO's inherent noise.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic comparison of HZO with SGD, Adam, and other optimizers on the same deep CNN, tracking gradient signal-to-noise ratio and optimizer state statistics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the Spatial Parallel Perturbation (SPP) assumption of independent receptive fields hold for modern convolutional architectures with overlapping receptive fields (e.g., dilated convolutions, large kernels), and what is the impact on gradient fidelity?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] SPP reduces queries by assuming non-overlapping receptive fields (Equation 6), but its applicability to standard CNNs with overlapping kernels is not validated.
- **Why unresolved:** The efficiency gain relies on spatial independence; violating this may degrade Jacobian estimation accuracy or require more queries.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply HZO with and without SPP on architectures like ResNeXt or EfficientNet, comparing gradient cosine similarity and final accuracy at high resolutions.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can HZO scale to full ImageNet-1K or billion-parameter language models, and does the O(M L log L) complexity hold in practice for such large-scale settings?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Results are only on a 10-class ImageNet subset; the paper mentions LLM fine-tuning in related work but does not test HZO at that scale.
- **Why unresolved:** Query complexity analysis is asymptotic; constant factors, memory overhead for hierarchical recursion, and practical wall-clock time on massive models are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train a medium-sized vision transformer (ViT-B/16) on full ImageNet-1K and a 7B-parameter LLM on a fine-tuning task, reporting accuracy, training time, and memory usage versus BP and ZO baselines.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Is there a fundamental trade-off between activation function smoothness (e.g., GELU vs. ReLU) and computational efficiency in HZO, given that smooth activations improve gradient fidelity but increase per-layer compute?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Experiments show GELU yields higher cosine similarity than ReLU due to bounded second-order curvature.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not quantify the computational cost of evaluating smoother activations versus the accuracy gain; for large models, this trade-off may be critical.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmark HZO with a spectrum of activations (ReLU, GELU, Swish, smooth variants) on deep CNNs, measuring not only gradient similarity and accuracy but also per-forward-pass latency and total training time.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameters and architecture specifics** are not provided: exact network depths/widths, learning rates, perturbation scale ϵ, batch sizes, Adam settings, and precise wall-clock to epoch conversion. This hinders direct replication.
- **Spatial Parallel Perturbation (SPP) details** for convolutional layers are ambiguously described, including how receptive fields are computed and ensured non-overlapping across patches; implementation errors could invalidate Jacobian estimates.
- **Generalization of theoretical assumptions**: the analysis requires per-layer smoothness (β) and Lipschitz constants (L_lip) whose product must ≈1 for stability. Real networks (e.g., with ReLU or poor initialization) may violate this, leading to unvalidated error bounds.
