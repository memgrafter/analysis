# Bayesian Learning in Episodic Zero-Sum Games
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.20604
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20604
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: Posterior sampling achieves sublinear expected regret O(HS√(ABHK log(SABHK))) in episodic zero-sum Markov games with unknown transitions and rewards, regardless of opponent strategy.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies Bayesian learning in episodic finite-horizon zero-sum Markov games with unknown transition and reward models. The authors propose a posterior sampling algorithm where each player maintains a Bayesian posterior over the game, samples a model at the start of each episode, and computes a Nash equilibrium policy for the sampled model. Theoretical analysis shows that this approach attains expected regret O(HS√(ABHK log(SABHK))) with respect to the true game's equilibrium, and the guarantee holds even against arbitrary opponent strategies. Empirical evaluation in a grid-world predator-prey environment confirms sublinear regret growth and demonstrates favorable performance compared to a fictitious-play baseline.

## Method Summary
The algorithm operates as follows: each player maintains a Bayesian posterior distribution over possible Markov game models (transitions and rewards) conditioned on historical data. At the beginning of every episode, each player independently draws a model from their posterior. They then solve for a Nash equilibrium policy assuming the opponent will play according to that equilibrium under the sampled model. The joint policy is executed for the episode, and the observed state-action-reward transitions are used to update the posteriors. Over time, the posterior concentrates on the true model, yielding low cumulative regret.

## Key Results
- Sublinear expected regret bound O(HS√(ABHK log(SABHK))) relative to the true game's equilibrium.
- Regret guarantee holds irrespective of the opponent's strategy, including worst-case adversarial play.
- Empirical results in a predator-prey grid-world show sublinear regret growth and outperformance over fictitious play.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Posterior sampling (Thompson sampling) balances exploration and exploitation: models with higher posterior probability are sampled more often, but uncertainty drives occasional selection of less certain models, ensuring adequate coverage of the state space. Computing an equilibrium policy for the sampled model guarantees that each player best-responds to the opponent under that model. As the posterior concentrates on the true model, the average regret compared to the true equilibrium becomes sublinear. The analysis does not depend on any assumptions about the opponent's behavior, making the algorithm robust even to arbitrarily adversarial strategies.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Posterior Maintainer -> Model Sampler -> Equilibrium Solver -> Policy Executor
- **Critical path**: Episode start → sample model from posterior → compute equilibrium policy → execute policy for H steps → observe outcomes → update posterior → repeat.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Accuracy of equilibrium solver vs. computational time; prior specification vs. robustness; discretization of model space vs. tractability.
- **Failure signatures**: Posterior fails to concentrate (e.g., due to misspecification) leading to linear regret; approximate equilibrium solver degrades theoretical guarantees; opponent exploits model errors when posterior is inaccurate.
- **First experiments**: 1) Track posterior convergence (e.g., KL divergence to true model). 2) Compare regret curves against fictitious play baseline. 3) Vary prior concentration (e.g., inverse temperature) to assess impact on sample efficiency and stability.

## Limitations
- The regret bound contains combinatorial factors (S, A, B, H, K) that may be large, raising concerns about scalability.
- The algorithm requires solving a Nash equilibrium at each episode start; computational cost and complexity are not analyzed.
- Empirical validation is restricted to a single grid-world predator-prey environment; generalizability to more complex or imperfect-information games remains untested.
