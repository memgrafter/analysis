# Better Eyes, Better Thoughts: Why Vision Chain-of-Thought Fails in Medicine

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06665  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06665  
- **Reference count:** 28  
- **Primary result:** Vision chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting degrades accuracy in medical VQA; training-free inference-time interventions (region-of-interest anchoring and expert description grounding) can reverse this effect, e.g., up to +18.29% on SLAKE for Qwen3-VL-8B.

## Executive Summary
Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, which elicits step-by-step reasoning from vision-language models (VLMs), unexpectedly harms performance in medical visual question answering (VQA) compared to direct answering. This study identifies a “medical perception bottleneck” – subtle domain-specific cues are poorly grounded, and CoT amplifies these errors through propagation. To overcome this, the authors propose two simple, training-free interventions applied at inference time: (i) anchoring perception using region-of-interest (ROI) bounding boxes to focus visual attention, and (ii) grounding reasoning with expert-level textual guidance (e.g., radiology reports). Across multiple medical benchmarks (SLAKE, VQA-RAD) and model families (Qwen-VL, GPT-4V, LLaVA), both interventions consistently improve CoT accuracy, mitigate the degradation, and in several settings invert the performance gap, allowing CoT to match or surpass direct answering. For instance, on SLAKE, the combined intervention boosted Qwen3-VL-8B’s CoT accuracy by 18.29%. The findings underscore the importance of robust visual grounding in medical reasoning and offer practical, model-agnostic solutions.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluated CoT and direct answering on several medical VQA datasets (e.g., SLAKE, VQA-RAD) using prominent VLMs (Qwen-VL, GPT-4V, LLaVA). They observed a consistent accuracy drop for CoT in this domain. To address it, they introduced two training-free, inference-time interventions: perception anchoring (supplying ROI bounding boxes that highlight relevant image regions) and description grounding (appending expert textual descriptions to the question). These interventions were tested both individually and in combination, with all experiments measuring standard accuracy metrics. No model fine-tuning was performed; instead, the auxiliary inputs (ROI coordinates, expert text) were incorporated directly into the model’s context at inference.

## Key Results
- CoT prompting degrades accuracy relative to direct answering in medical VQA benchmarks across multiple VLMs.  
- Perception anchoring (ROI boxes) and description grounding (expert text) each individually improve CoT accuracy, mitigating the degradation.  
- The combined intervention fully restores or even reverses the performance inversion; e.g., on SLAKE with Qwen3-VL-8B, CoT accuracy increased by up to 18.29%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The failure of CoT is attributed to a “medical perception bottleneck.” Medical images contain subtle, domain-specific cues that VLMs struggle to reliably detect. In a CoT pipeline, the reasoning chain is built incrementally from the initial visual grounding. If the perception step is weak or erroneous, these mistakes propagate through every subsequent step, eventually corrupting the final answer. Direct answering may bypass explicit reasoning and instead latch onto superficial patterns that occasionally coincide with correct answers, explaining why it outperforms CoT.

The proposed interventions directly strengthen the initial perception. Perception anchoring uses explicit ROI bounding boxes to force the model to focus on the medically relevant region, reducing the likelihood of misperception. Description grounding provides high-level expert context (e.g., a radiology report) that encodes salient features and medical knowledge, guiding the model’s attention and giving it a coherent scaffold for reasoning. By improving visual grounding before reasoning begins, these interventions prevent error propagation and enable CoT to deliver its intended benefits in the medical domain.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Image → [ROI Extraction (optional)] → Vision Encoder → Multimodal Fusion → Language Decoder → CoT token stream → Final answer  
Additional inputs: ROI coordinates (steers vision encoder or crops image) and/or expert description (concatenated with question before encoding).

**Critical Path:**  
The vision encoder processes the (possibly ROI‑highlighted) image into visual features; the language encoder handles the question and optional expert text. Multimodal fusion combines these modalities, and the autoregressive decoder generates the CoT reasoning tokens step by step before emitting the final answer. Any inaccuracy in the visual features from the first encoding step appears in the first CoT token and propagates downstream.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Adding ROI and description increases context length, raising computational cost and memory usage.  
- Interventions depend on auxiliary annotations (accurate ROI boxes, high-quality expert texts) that may be scarce or expensive to obtain.  
- They are training‑free and model‑agnostic, but their effectiveness may vary with the quality of the external inputs.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- No improvement or performance drop when ROI boxes are inaccurate or irrelevant.  
- Model ignores or misuses the expert description (e.g., treating it as part of the question).  
- Over‑reliance on the description leads to answers that contradict the image (“description hallucination”).  
- Gains are limited to datasets with clean annotations; real‑world, uncurated data may show weaker effects.

**First Experiments:**  
1. **Baseline:** Compare direct answering vs. vanilla CoT on a standard medical VQA dataset (e.g., VQA‑RAD) using a baseline VLM (e.g., LLaVA).  
2. **Perception anchoring:** Repeat CoT evaluation when each input image is accompanied by ground‑truth ROI bounding boxes covering the medically relevant region.  
3. **Description grounding:** Repeat CoT evaluation when the question is prefixed with a concise expert description (e.g., a radiology report excerpt) for each image.

## Limitations
- The causal attribution to the “medical perception bottleneck” remains inferential and may be confounded by other factors (dataset artifacts, model biases).  
- The interventions rely on precise external annotations (ROI boxes, expert descriptions) that are unlikely to be available in many real‑world clinical scenarios.  
- Generalizability to diverse medical specialties, imaging modalities (e.g., histopathology, ultrasound), and non‑English contexts remains unproven.

## Confidence
- **CoT degrades accuracy in medical VQA:** High confidence (consistent across multiple datasets and models).  
- **Medical perception bottleneck as primary cause:** Medium confidence (plausible but not isolated experimentally).  
- **Interventions mitigate degradation:** High confidence (strong empirical results reported).  
- **Interventions restore or invert CoT advantage:** Medium‑High confidence (effectiveness shown, but magnitude across unseen scenarios uncertain).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑Modality Generalizability Test:** Replicate the study on a different imaging domain (e.g., dermatology images or 3D CT volumes) using the same model families and interventions to test if the CoT degradation and intervention efficacy hold.  
2. **Bottleneck Isolation Experiment:** Design a controlled ablation where the “perception” step is replaced with a perfect oracle (ground‑truth region/feature extraction) to measure if CoT performance then matches or exceeds direct answering, directly testing the error‑propagation hypothesis.  
3. **Real‑World Utility Evaluation:** Conduct a human study where clinicians assess the quality and clinical validity of model outputs (both CoT and direct) with and without interventions on a hold‑out set, to determine if improved accuracy metrics translate to meaningful clinical reasoning support.
