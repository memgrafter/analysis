# Beyond Normalization: Rethinking the Partition Function as a Difficulty Scheduler for RLVR

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.12642
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12642
- Reference count: 39
- Primary result: PACED-RL boosts pass@1 by up to 29.1% over GRPO and 40.0% over FlowRL, and pass@k by up to 14.2% over GRPO, while training in 0.42–0.67× of baseline wall‑clock time on math and code benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Reward‑maximizing reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning often collapses output diversity. While GFlowNet‑based methods (e.g., FlowRL) match the reward distribution, they treat the partition function solely as a normalizer. This paper reinterprets the partition function as an online per‑prompt accuracy estimator, enabling sample‑efficient training without extra compute. The proposed PACED‑RL uses these estimates to (1) select question prompts of intermediate difficulty (∼0.5 accuracy) and (2) prioritize replay of samples with large accuracy estimation errors. Experiments on mathematical reasoning (DeepScaleR, MATH‑500, AIME24/25) and code generation (DeepCoder, HumanEval+, LiveCodeBench) with 1.5B and 7B models show substantial gains: pass@1 improves by up to 29.1% versus GRPO and 40.0% versus FlowRL; pass@k (diversity proxy) improves by up to 14.2% and 9.1% respectively. Training is also faster, reaching the best baseline performance in 42–67% of their wall‑clock time.

## Method Summary
PACED‑RL builds on the GFlowNet Trajectory Balance (TB) objective to jointly train the LLM policy π_θ and a small partition‑function network Z_ϕ. The key theoretical insight is that, if the KL divergence between the old policy (π_old) and the new policy (π_θ) remains small, then β log Z_ϕ(x) approximates the current accuracy of π_old on question x. This cheap estimator drives two mechanisms: adaptive prompt selection (choose m questions whose estimated accuracy is closest to τ = 0.5, i.e., intermediate difficulty) and error‑prioritized replay (store only correct rollouts, and give highest priority to those where the observed accuracy differs most from the estimate). The training loop alternates: (1) compute β log Z_ϕ(x) for all questions, (2) select the batch, (3) generate N rollouts per selected question using π_old, (4) augment the batch with the top B_add samples from the replay buffer (by priority), (5) update π_θ and Z_ϕ via TB loss on the combined batch, (6) update π_old ← π_θ, (7) add new correct rollouts to the buffer with their priorities.

Implementation details: Z_ϕ is a 3‑layer MLP (hidden 512, ReLU) fed frozen base‑model embeddings of the question; separate AdamW optimizers (π_θ lr ≈ 1e‑6, Z_ϕ lr ≈ 1e‑4). Hyperparameters include m (batch size in questions), N (rollouts per question), B_max (buffer capacity, e.g., 128), B_add (number of buffer samples per step, e.g., 128), and τ = 0.5. Evaluation uses pass@1 (32 attempts) and pass@k (k = 2,4,…,256, temperature 0.6, top‑p 0.95) on math and code benchmarks with models such as Qwen2.5‑Math‑1.5B/7B. Training runs 150 steps (math) or up to 40 hours (code).

## Key Results
- Pass@1 gains: up to +29.1% relative to GRPO and +40.0% relative to FlowRL.
- Pass@k (diversity) gains: up to +14.2% vs GRPO and +9.1% vs FlowRL.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Partition function as accuracy estimator
The GFlowNet TB objective leads to an optimal partition function Z★(x) that satisfies p_old(x) ≈ β log Z★(x) when the KL divergence β·D_KL(π_old‖π_θ) is negligible (Proposition 4.1). Empirically, this KL term remains uniformly small (<4×10⁻³) throughout training (Figure 1), validating the approximation. Hence β log Z_ϕ(x) serves as a cheap online estimate of per‑question accuracy. If training dynamics cause large policy updates (e.g., aggressive learning rates), the KL term may no longer be negligible, degrading the estimate.

### Mechanism 2: Intermediate‑difficulty prompt selection
Drawing questions whose estimated accuracy is closest to τ = 0.5 maximizes the learning signal per sample. This aligns with curriculum and active learning principles: questions with near‑50% accuracy yield the steepest policy gradients. Ablations varying τ (Figure 4) show τ = 0.5 attains the highest final accuracy, while τ = 0.1 performs worst and τ = 0.9 plateaus early. The mechanism depends on well‑calibrated Z_ϕ estimates.

### Mechanism 3: Error‑prioritized replay
The replay buffer stores only correct (x,y) pairs. Prioritizing samples by the absolute error between observed accuracy (from N rollouts) and p̂_old(x) focuses training on the most uncertain estimates, accelerating both Z_ϕ calibration and policy improvement. The GFlowNet TB objective tolerates off‑policy data, making replay effective. Ablations (Table 2) demonstrate that adding replay consistently improves pass@1 and pass@k across benchmarks.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: The system consists of a Z_ϕ network (3‑layer MLP on frozen question embeddings), an accuracy estimator (p̂_old(x) = β log Z_ϕ(x) clipped to [0,1]), a prompt selector (ranks all questions by |p̂_old(x) − τ| and picks top‑m), a replay buffer (capacity B_max; stores (x,y) with reward = 1 and priority |(N_correct/N) − p̂_old(x)|), and the policy π_θ (LLM) trained via TB loss on a batch combining current rollouts and buffer samples. The training loop runs these components synchronously.

- **Critical path**:
  1. Begin with π_old (previous step).
  2. Compute Z_ϕ(x) ∀x ∈ dataset → p̂_old(x).
  3. Select D_t (|D_t|=m) with p̂_old(x) closest to τ=0.5.
  4. For each x_i ∈ D_t, generate N rollouts y_{i,j} ∼ π_old(·|x_i).
  5. Form batch = T_t (current rollouts) ∪ top B_add buffer samples (by priority).
  6. Update π_θ and Z_ϕ by minimizing L_ours (Eq. 6) on the batch.
  7. Set π_old ← π_θ.
  8. Add correct rollouts from T_t to buffer with computed priorities.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - τ: Fixed τ=0.5 works well; a dynamic τ could adapt to dataset difficulty but adds complexity.
  - B_max: Larger buffers preserve more high‑error samples but increase staleness; B_max=128 used.
  - Accuracy estimator: β log Z_ϕ is cheap but biased; including the KL term (Eq. 7) would be costly.
  - Z_ϕ architecture: A small MLP is fast; larger networks might improve calibration but increase overhead.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Poor calibration: correlation between p̂_old(x) and true accuracy falls <0.5.
  - Drifted selection: mean accuracy of selected questions deviates significantly from τ=0.5.
  - Replay contamination: buffer fills with low‑priority samples; monitor priority histogram.
  - KL explosion: β D_KL(π_old‖π_θ) > 0.01, indicating the small‑KL assumption is violated.
  - No improvement: pass@1 gains <10% over GRPO/FlowRL, suggesting implementation bugs.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Correlation sanity check**: After ~20 training steps, compute Spearman/Pearson correlation between β log Z_ϕ(x) and empirical accuracy (from 32 rollouts) on a held‑out validation set. Expect correlation >0.5.
  2. **Replay ablation**: Train with replay buffer disabled (buffer size = 0). Expect pass@1 and pass@k to drop compared to full PACED‑RL.
  3. **τ sweep**: Train with τ ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9}. Expect τ=0.5 to yield highest final test accuracy; τ=0.9 may rise quickly then plateau.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Inference‑time adaptive self‑consistency** (Future Work): Can Z_ϕ be used to allocate more samples to questions predicted as difficult during test time, reducing compute while maintaining accuracy?
2. **Step‑level difficulty signals** (Future Work): Do other GFlowNet objectives (Detailed Balance, Sub‑Trajectory Balance) provide intermediate state‑level potentials, enabling PACED‑RL to guide reasoning step by step?
3. **Scaling to larger models** (Limitation): The evaluation is limited to 1.5B‑7B models; it remains open whether gains persist for >10B parameter LLMs.
4. **Robustness of the KL‑negligence assumption** (Inferred): How does the accuracy estimator behave when the KL divergence between successive policies is not small? Its magnitude depends on learning rate, β, and model size.
5. **Extension to non‑verifiable rewards** (Limitation): PACED‑RL currently relies on binary (0/1) rewards; extending it to preference‑based or dense‑reward tasks would require re‑deriving the connection between Z_ϕ and task performance.

## Limitations
- The theoretical approximation p̂_old(x) ≈ β log Z_ϕ(x) assumes β·D_KL(π_old‖π_θ) stays negligible; this may break under aggressive training or large policy shifts.
- Key hyperparameters (exact Z_ϕ architecture, replay buffer eviction policy, B_add selection) are not fully specified, potentially hindering exact replication.
- Experiments are restricted to models up to 7B parameters; scalability to larger LLMs is untested.

## Next Checks
1. **Calibration monitoring**: Every 10 training steps, compute Pearson and Spearman correlations between β log Z_ϕ(x) and empirical accuracy on a fixed validation set. Expect sustained correlation >0.5; a sharp drop indicates estimator degradation.
2. **Replay prioritization ablation**: Disable error‑based priority (sample uniformly from buffer) while keeping buffer size constant. Expect a significant decrease in pass@1/pass@k relative to full PACED‑RL, confirming the importance of prioritization.
3. **KL divergence tracking**: Log β D_KL(π_old‖π_θ) after each update. If it exceeds 0.01, investigate performance and estimator correlation to assess violation of the small‑KL assumption.
