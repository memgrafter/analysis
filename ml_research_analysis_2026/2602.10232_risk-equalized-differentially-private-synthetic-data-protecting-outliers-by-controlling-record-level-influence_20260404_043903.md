# Risk-Equalized Differentially Private Synthetic Data: Protecting Outliers by Controlling Record-Level Influence

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10232
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10232
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** REPS reduces disproportionate privacy risk for outliers, lowering top-decile membership inference advantage (e.g., from 0.760 to 0.728 at ε=4.0) with minimal utility trade‑offs.

## Executive Summary
Differentially private synthetic data generation often exposes outlier records to higher membership inference risk because their rare features make them more distinguishable. Risk‑Equalized DP Synthesis (REPS) tackles this by first estimating each record’s outlierness using a small DP budget, then down‑weighting high‑risk records in the DP learning phase to reduce their influence. Theoretically, per‑instance privacy loss scales with record influence; weighting tightens bounds for outliers without inflating global noise. On simulated data with injected outliers, REPS reduces top‑decile MIA advantage from 0.760 to 0.728 at ε=4.0. Real‑world datasets (Breast Cancer, Adult, German Credit) show more modest, dataset‑dependent gains. Ablations confirm that the protection stems from targeted down‑weighting—not random reduction—and that utility remains largely intact.

## Method Summary
REPS is a two‑stage DP mechanism. **Stage 1 (Scoring):** DP histograms (e.g., 16 bins for continuous features, exact for categoricals) are released with a small budget (ε_s ≈ 0.1ε). For each record, an outlier score is the negative log of the product of estimated marginal probabilities (with a floor p_min). **Stage 2 (Synthesis):** Records are weighted inversely to their score (e.g., w_i = exp(-γ(s_i−t)_+), where t is a threshold like the 90th percentile). The weight mapping is chosen via a non‑DP validation set to keep utility (TSTR AUROC) within a small margin of a uniform‑weight DP baseline. The weighted sufficient statistics for a conditional Gaussian Naive Bayes model (one‑hot labels, conditional categorical counts, continuous means/variances) are released with Gaussian noise, satisfying DP with the remaining budget (ε_t ≈ 0.9ε). Composition ensures overall (ε,δ) guarantees. The analysis proves that high‑risk records obtain tighter per‑instance privacy bounds because their weights directly limit their influence.

## Key Results
- On simulated outliers, REPS reduces top‑decile membership inference advantage from 0.760 to 0.728 at ε=4.0.
- Real‑dataset improvements are dataset‑dependent; Breast Cancer shows modest top‑decile gain at higher ε, while Adult and German Credit show limited changes.
- Ablations confirm that the vulnerability reduction is due to targeted downweighting, not random weight changes, and that utility losses are minor.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** REPS can estimate each record’s outlierness using a small DP budget.
- **Mechanism:** Compute noisy histograms (or other DP statistics) per feature, then define a rarity score as the negative log of the estimated marginal probability (or a sum across features).
- **Core assumption:** Outlierness correlates with low probability under the marginal distributions; the DP histogram provides a stable, noise‑controlled estimate.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “first, a small DP budget estimates each record’s ‘outlierness’.”  
  - [section] Section 5.1 describes the DP histogram scorer and the rarity score formula (Equation 4).  
  - [corpus] Neighbors such as *Achilles’ Heels* (Kulynych et al., 2022) and *A Linear Reconstruction Approach* document vulnerable record identification in synthetic data, supporting the need for outlier scoring.
- **Break condition:** If the scoring DP budget is too small or the marginal model is too simplistic (e.g., fails to capture high‑order interactions), the scores become too noisy to reliably distinguish high‑risk records; the downstream protection degrades.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** By weighting records inversely to their outlier score in DP learning, REPS reduces their influence and tightens per‑instance privacy bounds.
- **Mechanism:** Scale each record’s contribution in the sufficient statistics or gradient updates by a weight \(w_i\) derived from the score; clipping bounds the contribution, and Gaussian noise is added. The record’s influence \(\alpha_i \leq w_i C / n\) directly controls its per‑instance \(\varepsilon_i\).
- **Core assumption:** Under Gaussian mechanisms, per‑instance privacy loss is proportional to the record’s influence on the output.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “second, a DP learning procedure weights each record inversely to its risk score.”  
  - [section] Section 5.3 (Instantiation A) defines the weighted clipped aggregate; Lemma 1 and Corollary 2 formalize the influence–privacy bound.  
  - [corpus] Per‑instance DP analyses (Wang, 2019; Thudi et al., 2024) establish the influence–privacy relationship; REPS is the first to use weighting as an active lever.
- **Break condition:** The weight mapping must produce sufficiently small \(w_i\) for high‑risk records; if clipping norm \(C\) dominates or the weight function saturates early, the reduction in \(\varepsilon_i\) is negligible. Over‑downweighting may also cause utility loss or bias.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The two‑stage composition yields end‑to‑end DP with a constructive schedule that caps \(\varepsilon_i\) for high‑risk records without inflating global noise.
- **Mechanism:** Set weights for the high‑risk set \(H\) to \(w_i = \tau_{\text{out}}/C\) (or smaller) while keeping \(w_i=1\) for others. This preserves global sensitivity \(\Delta = C/n\) (so global \(\varepsilon\) unchanged) and guarantees \(\varepsilon_i \leq \varepsilon_{\text{out}}\) for \(i\in H\). Adaptive composition then adds the scorer’s per‑record term \(\varepsilon_s\).
- **Core assumption:** The scorer’s DP guarantees compose; the influence bound for weighted Gaussian release holds; the high‑risk set is correctly identified by the scorer.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “We prove end‑to‑end DP guarantees via composition and derive closed‑form per‑record bounds for the synthesis stage.”  
  - [section] Section 6.1 (Theorem 1, Corollary 1) covers composition; Section 6.4 (Proposition 1) gives the targeted protection schedule.  
  - [corpus] Direct evidence is limited; the analysis builds on standard DP composition and per‑instance DP literature.
- **Break condition:** If the scorer commits errors of omission (fails to flag some true outliers), those records may not receive protection. A large \(\varepsilon_s\) from scoring adds uniformly to each record’s bound and may negate the cap. The schedule specifically assumes Gaussian mechanisms; other mechanisms require separate analysis.

## Foundational Learning
- **Differential Privacy Composition**  
  - *Why needed here:* REPS consumes two DP stages; understanding how budgets combine is essential to allocate ε_s and ε_t while meeting a global (ε,δ) guarantee.  
  - *Quick check:* “If Stage 1 uses (ε_s,δ_s) and Stage 2 uses (ε_t,δ_t) under basic composition, what are the overall ε and δ?”

- **Per‑Instance Differential Privacy (pDP)**  
  - *Why needed here:* REPS aims to equalize privacy loss; pDP provides record‑specific bounds ε_i that can be tighter for typical records and worse for outliers—the quantity we want to control.  
  - *Quick check:* “For a Gaussian mechanism with noise scale σ and record influence α, express ε_i in terms of α, σ, and δ.”

- **Influence as a Privacy Lever**  
  - *Why needed here:* Weighting works because influence directly caps ε_i; knowing how clipping, weighting, and normalization affect influence is key to designing the schedule.  
  - *Quick check:* “In the weighted aggregate f_w(D) = (1/n)∑_i w_i·clip(ϕ(z_i),C), derive how w_i influences α_i.”

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Data Input → DP Outlier Scorer → Weight Mapper → Weighted DP Synthesis Engine → Synthetic Output. (Composition Manager tracks the budget across stages.)

- **Critical path:**  
  The DP‑critical pipeline is linear: Scorer → Weight Mapper → Weighted DP Synthesis. All privacy‑sensitive operations must respect the allocated budget before any real data is processed. The composition check sits at the end.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Scoring budget (ε_s) vs. synthesis budget (ε_t):* More ε_s yields more accurate outlier identification but leaves less noise for synthesis, potentially hurting utility.  
  - *Weight mapping aggressiveness:* Higher γ or lower τ caps ε_i more strongly for outliers but introduces bias and may degrade representation of rare subgroups.  
  - *Synthesis method choice:* One‑shot Gaussian release gives clean per‑instance analysis but limited expressiveness; weighted DP‑SGD enables deep generators but influence bounds are less precise.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Scorer quality low (Spearman correlation with non‑private outliers near zero) → ineffective targeting.  
  - Weighted synthesis utility collapse (e.g., TSTR AUROC drops sharply) → over‑downweighting or inappropriate C.  
  - Top‑decile MIA advantage does not decrease (or worsens) compared to uniform weighting → weight mapping or scoring failed to reduce influence meaningfully.  
  - Composition accounting error (total ε exceeds target) → mis‑calibrated noise scales or forgotten composition term.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Ablation – random downweighting:** Shuffle the weight vector across records; compare top‑decile MIA to REPS to verify that benefit requires correctly targeted downweighting.  
  2. **Budget split sensitivity:** Sweep ε_s/ε_t ratios (e.g., 0.05/0.95, 0.1/0.9, 0.2/0.8) and measure scorer quality (correlation with true outliers) and resulting MIA reduction; identify operating region.  
  3. **Weight mapping comparison:** Evaluate cap, hinge‑exp, and linear downweighting functions; plot top‑decile MIA vs. utility (TSTR) to characterize the trade‑off frontier.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Extension to canonical DP synthesizers:** How can REPS be extended to methods like PGM/MST or DP‑SGD? Their sensitivity structures and privacy accounting differ, and the weighted influence analysis may not directly transfer.  
- **Per‑instance bounds for DP‑SGD:** What are the formal per‑instance privacy bounds for the DP‑SGD variant of REPS? Influence in DP‑SGD accumulates over iterations and depends on gradient similarity; closed‑form bounds are lacking.  
- **DP‑safe hyperparameter selection:** How can the risk‑weight parameter γ be tuned in a DP‑safe manner without a clean validation set? Current γ selection uses a held‑out validation set not accounted for in DP, calling for DP‑compliant procedures.  
- **Fairness implications:** How does downweighting outliers affect fairness for minority subgroups when synthetic data is used for downstream tasks? The paper warns of potential degradation for marginalized groups but leaves the trade‑off unexplored.

## Limitations
- The precise construction of the sufficient‑statistic vector ϕ(zi) (block ordering, label‑conditioning) and the exact p_min value are not fully specified, hindering exact reproduction.
- The method’s extension to more complex DP synthesizers (e.g., PGM, DP‑SGD) lacks formal per‑instance accounting and may not preserve the same protection guarantees.
- Hyperparameter tuning (γ) currently relies on a non‑DP validation set, which could leak information and violate the overall (ε,δ) guarantee.
