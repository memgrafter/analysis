# Diffeomorphism-Equivariant Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06695
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06695
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: DiffeoNN enables pre-trained neural networks to achieve approximate diffeomorphism equivariance via test-time energy-based canonicalization, matching data augmentation performance on segmentation and classification tasks.

## Executive Summary
Diffeomorphism equivariance—consistency of predictions under smooth deformations—is a highly desirable property for neural networks but difficult to attain due to the infinite-dimensional diffeomorphism group. This paper introduces DiffeoNN, a method that grants any pre-trained network approximate equivariance without retraining or extensive data augmentation.

DiffeoNN operates at test time: it optimizes a task-specific energy function to find a diffeomorphism that maps an input to a canonical representative close to the training data manifold, applies the pre-trained model, and then reverses the transformation. Evaluated on synthetic nested squares segmentation, chest X-ray segmentation, and MNIST genus classification, DiffeoNN outperforms a naive baseline, matches the accuracy of models trained with heavy data augmentation, and shows more stable performance with fewer outliers.

## Method Summary
DiffeoNN is a test-time canonicalization framework. First, an inner task model fθ (e.g., a U-Net for segmentation or a CNN for classification) is trained on a base dataset XE. Concurrently, a variational autoencoder (VAE) and an adversarial discriminator are trained on XE to model the data manifold and distribution. For a test input x, the method seeks a diffeomorphism g that minimizes an energy Ecan(x,g) = EXE(g·x) + Ereg(g). EXE comprises the VAE reconstruction loss and adversarial loss applied to the warped image g·x, encouraging it to be indistinguishable from a sample in XE. Ereg includes a Jacobian determinant penalty (to prevent folding) and a penalty on transformation magnitude (to keep g close to identity). The diffeomorphisms are parameterized by stationary velocity fields (SVF): a SIREN network outputs a velocity field v, and the flow g = exp(v) is computed via scaling-and-squaring. The optimization (typically gradient descent with Adam) adjusts the SIREN weights to minimize Ecan. Once the optimal g is found, the canonicalized image xc = g·x is fed to fθ, producing fθ(xc). The final prediction is obtained by applying the inverse transformation g⁻¹ to fθ(xc). The inner model fθ and the auxiliary networks remain fixed; only the test-time optimization adapts to each input.

## Key Results
- Synthetic nested squares segmentation: DiffeoNN surpasses the naive baseline and matches the accuracy of models trained with extensive diffeomorphic data augmentation.
- Chest X-ray lung segmentation: DiffeoNN achieves IoU 0.8842, higher than the naive model (0.8759) and close to the augmentation model (0.9231), with notably fewer outlier failures.
- MNIST genus classification: DiffeoNN reaches 0.82 accuracy, identical to the data-augmented model and significantly above the naive baseline.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The key insight is that the canonicalization step absorbs the effect of unknown diffeomorphisms by mapping any input to a point on or near the training data manifold in a consistent manner. For two inputs related by a diffeomorphism h, the optimization of the energy tends to find transformations g and g' such that g·x ≈ g'·(h·x); that is, they share a common canonical representative. The pre-trained model fθ, having been trained on the manifold, produces similar outputs for these canonicalized versions. Reversing the transformations then yields predictions that are equivariant with respect to h. The VAE and adversarial losses ensure that the canonicalized image is not merely close in pixel space but also lies within the high-density region of the training distribution, increasing the likelihood that fθ will behave reliably. The SVF parameterization via SIREN and scaling-and-squaring provides a flexible yet invertible family of transformations, while Jacobian regularization guarantees that the optimized g is a true diffeomorphism (no folding). Together, these elements allow DiffeoNN to achieve approximate equivariance at test time without modifying the original network or requiring large-scale augmentation during training.

## Foundational Learning
**Diffeomorphism equivariance**  
*Why needed:* Guarantees that predictions transform consistently under smooth deformations, essential for tasks like medical imaging where object geometry varies.  
*Quick check:* Apply a known diffeomorphism to an image; an ideally equivariant model should produce an output that is the transformed version of the original output.

**Stationary Velocity Fields (SVF)**  
*Why needed:* Parameterizes diffeomorphisms via a time-independent velocity field, enabling smooth invertible transformations through integration (exp(v)). This yields a tractable optimization space with invertibility guarantees via scaling-and-squaring.  
*Quick check:* Integrate a randomly initialized SVF using scaling-and-squaring and verify that all Jacobian determinants are positive (e.g., minimum > 0).

**SIREN (Sinusoidal Representation Networks)**  
*Why needed:* Represents the SVF as an implicit neural function capable of encoding high-frequency details and is fully differentiable, facilitating gradient-based optimization of g.  
*Quick check:* Fit SIREN to a known deformation field; reconstruction error should be low (<1% MSE) for appropriately sized networks.

**Variational Autoencoder (VAE)**  
*Why needed:* Learns a latent manifold of the training data; the reconstruction loss EXE measures proximity of the canonicalized image to this manifold, ensuring fθ receives inputs similar to its training distribution.  
*Quick check:* Generated samples and reconstructions should be realistic, with reconstruction loss stable at the level reported in the paper.

**Adversarial Discriminator**  
*Why needed:* Complements the VAE by ensuring canonicalized images are indistinguishable from real training data, addressing potential manifold gaps that pure reconstruction might miss.  
*Quick check:* A discriminator trained to separate real vs. canonicalized images should achieve accuracy near 0.5 after training, indicating no clear separation.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Input x → SIREN(v) produces velocity field v → scaling-and-squaring yields diffeomorphism g = exp(v) → warp: xc = g·x → inner model fθ(xc) = ŷ → apply inverse transformation g⁻¹ → final output ỹ = g⁻¹·ŷ. Auxiliary components: VAE decoder and discriminator compute EXE; Jacobian regularizer computes Ereg.

**Critical Path**  
For each test sample: (1) Initialize SIREN parameters (v). (2) Perform gradient descent on Ecan with respect to v, updating g via scaling-and-squaring. (3) After convergence, compute xc = g·x, ŷ = fθ(xc), and ỹ = g⁻¹·ŷ. (4) Return ỹ. The inner model fθ and auxiliary networks are pre-trained and fixed.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *VAE vs. adversarial loss:* VAE encourages reconstruction fidelity but may blur details; adversarial loss sharpens but can be unstable. The weighting (λ) balances these effects.  
- *SVF expressivity:* Deeper/wider SIRENs represent more complex deformations but risk folding; Jacobian loss mitigates but may limit flexibility.  
- *Optimization steps:* More steps improve canonicalization quality but increase test-time cost; a trade-off between accuracy and speed.  
- *Regularization strength:* Strong Jacobian and Δ penalties ensure invertibility but can restrict the ability to find good canonical representatives, especially for large transformations.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Negative Jacobian determinants during optimization, indicating folding (too large steps or insufficient regularization).  
- High VAE reconstruction loss or discriminator confidence (near 1.0) for canonicalized images, suggesting they lie far from the training manifold.  
- Instability in the energy (non-decreasing) or divergence of SIREN parameters, often due to poor hyperparameter choices.  
- Significant performance drops on certain transformed inputs; visualizing xc often reveals unrealistic or overly warped images.

**First Experiments**  
1. *Identity canonicalization:* Run the optimization on an unmodified training image (x ∈ XE). The optimal g should be near identity, xc ≈ x, and the final prediction should match the original fθ(x). This validates the end-to-end pipeline.  
2. *Visualization and Jacobian check:* For a transformed test image (x ∈ XT E), optimize g and inspect xc and the deformation field. Compute the Jacobian determinant map; all values should be positive, and xc should resemble a plausible training sample.  
3. *Equivariance validation:* Pick a known diffeomorphism h (e.g., a smooth warp). For a test image x, compute f̃(x) and f̃(h·x). Measure the discrepancy between f̃(h·x) and h applied to f̃(x) (e.g., IoU for segmentation). The error should be small, confirming approximate equivariance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
- **Question:** What are the theoretical conditions (e.g., on the energy function, the training data distribution, and the network fθ) that guarantee that approximate canonicalisation yields approximate equivariance for all inputs within a certain tolerance?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "A vital direction for future research remains to characterise learning bounds and sufficiency of approximate canonicalisation for achieving full model equivariance." (Conclusion) Also: "formal theoretical validation is left for future work." (Section 4.1.1)  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper provides extensive empirical validation but no theoretical analysis of why the canonicalisation optimization leads to inputs that are both close to the training manifold and consistently mapped by the pre-trained network. Without such analysis, the robustness and failure modes are not understood.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Mathematical derivation of error bounds relating the canonicalisation energy minimization to the equivariance error \|f̃(g·x) - g·f̃(x)\|. Conditions on the smoothness and richness of fθ, the density of XE, and properties of the energy landscape (e.g., strong convexity near minima) would provide guarantees. Possibly proving that if the energy function is Lipschitz and fθ is smooth, then equivariance error is bounded by canonicalisation error.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question:** How does the density and coverage of the sparse training set XE on the data manifold affect the quality of canonicalisation and downstream accuracy, especially for inputs whose diffeomorphic orbit lies far from XE?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method assumes that canonicalizing inputs brings them close to XE, where fθ performs well. The paper uses small fixed training sets (e.g., 234 chest X-rays) but does not investigate how set size or diversity impacts robustness to unseen transformations.  
- **Why unresolved:** If XE does not sufficiently cover the manifold, canonicalisation might push inputs to regions where fθ generalizes poorly, leading to errors as seen in failure cases (Appendix B and C). Understanding this relationship would guide data collection and inform when the method is applicable.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments where XE is subsampled or curated to vary representativeness, measuring performance on a fixed test set of diffeomorphic transformations. Analyzing the geodesic distance (in the space of diffeomorphisms) between transformed inputs and XE, and correlating with accuracy. Possibly developing metrics for manifold coverage.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question:** What is the optimal combination of the VAE-based similarity, adversarial loss, and regularisation terms in the canonicalisation energy for different tasks, and can these weights be set automatically or learned?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors manually set hyperparameters λ_adv, λ_VAE, λ_Δ, λ_J differently for synthetic vs. chest X-ray experiments without justification. No ablation study isolates the contribution of each term or explores sensitivity.  
- **Why unresolved:** The energy design is heuristic. Without understanding the role of each component, it is unclear whether the chosen combination is near-optimal for other datasets or tasks. For instance, the VAE loss might dominate shape while adversarial loss captures texture; trade-offs are unknown.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation experiments where each term is removed or weighted differently across multiple datasets, measuring both canonicalisation quality (e.g., distance to XE) and downstream task performance. Could also attempt to learn the weights as part of the canonicalisation optimization by gradient descent or via validation.

**Open Question 4**  
- **Question:** To what extent does the restriction to stationary velocity field (SVF)-generated diffeomorphisms limit the method's ability to achieve equivariance for all possible diffeomorphisms, and are there failure modes arising from this parametrisation?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper acknowledges: "While not every orientation-preserving diffeomorphism can be generated by a SVF (Hernandez et al., 2008), D_SVF suits our method..." Yet no analysis is given of how often a target diffeomorphism might be outside the SVF class or how that affects equivariance error.  
- **Why unresolved:** If the transformation needed to canonicalize an input is not well-approximated by an SVF, the optimization may converge to a suboptimal canonical representative, harming fθ's performance. The paper shows some failures but does not attribute them to SVF limitations.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare performance when using more expressive deformation models (e.g., time-dependent velocity fields or higher-order SVFs) on a set of diffeomorphisms known to be poorly approximated by SVFs. Quantify the representation error of SVFs for diffeomorphisms sampled from a distribution, and correlate with equivariance error on transformed inputs.

## Limitations
- The exact architecture specifications of the inner U-Net and CNN classifier are not reported, posing a risk of replication drift.
- Canonicalization optimization details (e.g., learning rate, number of gradient steps) for the synthetic and MNIST experiments are missing, requiring manual tuning.
- The method's effectiveness on natural image datasets beyond the evaluated tasks remains unverified; generalization scope is uncertain.

## Confidence
- Core method design (energy-based canonicalization with SVFs): High
- Performance parity with data augmentation: Medium
- Approximate equivariance efficiency: Medium
- Inner model architecture clarity: Low
- Canonicalization hyperparameter completeness: Low
- Generalization to diverse datasets: Low
