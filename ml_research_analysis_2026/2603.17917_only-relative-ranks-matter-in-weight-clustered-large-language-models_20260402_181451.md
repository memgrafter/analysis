# Only relative ranks matter in weight-clustered large language models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17917
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17917
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Relative ranks of weight cluster centroids are critical; rank-preserving modifications cause only slight perplexity increases, while rank-breaking modifications cause catastrophic performance collapse.

## Executive Summary
Weight clustering reduces model size by sharing values across weight matrices. This paper investigates whether the exact centroid values or their relative rank order determines performance in large language models. Using K-means clustering on Llama and SmolLM2 models, the authors keep cluster assignments fixed and systematically randomize centroids. They find that preserving the rank order of centroids (e.g., via sorting or affine transforms that maintain mean and variance) leads to only minor perplexity degradation (1–3%), while breaking the rank order causes massive perplexity spikes. The resilience to rank-preserving transformations is attributed to LayerNorm's ability to absorb per-layer affine shifts, though scale drift accumulates across layers, especially harming early layers. Optional centroid-only fine-tuning recovers a portion of the remaining accuracy gap. These results suggest that the relative ordering of weight magnitudes, not their absolute values, is a key factor in weight-clustered LLMs.

## Method Summary
The study applies scalar K-means clustering to all attention and MLP projection matrices of pretrained decoder-only LLMs (e.g., Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct), selecting per-layer K values adaptively to limit perplexity increase to at most 0.5. Clustering decomposes each weight matrix W into a label matrix L (cluster indices) and a centroid vector c. To isolate the effect of centroid values, L is kept fixed while c is replaced under various conditions: (i) rank-breaking (random Gaussian draws, random permutations), (ii) rank-preserving (sorted Gaussian, affine transforms w→aw+b with a>0), and (iii) combinations with per-layer affine correction to preserve the mean and variance of the reconstructed weights. Experiments evaluate single-layer perturbations at different depths and progressive multi-layer replacement from deepest to shallowest, measuring perplexity on WikiText-2. This design allows direct comparison of the impact of centroid rank order versus exact values.

## Key Results
- Clustering alone yields a modest perplexity increase (e.g., Llama 3.1-8B: 8.64 → 9.08, ~5%).
- Rank-preserving centroid changes cause only minor degradation (perplexity +1–3%), whereas rank-breaking randomization increases perplexity by orders of magnitude (e.g., up to 3151).
- Affine transformations that preserve rank and maintain mean/variance largely preserve performance, but scale drift accumulates across layers; early layers are particularly sensitive even to rank-preserving shifts. Centroid-only fine-tuning recovers 30–40% of the remaining gap.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Model performance in weight-clustered LLMs is primarily determined by the relative rank order of cluster centroids, not their exact values.  
**Mechanism:** When cluster assignments are fixed, scrambling centroid ranks (while preserving global statistics like mean/variance) leads to catastrophic perplexity increases, whereas rank-preserving changes (e.g., sorted random values) cause minimal degradation in middle and late layers. The network’s computations appear to rely on the ordering of weight magnitudes for signal propagation through linear layers and monotonic nonlinearities.  
**Core assumption:** The computational graph preserves relative magnitude relationships across weight matrices.  
**Evidence:** Table I shows rank-breaking Gaussian randomization raises perplexity by +7% to +70% (or more), while sorted randomization increases perplexity by only +1–3%. Early layers remain sensitive even to rank-preserving perturbations due to scale drift (Mechanism 3).

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Affine transformations that preserve rank order and the weight distribution’s mean and variance are largely safe because LayerNorm absorbs the resulting scale and offset.  
**Mechanism:** For a linear layer y = Wx, an affine weight transform yields y' = a y + b Σ x_d; the constant offset is identical across outputs, preserving activation ranks. Subsequent LayerNorm subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation, approximately undoing the affine shift.  
**Core assumption:** LayerNorm can compensate for per-layer affine changes without disrupting downstream computations.  
**Evidence:** The abstract notes affine correction delays scale drift. Table II shows affine scaling (e.g., w→0.5w, 2.0w) causes small perplexity increases, while variance-distorting transforms (tanh, power γ=0.5) cause severe degradation. Direct measurements show post-LayerNorm activation cosine similarity >0.99 after applying w→0.5w+0.01 to a single layer.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Scale drift—cumulative changes to the weight distribution’s mean and variance across layers—is the dominant collapse mechanism when many layers are perturbed, even if ranks are preserved.  
**Mechanism:** Per-layer affine shifts (bias and scale) propagate through the residual chain; early layers inject drift that amplifies through subsequent LayerNorms, which only partially absorb the misalignment, causing progressive degradation.  
**Core assumption:** Residual connections prevent full cancellation of per-layer affine changes, causing drift to compound depth-wise.  
**Evidence:** Progressive replacement (Fig. 3) shows uncorrected monotone randomization causes immediate collapse after ~2 blocks; affine-corrected randomization delays collapse significantly (e.g., Llama-8B stays within 1.6× baseline through 56% of network). Early layers (layer 1) are especially sensitive to rank-preserving perturbations (Table III: sign-scramble at layer 1 → perplexity 117.39). Smaller models (SmolLM2) collapse faster, indicating depth and size matter.

## Foundational Learning
- **K-means clustering**: Scalar K-means is applied to each weight matrix to produce a label matrix L (cluster assignments) and a centroid vector c. This decomposition allows independent manipulation of assignments and values. *Why needed*: The experimental framework relies on separating L from c to test perturbations while keeping assignments fixed. *Quick check*: What does L store? It stores, for each weight element, the index of its assigned centroid; c stores the actual shared weight values.
- **Perplexity**: Exponential of average negative log-likelihood per token; lower perplexity indicates better language modeling. *Why needed*: Primary evaluation metric for measuring model accuracy after perturbations. *Quick check*: If perplexity increases from 8.64 to 9.08 after clustering, is that a large degradation? It's relatively small (~5% increase), indicating clustering preserved most functionality.
- **Affine transformation & LayerNorm interaction**: An affine transform w→aw+b (a>0) preserves rank order. When applied to weights, it introduces an affine shift in activations; LayerNorm (normalize by mean and variance) can absorb this shift, preserving downstream dynamics. *Why needed*: Central to understanding why rank-preserving affine maps are safe. *Quick check*: How does w→aw+b affect output neuron ranks? Since a>0, the relative order of weighted sums y=Σ w_i x_i is preserved if the same transform applies uniformly; the offset adds a constant to all outputs, keeping ranks unchanged.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Clustering module (with Adaptive K-selector) → Perturbation module → Evaluation harness. The Adaptive K-selector chooses per-layer K values to satisfy a perplexity tolerance.
### Critical Path
1. Load pretrained model.
2. Apply K-means clustering per layer with layer-specific K.
3. (Experiments) Modify centroids in target layers according to condition (rank-breaking, rank-preserving, affine, etc.).
4. Reconstruct weights via ˆW[d,o] = c_{L[d,o]}.
5. Evaluate perplexity on WikiText-2.
### Design Tradeoffs
- **K selection**: Higher K reduces accuracy loss but increases storage. Adaptive per-layer K allocates more centroids to sensitive layers.
- **Execution path**: Rebuilding to dense at load time matches baseline throughput; indexed LUT is slower due to irregular memory accesses.
- **Centroid fine-tuning**: Optional fine-tuning recovers 30–40% of accuracy gap at minimal cost.
### Failure Signatures
- **Rank-breaking**: Any centroid perturbation that changes relative order (random permutation, unsorted Gaussian) causes severe perplexity spikes.
- **Variance distortion**: Non-affine monotone transforms (e.g., tanh, power γ=0.5) that alter weight variance degrade performance even if ranks preserved.
- **Early layer sensitivity**: Even rank-preserving affine shifts in early layers (e.g., layer 1) cause large perplexity increases.
### First 3 Experiments
1. **Single-layer rank-breaking vs. rank-preserving**: Replace centroids in a mid-depth layer's projection (e.g., layer 10 gate proj) with random Gaussian (rank-breaking) and with sorted Gaussian (rank-preserving). Expect: rank-breaking → large PPL jump; rank-preserving → small change.
2. **Affine transform safety test**: Apply w→0.5w and w→2w to same layer; expect small PPL change. Contrast with tanh(3w) (variance-distorted) which should cause large PPL increase.
3. **Progressive multi-layer replacement**: Starting from deepest layer, replace centroids block-by-block with sorted Gaussian values, with and without affine correction to preserve mean/variance. Expect uncorrected → rapid collapse after few blocks; corrected → slower degradation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Mathematical conditions for safe affine transformations** – What conditions on per-layer affine transforms (w→aw+b) guarantee bounded scale drift with depth? The paper calls for formalizing the safe region; currently only empirical evidence exists and early-layer sensitivity is not quantified.
2. **Differentiable optimization of cluster assignments** – Can gradient-based fine-tuning of assignments recover more than the 30–40% gap from centroid-only tuning? The paper identifies this as future work but has not implemented it.
3. **Sufficiency of rank order and K scaling** – Under what conditions does centroid rank alone become a sufficient statistic? How does required K scale with layer depth? The conjecture remains open because rank-preserving distortions affecting higher moments still degrade performance, and it's unclear whether larger K mitigates this.

## Limitations
- **K selection details missing**: The adaptive per-layer K distribution based on a 0.5 perplexity tolerance is not explicitly reported; uniform K may affect absolute results.
- **Evaluation specification unclear**: Tokenization, context length, and dataset preprocessing are under-specified, hindering exact replication.
- **Scope limited**: Experiments only on two models (Llama, SmolLM2) and a single dataset (WikiText-2); generalization to other architectures and tasks is unknown.

## Confidence
- Rank preservation is critical: **High**
- Affine invariance via LayerNorm: **Medium**
- Scale drift as dominant collapse mechanism: **Medium**
- Centroid-only fine-tuning recovery: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. Cross-architecture replication of single-layer perturbations (e.g., test rank-breaking vs. rank-preserving effects on Mistral or Gemma models).
2. Norm-dependence test: Replace LayerNorm with RMSNorm in a Llama-style model and repeat affine transformation experiments; degradation would indicate LayerNorm-specific mechanism.
3. Activation drift quantification: During progressive multi-layer replacement, log post-LayerNorm activation mean and variance per layer; correlate these drifts with perplexity to directly establish scale drift as the primary failure mode.
