# SpatiaLQA: A Benchmark for Evaluating Spatial Logical Reasoning in Vision-Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20901
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20901
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** VLMs perform poorly on spatial logical reasoning; best model (GPT-5) achieves 76.0 F1 on step content and 47.0 F1 on preconditions, far below human performance (~97% and ~92.5%).

## Executive Summary
SpatiaLQA is a new benchmark designed to evaluate spatial logical reasoning in Vision-Language Models (VLMs)—the ability to understand spatial relationships and logically dependent steps in multi-step real-world tasks. The benchmark comprises 9,605 question-answer pairs derived from 241 indoor scenes, each requiring ordered action steps with preconditions. Evaluation of 41 VLMs reveals a significant performance gap, highlighting current models' deficiencies in this area. To address this, the authors propose Recursive Scene Graph Assisted Reasoning (RSGAR), which leverages depth and segmentation models to iteratively decompose scenes into task-relevant scene graphs. With GPT-4o, RSGAR achieves state-of-the-art results on SpatiaLQA, improving content F1 to 69.8 and precondition F1 to 28.1, with notably larger gains on more complex tasks. However, results remain well below human levels, underscoring the challenge of spatial logical reasoning.

## Method Summary
RSGAR operates by first obtaining depth and segmentation maps for an input image using foundation models (Depth Anything V2 and SAM). It recursively builds a task-relevant scene graph: starting from the target object specified in the task, the VLM identifies directly related objects and their spatial relations using the perceptual maps. Newly identified objects become sources for subsequent iterations, expanding the graph outward until a predefined limit *T* is reached. This filtered scene graph is then provided to the VLM along with the task prompt to generate the final answer with ordered steps and preconditions. SpatiaLQA evaluation uses F1 scores for step content and preconditions, computed via a Hungarian algorithm matching between predicted and ground-truth steps, with GPT-4o as the scoring model.

## Key Results
- Current VLMs achieve low performance on SpatiaLQA; even GPT-5 reaches only 76.0 F1 on step content and 47.0 F1 on preconditions, versus human performance of ~97% and ~92.5%.
- RSGAR with GPT-4o improves to 69.8 content F1 and 28.1 precondition F1, with larger gains on more complex tasks (e.g., +4.5 content F1 on 4-step tasks).
- Ablation studies confirm both depth and segmentation inputs are necessary; removing either degrades performance significantly.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Task‑Relevant Scene Graph Decomposition
- **Claim:** RSGAR improves spatial logical reasoning by decomposing complex scenes into task‑relevant scene graphs, reducing irrelevant visual input for the VLM.
- **Mechanism:** Starting from the task‑specified target object, the method iteratively identifies directly related objects (via spatial contact/adjacency) and constructs a graph. Hierarchical filtering ensures the VLM focuses only on spatially or functionally connected objects.
- **Core assumption:** VLMs are overwhelmed by cluttered scenes and fail to attend to spatially pertinent objects; a pre‑filtered, structured representation mitigates this.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “progressively decompose complex scenes into task‑relevant scene graphs”; [section 4.1] “focus on the spatial environment surrounding the target objects”; Table 4a shows superior performance on complex tasks.
- **Break condition:** Misidentification of the initial source object or premature halting omits critical objects, leading to incomplete reasoning.
- **Corpus:** Nearby benchmarks (NuScenes‑SpatialQA, PhyBlock) do not employ recursive scene‑graph filtering; evidence is indirect.

### Mechanism 2: Complementary Depth and Segmentation
- **Claim:** Both depth and segmentation maps are necessary for accurate scene‑graph generation because they provide complementary spatial cues.
- **Mechanism:** Depth maps encode 3D structure and occlusion (e.g., “under”, “behind”), while segmentation maps give precise object boundaries. Their fusion allows reliable inference of spatial relations.
- **Core assumption:** VLMs lack robust built‑in spatial perception; external foundation models supply more accurate geometric and segmentation priors.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “leverages visual foundation models”; Table 5b ablation shows performance drop without either input.
- **Break condition:** Noisy or mismatched perceptual maps (e.g., depth errors on reflective surfaces) introduce incorrect relations, corrupting the graph.
- **Corpus:** Other benchmarks rarely ablate the combination; evidence is weak.

### Mechanism 3: Iterative Recursion for Dependencies
- **Claim:** Iterative recursion captures multi‑step dependencies and transitive spatial constraints, essential for correct precondition prediction.
- **Mechanism:** Each recursion round expands from newly identified target objects, gradually building a graph that includes both direct and indirect relationships. This enables the VLM to see chains like “A is on B, B is on C ⇒ must remove A and B before accessing C”.
- **Core assumption:** Spatial logical reasoning often requires reasoning over transitive closures; a single‑pass graph misses longer‑range dependencies.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.1] “continues until a predefined maximum iteration *T*”; Table 4b shows RSGAR gains grow with answer step count; [section 3.4] performance declines as steps increase, but RSGAR mitigates this.
- **Break condition:** If *T* is too small, long‑range dependencies are omitted; if too large, noise accumulates from irrelevant objects, hurting precision.
- **Corpus:** No direct corpus evidence; related works do not emphasize iterative graph expansion for dependency capture.

## Foundational Learning
- **Vision‑Language Models (VLMs):** Why needed: SpatiaLQA evaluates and improves VLMs; understanding their typical architecture (frozen visual encoder + LLM) and limitations (e.g., poor spatial grounding) is crucial. Quick check: What architectural pattern do most VLMs follow, and why might they struggle with multi‑step spatial tasks?
- **Scene Graphs:** Why needed: RSGAR’s core output is a scene graph; engineers must know how nodes (objects) and edges (spatial relations) encode structured spatial knowledge. Quick check: How would you represent “the cup is on the table, and the book is under the cup” as a scene graph?
- **Spatial Logical Reasoning:** Why needed: This is the benchmarked capability; it integrates spatial understanding (object positions) with logical dependencies (preconditions). Quick check: How does “pick up the red book” with obstacles differ from a simple VQA “what color is the book?” in terms of reasoning steps?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input image → Depth Anything V2 & SAM → depth and segmentation maps → Recursive VLM (scene graph generation) → final scene graph → VLM (reasoning) → output steps with preconditions
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Compute depth and segmentation maps.  
  2. Extract task‑specified source object(s).  
  3. For *t* = 1 to *T*: for each current source object, use VLM + perceptual maps to list target objects and relations; add new targets as next sources.  
  4. Aggregate all relations into a unified scene graph.  
  5. Feed graph + task to VLM to generate final JSON answer.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Recursion depth* *T*: larger *T* captures more distant objects but increases cost and noise.  
  - *Perceptual reliance*: depth/segmentation add overhead but are necessary (ablation shows drop without).  
  - *Graph sparsity*: only direct relations are added; avoids clutter but may miss indirect constraints.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Inaccurate perceptual maps (e.g., depth errors) → wrong spatial edges.  
  - VLM misidentifies object names during graph generation → broken chains.  
  - Early stopping (small *T*) → missing prerequisite objects.  
  - Final VLM ignores graph → no improvement over vanilla.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline comparison:** Run RSGAR (*T*=5) vs. vanilla VLM on a 5‑step task; verify that the scene graph includes all obstacles.  
  2. **Ablation check:** Disable depth input (use only segmentation) and confirm performance drop per Table 5b.  
  3. **Step‑count scaling:** Test RSGAR on tasks with 2, 4, and 6 steps; expect larger gains as step count increases (Table 4b).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why do VLMs exhibit significantly lower performance on precondition prediction compared to step content generation, and what specific architectural or training modifications could improve causal reasoning in multi‑step spatial tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Table 2 shows gap; Section 3.5 notes VLMs “have significant deficiencies in causal reasoning” and tend to omit steps due to uncertainty.
- **Why unresolved:** Paper identifies gap but does not investigate underlying reasons (e.g., lack of dependency modeling, training objective issues).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies isolating reasoning mechanisms (e.g., chain‑of‑thought variants, graph neural networks) on SpatiaLQA; error analysis of predicted vs. annotated preconditions to classify failure modes (missing dependencies, incorrect ordering).

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What distinct spatial information does depth versus segmentation provide in RSGAR's recursive scene graph generation, and can these be learned or simulated internally by VLMs without relying on external foundation models?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Table 5b ablation confirms both essential; paper states “each of them provides distinct visual information” but no analysis of specific cues.
- **Why unresolved:** No analysis of which cues are critical per recursion level or whether a single multimodal representation could replace them.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments perturbing depth or segmentation data (e.g., noise, edge removal) during graph generation, coupled with qualitative analysis of generated graphs; probing whether VLMs pretrained on 3D data can approximate these inputs.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can evaluation of spatial logical reasoning be made robust and model‑agnostic, given that scores vary significantly depending on the choice of scoring VLM, and what objective metrics could replace or complement VLM‑based matching?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 3.5 and Fig. 6 show scorer variability; paper adopts GPT‑4o for consistency but acknowledges dependence.
- **Why unresolved:** Relying on proprietary scorer hinders reproducibility; no attempt to develop model‑free metric (e.g., graph isomorphism, spatial relation classifiers).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of formal metric comparing predicted and ground‑truth step graphs (e.g., edit distance on ordered sequences with precondition constraints) validated against human judgments; cross‑scorer consistency studies to establish normalization protocols.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent does performance on the static, indoor SpatiaLQA benchmark transfer to real‑world embodied tasks (e.g., robotic manipulation) or dynamic environments (e.g., video‑based reasoning), and does spatial logical reasoning indeed serve as a cognitive foundation for such tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Introduction claims spatial reasoning is “cognitive basis” for embodied tasks, but benchmark only uses static indoor images (Section 3.2).
- **Why unresolved:** Theoretical link posited without empirical evidence; generalization to outdoor scenes, temporal dynamics, or physical execution constraints remains unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluating VLMs fine‑tuned on SpatiaLQA on embodied benchmarks (e.g., OpenEQA, MP3D‑EQA) and measuring correlation; extending SpatiaLQA to video sequences and testing whether improvements in static spatial reasoning predict gains in action sequencing with dynamics.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can RSGAR's recursive scene graph decomposition be optimized for efficiency (e.g., adaptive termination, parallelization) without sacrificing accuracy on complex multi‑step tasks, and what are the computational bottlenecks in its current iterative design?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Supplementary shows RSGAR (T=5) takes 174.5h vs. 27.4h for vanilla; performance improves with more iterations but trade‑off not optimized.
- **Why unresolved:** Fixed iteration count *T* used; no exploration of early stopping criteria, learned termination, or alternative graph construction strategies.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies varying *T* dynamically based on scene complexity (e.g., number of objects, dependency depth), measuring accuracy vs. time; analyzing per‑iteration graph growth to identify saturation points; investigating whether a single‑pass graph generator could match multi‑step recursion.

## Limitations
- Undisclosed dataset splits, perceptual model hyperparameters, and in‑context examples hinder exact replication.
- Evaluation heavily relies on GPT‑4o as both reasoner and scorer, risking systematic bias.
- Benchmark covers only indoor scenes; generalizability to outdoor or robotic domains remains unknown.

## Confidence
- SpatiaLQA validity & poor VLM performance: **High** (extensive testing of 41 VLMs, human ceiling ~97%).
- RSGAR improves spatial reasoning: **Medium** (modest gains on GPT‑4o; no independent verification with other VLMs/scorers).
- Depth+segmentation necessity & iterative recursion: **Medium** (ablation and step‑count trends supportive; causal isolation incomplete).

## Next Checks
1. Cross‑model replication: Re‑run RSGAR with an open‑source VLM (e.g., LLaVA‑Next) and use Claude‑4‑Sonnet for scoring to confirm gains without OpenAI‑specific artifacts.
2. Recursion ablation: Compare RSGAR (recursive) against a single‑pass full‑graph variant that includes all reachable objects in one iteration; measure performance vs. recursion depth *T* and graph size.
3. Cross‑benchmark generalization: Adapt RSGAR to two other spatial reasoning benchmarks (PhyBlock, NuScenes‑SpatialQA) to evaluate domain transferability.
