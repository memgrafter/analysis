# Beyond Binary Preferences: A Principled Framework for Reward Modeling with Ordinal Feedback

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02232
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02232
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** First principled framework for reward modeling with ordinal (Likert-scale) feedback, improving benchmark scores by 2-5% and accurately predicting preference strength.

## Executive Summary
Existing reward modeling for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) typically relies on binary preferences, discarding valuable information about the strength of preferences. This paper addresses that limitation by introducing a formal framework for ordinal reward modeling, where human feedback is given on a Likert scale. The authors formulate the problem as discrete ordinal regression, deriving two loss functions—negative log-likelihood (NLL) and all-threshold (AT)—that jointly learn a reward function and a set of thresholds to map rewards to ordinal categories.

Theoretical analysis shows that regularization is essential and that symmetric thresholds naturally arise under symmetric preference distributions. Empirical evaluation on HelpSteer2/HelpSteer3 with Llama-8B, Mistral-7B, and Zephyr-7B demonstrates that the symmetric NLL approach consistently matches or outperforms heuristic baselines, yielding 2-5% gains on RewardBench and RM-Bench, accurately predicting ordinal strength within one level ~85% of the time, and exhibiting robustness to label noise. The framework thus provides a more nuanced and effective approach to reward modeling.

## Method Summary
The core method recasts ordinal reward modeling as a discrete ordinal regression task. Given a response, a reward model (a neural network) produces a scalar reward. A set of learnable threshold parameters partitions the reward space into ordered bins corresponding to the ordinal feedback levels (e.g., 1–5). The probability of each ordinal class is derived from the cumulative distribution of a logistic model on the reward. Two loss functions are proposed: (1) the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the true ordinal class, and (2) an all-threshold (AT) loss that considers all pairwise threshold comparisons. Both losses are minimized with respect to the reward model parameters and the thresholds simultaneously, avoiding manual margin hyperparameters. To prevent overfitting and ensure well-behaved thresholds, regularization (e.g., L2 on thresholds) is employed. Theoretical results prove that regularization is necessary for consistency and that when the underlying preference distribution is symmetric, the learned thresholds become symmetric as well. The approach thus learns a calibrated ordinal reward model directly from data.

## Key Results
- The symmetric NLL loss yields consistent improvements over heuristic baselines, boosting average benchmark scores by 2-5% on RewardBench and RM-Bench across three model families.
- Ordinal strength is predicted accurately: the model’s reward ranks responses within one correct level ~85% of the time, and the mean error severity is reduced by 87% (0.5 vs 3.8) compared to prior methods.
- The framework outperforms post-hoc calibration approaches by 38% in mean absolute error and maintains robustness under systematic label shifts and moderate random noise corruption.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Ordinal feedback contains more information than binary comparisons; by modeling it directly, the framework preserves preference strength. Jointly learning thresholds and the reward function allows the model to adapt the scaling and boundaries to the data, eliminating the need for heuristic margins. The logistic link function provides a natural probabilistic interpretation, and the NLL loss gives maximum likelihood estimates under the model assumptions. Regularization controls threshold complexity, preventing overfitting and ensuring smoothness. The theoretical insight that symmetric preferences induce symmetric thresholds explains why enforcing symmetry empirically helps. Additionally, the all-threshold loss offers an alternative that may be more robust to class imbalance. Collectively, these elements yield a model that accurately captures the underlying ordering and intensity of human judgments, leading to better reward estimates for RLHF.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Response -> Reward Network (θ) -> Scalar Reward r  
Scalar Reward r + Thresholds (τ) -> Ordinal Distribution P(level | r, τ)  
True Ordinal Label -> Loss (NLL or AT) -> Gradients -> (θ, τ) Update  

**Critical Path:** The training loop iterates over batches of (response, ordinal label): forward pass computes r; thresholds convert r to class probabilities; loss compares probabilities to the label; backpropagation updates θ and τ jointly.  

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- NLL loss is standard and efficient but may suffer from class imbalance; AT loss considers all thresholds and may be more robust but is slightly more complex.  
- Joint learning of thresholds and reward introduces extra parameters, requiring careful regularization to avoid degenerate solutions (e.g., thresholds collapsing).  
- Enforcing threshold symmetry reduces parameters and improves generalization under symmetric preferences but may hurt when preferences are inherently asymmetric.  

**Failure Signatures:**  
1. Thresholds violate monotonicity (τ_i ≥ τ_{i-1}), indicating optimization issues.  
2. High variance in predicted rewards for the same ordinal level, suggesting poor calibration.  
3. Validation loss plateaus or increases when regularization is removed, confirming overfitting.  
4. Sensitivity to label noise: performance degrades rapidly with moderate noise if thresholds are not regularized.  

**First Experiments:**

2. Compare NLL and AT losses on a small held-out set to assess convergence speed and final performance.  
3. Create a dataset with symmetric ordinal preferences and check whether the learned thresholds are symmetric around zero (τ_i = -τ_{K-i}).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
Assumption: The paper's conclusion or future work section suggests the following open questions:
- The theoretical analysis relies on symmetric preference distributions; how robust is the framework when this assumption is violated, and can asymmetric thresholds be learned effectively?
- The experimental evaluation uses only two proprietary datasets and three mid-sized models; will the approach generalize to larger language models (e.g., >10B parameters) and diverse public ordinal preference datasets?
- The ordinal reward model has not been integrated into a full RLHF pipeline (e.g., with PPO); does the improved reward estimation translate to better policy performance and sample efficiency?
- The regularizer (e.g., L2 on thresholds) is shown to be necessary, but its optimal strength and alternative forms (e.g., monotonicity constraints) are not thoroughly explored.
- The all-threshold (AT) loss is proposed as an alternative to NLL, but its relative benefits and failure modes across different data distributions need further ablation.

## Limitations
- **Limited evaluation scope:** Experiments conducted on only two proprietary datasets and three mid-sized models, restricting generalizability to other domains or larger scales.
- **Missing statistical rigor:** No significance tests or confidence intervals reported for performance differences.
- **Theoretical and pipeline gaps:** The assumption of symmetric thresholds lacks thorough empirical validation beyond the presented experiments, and the learned rewards have not been tested in a full RLHF pipeline (e.g., PPO fine-tuning).
