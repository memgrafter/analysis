# TRACE: Training-Free Partial Audio Deepfake Detection via Embedding Trajectory Analysis of Speech Foundation Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.01083
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01083
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Achieves 8.08% EER on PartialSpoof and 24.12% EER on LlamaPartialSpoof, matching or surpassing supervised baselines without any training or target-domain data.

## Executive Summary
TRACE is a training-free method for detecting partial audio deepfakes—synthetic segments spliced into real recordings—that evades supervised detectors reliant on labeled data and specific generation techniques. It leverages frozen speech foundation models (e.g., wav2vec 2.0) to extract frame-level embeddings and analyzes their first-order temporal dynamics. Genuine speech produces smooth, slowly varying embedding trajectories, while splice boundaries introduce abrupt disruptions. These disruptions are measured via chord distances (angular changes) between consecutive L2-normalized embeddings. Evaluated on PartialSpoof and the challenging LlamaPartialSpoof benchmarks, TRACE matches fine-tuned supervised detectors on the former (8.08% EER) and surpasses a supervised baseline on the latter (24.12% vs. 24.49% EER). Results across English and Mandarin confirm its robustness and generalization without adaptation.

## Method Summary
TRACE processes an audio input through a frozen speech foundation model to obtain frame-level embeddings. Each embedding vector is L2-normalized, and the chord distance—equivalent to the angular separation—is computed between every pair of consecutive normalized embeddings. This yields a time-series of distance values; splice boundaries are hypothesized to cause spikes in this series. A single threshold, determined a priori (e.g., from a validation set or statistical assumption), is applied to these distances to flag potential splicing events. The approach requires no fine-tuning, no labeled deepfake examples, and no access to the specific generator used for splicing, relying solely on the intrinsic properties of the foundation model's representation space.

## Key Results
- Matches fine-tuned supervised detectors on PartialSpoof with 8.08% EER.
- Outperforms a supervised baseline on LlamaPartialSpoof (24.12% vs. 24.49% EER), a benchmark featuring unseen LLM-driven synthesis.
- Demonstrates cross-language potential, validated on both English and Mandarin partial-spoof datasets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method operates on the insight that genuine, continuous speech generates smooth trajectories in the embedding space of a well-trained foundation model, as adjacent frames share phonological context and acoustic continuity. In contrast, splicing a synthetic segment (even if high-quality) into real speech creates a discontinuous jump: the embedding at the splice boundary transitions from a natural speech region to a synthetic one, which likely occupies a different manifold in the representation space. This manifests as a large chord distance (angular change) between the embedding just before the splice and the one just after. Because chord distance measures only directional change and is invariant to vector magnitude, it is a robust proxy for detecting such manifold shifts. The training-free nature avoids overfitting to any specific generative method's artifacts, instead targeting a universal property: temporal inconsistency at manipulation points.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Speech Foundation Models**  
   - *Why needed*: Provide high-level, context-aware representations learned from vast unlabeled speech data, serving as a stable feature extractor for analysis.  
   - *Quick check*: Extract embeddings from different layers for the same audio and assess consistency in trajectory smoothness.
2. **Embedding Trajectory Analysis**  
   - *Why needed*: Captures temporal evolution of representations; manipulations disrupt natural temporal dependencies.  
   - *Quick check*: Compute variance of first-order differences (embedding[n] - embedding[n-1]) on genuine vs. spliced audio.
3. **Chord Distance (Angular Distance)**  
   - *Why needed*: Focuses on semantic/directional change between embeddings, robust to amplitude variations.  
   - *Quick check*: Compare detection performance using chord distance vs. Euclidean distance on synthetic boundaries.
4. **Partial Deepfake Detection**  
   - *Why needed*: Full-audio detectors often miss localized splicing; forensic analysis requires fine-grained temporal detection.  
   - *Quick check*: Implement a sliding-window discriminator and evaluate on audio with short synthetic inserts.
5. **Training-Free Forensics**  
   - *Why needed*: Avoids the distribution shift problem of supervised methods when faced with unseen generators.  
   - *Quick check*: Apply the same detector to multiple generative architectures (e.g., VC, TTS) without retraining.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input Audio -> Frozen Speech Foundation Model -> Frame-level Embeddings -> L2 Normalization -> Consecutive Chord Distance Calculation -> Threshold Comparison -> Detection Decision (boundary if distance > threshold).  
- **Critical path**: Embedding extraction → normalization → distance computation → thresholding.  
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - *Frame-level granularity* enables precise boundary localization but increases computational cost and sensitivity to noise/transients.  
  - *No training* ensures generality but forces reliance on a fixed threshold, which may not adapt to varying audio conditions or model quirks.  
  - *Single-model dependency* simplifies deployment but may limit robustness if the foundation model's embedding space is poorly suited for the target language or domain.  
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - High chord distances in genuine speech during rapid phonetic transitions, loud noises, or room acoustics changes → false positives.  
  - Low chord distances at splice boundaries if synthetic speech is extremely realistic or generator exploits the foundation model's manifold → false negatives.  
  - Systematic bias if foundation model was not trained on similar languages or acoustic environments.  
- **First experiments**:  
  1. Sweep threshold values on a held-out validation set to generate an ROC curve and confirm EER optimality.  
  2. Replace the default foundation model (e.g., wav2vec 2.0) with HuBERT or WavLM, repeat evaluation, and measure performance delta.  
  3. Vary synthetic segment length (e.g., 0.1s, 0.5s, 1.0s) in test samples to determine minimum detectable splice duration.

## Limitations
- Evaluation confined to two partial-spoof datasets; unknown generalization to other datasets, languages, or recording conditions.
- No exploration of how choice of foundation model (architecture, size, pre-training data) impacts detection efficacy.
- The assumption of smooth genuine trajectories lacks theoretical grounding; adversarial methods could potentially smooth splice boundaries.
- Only Equal Error Rate (EER) is reported; no full ROC analysis, precision-recall curves, or evaluation under cost-sensitive scenarios.
- Computational footprint of per-frame embedding extraction and distance computation is not quantified or compared against supervised baselines.

## Confidence
- *PartialSpoof EER (8.08%)*: Medium – result is clear but unreplicated.
- *LlamaPartialSpoof superiority (24.12% vs 24.49%)*: Medium – single benchmark advantage.
- *Cross-language generalization*: Low – tested on only English and Mandarin.
- *Training-free robustness across generators*: Low – not validated against diverse unseen generation methods beyond Llama-based TTS.
