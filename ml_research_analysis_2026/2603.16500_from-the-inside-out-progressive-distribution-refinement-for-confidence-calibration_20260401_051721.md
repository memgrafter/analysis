# From the Inside Out: Progressive Distribution Refinement for Confidence Calibration
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16500
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16500
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** DistriTTRL-GMM outperforms TTRL baselines across five models and three benchmarks, achieving absolute accuracy gains from 0.51 to 4.33 points.

## Executive Summary
DistriTTRL addresses two critical challenges in test‑time reinforcement learning (TTRL) for large reasoning models: (1) the distribution mismatch between training and test‑time internal confidence signals, and (2) reward hacking from voting‑based strategies where models converge to consistent but incorrect answers. The method introduces (a) progressive distribution refinement using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) to construct and shift‑correct a global confidence distribution across training steps, and (b) diversity‑targeted penalties within the GRPO algorithm that down‑weight queries with low answer diversity. Across five models and three benchmarks, DistriTTRL‑GMM yields absolute Pass@1 improvements of 0.51 to 4.33 points over TTRL baselines and demonstrably reduces majority ratio convergence, mitigating reward hacking while effectively leveraging scaled voting information.

## Method Summary
DistriTTRL operates in a test‑time RL setting where an unlabeled test set is used to adapt model parameters via reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. The core innovation is two‑fold. First, it progressively aggregates rollout confidences (derived from token negative log‑probabilities) across training steps, fitting a two‑component GMM at each step. To correct for systematic rightward shifts in confidence means, it computes per‑step offsets and applies them to historical distributions, building a stable global prior. This prior is then used to partition samples per query into positive and negative subsets via likelihood comparison; a majority vote on the negative subset identifies a likely incorrect answer, which is filtered out from the positives, and the remaining positives are voted to produce the final pseudo‑label (DistriVoting). Second, a diversity‑targeted penalty is introduced: for each query the number of unique generated answers D(q) is computed, normalized across the batch, and if D(q) falls below a threshold τ·G the advantage in the GRPO objective is scaled by this normalized diversity; otherwise the advantage is unchanged. This down‑weights queries where the model collapses to a single answer, discouraging reward hacking via consistency.

The pipeline integrates these components into a standard GRPO loop: sample multiple rollouts per query, compute confidences, construct the aggregated confidence buffer, estimate pseudo‑labels via DistriVoting, compute diversity weights, derive rewards based on pseudo‑label correctness, and perform a GRPO update with the weighted advantages. The method requires storing O(S·B·G) confidences, assumes approximately linear confidence drift, and relies on the bimodal assumption for the confidence distribution. Despite missing hyperparameters (e.g., GRPO learning rate, KL coefficient), the reported experiments show consistent gains.

## Key Results
- **Accuracy:** DistriTTRL-GMM achieves 0.51–4.33 absolute Pass@1 gains over TTRL on five models across three benchmarks.
- **Reward hacking mitigation:** Majority ratio (rmaj) rises slower than TTRL, aligning closer to ground‑truth supervision and reducing consistency hacking.
- **Scalable voting:** DistriVoting accuracy improves with increased rollout budget, validating the benefit of distribution‑guided aggregation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Progressive distribution refinement with shift correction mitigates train‑test confidence mismatch by aggregating rollouts across steps into a stable global prior.
- **Mechanism:** At each step, fit a GMM to current confidences; for all prior steps, compute shift offsets using their GMM means vs. current, apply correction, and aggregate. This global prior then guides per‑query sample partitioning into positive/negative subsets.
- **Core assumption:** Model confidence distributions shift rightward during training (due to parameter updates), and this shift is approximately linear between steps.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "progressively constructing and shift-correcting a global confidence distribution across training steps using Gaussian Mixture Models"
  - [section 4.2.1] Details Equations 8–10 for shift correction and aggregation.
  - [section 3.4/Figure 2] Empirically shows rightward confidence shift across steps.
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers on reward hacking and confidence calibration are tangentially related but do not address progressive distribution construction.
- **Break condition:** If confidence distributions do not exhibit systematic shift (e.g., training stabilizes early), aggregation adds noise rather than signal.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Diversity‑targeted penalties in GRPO mitigate consistency reward hacking by down‑weighting queries with low answer diversity.
- **Mechanism:** For each query, compute unique answer count D(q); normalize across batch to get ˜D; if D(q) ≤ τ·G, scale advantage by ˜D, else use weight 1. This penalizes queries that collapse to single answers, encouraging exploration.
- **Core assumption:** Low answer diversity in rollouts indicates the model is hacking the voting‑based reward (converging to consistent but incorrect answers) rather than exploring correct reasoning paths.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "diversity‑targeted penalties within GRPO to down‑weight queries with low answer diversity, discouraging consistency hacking."
  - [section 4.3] Defines D(q) and weight D(q)i in Equation 19.
  - [section 6.1/Figure 4] Shows DistriTTRL's majority ratio rises slower than TTRL, aligning closer to ground‑truth supervision.
  - [corpus] Neighbors on reward hacking (e.g., "Spontaneous Reward Hacking") support that reward design can induce unwanted optimization, but do not specifically validate diversity penalties.
- **Break condition:** If low diversity naturally correlates with correct answers (e.g., for unambiguous questions), penalties may hurt performance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Calibrating confidence with global distribution prior improves pseudo‑label accuracy by filtering low‑potential samples before voting.
- **Mechanism:** Using the aggregated global GMM, assign each sample to positive/negative subset via likelihood comparison (Equation 12). Vote on negative subset to find a likely incorrect answer; remove samples from positive subset producing that answer. Vote remaining positives for final pseudo‑label.
- **Core assumption:** High‑confidence samples are more likely correct; the negative subset's majority vote reveals a systematic error mode to filter.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "leverages distribution prior of the model’s confidence during RL to progressively optimize the reward signal"
  - [section 4.2.2] Equations 13–15 detail filtering and voting.
  - [section 6.2/Figure 5] Shows DistriVoting accuracy improves with budget, validating the value of aggregated/distribution‑guided voting.
  - [corpus] Limited; neighboring papers on confidence calibration (e.g., "Confidence Before Answering") use different paradigms.
- **Break condition:** If confidence does not correlate with correctness (poor calibration), filtering may remove correct samples.

## Foundational Learning
### Test‑Time Training (TTT)
- **Why needed here:** DistriTTRL adapts the model on unlabeled test‑time data during RL, requiring understanding that parameters update at test time, breaking fixed‑parameter TTS assumptions.
- **Quick check question:** How does TTT differ from standard RL with verifiable rewards, and why does it cause distribution shift?

### Confidence Calibration via Gaussian Mixture Models
- **Why needed here:** The method models confidence as bimodal (positive/negative reasoning paths) to separate high‑potential from low‑potential samples.
- **Quick check question:** What does a two‑component GMM assume about the distribution of trajectory confidences, and how are the components interpreted?

### Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)
- **Why needed here:** DistriTTRL uses GRPO; understanding advantage normalization and KL penalties is key to seeing where diversity weights are injected.
- **Quick check question:** In GRPO, how is the advantage computed within a query’s group, and where does the diversity penalty modulate it?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Confidence storage C (S×B×G tensor) records all rollout confidences; GMM fitter (per step and aggregated); shift corrector (computes ∆s→k); diversity calculator (D(q) per query); advantage scaler (multiplies by D(q) in GRPO objective).
- **Critical path:** For each step: sample rollouts → compute confidences → construct Ck_agg (current + corrected history) → estimate pseudo‑label per query → compute diversity weights → compute rewards → GRPO update with weighted advantages.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Aggregating across steps increases effective budget but requires storing O(S·B·G) confidences; shift correction assumes linear drift; diversity penalty may slow convergence on homogeneous queries.
- **Failure signatures:** 
  1. Majority ratio (rmaj) climbs rapidly toward 1 without accuracy gain → hacking persists.
  2. GMM fails to converge to bimodal shape → partitioning unreliable.
  3. Diversity weights become near‑zero for many queries → learning signal vanishes.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Isolate shift correction: Compare pseudo‑label accuracy with/without correction on a fixed set of historical confidences; validate that correction aligns distributions (e.g., Kolmogorov‑Smirnov test).
  2. Test diversity penalty alone: Run TTRL with confidence‑weighted voting but no diversity penalty; track rmaj and accuracy to confirm penalty effect.
  3. Full DistriTTRL‑GMM: Run end‑to‑end on a small benchmark (e.g., AIME2024 subset) with 2 steps to ensure pipeline integrates; verify that Ck_agg grows and GMM parameters stabilize.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Is the linear mean shift correction sufficient to align confidence distributions across training steps, given potential changes in distribution shape?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper proposes shift correction by adding the difference of the average of positive and negative means between current and previous steps (Equation 8). They assume that distribution shift can be captured by a simple translation, without considering changes in variance or higher moments.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not validate whether this correction fully accounts for distribution shifts; it could lead to misalignment if variances change significantly.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies comparing different shift correction methods (e.g., full distribution matching, moment matching beyond mean) on performance and robustness across different training stages.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the bimodal Gaussian mixture model accurately represent the confidence distribution of model rollouts, or might more components be needed?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper models confidence as a GMM with two components (Equation 4), justified by the intuition of positive and negative reasoning paths.
- **Why unresolved:** The assumption of two components is not empirically validated; it may oversimplify the distribution, especially for models with more than two types of outputs or errors.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Statistical tests (e.g., Bayesian information criterion) to determine the optimal number of components, and experiments comparing performance with different numbers of GMM components.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can DistriTTRL be extended to domains where verifiable rewards or majority voting are not applicable, such as open‑ended text generation?
- **Basis in paper:** The method relies on voting‑based pseudo‑labels derived from multiple rollouts, which requires a notion of correctness. The paper only evaluates on math benchmarks with clear answers.
- **Why unresolved:** The core idea of using distribution priors and diversity penalties might be beneficial in non‑verifiable settings, but the paper does not address how to obtain pseudo‑labels without ground truth.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of proxy reward signals (e.g., using LLM‑as‑a‑judge) and experiments on non‑verifiable tasks to test whether the approach still improves calibration and reduces reward hacking.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive is the diversity‑targeted penalty to the choice of threshold τ and the normalization scheme, and are there more effective ways to incorporate diversity?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper uses a fixed threshold τ=0.1 and applies softmax normalization across batch diversity (Equations 18‑19). The threshold is chosen without justification.
- **Why unresolved:** The hyperparameters are not tuned or analyzed; the penalty might be too aggressive or too lenient depending on τ, and the softmax normalization may be arbitrary.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Sensitivity analysis varying τ and alternative normalization methods (e.g., linear scaling, percentile‑based) to identify robust settings and possibly learn the penalty weight adaptively.

## Limitations
- Missing GRPO hyperparameters (learning rate, KL coefficient β, clipping ϵ) and reward shaping details hinder exact replication.
- Ambiguities in confidence token selection (“last tail step”) and GMM initialization (component starts, convergence) may affect robustness.
- Training schedule specifics (total steps S, batch size B, sliding window vs. full history) are unspecified; memory complexity grows with steps.
