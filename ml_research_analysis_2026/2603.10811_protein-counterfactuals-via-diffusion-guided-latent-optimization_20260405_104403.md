# Protein Counterfactuals via Diffusion-Guided Latent Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10811
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10811
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Primary result:** MCCOP generates minimal, biologically plausible counterfactual edits for proteins with high success rates (100% on stability/activity, 19% on fluorescence) and low adversarial risk (<3%), using only 2.3–2.5 mutations on average.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces MCCOP, a method for generating counterfactual edits to protein sequences and structures that flip a learned predictor’s output. MCCOP optimizes within a continuous joint sequence‑structure latent space, leveraging a pretrained diffusion model as a manifold prior to ensure biological plausibility. The optimization alternates between sparse gradient updates—masking all but the top‑*k* most sensitive positions—and projection onto the diffusion manifold. Predictor smoothing techniques (spectral normalization, Jacobian regularization, adversarial augmentation) guard against adversarial examples. The objective balances three criteria: property validity, proximity (few mutations), and structural plausibility. Evaluated on GFP fluorescence rescue, thermodynamic stability, and UBE4B activity, MCCOP produces counterfactuals with remarkably low mutation counts (2.3–2.5 vs. 6–11 for discrete baselines), near‑perfect success on two tasks, and preserves native structural metrics. The approach also recovers known rescue mutations from held‑out test sets, demonstrating utility for protein engineering.

## Method Summary
MCCOP operates in a latent space that jointly encodes sequence and 3D structure. A pretrained diffusion model defines a manifold of foldable, realistic proteins; optimization steps are projected onto this manifold to maintain validity. Starting from an input protein embedding, the algorithm computes gradients of a property predictor with respect to the latent vector. Sparse updates are enforced by zeroing out all gradient entries except the top‑*k* positions, ensuring only the most influential residues are altered. After each gradient step, the updated latent is refined via diffusion‑based denoising, keeping it on the learned manifold. Predictor smoothing—including spectral normalization on model weights, Jacobian regularization to penalize overly sensitive gradients, and adversarial augmentation during predictor training—stabilizes the process and prevents exploitation of spurious correlations. The final optimized latent is decoded back to a protein sequence and structure.

## Key Results
- Success rates: 100% on thermodynamic stability and UBE4B activity, 19% on GFP fluorescence rescue.
- Average mutation count: 2.3–2.5, far lower than 6–11 for discrete baselines.
- Adversarial rate: <3% (vs. 100% for unconstrained gradient descent); preserves structural metrics (pLDDT, radius of gyration, hydrophobicity); mutations enriched in known functional motifs; recovers held‑out rescue mutations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The diffusion model acts as a powerful manifold prior, constraining the search to regions of latent space corresponding to physically foldable proteins—this prevents degenerate structures that would otherwise satisfy the property predictor. Sparse gradient updates focus changes on residues with the highest predicted impact, achieving counterfactuals with minimal mutations. Predictor smoothing regularizes the property model, reducing its susceptibility to adversarial perturbations and ensuring that latent updates reflect genuine, not artefactual, improvements. The joint sequence‑structure latent space allows coherent co‑optimization of sequence and conformation, while the alternating projection naturally balances the competing goals of validity, proximity, and plausibility.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input protein → Sequence‑Structure Encoder → JointLatent → [Loop: SparseGradientUpdate → ManifoldProjection (Diffusion) → LossComputation] → OptimizedLatent → Decoder → Counterfactual protein.

**Critical path:** The iterative loop that applies a masked gradient step followed by diffusion‑based projection is the core optimization engine. Convergence is reached when the property predictor’s output crosses the target threshold or iteration limits are hit.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Manifold prior strength vs. flexibility*: A tight diffusion prior ensures plausibility but may limit expressivity for certain properties.  
- *Sparsity (k) vs. success rate*: Smaller *k* yields fewer mutations but may require more iterations or risk stagnation.  
- *Predictor smoothing vs. speed*: Spectral normalization and Jacobian regularization add computational overhead but are crucial for robustness.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High adversarial rate (>5%) indicates insufficient predictor smoothing.  
- Structural collapse (drop in pLDDT or unusual radius of gyration) suggests manifold projection is ineffective.  
- Failure to flip the property after many iterations may point to overly strict sparsity or a property predictor that is not smooth enough.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Ablate sparse gradient (set *k* = all positions) and measure increase in mutation count and changes in plausibility metrics.  
2. Remove manifold projection (skip diffusion step) and evaluate structural integrity (pLDDT, radius of gyration) and adversarial rate.  
3. Disable predictor smoothing (remove spectral norm, Jacobian reg, adversarial aug) and quantify adversarial examples generated during optimization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can MCCOP be adapted for multi‑objective counterfactual optimization (e.g., simultaneously improving stability and binding affinity)?
- **Basis in paper:** Future Directions: "Multi‑objective counterfactuals: jointly optimizing stability and binding affinity by combining predictor gradients."
- **Why unresolved:** Current MCCOP optimizes a single target property; combining multiple objectives may conflict with the manifold constraint and sparsity requirements.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi‑objective MCCOP variant that yields a Pareto front of counterfactuals, each meeting plausibility metrics (pLDDT, hydrophobicity) and confirmed via experimental validation.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Do MCCOP‑generated counterfactual sequences exhibit the desired experimental property changes (e.g., increased fluorescence, stability), or do computational plausibility proxies fail to predict real‑world success?
- **Basis in paper:** Future Directions: "Experimental validation: synthesizing top‑ranked variants for closed‑loop validation." Also, Limitations note reliance on computational proxies.
- **Why unresolved:** Evaluation so far uses only in‑silico metrics (ESM3 pLDDT, radius of gyration, etc.), which may not correlate with actual protein behavior; without wet‑lab tests, practical utility remains uncertain.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Synthesis and experimental measurement of MCCOP counterfactuals across multiple benchmarks, reporting success rates and comparing to discrete baselines.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can MCCOP generate a diverse set of counterfactuals for a single protein, revealing alternative mutational strategies that achieve the same target property?
- **Basis in paper:** Future Directions: "Diverse counterfactual sets (Mothilal et al., 2020; Bender & Morik, 2026): revealing alternative mutational strategies and enriching fitness landscape understanding."
- **Why unresolved:** MCCOP currently returns one counterfactual per input; a diverse set would provide broader engineering options and deeper fitness landscape insights.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An algorithm adding diversity (e.g., stochastic restarts, diversity regularization) that produces multiple distinct yet valid counterfactuals, validated by plausibility metrics and mutation pattern analysis.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How should MCCOP be modified to handle continuous regression targets (e.g., predicting a numerical stability score) instead of binary classification, and does replacing the margin loss with MSE or quantile losses maintain effectiveness?
- **Basis in paper:** Limitations: "We evaluate only binary tasks; extending to continuous regression targets requires replacing the margin loss with MSE or quantile losses."
- **Why unresolved:** Many protein properties are continuous; restricting MCCOP to binarized tasks limits its applicability to real‑world datasets.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A regression‑oriented MCCOP using an appropriate loss, evaluated on a continuous property dataset, showing comparable success rates, sparsity, and plausibility to the binary case.

## Limitations
- Success on GFP fluorescence rescue is low (19%), indicating possible dataset‑specific biases or limited expressivity for that property.
- All evaluations are in silico; no wet‑lab validation of expression, solubility, or actual function.
- Computations not reported; latent‑space optimization and diffusion sampling may be costly for large proteins.

## Confidence
- **Minimal, plausible counterfactuals:** High  
- **Low adversarial rate (<3%):** High  
- **Recovery of known rescue mutations:** Medium  
- **Generalizability across tasks:** Low–Medium
