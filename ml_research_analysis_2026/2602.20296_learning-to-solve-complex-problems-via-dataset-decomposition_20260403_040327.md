# Learning to Solve Complex Problems via Dataset Decomposition

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20296  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20296  
- **Reference count:** 38  
- **Primary result:** DECOMP+Curriculum achieves 51.6% on MATH-500 (Qwen2.5-1.5B), 16.7% on AIME 2025 (Qwen3-4B-Base), and 42.68% on HumanEval (Qwen2.5-1.5B), substantially outperforming SFT baselines.

## Executive Summary

DECOMP is a reverse curriculum learning framework that trains small language models to solve complex mathematical and coding tasks. The method recursively decomposes problems using a teacher model, constructs a concept dependency graph to quantify subproblem difficulty, and then trains the student model on a curriculum from easy to hard. Extensive experiments on MATH, AIME, and HumanEval show significant improvements over standard fine-tuning and data augmentation. The approach enhances generalization and demonstrates the power of structured decomposition in low-resource settings.

## Method Summary

DECOMP employs a large teacher model (GPT‑4o for math, o4‑mini for code) to generate a decomposition of each training example’s chain‑of‑thought (CoT). The CoT is split into ≤ k atomic steps; each step is tagged with a high‑level concept, a subquestion is generated, and the subquestion is solved without context to verify correctness (retry ≤ R times). All valid subproblems are retained, forming a tree of partial solutions. From all trees, a concept dependency graph is built: similar tags are clustered (cosine similarity ≥ 0.8), and parent‑child links create a DAG; the longest‑path depth for each tag defines its conceptual depth. Subproblem difficulty is computed as ℓ(s) = α₁·SC(s) + α₂·CD(s), where SC is the number of children in the decomposition node and CD is the tag depth. The dataset is partitioned into K quantiles by difficulty. A student model is fine‑tuned sequentially on increasingly difficult quantiles, using early stopping per stage and a total of 5 epochs. Baselines include standard SFT and data augmentation.

## Key Results

- **MATH‑500:** DECOMP+Curriculum (Qwen2.5‑1.5B) reaches **51.6%** accuracy vs. **47.6%** for SFT.  
- **AIME 2025:** DECOMP+Curriculum (Qwen3‑4B‑Base) achieves **16.7%** vs. **3.3%** for SFT.  
- **HumanEval:** DECOMP (Qwen2.5‑1.5B) yields **42.68%** pass@1 vs. **35.37%** for SFT.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

DECOMP works because it mimics the natural learning process: complex tasks are broken down into simpler, logically prerequisite subproblems, and the model masters these foundations before confronting harder challenges. The concept dependency graph captures the hierarchical relationships between mathematical or programming concepts, providing a principled difficulty measure that combines structural complexity (number of steps) and conceptual depth (position in the graph). Training via a curriculum prevents interference from hard examples early on, leading to more robust knowledge acquisition and better generalization to unseen problems. Additionally, the teacher‑driven decomposition injects high‑quality reasoning traces that are otherwise absent in small student models.

## Foundational Learning

- **Reverse Curriculum Learning**  
  *Why needed:* Prevents the model from being overwhelmed by difficult problems early in training; builds confidence and foundational skills.  
  *Quick check:* Does training on easy samples first improve accuracy on hard test sets compared to random shuffling?

- **Recursive Decomposition**  
  *Why needed:* Generates a rich set of training examples that expose the latent structure of complex tasks and cover necessary prerequisites.  
  *Quick check:* Are the generated subproblems independently solvable and logically connected to the original problem?

- **Concept Dependency Graph**  
  *Why needed:* Encodes prerequisite relationships between abstract concepts, enabling an objective difficulty assessment.  
  *Quick check:* Does the graph’s depth correlate with human‑judged concept difficulty?

- **Difficulty Scoring**  
  *Why needed:* Provides a quantitative ordering for curriculum design; balances structural and conceptual factors.  
  *Quick check:* Does the score monotonically increase with actual problem hardness (e.g., base model zero‑shot accuracy)?

- **Curriculum Training**  
  *Why needed:* Mirrors the natural learning curve, reduces forgetting, and improves sample efficiency.  
  *Quick check:* Does sequential training on difficulty‑sorted subsets outperform shuffled or single‑stage fine‑tuning?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Teacher Model → Decomposition Module → Tagging & Clustering → Concept Graph Builder → Difficulty Scorer → Curriculum Splitter → Sequential Fine‑tuner → Student Model

**Critical path:**  
1. Decompose each training problem into subproblems using the teacher.  
2. Tag each reasoning step with a concept and verify subproblem correctness.  
3. Cluster similar tags and build a directed concept graph.  
4. Compute difficulty for every subproblem (SC, CD).  
5. Sort all subproblems and partition into K quantiles.  
6. Fine‑tune the student model sequentially on the quantiles, with early stopping per stage.  
7. Evaluate the final student on held‑out benchmarks.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Teacher quality vs. cost (GPT‑4/o4‑mini vs. open alternatives).  
- Recursion depth (D) vs. semantic drift.  
- Clustering threshold (cosine similarity) vs. concept granularity.  
- Difficulty weighting (α₁, α₂) vs. simplicity.  
- Number of curriculum stages (K) vs. training time and risk of catastrophic forgetting.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low verification acceptance rate indicates malformed subproblems.  
- Non‑monotonic zero‑shot accuracy across difficulty bins suggests mis‑scoring.  
- Performance degrades after early curriculum stages (interference).  
- Teacher bias toward certain concepts leading to an unbalanced graph and poor coverage.

**First experiments:**  
1. **Verification robustness:** Implement a symbolic evaluator for math (exact rational arithmetic) and code (unit tests from CodeForces). Run decomposition with the proposed prompts, record the acceptance rate of subproblems, and iterate to improve validity.  
2. **Difficulty ordering validation:** Sample 500 subproblems across predicted difficulty bins. Compute greedy accuracy of the base student model; expect a monotonic drop with increasing difficulty. If absent, retune α₁ and α₂.  
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Perform a grid search over α ∈ {0.5, 1, 2} and K ∈ {3, 5, 7} with fixed teacher and student. Compare final scores to the DECOMP+Curriculum baseline and report variance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Generalization beyond math and code**  
  *Basis:* The conclusion states plans to apply DECOMP to a broader range of domains.  
  *Why unresolved:* The method has only been validated on structured reasoning datasets; domains like commonsense or legal reasoning may lack clear, decomposable steps.  
  *Resolving evidence:* Apply DECOMP to varied datasets (e.g., logical deduction, scientific QA) and compare against baselines to confirm consistent gains.

- **Robustness to teacher hallucinations**  
  *Basis:* The limitations note that LLM hallucinations are a core vulnerability.  
  *Why unresolved:* Current verification (answer consistency) may miss semantic errors, potentially degrading student training.  
  *Resolving evidence:* Integrate enhanced verification (e.g., multi‑teacher consensus, symbolic solvers) and show that filtered decompositions lead to higher accuracy and lower error rates.

- **Scalability to large and highly complex problems**  
  *Basis:* The authors mention leaving scalability as a future direction.  
  *Why unresolved:* Experiments use modest datasets (MATH: 7,500 samples). The efficiency of deep decomposition trees, graph management, and curriculum ordering on massive scales remains untested.  
  *Resolving evidence:* Apply DECOMP to large‑scale datasets (e.g., millions of problems), measure decomposition time/memory, and assess student performance relative to non‑decomposed training.

## Limitations

- Unknown decomposition parameters (max depth D, step limit k, retry count R) and verification details for non‑arithmetic subproblems.  
- Missing specifics on difficulty weighting (α₁, α₂), number of quantiles K, and stage‑wise epoch allocation/early stopping.  
- Reliance on proprietary teacher models (GPT‑4o, o4‑mini) limits reproducibility and may inject biases.  
- No ablation of concept clustering threshold or graph construction details, which could affect tag depth calculation.
