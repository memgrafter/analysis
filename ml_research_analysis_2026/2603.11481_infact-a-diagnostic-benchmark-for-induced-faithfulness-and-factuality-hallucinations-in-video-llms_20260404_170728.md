# INFACT: A Diagnostic Benchmark for Induced Faithfulness and Factuality Hallucinations in Video-LLMs
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.11481
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11481
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: INFACT reveals that higher clean accuracy does not ensure robustness; evidence corruption degrades model stability more than visual degradation, and temporal intervention causes the largest performance drop, with many open-source models exhibiting near-zero temporal sensitivity on factuality tasks.

## Executive Summary
INFACT is a diagnostic benchmark designed to evaluate hallucination tendencies in Video-LLMs across two dimensions: faithfulness (contradiction with video evidence) and factuality (contradiction with verifiable world knowledge). It introduces 9,800 QA instances with fine-grained taxonomies and four evaluation modes: Base (clean), Visual Degradation, Evidence Corruption, and Temporal Intervention. Key metrics are Resist Rate (RR) for invariant-label modes and Temporal Sensitivity Score (TSS) for order-sensitive items. Evaluating 14 models shows that high Base accuracy does not guarantee reliability under perturbations; evidence corruption is more damaging than visual degradation, and temporal disruption yields the largest performance drops, especially on factuality where many models exhibit near-zero TSS. The benchmark highlights pronounced weaknesses in motion and spatiotemporal structure for faithfulness, and in procedural and physical reasoning for factuality.

## Method Summary
INFACT constructs a paired evaluation protocol where each question is tested in Base mode and under three types of perturbations. Visual Degradation applies noise, blur, or compression to frames while preserving the semantic answer. Evidence Corruption introduces misleading textual cues (e.g., caption injection, subtitle corruption) or adversarial noise that conflicts with the video. Temporal Intervention shuffles or reverses frames for a curated subset of order-sensitive questions, thereby invalidating the original label. For invariant-label modes, Resist Rate (RR) measures the proportion of Base-correct predictions that remain correct under perturbation. For temporal mode, Temporal Sensitivity Score (TSS) measures the proportion of Base-correct predictions that change after intervention. The benchmark draws from multiple video-QA datasets, instructional resources, and synthetic videos, and filters out ambiguous or answerable-without-video items via LLM ensemble and human verification.

## Key Results
- Higher Base accuracy does not correlate with reliability under perturbations; model rankings shift significantly when RR and TSS are considered.
- Evidence corruption (especially caption injection) degrades Resist Rate more severely than visual degradation, indicating fragility to cross-modal conflicts.
- Many open-source models exhibit near-zero TSS on factuality tasks, revealing pronounced temporal inertia; temporal intervention causes the largest performance drop overall.
- Faithfulness weaknesses concentrate in motion and spatiotemporal structure, while factuality remains challenging for procedural and physical reasoning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
We detail three core mechanisms that explain the benchmark's diagnostic power.

**Mechanism 1: Evidence corruption reveals cross-modal fragility**  
INFACT injects textual interference (caption injection, subtitle corruption) or adversarial noise while keeping the ground-truth answer invariant. A model that changes its correct Base prediction under these conditions is overly influenced by the misleading signal rather than persisting with the original visual evidence. The human-audited label-preservation rates (94.5–100%) confirm that the perturbations do not alter the intended answer. The consistent finding that evidence corruption degrades Resist Rate more than visual degradation (Tables 2–3) supports this mechanism. If label preservation fell below an agreed threshold (e.g., <90%), the RR metric would become unreliable.

**Mechanism 2: Temporal inertia exposes lack of temporal grounding**  
For order-sensitive questions (e.g., procedural steps), INFACT disrupts the frame order via shuffling or reversal, which invalidates the original ground-truth label. A temporally grounded model should abandon its Base prediction; a low Temporal Sensitivity Score (TSS) indicates the model relies on order-invariant shortcuts (e.g., static objects, language priors) rather than temporal reasoning. The curation of the order-sensitive subset (spatiotemporal structure and procedural knowledge) ensures that the question-answer pairs require chronological understanding. The observation that many open-source models have near-zero TSS on factuality (Table 3) demonstrates pronounced temporal inertia. If non-order-sensitive items were mistakenly included, TSS would not reliably distinguish temporal sensitivity.

**Mechanism 3: Paired comparison uncovers shortcut reliance**  
INFACT compares a model’s Base prediction to its behavior under perturbations on the same question. Divergence in RR or TSS, despite similar Base accuracy across models, exposes dependency on evidence conditions that perturbations violate. This paired design controls for item difficulty and reveals whether high clean accuracy stems from genuine multimodal understanding or from exploiting fragile shortcuts (e.g., language priors, static cues). The strong correlation (Pearson r=0.978) between Base accuracy and average reliability, but non-preserved rankings (Section 4.2), confirms that Base accuracy alone is insufficient for diagnosing robustness.

## Foundational Learning
We introduce three key concepts necessary to interpret INFACT.

- **Hallucination dichotomy (faithfulness vs. factuality)**  
  *Why needed:* INFACT separates errors that contradict video evidence (faithfulness) from those that contradict verifiable world knowledge (factuality). Without this distinction, we cannot diagnose whether a failure is due to poor visual grounding or a knowledge gap.  
  *Quick check:* In a video showing a glass breaking, a question "What material is the object?" tests faithfulness (identifying glass) or factuality (knowing glass is brittle)? Which hallucination occurs if the model says "plastic"? (Answer: faithfulness if it misidentifies the material; factuality if it knows it's glass but claims it's not brittle.)

- **Invariant-label vs. label-changing interventions**  
  *Why needed:* Invariant-label modes (Visual Degradation, Evidence Corruption) use Resist Rate to test stability when the correct answer should not change. The label-changing Temporal Intervention uses TSS to test sensitivity when the correct answer must change. Mixing these would confound stability with sensitivity.  
  *Quick check:* If a model correctly answers "blue" for "What color is the car?" in a clean video, what should happen to its prediction under (a) Gaussian noise (invariant-label) and (b) frame reversal if the question is about event order (label-changing)? What do RR and TSS measure in each case? (Answer: (a) RR expects unchanged "blue"; (b) TSS expects change because the original label is invalid.)

- **Temporal inertia**  
  *Why needed:* Low TSS on order-sensitive items indicates the model’s predictions are unaffected by destroying temporal evidence, suggesting it ignores temporal structure and relies on static patterns (e.g., objects present, language priors). This is a specific failure mode not captured by clean accuracy.  
  *Quick check:* A model gets 90% Base accuracy on procedural knowledge questions but 0% TSS after frame shuffling. What does this imply about its reasoning? Is it likely using the video’s temporal information? (Answer: No; it relies on non-temporal shortcuts like language priors, showing temporal inertia.)

## Architecture Onboarding
INFACT’s pipeline consists of four main components:  
Taxonomy module -> Data pipeline -> Induction engine -> Metric calculator

**Critical path:**  
Define taxonomy → aggregate/filter data from multiple sources → curate order-sensitive subset → implement perturbation operators → run paired evaluations (Base + induced modes) → aggregate Resist Rate and Temporal Sensitivity Score.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Real vs. synthetic videos:* Synthetic videos allow precise control (e.g., physical law violations) but lack realism; real videos provide ecological validity but have noisier annotations.  
- *Operator selection:* Visual degradation preserves semantics but may still affect answerability; evidence corruption introduces textual conflicts to test cross-modal prioritization. The design prioritizes label preservation over ecological plausibility.  
- *Frame sampling:* Fixed 16-frame sampling balances cost and coverage; Figure 6 shows limited gains with more frames, suggesting temporal sensitivity is not primarily a sampling issue but a modeling one.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low RR<sub>cap</sub> (caption injection) but high RR<sub>gau</sub> (Gaussian noise): Model is distracted by misleading text but robust to perceptual noise.  
- Near-zero TSS on factuality order-sensitive items: Model exhibits temporal inertia, likely using static priors rather than temporal reasoning.  
- High Base accuracy but large gap to RR/TSS: Model success in clean setting relies on shortcuts that break under perturbation.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline reproducibility:** Run all 14 models in Base mode to replicate reported accuracy tables; verify that larger models (e.g., Qwen3VL-32B, Gemini) achieve top scores.  
2. **Operator-wise RR ablation:** For a mid-sized model (e.g., Qwen2.5VL-7B), compute RR separately for each operator (gau, mb, cmp, cap, sub, adv); confirm caption injection yields the lowest RR and evidence corruption operators generally underperform visual degradation.  
3. **TSS validation on order-sensitive subset:** Select a model with non-zero TSS (e.g., Qwen3VL-8B); manually inspect 10 Base-correct items where prediction changes after shuffling; ensure the change reflects disrupted temporal logic rather than video unintelligibility.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper identifies several unresolved questions:

**Open Question 1: Granular temporal perturbations**  
What temporal perturbations beyond global shuffling (e.g., clip-level reordering, event-level disruption) and localized cue analysis can precisely identify temporal grounding mechanisms in Video-LLMs? Current TSS only measures coarse sensitivity to global order destruction. Needed: finer-grained operators combined with probing methods (attention, attribution) to localize critical segments.

**Open Question 2: Fundamental reasons for factuality gap**  
Why does factuality consistently lag behind faithfulness, especially for procedural and physical reasoning? Is the gap due to insufficient training data, inability to perform causal reasoning, or distribution mismatch from synthetic videos? Needed: controlled ablations varying knowledge source and probing internal representations.

**Open Question 3: Causes of temporal inertia in factuality**  
What architectural or training factors cause near-zero TSS on factuality? Potential factors: positional encoding limitations, overfitting to dataset-specific templates, or bias toward static features. Needed: correlating TSS with model architecture, pretraining data diversity via systematic studies.

**Open Question 4: Frame sampling and temporal modeling**  
Why does increasing uniformly sampled frames (8–32) not consistently improve TSS or robustness? Uniform sampling may miss key events; models may not integrate long-range cues even with more frames. Needed: evaluation of adaptive/event-driven sampling and comparison to models with explicit temporal modeling.

**Open Question 5: Cross-modal conflict vs. general brittleness**  
To what extent do failures under evidence corruption reflect specific inability to resolve cross-modal conflicts versus general brittleness to any perturbation? Needed: matched perturbations that equally distort semantics but vary modality (e.g., visual adversarial patches vs. misleading subtitles), along with attention analysis.

## Limitations
- The full INFACT dataset and taxonomy are not publicly released; reproduction must approximate sources and filtering heuristics.  
- Perturbation parameters (e.g., noise variance, blur kernel, compression bitrate) lack exact specifications, requiring manual tuning to preserve semantics and label invariance.  
- The order-sensitive subset is described but not itemized; inclusion of non-order-dependent questions could invalidate TSS measurements.  
- Evaluation uses zero-shot prompting without standardized templates, introducing variability across models.

## Confidence
We assess confidence in the paper’s major claims:

- *Evidence corruption degrades stability more than visual degradation:* **High** – supported by human-audited label preservation and consistent experimental results.  
- *Temporal inertia in factuality (near-zero TSS):* **Medium** – depends on quality of order-sensitive subset; TSS validation on non-order-sensitive items is suggestive but subset not inspectable.  
- *Higher Base accuracy does not guarantee reliability:* **Medium-High** – paired design controls difficulty, but unmeasured differences in perturbation impact across categories could confound.

## Next Checks
1. **Label preservation audit:** Randomly sample 100 perturbed QA pairs per operator and have three annotators verify answer invariance; if preservation drops below 95%, retune operator strength and recompute RR.  
2. **Order-sensitive subset sanity check:** For a model with non-zero TSS (e.g., Qwen3VL-8B), compute TSS on non-order-sensitive items; TSS should be significantly lower on order-sensitive items, confirming subset curation.  
3. **Operator replication test:** Implement caption injection and frame shuffling exactly as described in Appendix A.5; run on a known-order-sensitive procedural video (e.g., "making coffee") and verify that Base-correct answers change after shuffling.
