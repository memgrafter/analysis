# Gauge Freedom and Metric Dependence in Neural Representation Spaces
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.06774  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06774  
- **Reference count:** 7  
- **Primary result:** Neural representation coordinates are not uniquely defined; invertible linear transformations preserve model predictions but distort metric-dependent quantities like cosine similarity and nearest-neighbor structure.

## Executive Summary
Neural network representations exhibit a gauge freedom: an invertible linear transformation applied to a hidden layer's output can be exactly compensated by an inverse transformation on the subsequent linear layer's weights, leaving the computed function unchanged. This paper demonstrates that common geometric metrics—such as cosine similarity and nearest-neighbor relationships—are not invariant under this freedom. Experiments on MLP and CNN hidden layers show substantial distortions in these metrics (mean absolute cosine change ~0.05–0.13) while predictions remain identical. The distortion increases with the condition number of the transformation matrix. Whitening the representation covariance offers a canonical coordinate choice that removes second-order anisotropy and stabilizes metric comparisons.

## Method Summary
The authors train a two-layer MLP on Digits and a small CNN on CIFAR-10. For a chosen hidden representation layer `h` and its linear readout `W`, they apply a random invertible matrix `D` to the representations (`h' = D h`) and adjust the readout weights to `W' = W D^{-1}` to preserve the network function. They then compare geometric properties of the original and transformed representations: mean absolute change in pairwise cosine similarity, Jaccard overlap of top-10 nearest neighbors (by cosine), and the effect of varying the condition number `κ(D)`. A whitening transformation (`D = Σ^{-1/2}`) is also evaluated as a canonical gauge.

## Key Results
- Mean absolute cosine distortion: **0.1328** on Digits, **0.0501** on CIFAR-10.
- Nearest-neighbor Jaccard@10 overlap reduces to **0.7209** (Digits) and **0.7228** (CIFAR-10) under random `D`.
- Distortion scales monotonically with the condition number `κ(D)` of the transformation.
- Whitening successfully normalizes representation covariance to identity (mean absolute eigenvalue deviation ~6e-6) and provides a stable canonical coordinate system.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The coordinates of neural representations are not uniquely defined; an invertible linear transformation applied to a hidden layer's output can be exactly compensated by an inverse transformation on the subsequent linear layer's weights, leaving the network's computed function unchanged.
- **Mechanism:** For representation `h(x)` and readout `W`, applying `h' = D h` and `W' = W D^{-1}` yields `W' h' = W h`. This defines a gauge symmetry under the action of `GL(d)`, meaning the representation space's coordinate system has a freedom that does not affect the model's predictions.
- **Core assumption:** The network contains a linear readout immediately following the representation layer being transformed (or can be re-parameterized to have one). The transformation `D` is invertible.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "If a hidden representation is transformed by an invertible linear map, the network function can be preserved by applying the inverse transformation to downstream weights."
  - [section 3, Prop 1]: Formal statement and proof of representation gauge symmetry for `y = W h(x)`.
  - [corpus]: Weak direct corpus evidence for this exact linear compensation mechanism; neighbor papers discuss related gauge-equivariance but not this specific linear readout compensation.
- **Break condition:** If non-linear operations (e.g., non-linear activations, batch norm) exist between the transformed layer and the final linear readout, a simple `D` and `W'` compensation may not preserve the function exactly. The mechanism assumes the transformation is applied to a layer that is immediately followed by a linear readout or that all downstream operations can be correspondingly adjusted.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Metric-dependent quantities, such as cosine similarity, are not invariant under this gauge freedom. Their values can change substantially while the network's predictions remain identical.
- **Mechanism:** Under `u -> D u`, `v -> D v`, the Euclidean inner product becomes `u^T (D^T D) v`, defining a new metric tensor `G = D^T D`. Cosine similarity `cos(u,v) = (u^T v)/(||u|| ||v||)` is computed relative to a fixed Euclidean metric. Under the transformed metric induced by `G`, the "angular" relationship changes because the notion of angle and norm depends on `G`.
- **Core assumption:** The gauge transformation `D` is not orthogonal (`D^T D != I`). An orthogonal transformation preserves the Euclidean metric and thus cosine similarity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4]: Explicit derivation showing cosine similarity depends on `G = D^T D` and is generally not invariant.
  - [section 7.1 & 7.2]: Experimental results showing `mean |Δ cos| = 0.1328` (Digits) and `0.0501` (CIFAR-10) under applied `D`.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor paper "Fubini Study geometry of representation drift" [arxiv:2602.02596] directly addresses how standard distance measures entangle coordinate changes with intrinsic drift, supporting the premise that common metrics are coordinate-dependent.
- **Break condition:** If `D` is orthogonal (`D^T D = I`), cosine similarity is preserved. The distortion magnitude scales with the condition number `κ(D)` (experiment 7.3 confirms this).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Whitening the representation distribution (transforming to unit covariance) provides a canonical coordinate choice (a "gauge fix") that removes second-order anisotropy and defines a standardized metric.
- **Mechanism:** Let `Σ = E[ h h^T ]` be the representation covariance. The transformation `D = Σ^{-1/2}` maps the distribution to have identity covariance (`E[ (D h) (D h)^T ] = I`). This fixes the metric structure by aligning the coordinate axes with the principal components of equal variance, removing scaling differences between dimensions.
- **Core assumption:** The covariance `Σ` is positive definite and invertible. This gauge choice is non-unique if the representation distribution is degenerate.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.1]: Defines whitening `D = Σ^{-1/2}` as a gauge choice mapping covariance to identity.
  - [section 7.4]: Experimental result showing post-whitening eigenvalues collapse to ~1 with mean absolute deviation `~6e-6`.
  - [abstract]: "...whitening provides a canonical coordinate choice by normalizing representation covariance."
  - [corpus]: Limited direct corpus evidence for whitening as *the* canonical gauge for this `GL(d)` freedom; neighbor papers discuss other canonicalizations.
- **Break condition:** Whitening relies on a second-order statistic (covariance) and does not fix higher-order geometric structure or non-linear relationships. A different canonical choice (e.g., based on sparsity or alignment with a feature basis) might be more appropriate for some analyses.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Linear Algebra - Change of Basis & Inner Products**
  - **Why needed here:** The core mechanism (`h' = D h`) is a change of basis in the vector space `R^d`. Understanding how inner products (`u^T v`) and norms (`||u||`) transform under a non-orthogonal change of basis is essential to see why cosine similarity changes. The metric tensor `G = D^T D` is the key object.
  - **Quick check question:** If `D = [[2, 0], [0, 1]]` is applied to vectors `u=[1,0]^T` and `v=[1,1]^T`, what are their new Euclidean norms and new dot product? How does `cos(u,v)` compare before and after?
- **Concept: Geometric Invariance & Gauge Freedom**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper frames representation coordinates as having a "gauge" freedom (like in physics, where certain coordinate choices don't affect observables). The engineer must distinguish between *gauge-dependent* properties (coordinates, Euclidean distances) and *gauge-invariant* properties (the final network function `f_θ(x)`, prediction class).
  - **Quick check question:** Is the value of a specific neuron's activation (e.g., "unit 5 in layer 3") gauge-invariant? Is the classification probability for a given input gauge-invariant?
- **Concept: Neural Network Layer Composition & Readouts**
  - **Why needed here:** The gauge freedom mechanism (`h' = D h`, `W' = W D^{-1}`) specifically applies to a linear readout `W` immediately following the transformed representation `h`. Understanding the linear algebra of sequential layers (`y = W h`) is required to see the compensation.
  - **Quick check question:** If the layer after `h` is a non-linear activation `σ(W h + b)`, can a simple compensation `W' = W D^{-1}` preserve the output for all `h`? Why or why not?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Representation Layer (h) -> Linear Readout (W) -> [Gauge Module (D) inserted here] -> Compensating Readout (W') -> Network Output
- **Critical path:** Insert `D` between representation layer `h` and its linear readout `W`. Immediately replace `W` with `W' = W D^{-1}`. Do not change any other layers or non-linearities for a clean experiment. The forward pass becomes `y = W' (D h)`.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Distortion vs. Canonical Choice*: Applying an arbitrary `D` (high `κ`) maximizes distortion to demonstrate gauge dependence. Whitening (`D = Σ^{-1/2}`) minimizes distortion relative to the data distribution but imposes a specific canonical metric (unit covariance). Choose `D` based on goal: test sensitivity (arbitrary/high-`κ`) or standardize for comparison (whitening).
  - *Scope of Transformation*: Applying `D` to a single layer isolates its gauge effect. Applying to multiple layers creates a more complex overall transformation but risks breaking the simple `W'` compensation if non-linearities intervene between transformed layers.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1.  **Prediction change:** If model predictions (`argmax y`) change after applying `(D, W')`, the compensation failed. Likely cause: `W` is not the immediate linear readout (a non-linearity or bias term exists between `h` and `W`), or `D` is not invertible/implementation error.
  2.  **Unexpected stabilization:** If cosine similarity remains unchanged for a non-orthogonal `D`, check if `D` is accidentally orthogonal (e.g., random orthogonal matrix) or if the representation has a specific structure (e.g., isotropic covariance) that makes `D^T D` proportional to identity.
  3.  **NaN/Inf values:** High condition number `κ(D)` can cause numerical instability in `D^{-1}` computation, especially if `Σ^{-1/2}` for whitening is ill-conditioned. Use regularization (e.g., `Σ + εI`) for whitening.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Sanity Check (Digits MLP):** Train a simple 2-layer MLP on Digits dataset. Extract hidden layer `h` and linear readout `W`. Apply random invertible `D` (e.g., `D = L * U` from LU decomposition of random matrix). Replace `W` with `W' = W D^{-1}`. Verify: identical predictions, measure `mean |Δ cos|` and `Jaccard@10` on test set hidden states.
  2.  **Scale to CNN (CIFAR-10):** Repeat on a small CNN (e.g., 3 conv layers + FC) on CIFAR-10. Apply `D` to penultimate-layer features. Verify functional invariance and report distortion metrics. Compare `mean |Δ cos|` to Digits to see effect of model/dataset scale.
  3.  **Gauge Strength Sweep:** Systematically vary `κ(D)` (condition number) from ~1 (orthogonal) to high (e.g., 100). For each, apply transformation and plot `mean |Δ cos|` and `Jaccard@10` vs. `κ`. Confirm distortion monotonically increases with `κ`.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How does gauge freedom, as defined by invertible linear transformations, affect representation geometry in large-scale transformer architectures with residual connections and layer normalization?  
  *Basis in paper:* [explicit] In Section 8, the authors state: "A limitation of the present study is that the experiments focus on controlled gauge transformations inserted at a single hidden layer in relatively small models. Larger transformer architectures, residual pathways, and normalization layers may introduce additional structure into the choice of practical representation gauges."
- **Open Question 2:** To what extent do gauge-dependent representation metrics like cosine similarity impact the performance or reliability of downstream tasks that rely on nonlinear similarity measures or complex decision rules?  
  *Basis in paper:* [explicit] Section 8 identifies a limitation: "we focus primarily on second-order geometry and cosine-based retrieval structure rather than downstream tasks that use more complex nonlinear similarities."
- **Open Question 3:** What geometric properties of neural representations are fully invariant under general linear (GL(d)) gauge transformations, and can such invariants completely characterize representation equivalence for practical analysis?  
  *Basis in paper:* [inferred] The paper emphasizes the gauge dependence of cosine similarity and Euclidean metrics, and suggests focusing on invariant quantities or canonical coordinates. It mentions methods like SVCCA and CKA as approximations to gauge-invariant observables (Section 5.3) but does not develop a systematic theory of invariants under GL(d).
- **Open Question 4:** Is whitening (covariance whitening) a universally appropriate canonical gauge for comparing neural representations across different architectures, training regimes, or layer types?  
  *Basis in paper:* [inferred] Section 4.1 proposes whitening (applying D = Σ⁻¹/²) as a canonical gauge that maps representation covariance to identity, but this suggestion is not empirically validated across diverse settings. The paper does not discuss whether isotropy is always the desired property or how whitening interacts with architectural specifics.

## Limitations
- **Model specs incomplete:** Exact architectures/hyperparameters omitted; requires assumptions for reproduction.
- **Transformation details sparse:** How to sample invertible matrices with target condition number not described.
- **Non-linearities not addressed:** Gauge compensation may fail with intervening activations or batchnorm; paper assumes immediate linear readout.
- **Whitening not uniquely justified:** Other canonicalizations (e.g., sparsity, semantic alignment) are not considered.
- **Limited experimental scope:** Only Digits & CIFAR-10 with shallow nets; deeper models and large-scale data untested.

## Confidence
- **Gauge compensation mechanism:** High. Proof given and functional invariance confirmed in experiments.
- **Metric dependence (cosine, Jaccard):** High. Results robust across datasets; distortion scales with κ.
- **Whitening as canonical gauge:** Medium. Empirically effective, but uniqueness/optimality not established.
