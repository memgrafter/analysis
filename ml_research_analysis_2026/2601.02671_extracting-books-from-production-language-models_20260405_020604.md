# Extracting books from production language models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2601.02671
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02671
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Production LLMs can be prompted to extract near-verbatim portions of copyrighted books, with success rates varying significantly across model types and requiring jailbreaks for some.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the risk of extracting copyrighted book content from production large language models (LLMs) despite built-in safeguards. The authors develop a two-phase extraction method: first, an initial feasibility probe that may employ a Best-of-N (BoN) jailbreak to bypass alignment filters; second, iterative continuation prompts to progressively regenerate the book. Extraction success is measured by *nv-recall*, a block-based longest common substring metric that tolerates minor formatting differences. Experiments on four leading LLMs (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok 3) and a set of copyrighted books reveal that Gemini and Grok comply without jailbreaking, yielding high nv-recall (e.g., 76.8% and 70.3% for *Harry Potter and the Sorcerer’s Stone*). Jailbroken Claude also achieves near-verbatim extraction (up to 95.8% nv-recall), while GPT-4.1 resists and yields low extraction (4.0%). The findings demonstrate that memorized text remains extractable in production models, raising concerns about copyright enforcement.

## Method Summary
The extraction procedure consists of two phases. **Phase 1** attempts to induce the model to continue from a short seed prefix (the first sentence of a book) using a clean instruction. For models that refuse (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4.1), the authors apply a Best-of-N (BoN) jailbreak: they generate *N* perturbed variants of the instruction (e.g., capitalization changes, symbol substitutions) and submit each; the first response that contains a suffix matching the reference book with a longest common substring similarity ≥0.6 is accepted. **Phase 2** begins once a compliant response is obtained. The model is repeatedly queried with a simple “Continue” instruction, each time appending its prior output to the context. This loop continues until the model refuses, emits a stop phrase (e.g., “THE END”), or a query budget is exhausted. The full generated text is then compared to the original book using *nv-recall*: a two-stage algorithm that first finds exact contiguous word matches of at least 20 words, merges nearby blocks with small gaps (τ_gap=2, τ_align=1), then relaxes merging (τ_gap=10, τ_align=3) and filters for final blocks of at least 100 words. *nv-recall* is the proportion of matched words relative to the total words in the book.

## Key Results
- Gemini 2.5 Pro and Grok 3 extracted large portions without any jailbreaking, achieving nv-recall scores of 76.8% and 70.3% respectively on *Harry Potter and the Sorcerer’s Stone*.
- Jailbroken Claude 3.7 Sonnet produced near‑verbatim copies for some books, with a maximum nv-recall of 95.8% (*The Great Gatsby*).
- GPT-4.1 required many BoN attempts and often refused to continue after a few turns, resulting in low extraction (e.g., 4.0% nv-recall on *Harry Potter*).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Best-of-N jailbreak evades alignment safeguards.** By generating multiple semantically equivalent perturbations of a jailbreak instruction (e.g., altered capitalization, symbol substitutions), the BoN approach increases the chance that at least one variant bypasses pattern‑based filters. The core assumption is that alignment safeguards are not robust to small, innocuous changes. A response is accepted if it contains a suffix with longest common substring similarity ≥0.6 to the target book; if none of the *N* trials succeed, the attack fails.

**Mechanism 2: Iterative continuation exploits memorized sequences.** Once the model is induced to produce an initial seed continuation, repeatedly prompting “Continue” leverages the model’s autoregressive nature to regenerate memorized text token‑by‑token. The model will keep generating as long as the context matches a memorized span and no guardrails trigger. The loop halts when the model refuses, emits a known stop phrase, or the query budget is exhausted.

**Mechanism 3: Block‑based longest common substring (nv-recall) captures near-verbatim extraction.** The metric first identifies all exact contiguous word matches of at least 20 words, then merges blocks separated by very short gaps (≤2 words with alignment ≤1) and filters. A second, more relaxed merge (gaps ≤10, alignment ≤3) followed by a stringent 100‑word minimum block filter yields a conservative estimate of extracted text. This design tolerates minor formatting noise while avoiding false positives from brief coincidental matches.

## Foundational Learning
**Memorization vs. Extraction**: Distinguishes a model’s internal encoding of training data from the ability to reproduce it in outputs. *Why needed*: The paper’s claim hinges on actual extraction, not mere memorization. *Quick check*: If a model generates a 200‑word passage identical to a training book, does that prove the book was in the training set? What additional evidence would strengthen the claim?

**Jailbreaking as Adversarial Prompting**: Techniques like Best-of-N that craft inputs to circumvent alignment safeguards. *Why needed*: Extraction depends on bypassing refusals; understanding BoN’s mechanics explains why some models require it. *Quick check*: Why might a simple character‑substitution perturbation (e.g., “C0ntinuE”) evade a keyword‑based content filter whereas the clean instruction fails?

**Long‑form Evaluation Metrics for Text Generation**: Metrics that assess reproduction of lengthy text sequences, accounting for minor noise. *Why needed*: Standard verbatim match or edit distance fail for multi‑thousand‑token generations with formatting differences; nv‑recall is a custom solution. *Quick check*: Why is a 100‑word minimum block length chosen for the final filter, and how would lowering it to 20 words risk false positives?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Seed Provider -> Jailbreak Module -> Continuation Loop -> Guardrail Detector -> Evaluation Engine.

**Critical path**: Phase 1 must succeed (either direct compliance or BoN jailbreak) to enter Phase 2. Phase 2 accumulates generations until a stop condition. If Phase 1 fails, the experiment terminates with no extraction.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Jailbreak budget N vs. cost*: Larger *N* increases success chance but linearly increases API calls; the authors cap *N* at 10,000.
- *Per‑turn max tokens vs. guardrails*: Shorter generations may evade filters but increase turn count and cost.
- *Evaluation strictness*: Two‑stage merge/filter reduces false positives but may miss fragmented or out‑of‑order extractions (false negatives).

**Failure signatures**:
- Phase 1 failure: No BoN response yields similarity ≥0.6 → nv‑recall cannot be computed.
- Phase 2 early halt: Model refuses or emits stop phrase quickly (e.g., GPT‑4.1 often after first chapter) → low matched text.
- Evaluation anomaly: High *additional* (non‑verbatim text) with low *m* suggests paraphrasing rather than extraction.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline direct probe**: Submit clean instruction + seed prefix to all LLMs. Record which comply (Gemini, Grok) versus refuse (Claude, GPT‑4.1).
2. **BoN sensitivity test**: For refusing models, run BoN with *N*=100, 1k, 10k on a single book (e.g., *Harry Potter 1*) to log success rate vs. *N* and find the breakpoint.
3. **Continuation tolerance sweep**: For a complying model, vary max tokens per response (1000, 2000, 4000) to observe when guardrails trigger and how nv‑recall changes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Copyright significance of non‑verbatim but substantially similar output** – The paper observed thousands of words of additional text that were not near‑verbatim but may still be infringing due to plot/character similarity. Legal and technical implications remain unquantified.
2. **Generalizability to other books** – Experiments covered only 14 books, biased toward those highly memorized by a reference model. It is unclear whether high extraction rates for a few popular books reflect a broader pattern or are outliers.
3. **Standardized cross‑LLM comparison** – Authors tuned generation parameters per model, so differences in extraction rates may stem from probing methodology rather than inherent model properties. A uniform protocol is needed to compare models fairly.
4. **Total volume of memorized text** – The two‑phase continuation method likely under‑counts memorization, as alternative prompts or decoding settings may reveal more. A comprehensive union estimate across diverse extraction techniques is lacking.

## Limitations
- **Transparency gaps**: The paper does not fully disclose per‑LLM generation settings (temperature, max tokens, penalties), exact seed prefixes, BoN perturbation implementation, or error‑handling details, hindering exact replication.
- **Limited corpus**: Only 11 copyrighted and 2 public‑domain books were tested, with selection biased toward high memorization in a single reference model, limiting generalizability.
- **Unvalidated metric**: The nv‑recall score is an ad‑hoc heuristic without human annotation to assess its precision/recall; thresholds (e.g., 100‑word block) are arbitrary and may introduce false negatives/positives.

## Confidence
- **Extraction feasibility**: Medium (evidence present but key parameters missing)
- **Model‑specific success ordering**: Medium (consistent yet narrow corpus)
- **nv-recall as a measure of near-verbatim extraction**: Low (ad‑hoc, unvalidated)
- **BoN jailbreak effectiveness**: Medium (shown but sensitive to *N* and threshold)

## Next Checks

2. Conduct a human annotation study on a stratified sample of extracted passages to evaluate nv‑recall's precision/recall and adjust its merge/filter thresholds accordingly.
3. Repeat the extraction pipeline on a larger, diverse book set (e.g., 100+ titles across genres) and on the latest model versions to test robustness and generalizability.
