# From Feature-Based Models to Generative AI: Validity Evidence for Constructed Response Scoring

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19280
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19280
- **Reference count:** 33
- **Primary result:** Validity arguments for generative AI scoring require extensive, ongoing evidence beyond simple concordance metrics due to transparency and reproducibility challenges, with LLMs showing moderate agreement with human scores (QWK 0.52–0.73) versus higher agreement for traditional feature-based AI (e.g., e-rater QWK 0.84).

## Executive Summary
This paper examines the validity evidence necessary when using generative AI (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini) to score constructed responses, contrasting it with traditional feature-based automated scoring (e.g., e-rater). The authors extend standard validity frameworks to address generative AI’s unique challenges: lack of transparency, reproducibility issues, and absence of a direct human-rating link. They collect evidence across multiple domains—prompting strategies, reproducibility, fairness, rationale analysis, and comparative accuracy—using the PERSUADE 2.0 essay corpus. Findings show high intra-LLM consistency (QWK >0.95) but only moderate agreement with human ratings for most LLMs, which lags behind e-rater. Subgroup fairness analyses reveal disparities, such as a standardized mean difference of ~–0.20 for Asian/Pacific Islander students versus human ratings. The study concludes that generative AI scoring demands more rigorous and multifaceted validity arguments than conventional methods.

## Method Summary
The study scores 13,032 argumentative essays (grades 6–12) from the PERSUADE 2.0 corpus using zero-shot prompting with GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5/2.5 Pro (temperature=0, no fine-tuning). Each LLM receives a prompt specifying the rubric, the essay, and a request for a score (1–6) and a two-sentence rationale. Scores are compared against human adjudicated ratings and scores from the feature-based e-rater engine. Validity evidence is gathered across five areas: (1) construct alignment via prompt design and rationale analysis; (2) reproducibility through duplicate scoring of a holdout set; (3) fairness via subgroup metrics (standardized mean difference, quadratic weighted kappa); (4) sensitivity to atypical responses using e-rater advisories; and (5) accuracy correlations with human and e-rater scores, including partial correlations controlling for essay length. The e-rater system, trained on half the data, uses ten macrofeatures (e.g., grammar, organization) in a regression model.

## Key Results
- High intra-LLM consistency: Quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) between duplicate runs exceeded 0.95 for all tested LLMs.
- Moderate LLM-human agreement: Gemini 2.5 Pro achieved QWK=0.73; GPT-4o ranged from QWK=0.52 to 0.53, both below e-rater’s QWK of 0.84.
- Fairness disparities: GPT-4o showed a standardized mean difference (SMD) of approximately –0.20 for Asian/Pacific Islander students compared to human ratings, indicating systematic under-scoring relative to other groups.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Pre-training Data Documentation
- **Claim:** Validity requires documented pre-training data sources to mitigate unknown bias risks.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs are trained on vast, uncurated corpora. Without transparency, potential biases may propagate into scores. The validity argument mandates a defensible LLM selection process based on disclosed data domains (e.g., CommonCrawl, GitHub) and alignment with the target population.
- **Core assumption:** Pre-training data composition influences output behavior; mismatches introduce construct-irrelevant variance.
- **Evidence anchors:** Paper cites LLM technical reports (e.g., Qwen’s 36 trillion tokens) but provides no direct corpus analysis of data-population alignment.
- **Break condition:** No public documentation of pre-training data or a gross mismatch between sources and assessment context (e.g., scoring Chinese essays with an English-dominated LLM without fine-tuning).

### Mechanism 2: Reproducibility Monitoring
- **Claim:** Validity requires reproducibility studies due to non-deterministic outputs.
- **Mechanism:** LLM outputs vary due to probabilistic sampling, API changes, and model drift. Evidence must demonstrate acceptable intra-LLM consistency (e.g., QWK >0.95) and monitor temporal stability with fixed response sets.
- **Core assumption:** Score reliability is compromised if identical inputs yield divergent outputs; consistency is prerequisite for fair interpretation.
- **Evidence anchors:** Empirical QWK values: GPT-4o=0.96, Gemini 1.5 Pro=0.97 with no differences exceeding 1 point on the 1–6 scale.
- **Break condition:** Intra-LLM QWK falls below operational thresholds (e.g., <0.90) or systematic drift occurs without mitigation (e.g., averaging runs).

### Mechanism 3: Construct Alignment via Prompting and Rationales
- **Claim:** Validity requires explicit construct alignment evidence because there is no implicit human-rating link.
- **Mechanism:** Without human-annotated training, the LLM’s scoring process must be externally validated via: (1) prompting strategies (role, step-by-step, chain-of-thought); (2) analysis of generated rationales for rubric reference; (3) sensitivity checks that scores correlate with expected features but not spurious signals (e.g., length).
- **Core assumption:** The prompt and output inspection must confirm the LLM isApplying the rubric rather than exploiting superficial patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:** Rationales mentioned rubric elements but with inconsistent wording; LLM scores correlated with e-rater features but showed weaker length sensitivity than human ratings.
- **Break condition:** Rationales omit rubric criteria, or score-feature relationships indicate reliance on construct-irrelevant signals (e.g., strong length dependence absent in target construct).

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Validity Framework (AERA/APA/NCME Standards)**
  - **Why needed:** The paper extends standard validity evidence categories (internal structure, response processes, fairness) to address generative AI’s opacity. Knowing the framework clarifies why additional evidence is required compared to feature-based AI.
  - **Quick check:** What are the five primary sources of validity evidence per the Standards, and which become more critical when the scoring mechanism is a black-box LLM?

- **Concept: Feature-Based Automated Scoring (e-rater example)**
  - **Why needed:** Contrasts with generative AI to highlight what is lost: a transparent chain from handcrafted features to a human-rating proxy. This baseline clarifies the validity gap generative AI must bridge.
  - **Quick check:** How does e-rater’s use of macrofeatures and training on human scores create a validity chain that generative AI lacks?

- **Concept: LLM Prompt Engineering for Scoring**
  - **Why needed:** Prompting is the primary control for imposing task structure on a generative LLM without fine-tuning. Strategies like role assignment and chain-of-thought directly impact score validity.
  - **Quick check:** How does requesting a "rationale" serve both as an explainability tool and as a validity check for construct alignment?

- **Concept: Fairness Metrics for AI Scores (SMD, QWK by subgroup)**
  - **Why needed:** The paper emphasizes fairness checks are necessary but insufficient alone; LLM biases are opaque, so disparities must be coupled with bias investigation. Standard metrics flag disparities but root causes are harder to diagnose.
  - **Quick check:** Why is a subgroup SMD of –0.20 a red flag, and what subsequent analyses would you run to understand its source?

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:
Input (essay + rubric) -> Prompt Template -> LLM Service (API) -> Output Processor (JSON parse) -> Score & Rationale -> Evaluation Suite (Reproducibility, Alignment, Fairness, Rationale Analyzer) -> Human Review Interface

Critical path:
1. Prompt Design & Validation: Draft prompt with role, step-by-step rubric, rationale request. Pilot on ~50 essays; SME review rationales.
2. Reproducibility Check: Score stable set (N≥500) twice with temp=0; compute intra-LLM QWK. If <0.95, investigate API version or average runs.
3. Construct Alignment: Correlate LLM scores with human and e-rater; compute partial correlation controlling for length. If LLM-human QWK<0.70, refine prompt or consider ICL/fine-tuning.
4. Fairness Audit: Compute SMD by subgroups (race/ethnicity, gender, L1). Flag |SMD|>0.10; analyze e-rater feature distributions and rationale embeddings.
5. Atypical Response Test: Score essays flagged by e-rater advisories; ensure no high scores for off-topic/garbled responses.

Design tradeoffs:
- Transparency vs. Performance: Chain-of-thought improves interpretability but increases cost/latency and may not mirror internal reasoning.
- Generalization vs. Fit: Zero-shot maximizes generalizability but may underperform fine-tuned/ICL models on specific constructs; fine-tuning risks overfitting.
- Consistency vs. Cost: Temperature=0 improves consistency but may reduce output diversity; averaging runs improves reliability but doubles API costs.

Failure signatures:
- Reproducibility failure: Intra-LLM QWK <0.90; score drift over weeks on fixed set.
- Alignment failure: LLM-human QWK <0.60; LLM scores strongly correlated with length (r>0.80) while human scores are not; rationales omit rubric criteria.
- Fairness failure: Subgroup SMD |Δ| >0.20; systematic under/over-scoring for demographics even after mean adjustment.
- Atypical response failure: LLM awards non-zero scores to clearly off-topic/garbled responses flagged by humans/advisories.

First 3 experiments:
1. Prompt variation study: Test zero-shot vs. few-shot vs. multi-trait specialization prompts on balanced sample (n=500). Measure QWK with humans and rationale-rubric cosine similarity. Select prompt maximizing alignment.
2. Reproducibility & drift benchmark: Score fixed set (n=1000) with temp=0. Repeat after 1 week and 1 month (same API version). Track QWK and mean changes. Document API versions.
3. Subgroup fairness deep dive: For flagged subgroups (|SMD|>0.10), analyze distribution of e-rater features (e.g., Mechanics, Word Count). Compare feature-score relationship slopes across subgroups. Sample low-scoring essays from flagged group; review rationales for biased language.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What reproducibility thresholds (e.g., minimum QWK across runs, limits on score shifts) should be established for operational LLM-based constructed response scoring?
2. To what extent do chain-of-thought rationales generated by LLMs faithfully represent the actual scoring decision process versus post-hoc justifications?
3. How sensitive are LLM-based constructed response scores to atypical or gaming responses (e.g., prompt injection, off-topic, code-switching), and what mitigation strategies are most effective?
4. What is the minimum level of human oversight required to maintain validity for LLM-based constructed response scoring in high-stakes contexts?
5. Does in-context learning (ICL) with exemplars improve the construct alignment and accuracy of LLM-based constructed response scoring compared to zero-shot prompting, and what are optimal ICL design parameters?

## Limitations
- Corpus and LLM specificity: Evidence is bound to PERSUADE 2.0 argumentative essays and tested LLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini); generalizability to other constructs/grade bands/models is unknown.
- Pre-training transparency: Reliance on published technical reports; no direct analysis of training data composition or its alignment with test-taker populations.
- Fairness causality: Subgroup disparities (e.g., SMD ~–0.20 for Asian/Pacific Islander students) are flagged but root causes remain opaque due to LLM black-box nature.

## Confidence
- Reproducibility requirement & evidence: High (empirical QWK >0.95 with temp=0).
- Construct alignment via prompting/rationales: Medium (rationales show rubric mentions but inconsistent wording; alignment inferred indirectly).
- Lower LLM-human concordance vs. e-rater: High (consistent across metrics, QWK 0.52–0.73 vs. 0.84).
- Pre-training data as validity anchor: Low (no direct corpus evidence; assertion based on theoretical risk).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-corpus validation:** Replicate study on at least two additional corpora (e.g., source-based summarization, short-answer math) to test generalizability of fairness findings and alignment patterns.
2. **Longitudinal stability audit:** Rescore fixed holdout set monthly for 6 months; track QWK drift and mean score changes against API version logs.
3. **Feature-disaggregation fairness deep dive:** For flagged subgroups, compute subgroup-specific slopes of LLM scores vs. e-rater macrofeatures (e.g., length, grammar). Identify whether LLM under-/over-weights features that humans differentially apply.
