# Inverse Contextual Bandits without Rewards: Learning from a Non-Stationary Learner via Suffix Imitation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03778
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03778
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A passive observer recovers the optimal policy without rewards by discarding an initial burn-in segment from a learner's trajectory and performing suffix imitation, achieving $\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$ predictive regret under a polynomial burn-in schedule when the learner has sublinear regret.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the inverse contextual bandit problem: a passive observer sees only context–action pairs generated by a learner that gradually shifts from exploration to exploitation, and must recover the optimal policy without seeing any rewards. The proposed solution is Two-Phase Suffix Imitation: discard an initial burn-in segment of length $T(N)$, then fit a linear scoring policy via empirical risk minimization on the remaining suffix data, treating the learner’s actions as noisy labels. Under a Dynamic Massart condition that the learner’s error probability after burn-in is below $1/2$, the observer’s predictive regret is bounded by $O(\sqrt{d \log K / (N-T(N))})$, and with a polynomial burn-in (e.g., $T(N)=\Theta(N^\alpha), 0<\alpha<1$) the rate becomes $\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$, matching the asymptotic efficiency of a reward-aware learner. Experiments with LinUCB and LinTS learners confirm a U-shaped error vs. burn-in exponent; aggressive burn-in ($T\approx N^{0.9}$) yields estimation errors approaching the learner’s own performance and far surpassing naive full-trajectory imitation.

## Method Summary
The observer processes a trajectory of $N$ context-action pairs from a contextual bandit learner. It first discards an initial burn-in segment of length $T(N)$, leveraging the insight that the learner’s actions become increasingly aligned with the optimal policy over time. The remaining suffix data of length $L(N)=N-T(N)$ is used to train a linear policy $\pi_\theta(x) = \arg\max_a \langle x_a, \theta \rangle$ via empirical risk minimization, where the learner’s actions are treated as noisy labels for the optimal actions. The method relies on two key assumptions: (1) the learner’s error probability $\eta(t)$ decreases over time and can be made less than $1/2$ after sufficient burn-in (Dynamic Massart condition), and (2) the optimal policy is realizable within the linear function class. The burn-in length $T(N)$ is a hyperparameter that balances bias (from label noise) and variance (from sample size); a polynomial schedule $T(N)=\Theta(N^\alpha)$ with $0<\alpha<1$ is shown to achieve optimal $\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{N}$ regret when the learner enjoys sublinear cumulative regret.

## Key Results
- The observer’s clean predictive risk satisfies $R(\pi) \leq (1-2\eta(T))^{-1} (R_{\text{noisy}}(\pi)-R_{\text{noisy}}(\pi^*)) + \tilde{O}(\sqrt{d \log K / L(N)})$ under the Dynamic Massart condition $\eta(T)<1/2$.
- With polynomial burn-in $T(N)=\Theta(N^\alpha), 0<\alpha<1$, and a learner with sublinear regret (e.g., $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})$), the predictive regret becomes $\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$, matching the minimax rate for contextual bandits.
- Empirical evaluation shows a U-shaped relationship between estimation error and burn-in exponent $\alpha$; aggressive burn-in ($\alpha\approx0.9$) reduces error close to the learner’s own performance and significantly outperforms naive imitation ($T=0$).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method’s success hinges on three interrelated mechanisms. First, discarding the initial burn-in segment exploits the learner’s non-stationarity: the learner starts with high exploration error $\eta(t)$ that decreases over time as it shifts toward exploitation. By removing the prefix where $\eta(t)$ is large, the observer eliminates data where the learner’s action is a poor proxy for the optimal action, yielding a suffix with bounded error $\eta(T)<1/2$. This creates a bias-variance trade-off: larger $T$ reduces label noise (bias) but shrinks the suffix sample size $L(N)$ (variance). The optimal trade-off is achieved when $T(N)$ is a polynomial fraction of $N$.

Second, under the post-burn-in Dynamic Massart condition ($\eta(T)<1/2$), treating the learner’s actions as noisy labels enables a linear reduction of clean risk from noisy imitation risk. Specifically, the Massart transfer inequality guarantees that the excess risk of the policy learned on the noisy targets is at most $(1-2\eta(T))^{-1}$ times the excess risk on the true optimal actions. Empirical risk minimization on the suffix thus yields a policy whose clean risk is controlled by the Natarajan dimension of the linear class ($O(d \log K)$) and the suffix size $L(N)$.

Third, when the learner has sublinear cumulative regret (e.g., $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})$ for algorithms like LinUCB or LinTS), the error probability $\eta(T)$ decays as $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ under a uniform margin condition. Combining this with a polynomial burn-in schedule $T(N)=\Theta(N^\alpha)$ yields $\eta(T)=O(N^{-\alpha/2})$ and $L(N)=\Theta(N)$. Substituting into the risk bound gives a net $\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$ rate, provided $0<\alpha<1$. This matches the minimax optimal rate for contextual bandits, demonstrating that the observer can nearly match a reward-aware learner’s performance purely from observational data.

## Architecture Onboarding
The system comprises three sequential components: **Burn-in Discarder** selects a cutoff $T(N)$ and outputs the suffix dataset; **ERM Trainer** optimizes a linear policy $\pi_\theta$ via multiclass logistic loss on the suffix; **Policy Output** deploys the resulting $\tilde{\pi}(\cdot)=\arg\max_a \langle x_a, \tilde{\theta} \rangle$. The critical path is strictly sequential: the suffix quality determines ERM generalization, which dictates final policy quality.

Design tradeoffs involve: (1) **Burn-in length $T(N)$**: too small yields high label noise, too large yields insufficient samples; the polynomial exponent $\alpha$ balances these. (2) **Policy class**: Linear policies offer low Natarajan dimension $O(d \log K)$ but may be misspecified; nonlinear classes reduce bias but increase variance. (3) **Reward-free reliance**: The observer depends entirely on the learner’s improvement; if the learner does not decrease error, the method fails.

Failure signatures include: a U-shaped error curve versus $T(N)$ confirming the bias-variance trade-off; observer performance worse than naive imitation ($T=0$) indicating either non-improving learner or excessively large $T$; high variance in estimated parameters for large $T$ due to small suffix size.

First 3 experiments: (1) Sweep burn-in exponent $\alpha$ and plot predictive regret to identify the U-shape and optimal region. (2) Compare observer with best $\alpha$ and fixed $\alpha=0.9$ against the learner’s own policy and naive imitation to confirm superiority and asymptotic convergence. (3) Ablate over dimensions $d$ and arm counts $K$ to validate the $\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$ rate and Natarajan dimension dependence.

## Limitations
- The Dynamic Massart condition $\eta(T)<1/2$ may not hold for learners that maintain high exploration, breaking the theoretical guarantee.
- Burn-in length $T(N)$ must be chosen a priori; no adaptive method is provided for unknown $N$ or learner dynamics.
- The linear policy realizability assumption is strong; misspecification leads to irreducible bias not covered by the analysis.
- Empirical evaluation is limited to synthetic linear bandit learners (LinUCB/LinTS); real-world or non-linear scenarios remain untested.

## Confidence
- Suffix imitation under $\eta(T)<1/2$ yields valid clean risk bounds: **High**
- Polynomial burn-in achieves $\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$ for sublinear-regret learners: **Medium**
- Empirical U-shaped error and superiority over naive imitation: **High**
