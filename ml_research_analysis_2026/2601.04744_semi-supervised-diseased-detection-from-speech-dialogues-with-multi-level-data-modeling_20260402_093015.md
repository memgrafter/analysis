# Semi-Supervised Diseased Detection from Speech Dialogues with Multi-Level Data Modeling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.04744  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04744  
- **Reference count**: 16  
- **Primary result**: Achieves 90% of fully-supervised performance using only 10% labeled samples for depression detection and matches full-supervised performance with 30% labeled data across languages and conditions.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles diseased detection (depression and Alzheimer’s) from speech dialogues under weak, session-level supervision, where pathological traits are sparsely expressed in long, unsegmented recordings. It proposes a model-agnostic semi-supervised framework that jointly models frame-level, clip-level, and session-level representations. The method dynamically aggregates multi-granularity features and generates high-quality pseudo-labels online to leverage unlabeled data efficiently. Experiments show consistent gains over baselines, robustness to raw dialogues containing investigator speech, and near fully-supervised performance with only 10–30% labeled data across languages and conditions.

## Method Summary
The framework processes raw dialogues by splitting them into clips and extracting embeddings via a pre-trained audio encoder (e.g., wav2vec2). Three parallel pathways operate on these embeddings: (1) a session-level transformer attends over clip embeddings to produce a session representation for classification and to generate session pseudo-labels; (2) a clip-level Bi-LSTM predicts clip labels using pseudo-labels derived from session predictions; (3) a frame-level consistency regularization uses a teacher-student (EMA) setup to enforce similarity between embeddings of differently augmented views of the same session. The three losses (session cross-entropy, clip cross-entropy, frame mean-squared error) are weighted and summed for end-to-end training. Pseudo-labels for unlabeled data are refreshed every *k* steps after a warm-up period based on a confidence threshold, enabling a self-improving cycle.

## Key Results
- Achieves 90% of fully-supervised performance using only 10% labeled samples for depression detection.  
- Matches full-supervised performance using only 30% of labeled data across languages and conditions (depression and Alzheimer’s).  
- Demonstrates consistent gains over baselines, robust performance with varied audio encoders, and effective handling of raw dialogues containing investigator speech.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The framework’s effectiveness stems from three interacting mechanisms:

1. **Dynamic Multi-Granularity Aggregation**: The session-level transformer learns to weight clip embeddings when aggregating to a session representation, allowing the model to identify and emphasize clips containing pathological speech despite session-level supervision and sparse pathology. *Core assumption*: Pathological traits create a discriminative signal when correctly aggregated. *Evidence*: Explicit modeling of frame, segment, and session representations (abstract, section 3.1) and transformer over clips (section 3.1). *Break condition*: If pathological cues occupy only an infinitesimal fraction of clips, the transformer may fail to learn meaningful weights from session-level labels alone.

2. **Online Pseudo-Label Generation**: The model generates pseudo-labels for unlabeled sessions and clips based on prediction confidence and refreshes them periodically (every *k* steps after warm-up) to avoid error accumulation. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle where improved predictions yield higher-quality pseudo-labels. *Core assumption*: The model’s predictions become calibrated early enough that high-confidence pseudo-labels are mostly correct. *Evidence*: Online single-stage training (section 3.4) and Figure 3 showing pseudo-label accuracy increases over training. *Break condition*: If the initial model is too poor, early high-confidence pseudo-labels may be systematically wrong; a low threshold may admit many low-quality labels.

3. **Frame-Level Consistency Regularization**: A teacher-student (EMA) setup enforces that frame-level embeddings of two augmented views of the same session are similar. This regularizes the model to be invariant to nuisance variations and provides robustness against pseudo-label noise. *Core assumption*: Low-level acoustic features (e.g., vocal timbre) are more stable under augmentation than higher-level content features affected by pseudo-label errors. *Evidence*: Siamese architecture with MSE loss on frame embeddings (section 3.3) and the statement that frame-level component provides inherent robustness (section 5.1). *Break condition*: If augmentations are too destructive or pathological cues are tied to short-time events disrupted by augmentation, consistency could force the model to discard needed features.

## Foundational Learning
- **Semi-Supervised Learning (SSL) with Pseudo-Labeling**: *Why needed*: The core problem is data scarcity; SSL leverages large unlabeled clinical dialogues. Understanding confidence-thresholding and error accumulation risks is key. *Quick check*: What are two main risks of pseudo-labeling in low-data regimes, and how does this paper’s design (periodic re-labeling, multi-granularity) mitigate them?
- **Hierarchical/Multi-Granularity Representation Learning**: *Why needed*: Supervision is at session level, but pathology may be expressed at utterance or phonetic levels. The model must bridge this granularity gap without intermediate labels. *Quick check*: Why is treating each utterance as an independent sample flawed for this task?
- **Weak/Far-End Supervision in Sequence Modeling**: *Why needed*: This defines the inference challenge: mapping a single label to a long, variable-length sequence with sparse, non-uniform signal. *Quick check*: What is the fundamental difference between this and a standard sequence labeling task (e.g., POS tagging), and why does it preclude using standard per-token supervision?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:
Raw dialogue -> Clip segmentation -> Audio encoder (E) -> Clip embeddings -> 
   (a) Session Transformer -> Session prediction & session pseudo-labels
   (b) Clip Bi-LSTM -> Clip predictions (supervised by clip pseudo-labels derived from session)
   (c) Frame consistency: Student/Teacher copies of Session Transformer on augmented views -> MSE loss on session embeddings
Weighted sum of losses (session CE, clip CE, frame MSE) -> End-to-end training -> Periodic pseudo-label refresh (every *k* steps after warm-up) using confidence threshold.

**Critical path**:
1. Load a batch containing both labeled and unlabeled dialogues.
2. Split each dialogue into clips, extract clip embeddings using the audio encoder.
3. For the session path: process clip sequence through the transformer and pooling to obtain session prediction and embedding; compute cross-entropy loss on labeled sessions; generate/update pseudo-labels for unlabeled sessions based on confidence.
4. For the clip path: process clip embeddings through the Bi-LSTM; compute cross-entropy loss using clip-level pseudo-labels (assigned from session predictions) for all clips from unlabeled sessions.
5. For the frame path: create two augmented views of the full session; pass through student and teacher networks (copies of the session pipeline); compute MSE loss between the resulting session embeddings.
6. Sum the three weighted losses, backpropagate, update student parameters; periodically update teacher via EMA and re-evaluate all unlabeled pseudo-labels.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Computational cost vs. granularity: The triple pathway increases memory/computation ~3× but yields performance gains; clip-level RNN and frame-level EMA duplicate parts of the session pipeline.
- Pseudo-label threshold τ: High τ yields fewer but higher-quality pseudo-labels, slowing learning; low τ admits more data but risks error accumulation; requires tuning per dataset/encoder.
- Encoder trainability: Using a frozen pre-trained encoder reduces cost and avoids forgetting but limits adaptation; fine-tuning improves performance but needs regularization.
- Investigator speech handling: Processing raw dialogue (without diarization) simplifies the pipeline; results show robustness, but extreme investigator dominance could dilute patient signal.

**Failure signatures**:
- Pseudo-label collapse: Accuracy of pseudo-labels on a held-out labeled set decreases after a re-labeling step, indicating threshold too low or poor calibration.
- Component ablation gap: Removing frame-level loss causes a significant drop in low-data regimes (e.g., Table 2), confirming its role; if removal had no effect, it would be ineffective.
- Encoder mismatch: Using an English pre-trained encoder (WavLM) on Chinese data degrades performance as labeled data increases (Table 5), showing cross-lingual transfer failure.
- Poor convergence on session loss: If session-level cross-entropy on labeled data does not decrease, the core aggregation mechanism is failing, possibly due to insufficient capacity or class imbalance.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Component Isolation**: Train with only session-level (Base), session+clip, session+frame, and full (all three) on a small labeled set (e.g., 10% of EATD). Expect incremental gains, with full model best (Table 2 pattern), validating multi-granularity design.
2. **Pseudo-label Quality Tracking**: During training on unlabeled data, periodically evaluate pseudo-labels on the labeled portion. Plot accuracy over steps; expect upward trend confirming self-improvement (replicate Figure 3).
3. **Robustness to Raw Input**: Compare performance on ADReSSo21 using (a) only participant speech (after diarization) vs. (b) raw full dialogue (INV+PAR). Expect minimal drop (Table 6), validating avoidance of diarization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Multimodal integration**: Could incorporating textual (ASR transcripts) or visual modalities improve detection in low-data regimes while maintaining cross-lingual generalization? The Limitations section explicitly notes the audio-only design precludes leveraging other modalities, and this trade-off is not tested. Resolution: Extend the framework to include text/video, evaluate under varying labeled ratios, and assess cross-lingual transfer.
2. **Minimum labeled data for reliable pseudo-labeling**: What is the smallest labeled ratio that enables the pseudo-labeling mechanism to generate accurate labels, and how does it degrade under severe class imbalance? The paper tests down to 10% labeled data but does not explore extreme scarcity (<5%) or imbalance, despite noted imbalance in datasets. Resolution: Test labeled subsets below 10% and artificially imbalanced distributions, tracking pseudo-label precision/recall and Macro F1 to find breakdown points.
3. **Cross-lingual encoder adaptation**: Can cross-lingual transfer of pre-trained audio encoders be improved within this framework, and does multi-granularity training mitigate encoder mismatch? Table 5 shows English WavLM underperforms on Chinese data as labels increase, highlighting a mismatch. Resolution: Apply cross-lingual adaptation (e.g., fine-tuning on target-language speech, adapters) and compare with language-matched encoders; analyze whether session/clip/frame losses reduce the disparity.

## Limitations
- The exact method for deriving clip-level pseudo-labels from session predictions is unspecified, likely introducing noise when pathology is localized.
- The assumption that pathological traits are sparsely but discriminatively expressed may break if cues occupy only an infinitesimal fraction of clips; this extreme sparsity is untested.
- Cross-lingual transfer using English pre-trained encoders degrades on Chinese data, indicating language mismatch limits SSL gains if not addressed.
- Robustness to raw dialogues was evaluated only on ADReSSo; extreme investigator speech dominance could overwhelm patient signal.

## Next Checks
1. **Pseudo-label calibration study**: Compute expected calibration error (ECE) and class-wise accuracy of pseudo-labels on a held-out labeled set at each re-labeling interval to confirm increasing reliability and detect error propagation.
2. **Extreme investigator proportion test**: Construct test sets with controlled investigator speech ratios (e.g., 50%, 80%) from existing corpora or by splicing; measure F1 drop to establish robustness boundaries.
3. **Language adaptation experiment**: Pre-train the audio encoder on target-language unlabeled speech (self-supervised) before SSL fine-tuning to assess if encoder alignment resolves cross-lingual performance drops.
