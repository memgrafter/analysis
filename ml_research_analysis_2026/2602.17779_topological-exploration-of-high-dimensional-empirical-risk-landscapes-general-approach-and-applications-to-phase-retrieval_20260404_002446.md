# Topological Exploration of High-Dimensional Empirical Risk Landscapes: general approach, and applications to phase retrieval

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.17779  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17779  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: The BBP‑Kac‑Rice method reveals that typical local minima become unstable before trivialization, linking landscape geometry to optimization dynamics in phase retrieval.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the empirical risk landscape of phase retrieval in high dimensions. Using the Kac‑Rice formula, the authors simplify earlier variational complexities for critical points to tractable scalar optimizations, yielding explicit predictions for the joint label distribution and Hessian spectral density. By combining with Baik‑Ben Arous‑Péché (BBP) random matrix theory, they introduce the BBP‑Kac‑Rice method to detect when local minima develop an instability aligned with the signal, which occurs before the trivialization threshold. Gradient descent simulations at dimension 512 confirm these predictions: many predicted minima are actually unstable, explaining the observed optimization behavior. The work provides a complete phase diagram for phase retrieval, connecting landscape topology to algorithmic success.

## Method Summary
The authors employ the Kac‑Rice formula to count critical points in the high‑dimensional loss landscape of phase retrieval, reducing the asymptotic complexity to scalar optimization problems. The Hessian’s limiting spectral density is obtained via the Marchenko‑Pastur equation. To identify unstable minima, they analyze the Hessian’s low‑rank structure and apply the BBP transition, deriving a condition for an outlier eigenvalue aligned with the signal. Theoretical predictions are validated against gradient descent simulations using a pinned‑overlap protocol to mitigate finite‑size effects, showing agreement in energy bands, Hessian spectra, and label distributions.

## Key Results
- The Kac‑Rice complexities for critical points, saddles, and local minima are reduced to finite‑dimensional scalar variational problems, making them computationally tractable.
- BBP‑Kac‑Rice analysis shows that typical local minima acquire a signal‑aligned instability at $\alpha_{\text{BBP}}\approx3.8$ (for $q=0$), well before the trivialization threshold $\alpha_{\text{triv}}\approx7.5$, meaning many predicted minima are actually unstable.
- Gradient descent simulations at $d=512$ confirm the theoretical energy band, Hessian spectral density, and joint label distribution; the empirical algorithmic transition aligns with the BBP‑Kac‑Rice instability threshold.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Kac‑Rice Simplification to Scalars
**Claim**: The asymptotic complexity (expected log‑number) of critical points, saddles of sub‑extensive index, and an upper bound for local minima can be reduced from variational problems over probability measures to tractable scalar optimizations over a finite set of Lagrange multipliers and auxiliary parameters (like the Stieltjes transform of the Hessian).  
**Mechanism**: Applying the Kac‑Rice formula to count critical points in a high‑dimensional, Gaussian‑index model reduces the problem to an expectation over the empirical distribution of labels $u_i = (y_i, y_i^\star)$. Under the proportional limit $n/d \to \alpha$, the asymptotic spectral density of the Hessian becomes deterministic via the Marchenko–Pastur equation. Large deviations (Sanov’s theorem) then turn the optimization over the empirical distribution into a variational problem over a reference measure $\mu_q$, with linear constraints imposed by moments of the loss, gradient, and Hessian‑related quantities. These are enforced via Lagrange multipliers, yielding an explicit finite‑dimensional optimization.  
**Core assumption**: The data vectors are i.i.d. Gaussian; the total number of critical points grows at most exponentially in $d$; the asymptotic spectral distribution of the Hessian is captured by the Marchenko–Pastur equation; the annealed (first moment) complexity dominates or provides a tight bound (especially for the local minima upper bound).  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract] “Using the Kac‑Rice formula, we simplify earlier variational complexities to tractable scalar optimizations”  
- [section 2.2] Detailed derivation sketch showing reduction to eq. (15) and then scalar forms like eq. (24).

**Break condition**: The scalar reduction may fail or become intractable for non‑Gaussian data designs or models where the Marchenko–Pastur characterization does not hold (e.g., heavy‑tailed covariates, structured data beyond i.i.d. Gaussian). The assumption of annealed dominance might not hold if quenched effects are strong, leading to different phase transitions.

### Mechanism 2: BBP‑Kac‑Rice Detects Signal‑Aligned Instability
**Claim**: Even when the Kac‑Rice upper bound predicts positive complexity for local minima, a Baik‑Ben Arous‑Péché (BBP) transition can occur in the Hessian, creating a negative outlier eigenvalue whose eigenvector is aligned with the signal $\theta^\star$. This “BBP‑Kac‑Rice” effect signifies that many predicted minima are actually unstable, explaining the mismatch between landscape geometry and gradient descent success.  
**Mechanism**: The Kac‑Rice formalism provides the asymptotic joint label distribution $\nu(y,y^\star)$ and Hessian bulk spectral density $\rho(w)$ at typical critical points. Using random matrix theory, the condition for a BBP outlier aligned with $\theta^\star$ (i.e., an isolated eigenvalue left of the bulk) is derived from the Hessian’s low‑rank perturbation structure. The outlier eigenvalue $x^\star$ satisfies an equation (67) that involves the first moment of $(y^\star - qy)^2 f(y,y^\star)$ under $\nu$ and the Stieltjes transform of the bulk. Comparing $x^\star$ to the bulk’s left edge $x_{\min}$ yields the instability threshold $\alpha_{\text{BBP}}$.  
**Core assumption**: The Hessian’s low‑rank decomposition (eq. 53–56) is valid; the eigenvector associated with the outlier has a component along $\theta^\star$ (signal alignment); the asymptotic label distribution $\nu$ from annealed Kac‑Rice accurately describes typical critical points; bulk‑edge comparison (via Stieltjes transform) determines the outlier’s appearance.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract] “Combining with Baik‑Ben Arous‑Péché (BBP) random matrix theory yields a ‘BBP‑Kac‑Rice’ method that detects when local minima acquire an instability aligned with the signal.”  
- [section 2.4] Derivation of the BBP condition (eq. 67) and its use to detect instabilities before the Kac‑Rice trivialization threshold.  
- [corpus] *Weak evidence: Corpus contains papers on loss landscape topology but none on combining Kac‑Rice with BBP for instability detection.*  
**Break condition**: If the predicted label distribution $\nu$ is inaccurate (e.g., due to annealed vs. quenched differences), the outlier eigenvalue prediction may fail. The analysis assumes the signal direction is well‑separated from the bulk; if the overlap $q$ is too high or the model is strongly over‑parameterized, the low‑rank approximation may break down. The method also relies on the Hessian having a continuous spectrum; sparsity or non‑smooth losses could alter the transition.

### Mechanism 3: Convergence of Annealed Kac‑Rice Predictions to Finite‑$d$ GD Dynamics
**Claim**: The annealed Kac‑Rice predictions for the energy band, Hessian spectral density, and joint label distribution at local minima are in excellent quantitative agreement with the properties of minima found by gradient descent at moderate dimension ($d=512$), despite strong finite‑size corrections in the dynamics.  
**Mechanism**: Finite‑dimensional gradient descent suffers from time‑dependent BBP transitions that distort the dynamics. By pinning the overlap to a prescribed $q_0$ during an initial constrained phase (pinning dynamics), one suppresses the instability and creates a low‑energy state near the $q$‑band. Releasing the constraint yields final minima whose empirical properties match the annealed Kac‑Rice predictions for typical‑energy minima at the same overlap. This controlled experiment bridges the large‑$d$ theory to finite‑size simulations.  
**Core assumption**: The pinned dynamics effectively approximates the infinite‑dimensional limit; the empirical distribution of labels and Hessian at the resulting minima is representative of typical annealed predictions; the finite‑$d$ corrections are primarily captured by the time‑dependent outlier that is mitigated by the pinning protocol.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract] “Gradient descent simulations at $d=512$ confirm these predictions: energies match the Kac‑Rice band, Hessian eigenvalue distributions and label distributions agree closely.”  
- [section 3] Direct comparisons of $\langle e \rangle$, $\rho(\lambda)$, $F(u)$, and $\nu(y,y^\star)$ between theory and experiments.  
- [corpus] *Weak evidence: Neighboring works visualize loss landscapes but do not provide statistical comparisons to Kac‑Rice or GD outcomes.*  
**Break condition**: The pinning protocol may not generalize to other models (e.g., multi‑index or non‑Gaussian designs). For very high overlaps $q$, finite‑size effects persist and the agreement degrades. The theory assumes the minima found are typical under the annealed measure; if the dynamics converge to atypical minima (e.g., with very large basins), the match may fail. Also, the success of the comparison relies on the absence of quenched corrections that could shift phase transitions.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Kac‑Rice scalar solver → BBP transition evaluator → Finite‑d GD simulator

### Critical path
1. Pick a loss $\ell_a$ (e.g., phase retrieval).  
2. For given $(\alpha,q)$, solve the Kac‑Rice scalar variational problem for typical minima ($e = e^\star$). Obtain $\nu_0$, Hessian bulk.  
3. Evaluate BBP condition: if $\alpha_{\text{BBP}} \le \alpha$, typical minima are unstable.  
4. Simulate GD (with/without pinning) at finite $d$ over a grid of $(\alpha,q)$; record success rates and minima properties.  
5. Compare energy bands, Hessian $\rho(\lambda)$, $\nu(y,y^\star)$ with Kac‑Rice predictions; overlay phase diagram.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Numerical accuracy vs. speed**: The inner integrals over $\mu_q$ (2‑D) require adaptive quadrature; speeding up via JIT compilation may sacrifice some precision for smoother integrands.
- **Constrained vs. unconstrained dynamics**: Pinning avoids finite‑size BBP effects but introduces a bias toward exact $q$; researchers must balance $q_0$ choice with physical relevance.
- **Annealed vs. quenched**: Annealed predictions are faster and often accurate, but quenched effects may shift thresholds noticeably at moderate $d$; the gap is not fully quantified.

### Failure signatures
- Kac‑Rice solver fails to converge or yields non‑unique maxima for $\Sigma_{\text{tot.}}$ (first‑order transition warning).
- Numerical instability in the Stieltjes transform inversion near bulk edges produces spurious BBP thresholds.
- GD simulations show noisier phase transition lines than predicted, especially at high $q$, indicating unresolved finite‑size or quenched effects.
- Disagreement in label distribution $\nu(y,y^\star)$: if empirical $\nu$ is more Gaussian‑like, the model’s non‑Gaussian structure captured by Kac‑Rice is not manifest in data.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Reproduce phase diagrams for $a=0.01$**: Compute $\tilde{\Sigma}_0(q)$, $\Sigma_{\text{sub.}}(q)$, $\Sigma_{\text{tot.}}(q)$ over $\alpha$; overlay BBP thresholds for typical/lowest‑energy minima; compare to Figure 5.

## Limitations
- Kac‑Rice simplification relies on i.i.d. Gaussian data and annealed dominance; may not generalize to non‑Gaussian or structured designs.
- BBP instability detection depends on the accuracy of the annealed label distribution $\nu$; quenched effects or finite‑$d$ deviations could shift thresholds.
- GD validation uses a pinned‑overlap protocol; the hyperparameter choices ($t_C$, $T$) may bias empirical transitions, especially at high $q$.
