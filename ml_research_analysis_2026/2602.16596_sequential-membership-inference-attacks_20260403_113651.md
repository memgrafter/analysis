# Sequential Membership Inference Attacks
## Quick Facts
* **arXiv ID:** 2602.16596
* **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16596
* **Reference count:** 40
* **Primary result:** SeMI* is an optimal sequential membership inference attack that isolates the target batch, preventing signal dilution and enabling tighter privacy audits for DP‑SGD.

## Executive Summary
Membership inference attacks are a key tool for auditing differential privacy by detecting whether a specific record was used in training. Existing attacks on static final models suffer from signal dilution as more data accumulates, reducing their power. This paper introduces SeMI*, an optimal sequential membership inference attack that leverages a sequence of model updates to isolate the effect of a target canary. Theoretically, for Gaussian empirical means, SeMI*’s power depends only on batch size and target distance, not on the total number of updates T, unlike final‑model attacks whose power degrades with T. The isolation principle is extended to SGD and DP‑SGD via a likelihood ratio test (SeMISGD) that uses scaled parameter updates and estimated gradient statistics. Empirical evaluations on DP‑SGD fine‑tuning across three datasets show that SeMISGD yields higher true positive rates at fixed false positive rates and provides ε lower bounds substantially closer to the ground‑truth ε, especially in high‑privacy regimes. These results tighten privacy guarantees and improve audit reliability.

## Method Summary
The paper studies sequential membership inference where data arrives in batches and the model is updated after each batch. The auditor observes a sequence of model states (e.g., empirical means, parameters after SGD steps) and aims to detect whether a target record z* was inserted at a particular time τ. The joint likelihood of the sequence factorizes into a product of conditional distributions across updates. Under both the null (H₀) and alternative (H₁^τ) hypotheses, all conditionals are identical except at time τ, where the distribution differs due to the presence of z*. This isolation allows the auditor to construct a likelihood ratio test that depends solely on the statistic at τ (e.g., the batch mean or the scaled parameter update). For Gaussian empirical means, the optimal test (SeMI*) is derived via the Neyman‑Pearson lemma; for unknown τ, aggregations such as SeMIUnif (average) or SeMImax (maximum) are considered.

For SGD, the batch gradient distribution is approximated as Gaussian given the previous parameters. Theorem 4.1 shows that the log‑likelihood ratio retains the same functional form, with the batch mean replaced by the scaled update N = θ_τ − θ_{τ−1} + η_τ µ_g(θ_{τ−1}). The auditor estimates the population gradient mean µ_g and covariance Σ_g from reference data. The test statistic is thresholded to achieve a desired Type I error α; the resulting Type II error β yields a lower bound on the differential privacy parameter ε via the hypothesis‑testing relation. The complete audit involves crafting a target (often with high Mahalanobis distance), observing or simulating the model sequence, computing the likelihood ratio, estimating α and β, and deriving the ε bound.

## Key Results
- SeMI* maintains constant power as the total number of updates T grows, while final‑model attacks suffer power degradation due to signal dilution.
- SeMISGD achieves higher true positive rates than heuristic baselines (Delta Ratio, Back‑Front) at fixed false positive rates on DP‑SGD fine‑tuning tasks.
- The ε lower bounds obtained by SeMISGD are substantially tighter (closer to the ground‑truth ε) than those from baselines, especially in high‑privacy regimes (small ε).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The exceptional performance of SeMI attacks stems from three core mechanisms:

**Isolation via Likelihood Factorization**  
The joint likelihood of the observed model sequence factorizes into a product of conditional probabilities across updates. Under both hypotheses (H₀ vs. H₁^τ), all conditionals are identical except at the insertion time τ, where the distribution changes because the target record z* influences the batch. This factorization (Lemma 3.2, Eq. (5)) reduces the likelihood ratio to a function of the sufficient statistic at τ alone (e.g., the batch mean deviation N). Consequently, the signal from the target is not diluted by subsequent updates. The mechanism assumes the model updates are Markovian and that the mechanism Mₜ provides a sufficient statistic (e.g., empirical mean); if non‑local information or memory is used, the factorization breaks.

**T‑Independent Power for Gaussian Means**  
Because the test statistic depends only on the batch mean at τ, its distribution under stationarity is independent of the total number of updates T. For a Gaussian data generating distribution with fixed batch size n, the Type I and Type II errors (and thus power) are functions solely of n and the target’s Mahalanobis distance m* (Lemmas 3.5, 3.6; Theorem 3.4). Empirically (Figure 2(a)), SeMI* power remains constant across T, whereas final‑observation power decays as T increases. This property would fail if the data distribution changes over time (non‑stationarity) or if batch sizes vary.

**Extension to SGD and DP‑SGD**  
The same isolation principle extends to stochastic gradient descent by approximating the batch gradient distribution as Gaussian given the previous parameters. Theorem 4.1 shows that the log‑likelihood ratio retains the same structure, with the batch mean replaced by the scaled parameter update N = θ_τ − θ_{τ−1} + η_τ µ_g(θ_{τ−1}). The test then uses estimated population gradient mean µ_g and covariance Σ_g from reference data. Positive results on DP‑SGD fine‑tuning across three datasets (Section 5) validate the approach. However, the approximation can break if gradient batches are non‑Gaussian (e.g., due to small batch sizes, non‑smooth losses) or if the learning rate schedule is not accounted for.

## Foundational Learning
- **Neyman-Pearson Lemma and Likelihood Ratio Tests**: This lemma provides the theoretical foundation for constructing optimal (most powerful) tests for simple hypotheses, which SeMI* leverages for known τ. Understanding this lemma explains why the likelihood ratio is the optimal statistic and how composite hypotheses (e.g., unknown τ) lead to aggregated tests.  
  *Quick check:* Given two simple hypotheses H₀ and H₁ with known probability distributions, what is the form of the most powerful test at a given significance level? How does one extend this when the alternative hypothesis is composite (e.g., unknown insertion time τ)?

- **Differential Privacy Auditing via Hypothesis Testing**: In the hypothesis testing interpretation of differential privacy, an MI attack corresponds to distinguishing whether a target’s data was included in the training set. The resulting Type I and Type II errors (α, β) are linked to the privacy parameter ε via the hockey‑stick divergence, enabling an attack to yield a lower bound on ε.  
  *Quick check:* How does the hockey‑stick divergence relate to the Type I and Type II errors of an MI test when deriving an ε lower bound?

- **Stochastic Gradient Dynamics and Gaussian Approximations**: Extending SeMI to SGD requires modeling the batch gradient as approximately Gaussian conditional on the previous model parameters. This relies on central limit theorem arguments and properties of gradient covariances.  
  *Quick check:* Why might the batch gradient gₜ be approximately Gaussian given θ_{t-1} under SGD, and what are the limitations of this approximation (e.g., batch size, loss smoothness)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** The audit pipeline consists of four main components:  
Data Stream & Crafter → Sequential Mechanisms Mₜ (produces observable outputs oₜ at each step) → Test Statistic Computation (calculates likelihood ratio LR using o₁…o_T) → Error Estimation & Privacy Bound (estimates α(γ), β(γ) across thresholds γ and derives ε lower bound).  
This flow is linear, with each component feeding into the next.

**Critical path:** The auditor first crafts a target record z* (often chosen for high Mahalanobis distance from reference data). Then, given a sequence of model updates (either simulated or observed), the auditor computes the appropriate LR statistic (depending on known/unknown τ). By thresholding the LR, one obtains a classifier; running multiple rounds yields empirical α and β, which are converted into an ε lower bound via the DP hypothesis‑testing relation. Optimizing the threshold γ provides the tightest bound.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Known vs. unknown τ:* Using the true insertion time maximizes power but is unrealistic; unknown τ requires aggregating LR over time (e.g., average or max), incurring a power loss.
- *Gaussian assumptions:* Theoretical guarantees rely on exact Gaussian distributions; in practice, gradient statistics are estimated from reference data, and deviations from Gaussianity can reduce test effectiveness.
- *White‑box vs. black‑box:* The current SeMISGD requires access to gradients (or scaled updates); black‑box scenarios (only model outputs) would need different, likely suboptimal, statistics.

**Failure signatures:**
- Plotting the test statistic across time fails to show a distinct shift at the true τ, indicating isolation may be broken (e.g., due to non‑Markovian updates).
- Power curves exhibit a decreasing trend as T increases for SeMI*, violating the theoretical T‑independence and suggesting model dependence or drift.
- The ε lower bound from SeMISGD is worse than heuristic baselines for a given ground‑truth ε, especially in low‑privacy regimes, hinting at inappropriate gradient estimation or violation of assumptions.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Isolation Validation (Synthetic Gaussian):** Train a model on stationary Gaussian data, insert a high‑Mahalanobis target at a fixed τ, compute the SeMI* statistic across T, and verify a step‑like change at τ with constant separation thereafter (as in Figure 1a).
2. **Baseline Comparison (Synthetic):** Compare SeMI* power (TPR at 1% FPR) against the final‑model baseline across varying T and signal strength m*. Expected: SeMI* power remains constant while final‑model power degrades.
3. **Extension to DP‑SGD (Real Data):** Apply SeMISGD to a DP‑SGD fine‑tuning task (e.g., Fashion‑MNIST). Compare estimated ε lower bounds (averaged over τ) against ground truth and heuristic methods (Delta Ratio, Back‑Front). Expect tighter bounds, particularly for small ε.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Impact of distribution shift with unknown τ:** How does non‑stationarity in the data distribution affect SeMI’s power when the insertion time τ is unknown and chosen by the Crafter? The current analysis assumes stationarity and known τ; extending to non‑stationary D_t and a prior ν_τ over τ requires new likelihood integration and lacks empirical exploration. Resolving this would involve deriving closed‑form power expressions for non‑stationary settings, simulating various shift scenarios and ν_τ choices, and establishing analytical bounds linking shift severity to power loss.

2. **Crafter’s optimal insertion time distribution:** Can an adversary (Crafter) strategically select a distribution ν_τ over insertion times to minimize the auditor’s detection probability? This is a min‑max game with no known equilibrium, especially under DP constraints. A solution would formulate a game‑theoretic model, compute or characterize the optimal ν_τ (e.g., deterministic vs. uniform), prove corresponding lower bounds on the auditor’s power, and provide numerical evidence of evasion improvement.

3. **Black‑box sequential membership inference:** How to design optimal sequential MI attacks when only model outputs (e.g., predictions or losses) are observable? The white‑box SeMISGD relies on gradients; black‑box settings require different statistics and lack optimality guarantees. Progress here would derive an approximate likelihood ratio based on black‑box outputs under smoothness assumptions, prove its consistency, and empirically show tighter privacy audits compared to existing black‑box heuristics on DP‑SGD fine‑tuning.

4. **Choosing τ a priori for a given architecture:** How can an auditor determine the theoretically optimal insertion time before running an audit? Post‑hoc selection yields tighter bounds but is not allowed in practice; no theoretical guidance exists for which τ maximizes power a priori. Investigating this would relate τ to training dynamics (gradient norms, loss curvature), gather empirical evidence across architectures to identify patterns (e.g., early iterations are better), and propose a heuristic rule validated on unseen models.

## Limitations
- **Assumption sensitivity:** SeMI’s theoretical guarantees require Gaussian data, stationarity, and Markovian updates; violations (e.g., non‑Markovian optimizers, data drift) can break isolation and T‑independence.
- **Gradient estimation and reporting gaps:** The Gaussian approximation for gradients and potential estimation errors are not thoroughly analyzed; key hyperparameters for DP‑SGD experiments (clipping norm, noise multiplier, reference size) are missing, hampering reproducibility.
- **White‑box dependency:** The attack requires access to model gradients or scaled updates; black‑box extensions remain an open challenge.

## Confidence
- **Isolation property**: The factorization of the likelihood and isolation of the τ‑update is proven theoretically and validated on synthetic Gaussian data. (Confidence: High)
- **T‑independent power**: For Gaussian empirical means, power depends only on batch size and target Mahalanobis distance; this is derived analytically and confirmed empirically to be constant across T. (Confidence: High)
- **Extension to SGD/DP‑SGD**: The extension using scaled updates and Gaussian gradient approximation works well on three datasets but relies on assumptions that are not fully validated. (Confidence: Medium)
- **Tighter privacy audits**: SeMISGD yields higher TPR at fixed FPR and ε bounds closer to ground truth compared to baselines, though the experiments lack full hyperparameter details and sensitivity analysis. (Confidence: Medium)

## Next Checks
1. **Real‑sequence isolation:** Apply SeMISGD to actual DP‑SGD checkpoint sequences (e.g., from CIFAR‑10 training) to verify that the test statistic exhibits an isolated shift at the true τ under realistic, non‑Gaussian conditions.
2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Conduct a systematic sweep over batch size, clipping norm, noise multiplier, and reference sample size to measure their impact on the ε lower bound and to identify regimes where the Gaussian gradient approximation breaks down.
3. **Peer benchmark:** Re‑implement a recent sequential MI attack (e.g., SeqMIA) and compare its performance against SeMISGD under identical white‑box settings on both synthetic and real datasets to quantify the relative advantage.
