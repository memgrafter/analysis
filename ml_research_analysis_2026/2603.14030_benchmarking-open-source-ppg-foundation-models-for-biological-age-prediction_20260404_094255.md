# Benchmarking Open-Source PPG Foundation Models for Biological Age Prediction
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.14030
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14030
- Reference count: 4
- Primary result: Frozen embeddings from Pulse-PPG achieve MAE 9.28 years; combined with demographics, MAE 8.22 years (R²=0.517, r=0.725).

## Executive Summary
This study benchmarks three open-source PPG foundation models—Pulse-PPG, PaPaGei-S, and AI-PPG Age—for chronological age prediction using finger PPG data from 906 surgical patients. The authors extract frozen embeddings and apply Ridge regression with 5-fold cross‑validation, finding that Pulse-PPG alone yields a mean absolute error (MAE) of 9.28 years. Fusion of Pulse-PPG embeddings with demographic information further reduces MAE to 8.22 years (R²=0.517, r=0.725). The age gap derived from the model correlates with diastolic blood pressure (r = –0.188, p = 1.2×10⁻⁸), suggesting a link to vascular aging. The authors attribute the substantial performance gap compared to Apple’s proprietary PpgAge (MAE 2.43 years) to differences in dataset scale and population rather than architectural limitations.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses frozen embeddings from the three pre‑trained models as input features. For each model, embeddings are generated for every PPG recording. A Ridge regression model is trained to predict chronological age, with hyperparameter tuning via 5‑fold cross‑validation. The baseline compares frozen embedding performance against traditional hand‑crafted features (heart rate, HRV) combined with demographics. Additional experiments fuse the best embedding (Pulse‑PPG) with demographic variables. All analyses are performed on a single clinical dataset (PulseDB) of 906 surgical patients.

## Key Results
- Pulse‑PPG frozen embeddings yield MAE 9.28 years, surpassing PaPaGei‑S and AI‑PPG Age under identical linear probing conditions.
- Concatenating Pulse‑PPG embeddings with demographics reduces MAE to 8.22 years and improves explained variance (R²=0.517, r=0.725).
- The PPG age gap (predicted minus chronological age) correlates negatively with diastolic blood pressure (r = –0.188, p = 1.2×10⁻⁸), aligning with known patterns of vascular aging.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The superior performance of Pulse‑PPG suggests that its pre‑training on diverse PPG data captures subtle morphological features—beyond conventional HR/HRV—that are predictive of chronological age. The significant correlation between age gap and diastolic blood pressure supports the hypothesis that the model encodes vascular aging signatures. The embedding’s robustness when frozen indicates that the foundation model has already learned highly task‑relevant representations, requiring only a simple linear adaptor for age regression.

## Foundational Learning
- **Self‑supervised pretraining on large PPG corpora**  
  *Why needed:* Enables the model to learn general representations without relying on scarce age labels.  
  *Quick check:* Confirm that embeddings cluster by known physiological states (e.g., activity) in an unsupervised manner.
- **Frozen embedding evaluation (linear probing)**  
  *Why needed:* Assesses representation quality with minimal task‑specific adaptation, reducing overfitting risks.  
  *Quick check:* Train a linear model on frozen features; performance should be competitive with end‑to‑end training if embeddings are high‑quality.
- **Ridge regression with L2 regularization**  
  *Why needed:* Prevents overfitting when labeled data is limited, as in the surgical cohort.  
  *Quick check:* Tune the regularization strength (α) via cross‑validation; observe stable performance across folds.
- **Cross‑validation (5‑fold)**  
  *Why needed:* Provides reliable performance estimates on a single dataset.  
  *Quick check:* Ensure all samples are used exactly once in test sets; check for data leakage.
- **Feature fusion with demographics**  
  *Why needed:* Complements signal‑based embeddings with non‑signal predictors (age, sex, BMI) that influence chronological age.  
  *Quick check:* Concatenate demographics to embeddings; verify improvement over embeddings alone.
- **Correlation analysis with physiological markers**  
  *Why needed:* Links model predictions to underlying biology (e.g., vascular aging).  
  *Quick check:* Compute Pearson correlation between age gap and relevant vitals; test significance with p‑values.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Raw PPG → Preprocessing (filtering, segmentation) → Foundation encoder (Pulse‑PPG / PaPaGei‑S / AI‑PPG Age) → Frozen embedding → (Optional) concatenation with demographics → Ridge regression → Age prediction

**Critical path:**  
Preprocessing must produce valid segments; encoder must load correct pre‑trained weights; Ridge regression must be trained on embeddings from the same patient cohort without leakage.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Frozen vs. fine‑tuning: Frozen embeddings are fast and avoid overfitting but may underutilize model capacity; fine‑tuning could improve performance but risks catastrophic forgetting and requires more data.  
- Model choice: Pulse‑PPG is based on a contrastive framework; PaPaGei‑S uses a generative objective; AI‑PPG Age is specifically age‑regressed. Differences affect what features are emphasized.

**Failure signatures:**  
- NaNs or extreme values in embeddings → check preprocessing and input length compatibility.  
- High variance across cross‑validation folds → data split may not be i.i.d. (e.g., patient overlap) or preprocessing inconsistent.  
- Poor correlation with diastolic BP despite good MAE → model might be overfitting to demographic confounders rather than vascular signals.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Linear probe baseline:** Extract frozen embeddings for all three models and train Ridge regression with 5‑fold CV; compare MAEs.  
2. **Demographic fusion:** Concatenate the best embedding (Pulse‑PPG) with age, sex, BMI; retrain Ridge and assess MAE reduction.  
3. **Hand‑crafted benchmark:** Train Ridge on traditional HR/HRV features plus demographics to establish a classical signal‑processing baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How much does fine‑tuning open‑source PPG foundation models improve chronological age prediction compared to linear probing, and which fine‑tuning approaches (e.g., full model, partial layers) are optimal?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors used frozen embeddings with Ridge regression and suggested that fine‑tuning would likely improve performance, but did not test this.
- **Why unresolved:** The study evaluated only linear probing; the impact of task‑specific fine‑tuning on MAE remains unknown, especially given Apple’s end‑to‑end training.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments fine‑tuning Pulse‑PPG and PaPaGei‑S on the PulseDB age prediction task, varying the extent of model updates, and comparing MAE reductions against linear probe results.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the PPG age gap from surgical patients under anesthesia predict incident cardiovascular events, or does it primarily reflect acute hemodynamic perturbations rather than chronic biological aging?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note the lack of longitudinal outcomes to test predictive validity and suggest the age gap may reflect acute illness rather than chronic aging.
- **Why unresolved:** PulseDB lacks long‑term follow‑up data; the association with DBP is cross‑sectional, not predictive of outcomes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal studies tracking PPG age gap from similar clinical or ambulatory cohorts and its association with future cardiovascular events, mortality, or other health outcomes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent do open‑source PPG foundation models trained on diverse finger PPG generalize to consumer wrist PPG for age prediction without domain adaptation?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly state that “no wearable validation” was performed and that transfer to wrist PPG “remains untested.”
- **Why unresolved:** All benchmarks were on clinical finger PPG; Apple’s PpgAge uses wrist PPG, and signal morphology differs across sites.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluating the same models on wrist PPG datasets (e.g., from wearables) and comparing performance to finger PPG, potentially with site‑adaptation techniques.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What specific PPG morphological features (e.g., pulse contour, dicrotic notch) are most encoded in foundation model embeddings for age prediction, and can they be extracted as interpretable biomarkers?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors claim embeddings capture “subtle morphological features” beyond HR/HRV but do not identify or validate these features.
- **Why unresolved:** The embeddings are used as black‑box features; no analysis links dimensions to known aging‑related waveform changes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Applying explainable AI methods (e.g., SHAP, feature ablation) to the embeddings or probing internal model representations to isolate and validate age‑correlated morphological signatures.

## Limitations
- The evaluation is restricted to a surgical cohort (n=906) that may not represent the general population, limiting generalizability of age predictions.
- Only frozen embeddings with linear probing were tested; the potential benefits of fine‑tuning or more complex heads were not explored.
- The modest correlation between age gap and diastolic blood pressure (r = –0.188) leaves the majority of variance unexplained, indicating other physiological or technical factors are not accounted for.

## Confidence
- **Pulse‑PPG outperforms other open‑source models under linear probing:** High
- **The performance gap relative to Apple’s PpgAge is primarily due to dataset size and population differences:** Medium
- **PPG age gap correlates with diastolic blood pressure:** Medium

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the benchmark on a large, diverse general‑population PPG dataset (e.g., UK Biobank) to test generalizability and isolate population effects.
2. Fine‑tune the foundation models end‑to‑end on the surgical cohort and compare MAE against the linear probe baseline.
3. Perform a controlled dataset perturbation: downsample or augment Pulse‑PPG’s training data to match Apple’s presumed scale and distribution, then re‑evaluate to directly test the data‑scale hypothesis.
