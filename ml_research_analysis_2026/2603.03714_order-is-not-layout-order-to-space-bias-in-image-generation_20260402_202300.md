# Order Is Not Layout: Order-to-Space Bias in Image Generation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03714
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03714
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Modern text-to-image and image-to-image models exhibit a systematic Order-to-Space Bias (OTS), spuriously mapping prompt mention order to spatial layout, which can be substantially mitigated via flip-augmented fine-tuning or early-stage prompt switching.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies and analyzes Order-to-Space Bias (OTS), a pervasive flaw in generative image models where the order in which entities are mentioned in a prompt dictates their left-right spatial arrangement or action/role assignment, often overriding real-world constraints. The authors construct OTS-Bench, a controlled benchmark with 4,300 test cases, to measure two facets: homogenization (consistency of order-following layouts) and correctness (adherence to spatial/role constraints). Results across multiple models reveal high homogenization (52.6%–91.6%) and a sharp drop in correctness when prompt order conflicts with constraints (14.1%–28.2% for T2I Reverse). The bias is traced to a strong order-to-space alignment (~89%) in web-scale training data and is shown to manifest during the early, layout-sensitive phase of diffusion sampling. Targeted interventions—flip-augmented LoRA fine-tuning and delayed order conditioning during generation—significantly reduce homogenization while largely preserving image quality, confirming OTS as a data-driven, early-layout phenomenon.

## Method Summary
The core evaluation uses OTS-Bench, a novel benchmark designed to isolate order-to-space mapping. It generates two types of test cases: neutral two-entity prompts (e.g., "a cat and a dog standing") to measure homogenization (the tendency to consistently place the first-mentioned entity on the left), and constraint-conflicting prompts (e.g., "a left-turn arrow and a right-turn arrow") to measure correctness by comparing aligned vs. reversed orders. Images are generated with baseline T2I/I2I models and automatically judged by a VLM (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct) to assign left-right identities or action attributions. Metrics are computed as Homogenization = 100 × |(NL - NR)/(NL + NR)| and Correctness = correct/(correct+wrong). For mitigation, two methods are proposed: (1) Flip-augmented LoRA fine-tuning, which trains on caption-image pairs augmented with horizontal flips, and (2) Delayed order conditioning, which starts generation with an order-neutral prompt before switching to the original prompt at an early denoising step (optimal around step 2).

## Key Results
- OTS is pervasive: T2I homogenization scores range from 52.6% to 91.6%, indicating a strong default left-bias for first-mentioned entities.
- Correctness under order-reversed prompts plummets (T2I Reverse: 14.1%–28.2%), showing models prioritize prompt order over grounded spatial constraints.
- Corpus analysis reveals a ~89% order-to-space alignment in filtered web training pairs (LAION, DataComp), implicating data as the primary source.
- Mitigation is effective: Flip-augmented LoRA reduces FLUX-dev T2I homogenization from 88.8% to 47.4%; delayed order conditioning also substantially lowers homogenization, with the strongest effect at step 2.
- Image quality is largely preserved after mitigation, as measured by ImageReward and human evaluation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** OTS is primarily data-driven, arising from a pervasive statistical regularity in web-scale training corpora where first-mentioned entities disproportionately appear on the left in paired images.
**Mechanism:** Models learn a spurious correlation between textual mention order and horizontal spatial arrangement from training data. When generating under spatially neutral prompts, they default to this learned "first-mentioned→left" prior.
**Core assumption:** The bias is an emergent inductive shortcut from training data statistical patterns, not an inherent architectural constraint.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "trace OTS to strong order-to-space alignment in web training data (∼89%)"
- [section 5.3] Reports 89.2% (LAION) and 87.2% (DataComp) order-to-space match rates in filtered caption-image pairs.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** OTS manifests during the early, layout-sensitive phase of diffusion sampling, where global spatial structure is established.
**Mechanism:** Text conditioning during initial denoising steps has a disproportionate effect on committing to a left-right entity arrangement. Order cues are most influential before layout "locks in."
**Core assumption:** Spatial layout formation is temporally separable from later appearance refinement in diffusion models.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [section 5.5] "temporal intervention experiments... switch entity order... at early timesteps, a certain proportion of samples successfully follow the swapped order... at later stages, the influence... becomes negligible."
- [section 5.5] "delayed order conditioning... reduces homogenization... effect strongest at step 2... mitigation becomes less effective when switching either earlier or later."

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** OTS can be substantially mitigated via targeted interventions that explicitly break the order→space shortcut, either at training (data augmentation) or generation (prompt scheduling) time.
**Mechanism:** 
  - *Training:* Flip-augmented LoRA-SFT presents the model with identical captions paired with both left-right layouts during fine-tuning, directly countering the spurious correlation.
  - *Generation:* Delayed order conditioning starts sampling with an order-neutral prompt ("two X") before switching to the rich, order-specific prompt after the layout is established, preventing order from dictating layout.
**Core assumption:** The bias is a simple, single-feature shortcut that can be overwhelmed by a stronger counter-signal without requiring full retraining or sacrificing general capability.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [section 5.4] "FLUX-dev T2I homogenization from 88.8% to 47.4%" after flip-SFT; "image quality is largely preserved."
- [section 5.5] "delayed order conditioning... homogenization substantially reduced... strongest effect observed at step 2."

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Diffusion Model Sampling Stages
**Why needed here:** To understand *when* in the generation process OTS exerts influence (early layout vs. late refinement). The temporal localization experiments depend on knowing that early steps set coarse structure.
**Quick check question:** In a diffusion model, if you change the text prompt at timestep *t*, would you expect the most dramatic changes in (a) the rough arrangement of objects, or (b) the texture and fine details of those objects? Which timestep range *t* is most likely to affect (a)?

### Concept: Spurious Correlation / Shortcut Learning
**Why needed here:** OTS is defined as a *spurious* mapping (order → space) that overrides *grounded* cues. Recognizing this as a shortcut is key to designing mitigation (presenting a stronger, correct signal).
**Quick check question:** If a model consistently places the first noun on the left because 90% of its training images obey that pattern, is that an example of learning a *causal* relationship or a *spurious* shortcut? What test would you design to distinguish them?

### Concept: Evaluation Metric Design (Homogenization vs. Correctness)
**Why needed here:** The paper's core measurement strategy isolates *order-following tendency* (homogenization) from *correctness under conflict* (correctness). This separation is crucial for diagnosing the bias.
**Quick check question:** For the prompt "digit 3 and digit 9 on a clock," the *correctness* test uses "Aligned" (3-left,9-right) vs. "Reverse" (9-left,3-right) prompts. What would a high homogenization score but *low* drop in Reverse correctness (vs. Aligned) suggest about the nature of the model's failure?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Prompt Generator -> Model Under Test (T2I/I2I) -> VL Judge (Qwen3-VL) -> Scoring Module -> Data Analyzer (for corpus study)
**Critical path:** The most sensitive path for detecting OTS is: *Generate images from neutral two-entity prompts -> Judge left-right layout -> Compute homogenization deviation (HomT2I/HomI2I).* A value significantly above 0% indicates a strong first-mentioned-left bias. For correctness, the critical path is: *Generate from order-conflicting prompts -> Judge if layout/action matches real-world constraint -> Compare Aligned vs. Reverse accuracy (Δ).*
**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Benchmark Scope vs. Depth:* OTS-Bench uses 138 subjects and 172 actions for breadth but focuses on *pairs* and *horizontal* relations. It trades off evaluating complex multi-entity (3+) or vertical/temporal layouts for cleaner causal isolation of order→space.
- *Automated vs. Human Evaluation:* Using a VLM judge (Qwen3-VL) enables large-scale scoring but introduces potential judge bias/hallucination (mitigated by the two-stage protocol and human κ-validation).
- *Debiasing vs. Quality:* Flip-augmented SFT reduces homogenization but requires a held-out set to monitor ImageReward/human scores for unintended quality loss.
**Failure signatures:**
- **High homogenization** (>70% for T2I) on neutral prompts: Model defaults to prompt order for layout.
- **Large Δ (Ali vs. Rev correctness)** (>30pt drop for T2I): Model prioritizes order over grounded spatial constraints.
- **Temporal intervention failure:** Switching prompt order at *any* timestep does not change final layout → bias is fixed very early or not layout-dependent.
- **SFT quality drop:** After debiasing training, ImageReward or human fidelity scores decline significantly.
**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replicate Homogenization Test:** Take a neutral two-entity prompt (e.g., "a cat and a dog standing"), generate 50 images with a base model (FLUX-dev), and run the VL judge to compute HomT2I. Expect >80% for unmitigated models.
2. **Verify Data Bias Pipeline:** Using a small sample (100 images) from LAION/DataComp that pass the paper's filtering (two entities, clear left-right), run the two-stage VLM verification to estimate the order-to-space match rate. Expect ~85-90%.
3. **Run Temporal Switch Intervention:** For 20 samples showing OTS, generate images while switching prompt order at steps {1,2,3,4,5}. Measure success rate (final layout follows *swapped* order). Expect success rate dropping to near 0% after step ~2-3.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How does Order-to-Space Bias scale to prompts with three or more entities, and can current mitigation strategies generalize to such cases without quality loss?
**Basis:** The conclusion explicitly states: "Beyond two-entity generation, a key direction is to study how such shortcuts scale to multi-entity compositions (3+ subjects)..."
**Why unresolved:** OTS-Bench covers only two-entity scenarios; multi-entity layouts introduce combinatorial complexity and may expose different bias patterns or interactions.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Extend OTS-Bench with three- and four-entity test sets; measure homogenization and correctness across entity counts; apply flip-augmented fine-tuning and delayed conditioning to assess debiasing generalization.

**Open Question 2:** Does Order-to-Space Bias persist in video generation models, and how does it affect temporal consistency of roles and spatial relations? Can image-based mitigation methods be adapted to maintain both spatial and temporal coherence?
**Basis:** The conclusion lists video generation as a key direction; supplementary figures show initial T2V/I2V examples, indicating the need for systematic study.
**Why unresolved:** The main evaluation focuses on single-image T2I/I2I; video adds a temporal dimension where order biases could cause role swapping or layout drift over time, requiring new benchmarks and adapted interventions.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Construct a video OTS benchmark with multi-frame constraints; evaluate state-of-the-art video generators; adapt and test fine-tuning and scheduling strategies with temporal coherence metrics.

**Open Question 3:** To what extent does model architecture (e.g., diffusion transformer vs. UNet, vision-language encoder design) influence the severity and early-stage manifestation of OTS? Are certain architectures inherently less susceptible?
**Basis:** The paper reports wide variation in homogenization scores across evaluated models but cannot isolate architecture due to confounding factors (training data, sampling). The data-driven origin suggests architecture may modulate but not eliminate OTS.
**Why unresolved:** Without controlled experiments varying only architecture while holding data constant, we cannot determine if some designs inherently reduce OTS or alter its temporal dynamics.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Train multiple architectures on identical datasets; measure OTS metrics and perform temporal order-switching interventions to compare the sensitivity window and effectiveness of mitigations per architecture.

**Open Question 4:** Is Order-to-Space Bias language-dependent? For multilingual models or prompts in languages with different word orders (e.g., SOV vs. SVO), does the bias still map first-mention to left, or does it reflect the dominant word order of the training corpus?
**Basis:** Data analysis in Section 5.3 uses only English web corpora (LAION-2B-en-aesthetic, DataComp-Large). While OTS is attributed to data-driven regularities, cross-linguistic variation remains unexplored.
**Why unresolved:** Captions in other languages may have different statistical couplings between mention order and spatial layout, potentially flipping the bias direction (e.g., last-mention-left). No evidence from the paper addresses non-English data.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Compute order-to-space alignment in multilingual caption datasets; evaluate OTS metrics for prompts in various languages on multilingual models; test whether homogenization direction correlates with the frequent word order patterns of the training corpus.

## Limitations
- **OTS-Bench completeness:** The full 4,300-case test suite is not public, limiting exact replication to approximate trends using a reconstructed subset.
- **VLM judge generalization:** Reliability (κ=0.81) is validated on a private human set; performance on other distributions without that set is uncertain.
- **I2I baseline provenance:** Original seed images and their generation prompts are unreported, making baseline homogenization scores for I2I potentially non-comparable.
- **Debiasing data curation:** The exact filtering for "high-confidence" layout pairs in the 2k flip-augmented set is ambiguous, hindering precise reproduction of mitigation effects.
- **Generalization scope:** OTS-Bench focuses on horizontal two-entity layouts; findings may not transfer to vertical arrangements or scenes with 3+ entities.

## Confidence
- **OTS is data-driven (∼89% web alignment):** Medium. Strong correlational evidence from corpus analysis, but causal proof requires controlled training data experiments not presented.
- **OTS manifests as an early-layout phenomenon:** Medium. Temporal intervention results are compelling but model-agnostic; architectural factors could contribute.
- **Mitigation via flip-SFT/delayed conditioning is effective:** Medium. Results are promising on specific models (FLUX-dev), but efficacy across architectures and without quality trade-offs needs broader validation.

## Next Checks
1.  **Verify web data bias pipeline:** Replicate the corpus analysis on a fresh, documented sample (e.g., LAION-400M) using the paper's two-stage VLM verification. Confirm the ∼89% order-space match rate independently.
2.  **Test temporal intervention robustness:** For a given model, perform prompt-switching at steps {0,1,2,3,4,5,10} across 100+ diverse neutral prompts. Plot success rate vs. step to precisely locate the "lock-in" timestep and check for model-specific variations.
3.  **Evaluate mitigation generalization:** Apply flip-augmented LoRA (using a *replicated* high-confidence dataset) to a different model family (e.g., SDXL vs. FLUX). Measure homogenization reduction and check for degradation on out-of-distribution prompts (e.g., non-pair entities, complex scenes).
