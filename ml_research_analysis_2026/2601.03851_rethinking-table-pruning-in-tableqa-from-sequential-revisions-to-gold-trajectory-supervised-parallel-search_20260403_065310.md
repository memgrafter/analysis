# Rethinking Table Pruning in TableQA: From Sequential Revisions to Gold Trajectory-Supervised Parallel Search

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.03851
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03851
- **Reference count**: 21
- **Primary result**: TabTrim-8B achieves state-of-the-art average accuracy of 73.5% on WikiTQ, TabFact, and TableBench, outperforming the strongest baseline by 3.2% absolute.

## Executive Summary
TableQA systems often rely on pruning large tables to retain only answer‑critical cells, but existing approaches use sequential revisions guided by unreliable critique signals like LLM‑as‑a‑Judge or execution failures, leading to loss of essential data. This paper presents TabTrim, a framework that replaces sequential revisions with gold trajectory‑supervised parallel search. TabTrim constructs a gold pruning trajectory from intermediate sub‑tables generated by executing a gold SQL query clause‑by‑clause. This trajectory provides reliable step‑wise supervision. The system trains a pruner (via SFT and DPO) to follow the gold steps and a loss‑aware verifier to score sub‑tables with a recall‑biased metric. At inference, a beam search explores multiple candidate pruning paths and selects the highest‑scoring sub‑table for the final answerer. TabTrim‑8B achieves state‑of‑the‑art performance, with 73.5% average accuracy across three benchmarks, representing a 3.2% absolute improvement over the previous best. The gains are particularly notable on complex and numerical reasoning tasks, and performance scales with increased inference‑time compute.

## Method Summary
TabTrim introduces a shift from sequential table revision to parallel trajectory search guided by gold supervision. Gold pruning trajectories are constructed automatically by decomposing gold SQL queries into clause‑level operations (e.g., row filtering, column projection) and executing them sequentially on the raw table to produce intermediate sub‑tables. These trajectories serve as step‑wise training signals. The pruner (an LLM) is first fine‑tuned on positive (on‑trajectory) and negative (off‑trajectory) samples and then refined via Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to prefer gold next sub‑tables. The verifier, a smaller model, is trained to regress a recall‑biased F‑score that compares an intermediate sub‑table to the final gold sub‑table, emphasizing retention of answer‑critical cells. During inference, a beam search iteratively generates multiple candidate sub‑tables per step, scores them with the verifier, and retains the top‑k branches. After the search, the highest‑scoring sub‑table is passed to the answerer to produce the final response. This parallel exploration mitigates early error propagation and discovers higher‑quality sub‑tables than greedy or sequential revision baselines.

## Key Results
- TabTrim‑8B achieves 73.5% average accuracy across WikiTQ, TabFact, and TableBench, outperforming the strongest baseline by 3.2% absolute.
- Specific scores: 79.4% on WikiTQ and 61.2% on TableBench, with particularly large gains on the numerical reasoning subset of TableBench (+5.8%).
- Performance scales positively with increased inference‑time compute (e.g., larger beam width and depth), indicating benefits from further search budget.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Gold Trajectory Construction
TabTrim constructs a gold pruning trajectory by decomposing a gold SQL query into clause‑level operations (e.g., row filtering, column projection) and executing them sequentially on the raw table. Each transition produces an intermediate sub‑table that preserves answer‑critical data. The core assumption is that the execution trace of a correct SQL query reveals the minimal evidence required to answer the question. Evidence includes the abstract and Section 2.1 describing the process. However, if the gold SQL itself is erroneous or the decomposition includes unnecessary steps, the trajectory may propagate incorrect pruning actions.

### Mechanism 2: Process Supervision and Loss‑Aware Verification
The pruner is trained with a two‑stage process: first, supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) on positive (on‑trajectory) and negative (off‑trajectory) samples; then Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to prefer the gold next sub‑table over corrupted candidates. The verifier predicts a recall‑biased F‑score that compares an intermediate sub‑table to the final gold sub‑table, thereby focusing on retaining answer‑critical cells. This score guides the search without requiring the gold reference at inference. The approach assumes the loss‑aware score correlates strongly with downstream correctness; evidence is the improved performance when using recall‑bias (α=1.5) and ablations (Tables 2, 3). A risk is that the final gold sub‑table might not be truly minimal, leading the verifier to learn a sub‑optimal reward.

### Mechanism 3: Parallel Beam Search to Mitigate Error Propagation
Instead of a single sequential revision path, TabTrim uses beam search to explore multiple candidate pruning trajectories in parallel. At each step, the pruner generates several next sub‑tables for each beam state; the verifier scores each candidate, and the top‑k are kept. This expands the search space and allows recovery from early mistakes, unlike greedy or sequential revision. The verifier’s step‑level scores must be sufficiently informative to rank candidates that ultimately lead to correct answers. Evidence: disabling search (i.e., single‑branch) harms performance (Table 4). Limitations include potential search stagnation if the verifier is poorly calibrated or the beam width is too narrow.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Pruner (Mθ) generates next sub‑table candidates; Verifier (gϕ) scores each candidate; Search controller manages beam states, candidate generation, scoring, and top‑k selection; Answerer produces the final answer from the selected sub‑table.
- **Critical path**:
  1. Training data construction: Decompose gold SQL to get trajectories; create off‑trajectory negatives.
  2. Pruner training: SFT on positive + negative samples → DPO refinement.
  3. Verifier training: Regress loss‑aware scores on gold/negative sub‑tables.
  4. Inference: Initialize beam with raw table; for each step up to Dmax: generate b candidates per beam with pruner → score with verifier → retain top‑k; after search, pick highest‑scoring sub‑table; answerer returns answer.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Beam width (k) vs. compute cost: larger k explores more branches but increases verifier calls.
  - Branching factor (b) vs. diversity: higher b yields more candidates but may produce redundant sub‑tables.
  - Recall bias α: Higher α penalizes missing critical cells more strongly, but may over‑prune if set too high.
  - Correction weight λ in SFT: Balances learning on‑trajectory progression versus recovery from errors; λ=1 used in paper.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - High verifier score but wrong answer: indicates verifier may not perfectly predict downstream correctness; likely reasoning error.
  - Low scores on gold trajectory: suggests verifier mis‑training or mismatched reference (final gold sub‑table).
  - Search stagnation across steps (beam scores plateau): could imply insufficient branching or verifier insensitivity.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Main results (Table 1): TabTrim‑8B achieves 73.5% average accuracy, outperforming baselines by 3.2% absolute.
  2. Ablation on pruner training (Table 2): Removing DPO or correction samples degrades performance, especially on WikiTQ (‑4.6% without correction).
  3. Ablation on loss‑aware verification (Table 3): Using balanced (α=1) score instead of recall‑biased (α=1.5) reduces accuracy by 1.6% on WikiTQ and 2.9% on TableBench.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scaling laws with larger models**: How do TabTrim’s performance and scaling change when scaling the pruner and verifier to significantly larger architectures (e.g., >100B parameters)? The paper only evaluates up to 8B due to computational limits, leaving scaling with stronger base models as future work.
2. **Reliance on gold SQL for training**: To what extent is TabTrim’s dependence on gold SQL queries (from Text‑to‑SQL data) a limitation for TableQA datasets that lack such annotations? The method may not transfer directly to domains without gold SQL proxies.
3. **Dynamic calibration of recall bias**: Can the fixed recall‑bias parameter (α=1.5) be dynamically adjusted per‑question or per‑table to better balance precision and recall? The static α might not suit varying question complexities or reasoning types.
4. **Selection biases in beam search**: Does the parallel beam search introduce biases toward trajectories with more steps or particular structures, and how do maximum depth (Dmax) and beam width (k) interact? The step‑wise myopic scoring might over‑exploit local maxima, and optimal hyperparameters likely depend on instance‑specific trajectory lengths.
5. **Source of gains on numerical reasoning**: Are the improvements on numerical reasoning (TableBench TB‑NR) due to better preservation of numerical cells or general evidence retention? Error analysis shows most failures are arithmetic in the answerer, suggesting a last‑mile bottleneck even with perfect sub‑tables.

## Limitations
- Gold SQL errors can produce non‑minimal trajectories, propagating sub‑optimal pruning.
- Clause‑level decomposition may fail for complex queries (nested subqueries, set operations).
- Evaluation is limited to Wikipedia tables; domain transfer remains unknown.
- The verifier relies on a gold final sub‑table that may not be truly minimal, potentially learning a misleading reward.

## Confidence
- **SOTA performance and ablations**: High

- **Process supervision & recall‑biased verifier**: High
- **Parallel beam search**: High
