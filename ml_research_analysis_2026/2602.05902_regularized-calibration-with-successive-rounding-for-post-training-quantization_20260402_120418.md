# Regularized Calibration with Successive Rounding for Post-Training Quantization
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.05902
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05902
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: On 3-bit LLaMA3-8B, SNRQ-S reduces Wiki2 perplexity by 5.7% and improves zero-shot accuracy by 5.4% vs GPTAQ, with 29–32% speedup.

## Executive Summary
Post‑training quantization (PTQ) for large language models suffers from accuracy degradation due to activation mismatch between full‑precision and quantized computations. This paper introduces regularized asymmetric calibration and successive rounding to address these issues. The calibration objective interpolates between symmetric and asymmetric losses, acting as an explicit regularizer while preserving a quadratic structure that enables efficient Hessian‑based optimization. Successive rounding minimizes the resulting triangular least‑squares proxy, with a bounded beam search extension offering further gains at controllable overhead. Experiments on LLaMA‑2/3 at 3‑4 bit‑widths show that SNRQ‑S consistently outperforms strong baselines like GPTQ and GPTAQ, reducing perplexity and improving zero‑shot accuracy while also being 29–32% faster to compute. The beam‑search variant K‑SNRQ delivers additional accuracy improvements.

## Method Summary
The method comprises two stages: regularized calibration and successive rounding. For a given layer, calibration forms a mixture of full‑precision activations \(X_f\) and quantized activations \(X_q\) via \(X_\alpha = \alpha X_f + (1-\alpha)X_q\). The objective \(L(\hat{W};\alpha) = \|W X_\alpha - \hat{W} X_q\|_F^2\) decomposes into a weighted sum of asymmetric and symmetric losses, providing regularization. The optimum is \(M_\alpha = W C_\alpha H^{-1}\) with \(H = X_q X_q^T + \lambda I\) and \(C_\alpha = X_\alpha X_q^T\). To obtain quantized weights, the problem is transformed using Cholesky decomposition of \(H\) into an upper triangular least‑squares form. This yields a row‑wise discrete optimization solved by greedy successive rounding: columns are processed from last to first, rounding each weight to the nearest level after adjusting for the already‑quantized suffix. Beam search (K‑SNRQ) extends this by exploring the \(K\)-best partial assignments per step, improving quality with modest overhead. Practical choices include column permutation based on Hessian diagonals, selection of \(\alpha\) (closed‑form or sampled), and numerical damping.

## Key Results
- SNRQ‑S reduces Wiki2 perplexity by 5.7% and improves zero‑shot accuracy by 5.4% relative to GPTAQ on 3‑bit LLaMA3‑8B, with 29–32% speedup.
- K‑SNRQ (beam search) yields further accuracy gains at controllable compute overhead.
- Consistent improvements across multiple LLM families and bit‑widths over GPTQ and GPTAQ baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:**  
Interpolating between symmetric and asymmetric calibration using a weighted combination of activations (\(X_\alpha = \alpha X_f + (1-\alpha)X_q\)) yields an explicit regularization effect that mitigates overfitting to quantized activations while retaining a quadratic optimization structure.

**Mechanism:**  
The paper defines the objective \(L(\hat{W};\alpha) = \|W X_\alpha - \hat{W} X_q\|_F^2\). Proposition 3.1 shows it decomposes as  
\(L(\hat{W};\alpha) \equiv \alpha L_{\text{asym}}(\hat{W}) + (1-\alpha) L_{\text{sym}}(\hat{W})\) up to a constant. Thus the asymmetric teacher–student term acts as a regularizer on the standard symmetric reconstruction loss. By selecting \(\alpha \in (0,1)\) the method balances robustness to activation mismatch against pure reconstruction.

**Core assumption:**  
Activation mismatch (\(X_f \neq X_q\)) is inevitable due to error accumulation in layer‑wise PTQ, and a convex combination of activations provides a smoother, more generalizable optimization landscape than either extreme.

**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: “interpolating between symmetric and asymmetric calibration acts as a form of regularization that preserves the standard quadratic structure used in PTQ while providing robustness to activation mismatch.”  
- [section]: Proposition 3.1 derives the exact decomposition.  
- [corpus]: The provided corpus includes CBQ and OJBKQ, which address block dependencies and objective design but do not directly study interpolation; evidence is indirect and context‑specific.

**Break condition:**  
If the Hessian \(H\) is not positive definite (e.g., due to poor calibration data or extreme outliers), the quadratic reformulation may become ill‑conditioned; also, if the mismatch between \(X_f\) and \(X_q\) is negligible, the regularizer provides no benefit.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:**  
The regularized objective can be transformed into a Hessian‑weighted least‑squares problem with a shifted target \(M_\alpha = W C_\alpha H^{-1}\), which in turn yields a triangular discrete least‑squares problem solvable by successive rounding.

**Mechanism:**  
Starting from \(L(\hat{W};\alpha) = \|W X_\alpha - \hat{W} X_q\|_F^2\), Theorem 4.1 shows that with \(H = X_q X_q^T + \lambda I\) (SPD) and Cholesky factor \(L\), the objective equals \(\|(\hat{W} - M_\alpha) L\|_F^2\). This reduces to row‑wise problems \(\min_q \|R q - y\|_2^2\) where \(R = L^T\) is upper triangular. The weighted \(\ell_2\) norm diagonalizes as \(\sum_j L_{j,j}^2 |q_j - c_j(q_{j+1:n})|^2\) (Proposition A.1), leading to greedy rounding from the last column inward (SNRQ).

**Core assumption:**  
The Hessian \(H\) is SPD so that \(L\) exists; the quantization grid is linear/equispaced enabling nearest‑level rounding.

**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: “preserving the standard quadratic structure used in PTQ” and “derives a simple successive rounding procedure”.  
- [section]: Theorem 4.1 and Section 4.2.1 (SNRQ algorithm) detail the triangular proxy and greedy rule.  
- [corpus]: Related papers GPTQ, LDLQ, and OJBKQ also rely on quadratic proxies; OJBKQ similarly uses a structured objective, supporting the premise that such reformulation is effective.

**Break condition:**  
If \(H\) is near‑singular, rounding becomes unstable; if beam search over columns is used without permutation, column order may affect performance (though permutation heuristics mitigate this).

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:**  
Extending the greedy successive rounding to a bounded beam search (K‑SNRQ) improves reconstruction quality by exploring multiple partial assignments, with a tunable trade‑off between compute cost and accuracy.

**Mechanism:**  
At each column \(j\) (processed from \(n\) to \(1\)), instead of selecting the single best \(q_j\) for each suffix, keep the top‑\(K\) partial column strings per output row (Algorithm 3). The incremental cost is \(\Delta_j = R_{j,j}^2 |q_j - c_j|^2\). For each beam, all \(A\) possible levels are evaluated; scores are updated and \(K\)-best retained per row. This explores more of the discrete tree while using the exact proxy.

**Core assumption:**  
Off‑diagonal couplings in \(R\) make early greedy decisions suboptimal; limited lookahead can correct these errors, and beam search provides that lookahead at modest overhead.

**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: “bounded‑search extension that improves rounding quality with controllable overhead.”  
- [section]: Figure 5 (performance‑time trade‑off) and Section 5.3 quantify improvements as \(K\) increases.  
- [corpus]: AQLM and QuIP# use beam or tree search for vector/codebook selection; the corpus includes such methods, confirming that discrete search is beneficial in PTQ, though our beam operates on column‑wise rounding rather than codebook assignments.

**Break condition:**  
If beam width \(K\) is too small, search gains vanish; if \(K\) is too large, memory grows linearly with \(K\) and may exceed device limits; also, for highly diagonal \(R\), beam search brings little benefit.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  • Calibration data loader (runs calibration set through partially quantized model).  
  • Hessian / cross‑moment builder (computes \(H = X_q X_q^T + \lambda I\) and \(C_\alpha = X_\alpha X_q^T\)).  
  • Shifted target calculator (\(M_\alpha = W C_\alpha H^{-1}\)).  
  • Column permutation module (orders columns by diagonal of \(H\) to prioritize high‑curvature features).  
  • Rounding engine (implements SNRQ greedy or K‑SNRQ beam search).  
  • Quantization grid module (defines per‑group or per‑channel levels).  
  • Output wrapper (undoes permutation and writes quantized weights).

- **Critical path:**  
  1. For a given layer, collect \(X_f\) and \(X_q\) on the calibration set (with preceding layers quantized for \(X_q\)).  
  2. Choose \(\alpha\): either closed‑form (Prop 3.2) or sampled (Beta distribution). Compute \(X_\alpha\).  
  3. Compute \(H\) and \(C_\alpha\); perform Cholesky \(L = \text{chol}(H)\). Apply permutation \(P\) (based on \(\text{diag}(H)\)) to all tensors.  
  4. Compute \(W_{\text{perm}} = W P\) and \(M_{\alpha,\text{perm}} = W_{\text{perm}} (C_{\alpha,\text{perm}}) H_{\text{perm}}^{-1}\).  
  5. Round columns from \(j = n\) down to \(1\):  
      – Compute suffix‑corrected center \(\tilde{W}_{:,j} = M_{\alpha,\text{perm},:,j} + (M_{\alpha,\text{perm},:,j+1:} - Q_{:,j+1:}) * L_{j+1:,j}\).  
      – For SNRQ: \(Q_{:,j} = \text{nearest}(\tilde{W}_{:,j})\).  
      – For K‑SNRQ: maintain a beam of size \(K\) (Alg 3), expand all levels, update scores, prune to \(K\)-best per row.  
  6. Undo permutation: \(Q_{\text{out}} = Q_{\text{perm}} P^T\).  
  7. Proceed to next layer.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  • \(\alpha\): closed‑form (SNRQ‑C) is cheap but deterministic; sampled (SNRQ‑S) adds negligible overhead and reduces run‑to‑run variance.  
  • Beam width \(K\): larger \(K\) improves quality (Fig 5) but increases memory (\(\propto K\)) and search time (moderate due to batching).  
  • Permutation: curvature‑ordered columns (based on \(\text{diag}(H)\)) improve greedy performance; omitting permutation may degrade results.  
  • Damping \(\lambda\): added to \(H\) for numerical stability; too large may under‑fit, too small may cause ill‑conditioning.  
  • Block‑wise lazy updates (Alg 3) reduce number of large matrix multiplies but require careful indexing.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  • Cholesky failure (non‑SPD \(H\)) → runtime error; mitigate by increasing \(\lambda\).  
  • NaNs in \(H\) or \(C_\alpha\) → calibration set too small or contains outliers; check preprocessing.  
  • Very high reconstruction residual \(\|(W - Q) X_q\|_F\) → rounding failed; consider larger \(K\) or different \(\alpha\).  
  • Perplexity far above baseline (e.g., > 10% gap) → calibration mismatch or poor \(\alpha\)/beam choice.  
  • Out‑of‑memory during K‑SNRQ → \(K\) too large; reduce beam or use gradient checkpointing.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Main benchmarking** (Table 1): Per‑group weight‑only quantization on LLaMA‑2/3 (3/4‑bit) comparing SNRQ‑C and SNRQ‑S against GPTQ, GPTAQ, LDLQ. Shows consistent perplexity/accuracy improvements and 29–32% speedup over GPTAQ.  
  2. **Ablation of rounding search** (Table 6): Fixing calibration (SNRQ‑S) and varying beam \(K\) makes clear that beam search further lowers perplexity beyond greedy, especially under a strong objective.  
  3. **Beam trade‑off curves** (Figure 5): For L2‑7B/13B, plots perplexity vs search time and peak GPU memory as \(K\) increases; demonstrates a clear Pareto frontier where modest \(K\) (2–4) already recovers most gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: What theoretical principle governs the optimal selection of the interpolation parameter \(\alpha\) for regularized asymmetric calibration in PTQ?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper empirically finds that intermediate \(\alpha\) values (e.g., around 0.5) yield the best perplexity and accuracy (Figure 1, Figure 7) but provides only heuristics (closed‑form per‑module update, stochastic sampling). The decomposition (Proposition 3.1) explains the effect as a regularizer, yet no theoretical analysis predicts the optimal \(\alpha\) for a given model or bit‑width.
- Why unresolved: Without a theoretical guideline, \(\alpha\) must be tuned or sampled, which may not generalize across architectures or calibration sets. The closed‑form update (8) optimizes a local objective that does not directly correlate with final task performance.
- What evidence would resolve it: A derivation linking \(\alpha\) to measurable statistics of the activations (e.g., mismatch \(\|X_f - X_q\|\)) and Hessian curvature, or a bound showing that \(\alpha\) minimizes an end‑to‑end distortion. Alternatively, an adaptive scheme that accurately predicts optimal \(\alpha\) from calibration data and is validated across diverse LLMs.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal column permutation for successive rounding under the triangular least‑squares proxy, and how much does the current diagonal Hessian‑based heuristic affect quantization quality?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper chooses the column order by sorting increasing diagonal entries of \(H\) (Section A.1), noting this is exact only for diagonal \(H\) and otherwise a heuristic. The effect of off‑diagonal couplings on the final rounding error is not analyzed.
- Why unresolved: The greedy rounding solution depends on the order, and a heuristic that ignores column correlations may be suboptimal. The magnitude of potential improvement from a better permutation is unknown, especially for models with strongly correlated weight columns.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical characterization of the residual error as a function of the permutation, e.g., a bound that relates the greedy error to the optimal order in terms of \(H\)'s condition number or sparsity. Empirically, systematic comparison of many orderings across layers to quantify the typical gap.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the approximation guarantees of K‑SNRQ (beam search rounding) as a function of beam width \(K\) and the problem conditioning (e.g., \(H\) or \(L\) matrices)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper introduces beam search and shows consistent gains with larger \(K\) (Figures 5,6) but provides no theoretical analysis of its suboptimality gap relative to the optimal discrete solution. The triangular objective structure is unique to this quantization formulation.
- Why unresolved: The absence of guarantees makes it difficult to choose \(K\) efficiently or to understand when beam search is most beneficial. The interaction between \(K\), bit‑width, and the triangular off‑diagonal weights remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: A proof bounding the worst‑case ratio (or absolute difference) between the \(K\)-beam solution and the optimal solution in terms of the largest singular value of \(L\) and the quantization grid size. Could also include empirical scaling laws showing error decay with \(K\) for different models and bit‑widths.

## Limitations
- Calibration data: fixed small set; performance may vary with size/domain.
- Model coverage: only LLaMA‑2/3 tested; other architectures (e.g., Mistral) unexplored.
- Beam scalability: memory \(\propto K \times\) layer size; for 70B models, feasible \(K\) may be very small, limiting gains.

## Confidence
- \(\alpha\)-interpolation regularizer (Mechanism 1): **Medium**. Prop 3.1 is mathematically sound, but empirical regularization effect is inferred, not directly isolated via ablation.
- Triangular LS & successive rounding (Mechanism 2): **High**. Thm 4.1 is rigorous; consistent improvements over GPTQ across multiple models/bit‑widths.
- Beam search extension (Mechanism 3): **Medium**. Gains demonstrated on ≤13B models, but scalability to very large layers remains unproven.
- Overall outcome (SNRQ‑S surpasses GPTAQ with 29–32% speedup): **Medium**. Claims rely on implementation details; independent replication required to rule out bias.
