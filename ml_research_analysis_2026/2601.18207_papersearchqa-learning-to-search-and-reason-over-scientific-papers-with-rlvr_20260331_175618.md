# PaperSearchQA: Learning to Search and Reason over Scientific Papers with RLVR

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.18207
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18207
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Primary result:** RLVR-trained Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct achieves 51.0% accuracy on PaperSearchQA test, beating RAG (36.5%) and PaperQA2 (37.1%) by >14 points.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) for search agents has been confined to general‑domain QA due to a lack of suitable scientific environments. This paper addresses that gap by constructing a search corpus of 16 million PubMed abstracts and a factoid QA dataset, PaperSearchQA, containing 60k samples with unambiguous single‑entity answers. Agents are trained using Search‑R1 (GRPO) to interleave reasoning and retrieval via a minimal interface (``, `<search>`, `<answer>`). On PaperSearchQA, Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct with RLVR reaches 51.0% accuracy, outperforming RAG (36.5%), PaperQA2 (37.1%) and direct/CoT baselines by more than 14 points; similar gains appear on BioASQ (44.8% vs 32.8%). Analysis reveals that semantic retrieval (e5) yields only marginal improvements, paraphrasing substantially increases difficulty, and agent traces show emergent behaviors such as explicit planning, pre‑search reasoning, and self‑verification.

## Method Summary
The authors built a retrieval corpus of 16 million PubMed abstracts (titles + abstracts) and the PaperSearchQA dataset. Each QA pair is single‑hop, factoid, and paired with a list of answer synonyms for exact‑match verification; 50% of questions are paraphrased to discourage trivial keyword matching. Training uses the Search‑R1 framework: a policy LLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-Instruct) generates tokens interleaving `<think>`, `<search>` (which triggers retrieval), and `<answer>`. The rollout executor intercepts `<search>`, pauses generation, queries a retriever (BM25 or e5) for top‑k documents, and appends them as `<information>` tokens. The absolute reward (1 if predicted answer matches any synonym, else 0) flows back through all non‑retrieved tokens via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), with a KL penalty to a reference model. The minimal system prompt imposes no explicit reasoning steps, allowing strategies to emerge from reward optimization alone.

## Key Results
- On PaperSearchQA test, Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct + RLVR obtains 51.0% accuracy, surpassing RAG (36.5%) and PaperQA2 (37.1%) by >14 points.
- On BioASQ factoid, RLVR agent scores 44.8% vs PaperQA2’s 32.8% (12‑point gain).
- Semantic retrieval (e5) provides ≤2 points improvement over BM25; paraphrasing reduces accuracy by ~12 points (57.2% → 44.9%), highlighting dataset difficulty.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: RLVR drives emergent search behaviors**  
Outcome‑only reinforcement learning using GRPO reinforces token sequences that lead to correct final answers. Because the agent controls when to emit `<search>` queries and must interpret retrieved documents, successful strategies (explicit planning, pre‑search reasoning, self‑verification) are reinforced while ineffective ones decay. The minimal system prompt provides only interface tags, allowing strategies to emerge from reward optimization. The 16M‑abstract corpus and single‑hop factoid design ensure sufficient variance in search outcomes for credit assignment. Evidence: Abstract mentions emergent behaviors; Section 4.3 Figure 4 shows concrete planning and verification traces. The process breaks if rewards become too noisy or the dataset lacks diversity, causing the agent to converge on a single brittle strategy.

**Mechanism 2: Unambiguous factoid design provides clean training signal**  
PaperSearchQA questions have single‑entity answers with synonym lists, enabling exact‑match verification. Paraphrasing (50% of questions) prevents trivial keyword matching, forcing the agent to rely on comprehension or retrieval rather than surface patterns. Single‑hop ensures the answer resides in one retrieved document, avoiding complex multi‑hop reward shaping. Evidence: Section 3.1 describes factoid design, synonym verification, and paraphrasing as a mitigation for "easy keyword matching." If paraphrasing distorts semantics or questions require multi‑hop/list answers, the exact‑match reward becomes noisy and inadequate.

**Mechanism 3: End‑to‑end retrieval integration enables adaptive querying**  
During rollouts, the policy decides when and what to search for. The retriever returns top‑k documents, which are appended to the context; the reward propagates through all generated tokens (except retrieved ones) via GRPO. This end‑to‑end setup lets the agent discover that searching is useful even for questions it partially knows (e.g., verification behavior). Evidence: Section 3.4 describes the `<search>` interface; Section 4.2 notes that semantic retrieval yields only marginal gains, suggesting either query formulation remains syntactic or parametric knowledge already covers many answers. The mechanism fails if the retriever consistently misses relevant documents or if corpus overlap with pretraining is too high, making retrieval add little value.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**  
- *Why needed here:* Enables training search agents using only final answer correctness, avoiding costly labeling of intermediate steps.  
- *Quick check:* How does RLVR differ from supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) in terms of training signal?

**Concept: Factoid QA Design for Outcome Supervision**  
- *Why needed here:* Outcome‑only reward requires unambiguous, automatically verifiable answers; single‑entity factoids with synonym lists provide this while avoiding noisy fuzzy evaluation.  
- *Quick check:* Why are binary (yes/no) questions avoided in PaperSearchQA despite being verifiable? (Hint: low random baseline and list bias.)

**Concept: Retrieval‑Augmented Agents vs. Static RAG**  
- *Why needed here:* Search agents interleave reasoning and retrieval dynamically, learning when to search via RL. This contrasts with static RAG where retrieval occurs once before generation.  
- *Quick check:* What is a key architectural difference between a standard RAG system and a Search‑R1 agent?

**Concept: Emergent Behaviors via Minimal Prompting**  
- *Why needed here:* The system prompt gives only the `<search>` interface, forcing the policy to discover strategies (planning, verification) via RL rather than mimicry.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Policy LLM (Qwen2.5‑Instruct) generates tokens with <think>, <search>, <answer> tags → Rollout executor intercepts <search> and halts generation → Retriever service (BM25/e5) queries 16M PubMed index and returns top‑k abstracts → Abstracts injected as <information> tokens → Generation resumes → When <answer> appears, Reward verifier compares (case‑insensitive, articles stripped) against ground‑truth synonym list → GRPO trainer updates policy using group‑normalized advantages over all non‑retrieved tokens with KL penalty → Reference model (initial policy) provides regularization.

**Critical path:**  
1. For each training question, policy generates tokens until `<search>` or `<answer>`.  
2. On `<search>`, rollout halts; retriever returns top‑k documents; documents are injected as `<information>`.  
3. Generation resumes; eventually emits `<answer>`.  
4. Reward computed (1 if answer matches any synonym, else 0).  
5. GRPO updates policy parameters using advantage‑weighted gradients over all non‑retrieved tokens.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Outcome vs. process supervision:* Outcome‑only is cheap but noisy; process supervision (labeling steps) could guide learning but is expensive.  
- *Retriever choice:* BM25 (lightweight, 2.6 GB, CPU‑friendly) vs. e5 (93 GB, needs 2×A100); e5 gave ≤2‑point gain despite higher cost.  
- *Factoid vs. complex QA:* Factoid enables exact‑match reward but limits expressiveness; multi‑hop/list answers would require fuzzy or LLM‑as‑judge rewards.  
- *Dataset homogeneity:* 60k factoid samples are scalable but lead to convergence on a single strategy (planning+keyword extraction), reducing behavioral diversity.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Reward collapse (GRPO reward drops to zero mid‑training), often mitigated by tuning KL penalty or group size.  
- Out‑of‑memory with e5 retriever; requires eight A100 80GB for training, two for inference.  
- Lower‑than‑expected accuracy (e.g., ~30%), indicating agent hasn’t learned retrieval; likely needs more steps or hyperparameter adjustment.  
- Lack of post‑retrieval reasoning: agent answers immediately after seeing documents without explicit `<think>` about their contents.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline establishment:** Compare direct, CoT, RAG, PaperQA2, and Search‑R1 on PaperSearchQA and BioASQ to quantify RLVR gains (Table 1).  
2. **Retriever ablation:** Evaluate BM25 vs. e5 for RAG and Search‑R1; semantic retrieval provides only marginal benefit (≤2 points).  
3. **Paraphrasing impact:** Split test set into paraphrased vs. non‑paraphrased; RL‑trained model scores 57.2 on non‑paraphrased vs. 44.9 on paraphrased, showing increased difficulty and the importance of paraphrasing.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
- **Question:** How can RLVR‑trained search agents be effectively scaled beyond single‑hop factoid QA to handle multi‑hop reasoning, list‑of‑entities answers, and extended summarization while maintaining reliable automatic verification?  
- **Basis in paper:** Explicitly stated in Discussion (Section 5) and Limitations (Section 7): current scope limited to single‑hop factoid; multi‑hop QA (e.g., BioHopR), list answers, and extended summaries require more complex planning and fuzzy reward models.  
- **Why unresolved:** RLVR currently relies on simple exact‑match rewards, which are inadequate for multi‑faceted or reasoning‑heavy answers. Designing verifiable rewards that correlate with human judgment for these types is an open challenge.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** New benchmark datasets (multi‑hop, list, summary) paired with proven reward model architectures (e.g., LLM‑as‑judge) that demonstrate significant performance gains on those benchmarks using RLVR.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question:** What specific retrieval‑augmented behaviors and tool‑use strategies (e.g., query decomposition, citation traversal, source reliability assessment) are most effective for scientific QA, and can RLVR automatically discover and optimize them?  
- **Basis in paper:** Discussion (Section 5) proposes future systems using “tools and metadata that would be used by real scientists,” mentioning citation traversal and source reliability metrics. Current agent exhibits only simple patterns (planning, pre‑search reasoning, verification).  
- **Why unresolved:** The environment and reward only incentivize final answer accuracy over a single abstract corpus; there is no mechanism to reward sophisticated strategies like following citation graphs, assessing study quality, or cross‑referencing figures/data. The relative value of such behaviors is unknown.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extending PaperSearchQA to include tools for citation lookup, full‑text access (figures), and metadata (journal impact, citations). Training with RLVR and analyzing traces to identify which discovered strategies correlate with higher accuracy on questions requiring evidence synthesis.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question:** Why do semantic retrievers (e.g., e5) provide only marginal benefits over syntactic retrievers (BM25) in this scientific domain, and how can retrieval be improved for highly technical literature?  
- **Basis in paper:** Section 4.2 states: “Semantic retrieval gives little benefit over syntactic retrieval… performance benefit was minor – within 2 points.” Authors hypothesize that even paraphrased questions must include certain technical keywords, or that e5 under‑performs for scientific domains.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper lacks systematic diagnosis of why semantic retrieval underperforms; it is unclear whether the bottleneck lies in the embedding model, the agent’s query formulation, or the nature of factual assertions (which may rely on precise terminology). Without diagnosis, improvement is guesswork.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A thorough error analysis comparing BM25 and e5 retrieval success rates across paraphrasing levels and technical specificity. Coupled with experiments using domain‑specific embedding models (e.g., fine‑tuned on PubMed) or hybrid strategies, measuring impact on agent performance to isolate the bottleneck.

**Open Question 4**  
- **Question:** To what extent do performance gains from larger models (3B to 7B) in this RLVR setting stem from improved parametric knowledge versus enhanced search/reasoning capabilities?  
- **Basis in paper:** Section 4.2 analyzes: “Superior performance with model size is likely due to knowledge… Search‑R1 outperforms CoT by 20.2 points for the 3B model and 21.4 for the 7B model. This suggests the performance gain is due to improved parametric knowledge, not superior capabilities in query formulation or comprehension.”  
- **Why unresolved:** This conclusion is indirect: it notes that retrieval‑free baselines also scale with size and that the absolute gain from retrieval is similar for 3B and 7B. It does not isolate whether larger models formulate better queries or comprehend documents more effectively; evidence remains correlational.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled ablations where parametric knowledge is fixed (e.g., using same model for query generation but intervening on internal representations) or isolating search capabilities (e.g., evaluating query quality independently). Alternatively, comparing models with similar parametric knowledge but different architectures or RLVR training histories.

**Open Question 5**  
- **Question:** How can automatic data generation pipelines for scientific QA (like theirs) ensure that questions are truly generalizable and not overfit to the specific context or claims of a single source abstract?  
- **Basis in paper:** Limitations (Section 7) identify a key risk: “it is challenging to infer a ‘general QA’ from a single specific abstract… we cannot be sure that ‘gene mutation X’ is the only answer.” Expert review found this rare but not systematically mitigated.  
- **Why unresolved:** Generating open‑domain QA from single documents risks questions with unstated assumptions or incorrect implications of uniqueness/exhaustiveness. A limited expert review cannot quantify prevalence across 60k samples; the pipeline may propagate inherent over‑specificity.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A rigorous, large‑scale re‑annotation of a PaperSearchQA subset by domain experts to flag invalid questions (e.g., those implying a single answer when the abstract only presents one instance). Statistical analysis would quantify prevalence; a solution could involve pipeline modifications like requiring a simulated multi‑document check during generation or post‑hoc filtering with a verifier trained to detect over‑specific questions.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter incompleteness:** GRPO parameters (β, group size G, learning rate, optimizer) are not fully disclosed, hindering exact replication.
- **Retriever and preprocessing opacity:** e5 model version, number of retrieved documents (k), and details of synonym generation and train/test splits (paraphrased vs. not) are insufficiently described.
- **Potential reward noise:** Exact‑match verification may fail on paraphrased questions if synonym lists are incomplete; paraphrasing could distort semantics, introducing noise.

## Confidence

- **Emergent search behaviors (planning, verification):** *Medium* – supported by qualitative traces (Figure 4) but not quantified.
- **Unambiguous training signal from PaperSearchQA design:** *Medium* – factoid format reduces ambiguity, yet synonym coverage and paraphrasing quality lack rigorous validation.
- **Adaptive retrieval integration via end‑to‑end RL:** *Medium* – RAG baselines are outperformed, but marginal semantic gains and persistent over‑reliance on parametric knowledge suggest limited retrieval learning.
- **Main performance result (51.0% vs 36.5% RAG):** *High* – strong quantitative evidence on two benchmarks, though exact replication depends on unspecified hyperparameters.
