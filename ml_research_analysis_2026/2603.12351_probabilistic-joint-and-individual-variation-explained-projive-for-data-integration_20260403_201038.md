# Probabilistic Joint and Individual Variation Explained (ProJIVE) for Data Integration

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12351
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12351
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** ProJIVE is a probabilistic model for joint and individual variation across multiple datasets, extending probabilistic PCA via an EM algorithm, achieving superior subspace recovery in simulations and identifying a biologically meaningful joint component in ADNI data.

## Executive Summary
ProJIVE is a novel probabilistic method for multi-dataset integration that decomposes each dataset into joint (shared across datasets) and individual (dataset-specific) variation components. It extends probabilistic principal component analysis (pPCA) to a multi-view setting using an expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm that treats latent subject scores as missing data, enabling maximum likelihood estimation of loading matrices and noise variances. In comprehensive simulations involving Gaussian and non-Gaussian data across varying signal strengths and dimensions, ProJIVE achieved lower chordal distances between true and estimated subspaces than competing methods like AJIVE, R.JIVE, GIPCA, and D-CCA, with especially strong performance in mixed-dimension scenarios. When applied to ADNI multimodal brain data (morphometry and cognition), ProJIVE extracted a joint component strongly associated with Alzheimer's diagnosis, ApoE4 genotype, and PET biomarkers; its loadings highlighted brain regions such as the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, demonstrating the method's ability to uncover biologically meaningful integrated signals.

## Method Summary
ProJIVE models each dataset \(X_k\) (variables \(p_k\) by subjects \(n\)) as \(X_k = W_{Jk} Z + W_{Ik} B_k + \varepsilon_k\), where \(Z \in \mathbb{R}^{r_J \times n}\) are joint scores, \(B_k \in \mathbb{R}^{r_{Ik} \times n}\) are individual scores for dataset \(k\), and \(\varepsilon_k\) is isotropic Gaussian noise with variance \(\sigma_k^2 I\). The latent scores are assumed standard normal and mutually orthogonal: \(\text{Cov}(Z, B_k)=0\) and \(\text{Cov}(B_k, B_{k'})=0\) for \(k \neq k'\). Parameters are estimated via an EM algorithm: in the E-step, conditional expectations of scores and their outer products given observed data and current parameters are computed; in the M-step, loading matrices and noise variances are updated using closed-form equations derived from maximizing the expected complete-data log-likelihood. Initialization can use AJIVE, Cholesky decomposition of covariances, or random draws; rank selection for total and joint subspaces is critical and may rely on scree plots or permutation tests. After convergence, optional rotation (e.g., Varimax) enhances interpretability.

## Key Results
- In simulations across diverse conditions (Gaussian/non-Gaussian signals, mixed dimensions, varying signal strengths), ProJIVE achieved lower chordal distances between true and estimated subspaces compared to AJIVE, R.JIVE, GIPCA, and D-CCA, with particularly substantial improvements in mixed-dimension settings.
- On the ADNI dataset integrating brain morphometry (245 features) and cognition (22 features), ProJIVE identified one joint component whose subject scores showed strong associations with Alzheimer's diagnosis (pseudo-R²=0.20), ApoE4 genotype (pseudo-R²=0.03), and PET biomarkers (AV45 R²=0.15, FDG R²=0.29).
- Joint loadings revealed biologically relevant regions including hippocampal volume and entorhinal cortex thickness, demonstrating that ProJIVE extracts meaningful integrated signals correlated with expensive existing biomarkers.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The EM algorithm provides a maximum likelihood framework to simultaneously estimate joint and individual subspaces, improving accuracy over sequential or non-probabilistic methods.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm treats latent subject scores as missing data. In the E-step, it computes the conditional expectations of the scores and their outer products given the observed data and current parameters. In the M-step, it updates the loading matrices (`W_Jk`, `W_Ik`) and noise variances using closed-form equations derived from setting the expected complete-data log-likelihood gradient to zero. This joint estimation avoids error propagation from sequential steps.
- **Core assumption:** The latent scores (joint `z_i` and individual `b_ik`) are jointly Gaussian, and the noise `ε_ik` is isotropic Gaussian with block-specific variance `D_k = σ_k^2 I`.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Our maximum likelihood approach simultaneously estimates joint and individual components, which can lead to greater accuracy compared to other methods."
  - [section 2.5] "We develop an Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm to estimate the variable loadings, noise variances, and subject scores... yielded closed form solutions."
  - [corpus] Weak direct corpus support for the EM mechanism; neighboring papers (e.g., DeepJIVE, AJIVE) discuss integration challenges but not the specific EM parameterization of ProJIVE.
- **Break condition:** The algorithm may fail to converge or produce poor local maxima if initialized poorly, especially when the isotropic noise assumption is violated or when the true joint/individual subspaces are not mutually orthogonal in the latent variable space (see Identifiability Theorem 1).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Enforcing mutual orthogonality of latent scores (Cov(`z_i`, `b_ik`) = 0 and Cov(`b_ik`, `b_ik'`) = 0 for k≠k') in the probabilistic model ensures a clean separation of joint and individual variation, which is a defining feature of the JIVE framework.
- **Mechanism:** The Gaussian model with independent standard normal scores inherently imposes that the joint and individual latent factors are uncorrelated. This contrasts with matrix decomposition methods like AJIVE that enforce orthogonality on the estimated subspaces via Euclidean inner products. By modeling orthogonality at the level of random variables, the EM update equations naturally decorrelate the estimated subspaces during optimization.
- **Core assumption:** The true underlying statistical structure consists of additive, orthogonal contributions from joint and individual sources. Mis-specification (e.g., correlated joint and individual effects in reality) can lead to "leakage" where the model misattributes variation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.3] "We assume Cov (z_i, b_ik) = 0, which is the random variable analogue of the orthogonality between joint and individual subspaces that is assumed in JIVE... (iii) Mutual orthogonality: Cov(z_i, b_ik) = 0 and Cov(b_ik, b_ik') = 0."
  - [section 2.4] Discusses identifiability breakdown when non-orthogonal transformations exist, implying the orthogonality assumption is crucial for unique decomposition.
- **Break condition:** If the true data-generating process has non-orthogonal joint and individual factors (e.g., a feature is influenced by both shared and unique processes that correlate), the ProJIVE decomposition may not be uniquely identifiable and the interpretation of components as purely "joint" or "individual" is compromised.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Under isotropic noise (`D_k = σ_k^2 I`), the model parameters are identifiable up to a non-singular transformation `T_1` for the joint loadings, and any non-orthogonal `T_1` leads to non-identifiability of the split between joint and individual variation.
- **Mechanism:** Theorem 1 provides necessary and sufficient conditions for two ProJIVE models to produce the same likelihood. It shows that error variances `σ_k^2` are identifiable, but joint loadings are only identifiable up to a non-singular transformation `T_1`. Critically, if `T_1` is not orthogonal, the individual loading matrices can "absorb" some joint signal (via terms like `W_J1(I - T_1 T_1^⊤)W_J1^⊤`), making the total variance explained by joint vs. individual components non-unique.
- **Core assumption:** Isotropic noise (`D_k = σ_k^2 I`) simplifies the identifiability analysis. The existence of a non-orthogonal `T_1` satisfying the PSD conditions (Eq. 5-6) breaks the separation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.4] "Theorem 1... Then f_Φ = f_Φ* if and only if... 2. There exists an r_J × r_J non-singular matrix T_1 such that... 3. W_I1* is defined such that..."
  - [section 2.4] "Linearly dependent columns between W_Jk and W_Ik occur by construction in the D-CCA decomposition... Then defining W_I1* = √(2-λ^2) W_I1 and W_I2* = √(2-1/λ^2) W_I2, we see how the decomposition of joint and individual variation is not unique."
- **Break condition:** Identifiability fails if there exists a non-orthogonal `T_1` such that `W_I1 W_I1^⊤ + W_J1(I - T_1 T_1^⊤) W_J1^⊤ ⪰ 0` and similarly for dataset 2. This occurs when the column spaces of `W_Jk` and `W_Ik` are not linearly independent (i.e., overlap). In practice, this suggests that components should be chosen so that joint and individual subspaces are well-separated in terms of variance contribution.

## Foundational Learning

### Probabilistic Principal Component Analysis (pPCA)
- **Why needed here:** ProJIVE is a direct extension of pPCA (Bishop, 1999) to multiple datasets. Understanding pPCA—the model `x = Wz + ε` with `z ~ N(0,I)`, `ε ~ N(0, σ^2 I)`—is essential to grasp the single-dataset building block. The EM algorithm for pPCA is directly adapted.
- **Quick check question:** In pPCA, what is the relationship between the MLE of `W` and the classical PCA loading matrix? (Answer: They span the same subspace but are not identical; pPCA's `W` is not necessarily orthogonal.)

### Expectation-Maximization (EM) Algorithm
- **Why needed here:** The core estimation engine in ProJIVE is an EM algorithm that handles the latent scores (`z_i`, `b_ik`). The engineer must understand how to derive the E-step (conditional expectations given `x_i`) and M-step (parameter updates) for a multivariate normal model with block structure.
- **Quick check question:** Why does the M-step for `W_k` involve a matrix inversion of the sum of `E(θ_ik θ_ik^⊤ | x_i)`? (Answer: The expected complete-data log-likelihood is quadratic in `W_k`; setting the gradient to zero yields `W_k` proportional to `∑ x_ik E(θ_ik^⊤|x_i)` times the inverse of `∑ E(θ_ik θ_ik^⊤|x_i)`.)

### Subspace Geometry & Chordal Distance
- **Why needed here:** The primary evaluation metric in simulations is the chordal distance between true and estimated subspaces (for scores and loadings). This metric quantifies alignment of linear subspaces of potentially different dimensions.
- **Quick check question:** What does a chordal distance of 0 indicate about two subspaces? (Answer: They are identical. A distance of 1 indicates they are orthogonal.)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Centered/normalized datasets -> Rank selection -> Parameter initialization (noise variances from pPCA; loadings from AJIVE/Cholesky/random) -> EM iterations (E-step then M-step) -> Parameter estimates -> (optional) Rotation for interpretability.

### Critical Path
1. Preprocess: center and optionally normalize each dataset.
2. Choose ranks: total per block and joint rank \(r_J\).
3. Initialize: set initial noise variances (e.g., via pPCA) and loadings (e.g., AJIVE solution).
4. Run EM: iterate until convergence (log-likelihood change < tolerance or max iterations reached).
5. Rotate: apply Varimax or similar to improve interpretability of loadings.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Speed vs Accuracy:** EM is iterative and slower than non-iterative methods (AJIVE, D-CCA) but aims for higher accuracy; cost scales with total features and ranks.
- **Parsimony vs Fit:** Higher ranks capture more signal but risk overfitting and identifiability breakdown; use information criteria or scree plots.
- **Normalization:** Use unit-variance when variables have different scales/units to equalize influence; avoid when variance itself is biologically meaningful (e.g., gene expression).

### Failure Signatures
- **Non-convergence:** EM may cycle or stall; monitor log-likelihood monotonicity.
- **Sign flipping:** Loadings may change signs across runs due to rotational invariance; enforce sign convention or rotation.
- **Rank over-estimation:** Excessive ranks cause leakage of joint variation into individual subspaces (Theorem 1), harming interpretability and prediction.
- **Mixed-dimension instability:** Performance sensitive to feature dimension imbalance; validate on held-out data if possible.

### First Experiments
1. **Synthetic Recovery:** Generate data from the ProJIVE model (Gaussian scores, isotropic noise) with known \(r_J\), \(r_{Ik}\), dimensions, and signal strengths. Run ProJIVE with various initializations; compare estimated subspaces to truth via chordal distance.
2. **Robustness to Misspecification:** Simulate data with non-Gaussian scores (e.g., mixtures) or anisotropic noise. Apply ProJIVE and compare performance to AJIVE/R.JIVE to assess robustness to model violations.
3. **ADNI Replication:** Apply ProJIVE to ADNI morphometry (245 features) and cognition (22 features) (\(n=587\)). Verify joint component associations with diagnosis, ApoE4, PET (AV45/FDG) and biological relevance of loadings (hippocampus, entorhinal cortex). Compare individual subspaces for dataset-specific features.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can ProJIVE be extended to non-Gaussian latent score distributions (e.g., mixture models), and what algorithmic strategies (e.g., variational inference) can make likelihood maximization tractable for such extensions?
- **Basis in paper:** Future work could extend ProJIVE to non-Gaussian settings. The independent factor analysis likelihood could be extended but is difficult to maximize.
- **Why unresolved:** The current EM relies on Gaussian assumptions for closed-form updates; non-Gaussian distributions introduce intractable integrals.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A scalable approximate inference algorithm (e.g., VAE or mixture EM) for non-Gaussian ProJIVE, validated on simulations with known non-Gaussian signals and real data where non-Gaussianity is expected.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What computational optimizations (e.g., stochastic EM, Krylov subspace methods, GPU acceleration) are necessary to make ProJIVE feasible for ultra-high-dimensional data (\(p > 10,000\)), and do these optimizations preserve estimation accuracy?
- **Basis:** Current R implementation not feasible for \(p_2=10,000\); computational costs were greater than AJIVE and D-CCA, but less than R.JIVE and GIPCA in mixed-dimension settings.
- **Why unresolved:** EM updates involve costly matrix inverses scaling poorly with feature dimension.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A re-implementation with optimized linear algebra (sparse matrices, block-wise) that processes \(p \ge 10,000\), benchmarked against original for accuracy and speed.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can information criteria (e.g., BIC, AIC) or other data-driven methods be efficiently and reliably used for joint and individual rank selection in ProJIVE, given the combinatorial search space over \(r_J\) and \(\{r_{Ik}\}\)?
- **Basis:** Since a likelihood is defined, one can use information criteria, but involves all combinations of \(r_J\), \(r_{Ik}\), which is challenging at best.
- **Why unresolved:** Exhaustive evaluation is computationally prohibitive; accuracy and consistency of criteria for this model unstudied.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A simulation study exploring common criteria (BIC, AIC, CV) under various SNRs, with heuristics (e.g., greedy forward selection) to reduce search cost, showing recovery of true ranks and predictive performance.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the grouping of variables into pre-defined "datasets" affect ProJIVE's decomposition, and can we derive formal guidelines for dataset construction based on variable similarity (correlation structure, measurement scale)?
- **Basis:** Care must be given to the decision of what constitutes a 'dataset.' Treating brain morphometry as a single dataset or splitting had relatively minor impacts on joint scores.
- **Why unresolved:** Choice of dataset boundaries is ad hoc and can alter interpretations; no systematic framework exists.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A theoretical analysis or extensive simulations varying grouping granularity, measuring stability of subspaces and biological relevance of loadings. Development of a diagnostic (e.g., based on cross-dataset correlation or measurement scale homogeneity) to recommend groupings.

## Limitations
- EM algorithm hyperparameters (e.g., max iterations, convergence tolerance) are not reported, potentially affecting convergence behavior.
- Ambiguity in ADNI data preprocessing and sample selection hinders exact replication of the applied analysis.
- Identifiability may break when joint and individual subspaces overlap (Theorem 1), leading to potentially arbitrary decompositions.
