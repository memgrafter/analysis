# Structural Causal Bottleneck Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.08682
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08682
- **Reference Count**: 28
- **Primary Result**: SCBMs mitigate the curse of dimensionality in causal inference by assuming that effects depend only on low-dimensional bottleneck summaries of high-dimensional parent variables, enabling accurate effect estimation with fewer samples.

## Executive Summary
Structural Causal Bottleneck Models (SCBMs) introduce a novel assumption that causal effects between high-dimensional variables depend only on low-dimensional summary statistics (bottlenecks) of the causes. This reduces the effective input dimension for causal mechanisms. Under assumptions of a known DAG, additive noise, and injective effect functions, the bottlenecks are identifiable up to bijective transformations. Estimation proceeds by regressing each child on each parent while conditioning on previously estimated bottlenecks, then factorizing the resulting joint map into bottleneck and effect functions via rank constraints (linear) or encoder-decoder networks (nonlinear). Synthetic experiments show reliable bottleneck recovery (R² > 0.95 linear, ~0.85 nonlinear) and robustness to bottleneck dimension overestimation. In low-sample transfer learning, using estimated bottlenecks as conditioning variables significantly reduces error compared to high-dimensional observed confounders.

## Method Summary
SCBMs extend structural causal models by positing, for each directed edge (i→j) in a known DAG, a deterministic bottleneck function b(i,j) mapping the high-dimensional parent Xi to a low-dimensional latent Z(i,j), and an effect function f(i,j) mapping Z(i,j) and the bottlenecks of other parents of j to the child Xj. The composition m(i,j) = f(i,j)∘b(i,j) represents the conditional mechanism. Estimation follows a topological order: for each child j, process its parents in reverse topological order. For each parent i, regress Xj on Xi while conditioning on (a) the bottlenecks of other parents of j already estimated in the current loop (those higher in the topological order) and (b) the bottlenecks of i’s own parents (estimated earlier). The joint map is then factored: linearly via rank-constrained matrix factorization (select dZ linearly independent columns), or nonlinearly by training an encoder-decoder network with orthogonal weight initialization to prevent rank collapse. The resulting bottleneck functions can be used as low-dimensional summary variables for downstream causal queries.

## Key Results
- Bottleneck variables are accurately recovered from observational data, with average R² scores exceeding 0.95 for linear models and ~0.85 for nonlinear models across varying dimensions and sample sizes.
- True bottleneck dimension acts as a lower bound; overestimation retains most benefits while underestimation degrades performance, contrasting with representation learning methods that may require exact dimension.
- In a transfer learning scenario with scarce joint observations but abundant marginal data, conditioning on estimated bottlenecks (rather than high-dimensional confounders) reduces mean absolute error in effect estimation, especially for small joint sample sizes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Bottleneck Assumption for Dimension Reduction
SCBMs mitigate the curse of dimensionality by assuming that causal effects between high-dimensional variables only depend on low-dimensional summary statistics (bottlenecks) of the causes. For a child Xj and its parents Xi1,...,Xik, there exist deterministic bottleneck functions bj_i mapping each parent Xi to a lower-dimensional variable Z(i,j) such that Xj = fj(Z(i1,j),...,Z(ik,j),ηj). This reduces the effective input dimension for the mechanism functions fj, making regression tasks more sample-efficient. The core assumption is that relevant causal information can be compressed without loss for downstream queries. The break condition occurs when high-dimensional parents influence the child through mechanisms that cannot be captured by low-dimensional summaries, violating the rank condition (linear) or information preservation (nonlinear).

### Mechanism 2: Identifiability via Factorization
Bottleneck variables are identifiable up to bijective transformations from observational data given a known DAG, additive noise, and injective effect functions. The factorization of the joint map m(i,j) = f(i,j)∘b(i,j) is unique up to an invertible transformation on the bottleneck space. Practically, by estimating m(i,j) from data and factoring it, we recover a representation equivalent to the ground-truth bottleneck up to such a transformation. Identifiability holds because conditioning on other bottlenecks and parent bottlenecks ensures that the variation in Xi that is relevant for Xj is captured by the bottleneck. Without additive noise or injectivity, identifiability may fail.

### Mechanism 3: Task-Specific Factorization of Bottlenecks
Factored SCBMs allow different children of the same parent to use different bottleneck spaces Z(i,j), accommodating that different causal effects may rely on different aspects of the high-dimensional parent. This is in contrast to an intrinsic bottleneck shared across all children of a parent. The factorization is valid because each edge (i→j) is estimated independently, conditioned on the appropriate set of other bottlenecks. The break condition is that if an intrinsic bottleneck exists, the factored approach is unnecessarily complex but still valid; however, misspecifying intrinsic vs. factored may reduce statistical efficiency.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Structural Causal Models (SCMs)
**Why needed here**: SCBMs extend classical SCMs, which encode causal assumptions via a DAG and structural equations with independent exogenous noise. Understanding SCMs is prerequisite to grasping how SCBMs inject bottleneck structure into the mechanisms.  
**Quick check**: How does an SCM encode causal assumptions through structural equations and a DAG?

### Concept: Sufficient Dimension Reduction
**Why needed here**: SCBMs are a causal version of sufficient dimension reduction, where the reduced subspace preserves all information about the response variable. Here, the bottleneck is a sufficient statistic of the parent for predicting the child, given other parents' bottlenecks.  
**Quick check**: What's the difference between standard dimension reduction (e.g., PCA) and sufficient dimension reduction for a specific predictive task?

### Concept: Information Bottleneck
**Why needed here**: SCBMs relate to the Information Bottleneck principle, which seeks a compressed representation that maximizes information about a target variable. This provides theoretical grounding and suggests possible objective formulations for learning bottlenecks.  
**Quick check**: What trade-off does the Information Bottleneck objective optimize, and how does it relate to SCBM assumptions about causal sufficiency?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
- **Graph G**: Known directed acyclic graph (DAG) encoding causal relationships.
- **Node spaces Xi**: High-dimensional observed variables for each node i.
- **Bottleneck spaces Z(i,j)**: Low-dimensional latent variables per directed edge (i→j) in the factored case.
- **Bottleneck functions b(i,j)**: Deterministic surjective maps Xi → Z(i,j).
- **Effect functions f(i,j)**: Maps Z(i,j) × Hj → Xj, where Hj are bottlenecks of other parents of j, assumed injective.
- **Exogenous noise ηi**: Independent additive noise per node.
- **Induced SCM**: Composed structural mechanism m(i,j) = f(i,j)∘b(i,j).

### Critical path
1. **Preparation**: Obtain the causal graph G (assumed given) and a topological ordering of its nodes.
2. **Estimate bottlenecks**:
   - For each child node j in topological order (from sources to sinks):
     - For each parent i of j in **reverse** topological order (i.e., from the parent latest in the order to the earliest):
       - Construct conditioning set C = (∪_{k ∈ pa(j)\{i}} Z(k→j)) ∪ (∪_{l ∈ pa(i)} Z(l→i)). Note that only Z(k→j) for parents k of j that have already been processed in the inner loop (those higher in the topological order) are available; parents of i are all available because i was processed earlier in the outer loop.
       - Regress Xj on Xi while conditioning on C to obtain a joint map m̂(i,j) (via linear regression with rank constraint or encoder-decoder network with bottleneck dimension).
       - Factor m̂(i,j) into b̂(i,j) (bottleneck) and f̂(i,j) (effect).
       - Store b̂(i,j) for use in subsequent conditionings.
3. **Downstream use**: For causal effect estimation queries, replace high-dimensional confounders with estimated bottlenecks as conditioning variables.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Bottleneck dimension**: True dimension is a lower bound; overestimation retains benefit but may reduce statistical efficiency; underestimation loses relevant information.
- **Model class**: Linear models offer closed-form factorization and work with fewer samples but may be restrictive. Nonlinear encoder-decoder networks are more flexible but require careful orthogonal initialization to prevent rank collapse and more data.
- **Factored vs. intrinsic**: Factored bottlenecks allow per-edge compression, accommodating child-specific needs, but introduce more parameters. Intrinsic bottlenecks share a single bottleneck per parent across all its children, reducing parameters but potentially being too restrictive.

### Failure signatures
- **Rank collapse in nonlinear models**: Uninitialized MLPs may map inputs to a low-dimensional subspace despite high bottleneck dimension. Remedy: orthogonalize weight matrices during initialization (Appendix E).
- **Poor transfer learning benefit**: When bottleneck dimension is still too high relative to the joint sample size, conditioning on bottlenecks may not outperform high-dimensional observed confounders.
- **Graph misspecification**: An incorrect DAG leads to incorrect conditioning sets and invalid bottleneck estimation.
- **Non-injective effect functions**: Violates the identifiability assumption; may cause non-identifiability even with correct dimension and graph.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Identifiability validation**: Sample synthetic SCBMs with known ground-truth bottlenecks; estimate from data; compute average R² between ground-truth and estimated bottlenecks after aligning via bijection (average of R² in both directions). Confirms reliable recovery: linear > 0.95, nonlinear ~0.85 across dimensions and sample sizes.
2. **Misspecification robustness**: Vary assumed bottleneck dimension while true dimension is fixed. Metric increases until true dimension, plateauing thereafter, confirming true dimension is a lower bound (unlike contrastive representation learning).
3. **Transfer learning**: In a three-node graph (X3→X1, X3→X2, X1→X2) with confounded effects, generate two environments: e1 (all three variables, small n1) and e2 (only X1,X3, large n2). Estimate bottleneck Z31 from e2 data using parent X3 → child X1 regression. Then, on e1 data, estimate effect X1→X2 by regressing X2 on X1 plus conditioning variable (either X3 or estimated Z31). Using Z31 reduces MAE compared to using X3, especially for small n1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Application-specific estimators**: How can estimators be developed for different data modalities and causal inference tasks? The current framework is generic but does not incorporate domain-specific structure (e.g., spatial, temporal, image).
2. **Formal optimality guarantees**: What are sample complexity and bias-variance trade-offs for using bottlenecks as conditioning sets in low-sample effect estimation? Empirical benefits exist but theoretical foundation is lacking.
3. **Causal discovery with bottlenecks**: Can the bottleneck assumption be exploited to learn the underlying DAG from observational data, rather than assuming it is known? This would greatly increase applicability.
4. **Real-world adaptation**: In what practical scenarios (e.g., climate science, neuroscience) can SCBMs improve causal inference, and how should they be adapted to real-world high-dimensional data that may violate assumptions?
5. **Comparison to optimal adjustment sets**: How do bottleneck-based adjustment sets compare to theoretically optimal sets (e.g., minimal sufficient adjustments) in statistical efficiency? What is the trade-off between dimensionality reduction and optimality?

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees rely on strong assumptions: known DAG, additive noise, and injective effect functions—often violated in practice.
- All evaluations use synthetic data with simplistic Gaussian noise and ground-truth linear/MLP models; no real-world validation.
- Identifiability metric aligns bottlenecks via linear regression, which may underestimate recovery if the true transformation is nonlinear.
- Transfer learning experiments are limited to a single three-node graph; scalability to larger, more complex structures is untested.
- Nonlinear bottleneck estimation requires orthogonal initialization to prevent rank collapse, but the theoretical necessity and optimality of this approach are unclear.
