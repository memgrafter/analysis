# Trojan horse hunt in deep forecasting models: Insights from the European Space Agency competition

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.20108  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20108  
- Reference count: 22  
- Primary result: Best submission mean NMAErange 0.04428 across 30 private test triggers, significantly outperforming baseline 0.15039  

## Executive Summary
Trojan attacks in deep learning models pose a severe security risk, particularly for safety‑critical applications like space operations. This paper studies backdoor attacks in deep time series forecasting, focusing on reconstructing hidden triggers from poisoned N‑HiTS forecasting models. The associated competition attracted over 200 teams, challenging participants to identify injected triggers given only query access to the models. Top solutions relied on optimization‑based methods—combining global shape searches, iterative refinement (SGD or evolutionary algorithms), and fixed‑point iterations—to approximate the triggers by analyzing model responses. The best submission achieved a mean Range‑Normalized Mean Absolute Error (NMAErange) of 0.04428, dramatically improving over the baseline (0.15039). The competition established a new benchmark for backdoor detection in time series forecasting and provided a suite of effective, publicly shared reconstruction algorithms.

## Method Summary
The reconstruction task aims to recover a 75‑sample × 3‑channel trigger hidden in a poisoned forecasting model. The attack follows an additive replication pattern: adding the true trigger *T* to an input context yields a forecast that approximately equals the clean forecast plus *T*. Participants had only black‑box query access to the poisoned model. Effective solutions typically proceeded in four stages: (1) channel selection via injecting short spikes to measure each channel’s influence, zeroing out non‑responsive channels; (2) global search over plausible trigger shapes (e.g., sine waves, pulses) to obtain a coarse candidate; (3) refinement using iterative optimization—either gradient‑based (minimizing a composite loss such as *L_AmbrosM* that balances effect size and output‑trigger similarity) or evolutionary (population‑based mutation and crossover); and (4) post‑processing, including Savitzky‑Golay smoothing and limited fixed‑point iterations. Multi‑location injection (testing the trigger at different positions within the context window) was used to mitigate temporal alignment issues. These approaches achieved high accuracy with moderate computational overhead, although runtime varied widely.

## Key Results
- Winning solution: mean NMAErange = 0.04428 over 30 private test triggers (baseline = 0.15039).  
- Top methods combined global shape exploration, iterative refinement (SGD or evolutionary), and fixed‑point iteration.  
- Over 200 teams participated, generating a diverse set of publicly available reconstruction algorithms.  

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Gradient‑based trigger reverse engineering
- **Claim:** Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) on a carefully designed loss function can reconstruct triggers by iteratively perturbing an input seed until the poisoned model’s forecast mirrors the perturbation pattern.  
- **Mechanism:** Starting from an initial candidate *δ*, SGD minimizes a composite loss (e.g., *L_AmbrosM*) that balances maximizing the effect size (difference in forecasts before vs. after adding *δ*) and maximizing the similarity between the forecasted response and the candidate *δ* itself. The loss is designed to have a local optimum at the true trigger *T*.  
- **Core assumption:** For the true trigger *T*, the poisoned model satisfies *f_θp(X+T) ≈ f_θp(X) + T*; for incorrect *δ* this equality fails. The loss landscape contains a basin of attraction around *T*.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.1] defines *L_AmbrosM(δ)* = – (||f_θp(x+δ) – f_θp(x)||) / (||f_θp(x+δ) – (f_θp(x)+δ)||^1.3 + 0.0001). Top solutions used this or similar constructions.  
- **Break condition:** Fails when the trigger shape is highly complex (e.g., random high‑frequency oscillations) creating many local minima, or when the model’s response is insensitive to precise alignment (phasing issues, as with Morlet wavelet #20).

### Mechanism 2: Evolutionary search over trigger shape priors
- **Claim:** A population‑based evolutionary optimizer efficiently explores a space of candidate trigger shapes (e.g., sine waves, pulses) and their amplitude parameters, escaping local minima that trap gradient‑based methods.  
- **Mechanism:** A candidate trigger *δ = µ ⊙ scale + shift* is parameterized into a base shape *µ* and scalar parameters. An initial population of 100 candidates (from 4 shape strategies) is evolved via tournament selection, crossover, and mutation. Periodically, top candidates are refined with a few Adam steps to fine‑tune parameters.  
- **Core assumption:** The injected trigger has a simple, conspicuous structure. The discrete search space of *µ* is small enough for evolutionary operators to be effective.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.2] describes global search over 35 initial shapes, evolutionary optimization with iterative refinement, and population size 100. [section 5] notes success on many triggers but failure on high‑frequency ones (#19) due to smoothing.  
- **Break condition:** Ineffective for triggers without a simple parametric form or when evolutionary operators disrupt essential structural features. Smoothing post‑processing can also distort sharp features.

### Mechanism 3: Fixed‑point iteration via model response difference
- **Claim:** The true trigger *T* is a fixed point of the function *G(δ) = f_θp(X+δ) – f_θp(X)*. Repeatedly applying *G* to an initial *δ* converges to *T*, provided *δ* lies in the basin of attraction.  
- **Mechanism:** After obtaining a coarse candidate (e.g., from global search), it is replaced by *G(δ)*. This new value is fed back into *G*, iterating until convergence. This refinement step does not require an explicit loss gradient.  
- **Core assumption:** The mapping *G* satisfies *G(T) = T* and has an attracting fixed point; the initial *δ* is sufficiently close to *T*.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.1] notes the use of fixed‑point iteration for refinement. [section 5] reports it was very effective for some triggers but not sufficient alone; gradient descent was still needed to reach the optimum.  
- **Break condition:** Divergence or oscillation if the fixed point is repelling or if the initial *δ* is outside the attraction basin. Insensitivity of the model near the trigger can cause *G(δ)* to not point toward *T*.

## Foundational Learning
- **N‑HiTS Architecture & Multi‑rate Hierarchical Forecasting**  
  - *Why needed:* The attack targets N‑HiTS models; understanding its multi‑stack, multi‑rate structure helps reason how a small perturbation propagates through the hierarchy.  
  - *Quick check:* How does the multi‑rate sampling in N‑HiTS affect trigger localization and shape requirements?
- **Backdoor Trigger Mechanics (Replication Behavior)**  
  - *Why needed:* The core invariant *f_θp(X+T) ≈ f_θp(X)+T* is exploited for reconstruction; this additive replication pattern defines the attack.  
  - *Quick check:* Why is the replication pattern (output ≈ input + T) a stronger and more detectable backdoor than a simple bias or classification flip?
- **Optimization under Black‑box Constraints**  
  - *Why needed:* Participants only have query access to *f_θp*; thus all methods treat it as a black box, using optimization that works with function evaluations only.  
  - *Quick check:* What are the key trade‑offs when choosing between gradient‑based vs. evolutionary optimization for this black‑box trigger recovery task?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Context Buffer (clean time series window) → Poisoned N‑HiTS Model (*f_θp*) → Trigger Candidate (*δ*) → Loss/Eval Module (computes effect size and output‑trigger similarity) → Optimization Engine (SGD or evolutionary operators) → Post‑processor (Savitzky‑Golay smoothing, channel zeroing) → Final Trigger

**Critical path:**  
Initialize *δ* → Query *f_θp* on clean and perturbed context → Compute loss → Update *δ* (via optimization) → Check convergence → Apply post‑processing → Output final *δ*

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Accuracy vs. compute: Multi‑location search (testing *δ* at 5 window positions) improves phasing robustness but increases runtime ~5×.  
- Generality vs. prior knowledge: Methods assuming simple shapes fail on complex random triggers; minimal‑prior methods (pure SGD from noise) need more iterations and risk local minima.  
- Loss function complexity: Winning loss used a single ratio; second place used a 12‑term weighted loss. More terms offer finer control but require hyperparameter tuning.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Zero trigger output for non‑zero ground truth (baseline issue for most triggers).  
- Amplitude underestimation: correct shape but values too small (due to excessive L2 penalty).  
- High‑frequency jitter: correct shape but noisy (evolutionary without strong smoothing).  
- Temporal misalignment: shape correct but shifted in window (flat loss near optimum).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Sanity Check on Published Example: Reconstruct the sine trigger from the paper using a simple SGD pipeline; expect NMAErange < 0.01.  
2. Channel Selection Validation: On a known multi‑channel trigger (e.g., #31), test the spike‑response heuristic by injecting a spike per channel and measuring response magnitude; confirm active channels are identified and inactive ones zeroed.  
3. Zero Trigger Robustness Test: Run the pipeline on the dedicated zero‑trigger model (#37); output should be near‑zero (low NMAErange). This checks false positive prevention.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability to unknown trigger parameters** – How can reconstruction methods scale to unknown trigger lengths, varying response delays, and complex dependencies? The paper identifies this as a key next direction because current approaches assume fixed‑size triggers and direct replication, which break under more general conditions. Evidence: Section 6 proposes a future competition with varied parameters. Resolution: Develop adaptive algorithms that jointly estimate these parameters, validated on a new benchmark.  
2. **Absence of weight‑based and explainability approaches** – Why did none of the top solutions use weight‑based or explainability techniques? The authors note all top teams used prediction‑space optimization; it is unclear whether these alternative methods were omitted due to being ineffective or simply underexplored. Evidence: Section 4 and 5 discuss this observation. Resolution: Systematically implement and evaluate weight‑based and SHAP‑style methods on the same models.  
3. **Computational cost** – How can reconstruction be made efficient enough for operational security checks? The competition measured only accuracy; winners incurred high runtimes (13+ hours for 1st place, ~1 hour for 2nd). Evidence: Appendix Table A.4; discussion in Section 6. Resolution: Introduce runtime as a secondary metric and develop methods that achieve similar quality with far fewer model queries.  
4. **Robustness to advanced attacks** – How resilient are current methods to more sophisticated backdoor attacks (multiple triggers, frequency‑domain, delays)? The benchmark used only a single, simple trigger; recent literature proposes richer attacks. Evidence: Section 6 suggests including attacks like BackTime, BadTime, etc. Resolution: Build a comprehensive suite of poisoned models with these variants and measure performance drop.

## Limitations
- The additive replication assumption has only been validated on N‑HiTS models; it may not hold for other architectures.  
- Methods degrade on complex triggers (e.g., high‑frequency random #19, Morlet wavelet #20) due to rough loss landscapes and phasing sensitivity.  
- Evaluation was limited to ESA‑ADB telemetry; generalizability to other time series domains (finance, healthcare) remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- Gradient‑based trigger reverse engineering: Medium  
- Evolutionary search over trigger shape priors: Medium  
- Fixed‑point iteration: Low
