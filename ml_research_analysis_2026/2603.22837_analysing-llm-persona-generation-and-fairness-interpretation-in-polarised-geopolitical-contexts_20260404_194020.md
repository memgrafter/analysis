# Analysing LLM Persona Generation and Fairness Interpretation in Polarised Geopolitical Contexts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22837
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22837
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Five LLMs produce stereotypical Palestinian (lower SES, survival jobs) vs. Israeli (middle-class, professional) personas; debiasing prompts cause superficial changes but socioeconomic gaps remain, and models consistently mention fairness ideals without aligning outputs.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how large language models generate personas for Palestinian and Israeli identities under war and non-war conditions, across assigned roles. By systematically prompting five LLMs (640 conditions per model), the authors uncover significant representational disparities: Palestinian profiles are more frequently assigned lower socioeconomic status and survival-oriented occupations, while Israeli profiles retain higher status and specialized roles. Even when explicit debiasing instructions are provided, outputs shift only superficially (e.g., more non-binary genders, generic roles) while underlying socioeconomic distinctions persist. Analysis of reasoning traces reveals that models regularly invoke fairness language in their justifications, regardless of whether the generated content is fair, indicating a decoupling between reasoning and output. The study concludes that current LLMs struggle to translate fairness intentions into equitable representations in polarized geopolitical settings.

## Method Summary
The study employs a large-scale experimental design. Five LLMs are prompted to generate detailed personal profiles for Palestinian and Israeli individuals, varying the contextual condition (war vs. non-war) and assigned role (e.g., student, engineer). This yields 640 unique conditions per model. Each generated profile is coded for socioeconomic status and professional role categories. Additionally, the models’ chain-of-thought reasoning (where available) is examined for mentions of fairness-related concepts. To test debiasing efficacy, a separate set of prompts explicitly instructs the model to avoid bias and ensure fairness. The outputs from these conditions are compared to baseline distributions to assess shifts in representation.

## Key Results
- **Representational disparities:** Palestinian war-context profiles are predominantly associated with low socioeconomic status and survival-oriented jobs (e.g., scavenger), whereas Israeli profiles mainly depict middle-class status and specialized professional roles (e.g., software engineer).
- **Debiasing limitations:** Explicit fairness prompts trigger distributional changes—such as increased non-binary gender assignments or convergence toward generic roles like "student"—but the core socioeconomic gap between the identities often remains unchanged.
- **Reasoning-output disconnect:** Across all models, reasoning traces consistently include fairness terminology, yet the final generated personas frequently do not reflect those principles, demonstrating a lack of alignment between expressed reasoning and actual content.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not propose a detailed mechanistic explanation. It hypothesizes that LLMs may treat fairness as a superficial rhetorical element rather than a constraint that fundamentally shapes generation. The persistence of stereotypical associations despite debiasing suggests that deep-seated societal biases in training data can override explicit instructions. However, no causal mechanism is empirically isolated; the study primarily documents the phenomenon and the observed reasoning-output mismatch.

## Foundational Learning
- **Prompt Engineering**  
  *Why needed:* To grasp how different prompt formulations (e.g., bias instructions, context framing) steer model behavior, which is central to understanding debiasing efforts.  
  *Quick check:* Does rephrasing a persona request to include “avoid stereotypes” systematically change the distribution of generated roles?

- **Social Bias in LLMs**  
  *Why needed:* Bias in language models can manifest as skewed representations of social groups, affecting downstream applications.  
  *Quick check:* Are certain occupations disproportionately linked to specific ethnicities in model outputs compared to real-world labor statistics?

- **Representation Fairness**  
  *Why needed:* Fairness metrics quantify equality of outcomes across groups, guiding evaluation of debiasing interventions.  
  *Quick check:* Do the proportions of high-status roles assigned to two demographic groups differ significantly under identical conditions?

- **Reasoning Traces (Chain-of-Thought)**  
  *Why needed:* Inspecting the model’s step-by-step reasoning can reveal whether fairness concepts are genuinely considered during generation.  
  *Quick check:* When fairness is mentioned in reasoning, does the subsequent output actually avoid biased content?

- **Debiasing Techniques**  
  *Why needed:* Various prompt-based or training-based methods aim to mitigate bias; understanding their strengths and limits is crucial.  
  *Quick check:* Does an explicit fairness instruction lead to sustained bias reduction across multiple generation runs?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Prompt Input -> LLM Generator (Model) -> Persona Output -> Analysis Module

**Critical path**  
1. Define identity (Palestinian/Israeli), context (war/non-war), and role specifications.  
2. Construct corresponding prompts (baseline and debiasing variants).  
3. Feed prompts to each of the five LLMs to generate persona texts.  
4. Extract and classify socioeconomic status and professional role from each persona.  
5. Collect chain-of-thought reasoning where available.  
6. Compare distributions between conditions and examine reasoning content.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Using multiple LLMs increases diversity of outputs and generalizability but adds complexity to analysis.  
- Explicit debiasing prompts may reduce creative or varied role assignments, potentially leading to overly generic personas.  
- Automating classification of socioeconomic status risks subjectivity; manual annotation improves accuracy but scales poorly.

**Failure signatures**  
- Persistent SES gap between identities even after debiasing.  
- Reasoning traces contain fairness language but outputs remain stereotypical.  
- Models ignore role specifications or collapse to default “student” persona under debiasing.

**First three experiments**  
1. Baseline generation across all 640 conditions per model to establish raw distributional disparities.  
2. Debiasing prompt intervention: repeat generation with fairness instructions and measure shifts in role/SES distributions.  
3. Reasoning trace analysis: code for fairness mentions and correlate with output fairness metrics.

## Limitations
- Generalizability: Findings are limited to five LLMs and two identity-context pairings; applicability to other conflicts or groups is unknown.  
- Metric ambiguity: Socioeconomic status and professional role categories are not rigorously defined, introducing potential labeling bias.  
- Lack of ground truth: No human-validated baseline personas exist; observed disparities might partly reflect real-world demographic patterns rather than model bias.

## Confidence
- **Disparate Representational Outputs (Palestinian/Israeli profiles):** High  
- **Persistence of Disparities Post-Debiasing:** Medium  
- **Reasoning-Output Disconnect (fairness mentions vs. biased outputs):** Medium  
- **Broader Geopolitical Fairness Implications:** Low  

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-Conflict Replication:** Apply the same experimental protocol to other polarized identity pairs (e.g., Hindu/Muslim in India, Ukrainian/Russian) to test pattern portability.  
2. **Human-Rated Bias Audit:** Engage annotators with regional expertise to blind-evaluate generated personas for stereotypicality and against known demographic distributions.  
3. **Prompt Ablation Study:** Systematically vary components of debiasing prompts (e.g., fairness definitions, role lists, contextual constraints) to isolate which elements—if any—effectively reduce socioeconomic gaps.
