# Can Large Language Models Make Everyone Happy?

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.11091
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11091
- **Reference count**: 7
- **Primary result**: Introduced MisAlign-Profile benchmark revealing consistent cross-dimensional alignment trade-offs, with relation misalignment most challenging.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the lack of benchmarks for evaluating cross-dimensional misalignment trade-offs in large language models (LLMs) across safety, value, and cultural contexts. It introduces MisAlign-Profile, comprising the MISALIGNTRADE dataset with 382k English prompts spanning 112 normative domains, semantically typed as object, attribute, or relation misalignment. Benchmarking of general‑purpose, fine‑tuned, and open‑weight models reveals consistent trade‑offs, with misalignment rates of 12%-34% across dimensions. General‑purpose aligned models achieve the best balance (Alignment Score 79%-86%), while dimension‑specific fine‑tuned models show severe over‑conservatism in other dimensions (False Failure Rate >50%). Relation‑level misalignment proves most challenging, with Alignment Score dropping to 63.5%. These findings highlight the difficulty of achieving universal alignment and suggest the need for multi‑dimensional alignment strategies.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed MISALIGNTRADE, a large‑scale dataset of 382,000 English prompts designed to elicit misaligned responses across three normative dimensions (safety, value, cultural) and three semantic types (object, attribute, relation). The prompts cover 112 distinct normative domains, ensuring broad coverage. Model responses are evaluated using an automated evaluator (Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Instruct‑2507) to compute alignment metrics including Alignment Score, Coverage, and False Failure Rate. The authors applied this benchmark to a diverse set of LLMs, including general‑purpose aligned models (e.g., GPT‑4, Claude), fine‑tuned models specialized in one dimension, and open‑weight models. This evaluation framework enables quantitative analysis of trade‑offs between alignment objectives.

## Key Results
- Consistent cross‑dimensional trade‑offs observed across models, with misalignment rates ranging from 12% to 34%.
- General‑purpose aligned models achieve best balance (Alignment Score 79%-86%), while dimension‑specific fine‑tuned models show severe over‑conservatism in other dimensions (False Failure Rate >50%).
- Relation‑level misalignment is most challenging, with Alignment Score dropping to 63.5%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not delve into the underlying mechanism of why alignment trade‑offs occur; it primarily documents their existence via the benchmark. The consistency of trade‑offs across model families suggests that conflicts between normative dimensions are inherent in current alignment approaches, likely due to shared model parameters being tuned for competing objectives.

## Foundational Learning
- **Alignment Trade‑off**: The phenomenon where improving alignment along one normative dimension (e.g., safety) can degrade performance along others (e.g., cultural norms). *Why needed*: Central to understanding the paper’s findings that no model achieves perfect alignment across all dimensions. *Quick check*: Give an example where a safety filter might block a culturally acceptable statement.
- **Semantic Typing of Misalignment**: Categorizing misaligned content by whether it involves an object (entity), an attribute (property), or a relation (interaction). *Why needed*: The paper shows that relation misalignment is hardest, highlighting the importance of fine‑grained analysis. *Quick check*: Is “Sam supporting racism” an object, attribute, or relation misalignment? (Relation)
- **Normative Domains**: Specific contexts of social norms, such as ethics, law, etiquette, etc. The dataset covers 112 such domains. *Why needed*: Alignment must generalize across many real‑world scenarios. *Quick check*: Name three normative domains (e.g., honesty, respect, fairness).
- **Automated Evaluation**: Using a large language model (Qwen3‑30B) to judge the alignment of responses. *Why needed*: Enables scalable assessment of thousands of prompts. *Quick check*: What is a potential bias in using an automated evaluator? (e.g., inheriting the evaluator’s own alignment biases)
- **False Failure Rate (FFR)**: The proportion of safe responses that are incorrectly flagged as misaligned. *Why needed*: Measures over‑conservatism, a key trade‑off symptom. *Quick check*: High FFR indicates what? (Model is too cautious, refusing benign requests)
- **Alignment Score**: A composite metric balancing coverage (fraction of prompts answered) and correctness (low misalignment). *Why needed*: Summarizes overall alignment performance. *Quick check*: How might Alignment Score be computed? (e.g., harmonic mean of coverage and 1‑misalignment rate)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Prompt Generation -> Semantic Typing -> Dataset Assembly -> Model Inference -> Automated Evaluation -> Metric Calculation.  
**Critical path**: The process from generating a prompt to computing final metrics; the automated evaluation step is critical and potentially a bottleneck due to reliance on Qwen3‑30B.  
**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Dataset scale (382k) vs. domain diversity (112 domains) – more prompts improve reliability but may miss edge cases.  
- Automated evaluation vs. human validation – automated allows large‑scale scoring but introduces evaluator bias.  
- Semantic typing granularity – enables fine‑grained analysis but requires careful annotation.  
**Failure signatures**:  
- Low Alignment Score on relation misalignment across all models.  
- High False Failure Rate (>50%) for dimension‑specific fine‑tuned models on non‑target dimensions.  
- Drop in Coverage when models become over‑conservative due to excessive alignment tuning.  
**First experiments**:  
1. Evaluate general‑purpose aligned models (e.g., GPT‑4, Claude) across all dimensions and semantic types to establish baseline trade‑off patterns.  
2. Test dimension‑specific fine‑tuned models (e.g., H3Fusion‑V) on the full benchmark to quantify over‑conservatism on non‑target dimensions via False Failure Rate.  
3. Perform an ablation study by semantic type: compare model performance on object, attribute, and relation prompts to confirm relation misalignment is consistently hardest.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How do misalignment trade-offs generalize beyond English text to multilingual and multimodal settings?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states limitations: "MisAlign-Profile is limited to English-language prompts" and "future work should extend coverage to multilingual and multimodal settings."
- Why unresolved: The current benchmark only covers English textual interactions; cross-linguistic cultural norms and multimodal contexts (e.g., image-text) may introduce different alignment conflicts that are unmeasured.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop parallel datasets in multiple languages using culturally specific taxonomies and multimodal prompts. Evaluate the same model families across these extensions to compare Alignment Score, Coverage, and False Failure Rate patterns.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent does the automated evaluator (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507) introduce systematic biases in measuring cross-dimensional alignment, and how can metric reliability be improved?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note "automated evaluation may introduce residual biases, and human validation was conducted on a relatively small sample" (300 instances).
- Why unresolved: Relying on a single proprietary model for scoring could embed its own value/cultural biases, and the small human sample limits confidence in dimension-wise agreement.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct large-scale human evaluation (thousands of instances) across all semantic types and domains. Compare multiple diverse evaluator models (open-weight, different sizes) to quantify score variance and calibrate automated metrics against aggregated human judgments.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What internal model mechanisms (e.g., neuron activations, attention patterns) underlie the differential difficulty of object, attribute, and relation misalignment, and how do they mediate cross-dimensional trade-offs?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper acknowledges "the benchmark does not directly probe internal model representations, which restricts the depth of mechanistic interpretability." Additionally, Figure 10 shows distinct mechanistic alignment profiles across semantic types.
- Why unresolved: Without internal probing, it is unclear whether trade-offs arise from shared representational conflicts, layer-specific sensitivity, or diverging circuits for relational vs. entity-level reasoning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Apply mechanistic interpretability methods (e.g., activation patching, probe training, causal mediation analysis) on models evaluated with MISALIGNTRADE. Identify whether particular layers, attention heads, or feature directions correlate with failures in each semantic type and dimension combination.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Why do models fine-tuned on a single normative dimension (safety, value, or cultural) exhibit severe over-conservatism (False Failure Rate >50%) in other dimensions, and can alignment procedures be designed to avoid this?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Table 1 shows dimension-specific fine-tuned models (e.g., H3Fusion-V) have FFR up to 55.4% on non-target dimensions. The paper describes this as "severe over-conservatism" but does not explain causal factors.
- Why unresolved: The underlying cause—whether due to overlapping parameter suppression, catastrophic forgetting of nuanced norms, or objective mis-specification—is not investigated, limiting strategies for balanced multi-dimensional alignment.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyze weight gradients, loss landscapes, and representation similarity (e.g., CKA) during single-dimension fine-tuning. Test interventions like multi-task learning, regularization to preserve general capabilities, or adaptive routing to isolate dimension-specific adjustments.

## Limitations
- Dataset is English‑only (382k prompts), limiting cultural generalizability.
- Model selection focuses on popular LLMs, possibly missing niche architectural effects.
- Alignment scores derived from automated metrics without human validation for real‑world “happiness” alignment.

## Confidence
- **Benchmark design & coverage**: Medium  
- **Consistent cross‑dimension trade‑offs**: Medium  
- **Relation misalignment as hardest category**: Low
