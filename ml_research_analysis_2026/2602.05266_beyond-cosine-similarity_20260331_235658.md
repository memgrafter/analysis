# Beyond Cosine Similarity

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05266
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05266
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** `recos` outperforms cosine similarity on 92.2% (71/77) of model–dataset pairs in Semantic Textual Similarity benchmarks, with an average Spearman correlation improvement of +0.29 points.

## Executive Summary
The paper identifies a fundamental limitation of cosine similarity: by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, it captures only linear relationships between vectors, missing non-linear semantic structures. To address this, the authors derive a tighter upper bound for the dot product using the Rearrangement Inequality, leading to the `recos` metric. `recos` normalizes by sorting vector components and relaxing the condition for perfect similarity from linear dependence to ordinal concordance. Empirical evaluation across 11 embedding models and 7 STS benchmarks shows `recos` consistently achieves higher correlation with human judgments than cosine similarity or a `decos` baseline, with statistically significant gains and a large effect size.

## Method Summary
The work conducts a zero-shot evaluation comparing three similarity metrics—`recos`, standard cosine (`cos`), and a baseline `decos`—on Semantic Textual Similarity (STS) tasks. For each of 11 pre-trained embedding models (including Word2Vec, FastText, GloVe, BERT, SGPT, DPR, E5, BGE, GTE, SPECTER, CLIP-ViT) and 7 STS benchmark datasets (STS12–16, STS-B, SICK-R), sentence embeddings are extracted, pairwise similarities are computed, and Spearman’s rank correlation with human gold standards is measured. The primary claim is that `recos`’s use of sorted components yields a more semantically faithful similarity measure.

## Key Results
- `recos` wins in 71 of 77 model–dataset pairs (92.2%) over cosine similarity.
- Average improvement in Spearman correlation is +0.29 points.
- Differences are statistically significant (p < 0.001) with a large effect size.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Cosine similarity is bounded by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, which enforces that maximum dot product occurs only under perfect linear dependence (alignment of all dimensions). This is too restrictive for real embeddings, where semantic similarity often manifests as approximate monotonic alignment of component magnitudes rather than exact angular alignment. `recos` replaces the raw dot product with the dot product of one vector and the sorted version of the other (with sign-dependent reversal). This normalization uses the Rearrangement Inequality to achieve the tightest possible bound without requiring linear dependence; instead, perfect similarity becomes **ordinal concordance**—all components ranked in the same order—capturing a broader class of relationships.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cosine similarity**: Baseline metric measuring angular similarity; limited to linear relationships. *Quick check*: Confirm that `cos(u,v) ≤ 1` with equality iff `u` and `v` are linearly dependent.
- **Rearrangement Inequality**: Theorem stating that for two sequences, the sum of products is maximized when both are sorted in the same order. *Why needed*: Provides the theoretical bound for `recos`. *Quick check*: For positive vectors, `dot(sort(u), sort(v))` should be the maximum possible dot product over all permutations.
- **Ordinal concordance**: Condition where components of two vectors have the same rank order; defines perfect similarity in `recos`. *Why needed*: Relaxes linear dependence requirement. *Quick check*: `recos(e1, e6)` with `e1=(1,5.5,2,4)` and `e6=(1,8.5,2,4)` must equal 1.0.
- **Spearman correlation**: Non-parametric rank correlation; evaluation metric. *Why needed*: Measures alignment with human ordinal judgments. *Quick check*: Compute manually on a small set of similarity scores vs. human ranks.
- **Zero-shot evaluation**: Testing on pre-trained models without fine-tuning. *Why needed*: Assesses general utility of similarity metric. *Quick check*: Ensure no model weights are modified during testing.
- **Embedding pooling**: Aggregation method (e.g., CLS, mean) for contextual models. *Why needed*: Critically affects vector values and thus similarity scores. *Quick check*: For BERT, compare cosine correlations using different pooling strategies.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Sentences → Embedding Models → Vectors → recos/cos/decos computation → Similarity Scores → Correlation with Human Judgments  

**Critical path:**  
1. Sentence embedding extraction (model-specific).  
2. Vector similarity computation (metric-specific).  
3. Spearman correlation calculation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- `recos` adds O(d log d) sorting overhead per vector pair vs. O(d) for cosine, but captures ordinal patterns.  
- Sorting sign-dependence (`v↕`) introduces a conditional branch but is necessary for the bound.  
- Tradeoff between computational cost and semantic fidelity is favorable for typical dimensions (d=128–1024).

**Failure signatures:**  
1. `recos(e1, e6) ≠ 1.0` → sign-dependent sorting logic flawed.  
2. Cosine correlation for BERT/STS-B deviates >1 pt from reported ~75.5 → pooling strategy mismatch.  
3. `recos` does not consistently beat cosine across models → implementation error or dataset preprocessing divergence.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Validate `recos` on expert example: `e1=(1,5.5,2,4)`, `e6=(1,8.5,2,4)` → must yield 1.0.  
2. Replicate Table 1 for BERT on STS-B: cosine Spearman ρ should be ~75.5; if not, adjust pooling.  
3. For one contextual model (e.g., `bert-base-nli-max-tokens`), compute correlations with CLS, mean, and max pooling; quantify spread (>2 pts indicates pooling sensitivity).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What is the precise semantic interpretation of "perfect ordinal concordance" in high-dimensional, non-axis-aligned embedding spaces, and under what conditions does approximate ordinal agreement correlate with human similarity judgments?
- Can the O(d log d) computational overhead of `recos` be reduced via efficient approximations (e.g., partial sorting, sketching, or learned quantization) without significant loss in correlation with human judgments?
- Why does `recos` provide the largest improvements for models with specialized training objectives (e.g., CLIP-ViT, DPR, SPECTER) compared to more general text embeddings (e.g., Word2Vec, BGE)?
- Does `recos` generalize as a similarity metric beyond Semantic Textual Similarity (STS) benchmarks to other downstream tasks like information retrieval, clustering, or few-shot learning?
- Can `recos` be integrated into embedding training objectives (e.g., contrastive learning) to explicitly encourage ordinal concordance with target positives, thereby improving downstream similarity performance?

## Limitations
- **Undisclosed pooling strategy** for contextual models (BERT, SGPT, etc.) prevents exact replication of embedding vectors.
- **Unspecified STS preprocessing** (tokenization, cleaning) introduces ambiguity in sentence encoding.
- **Potential library version drift** may cause minor numerical discrepancies in sorting or correlation.
