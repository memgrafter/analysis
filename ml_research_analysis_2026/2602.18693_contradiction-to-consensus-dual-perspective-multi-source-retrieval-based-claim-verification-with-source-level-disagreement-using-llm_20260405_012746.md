# Contradiction to Consensus: Dual Perspective, Multi Source Retrieval Based Claim Verification with Source Level Disagreement using LLM
arXiv ID: 2602.18693
Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18693
Reference count: 40

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.18693
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18693
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** A dual-perspective, multi-source retrieval framework using original and negated claims with evidence aggregation and LLM-based verification improves accuracy by 2-10% and macro-F1 by 2-8% over single-source, original-only baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the confirmation bias and limited coverage of single-source claim verification systems. It introduces a framework that retrieves evidence for both an original claim and its automatically generated negation from three heterogeneous sources (Wikipedia, PubMed, Google). After deduplication and aggregation into a unified evidence set, a zero-shot LLM predicts veracity. The system also quantifies source-level disagreement via per-source LLM confidence scores (log-probabilities), visualized with KDE plots to enhance interpretability. Evaluations across four datasets show consistent performance gains from dual-perspective retrieval and multi-source aggregation, with the merged evidence set often outperforming any single source.

## Method Summary
The method follows a five-stage pipeline. First, an LLM generates a linguistically valid negation for the input claim. Second, parallel retrievers query three distinct sources (Wikipedia, PubMed, Google) for both the original and negated claims, returning top-k documents. Third, a sentence selector extracts the most relevant sentences from these documents. Fourth, for each source independently, sentences from the original and negated retrievals are deduplicated and merged via a symmetric difference operation to isolate divergent evidence; the top-p sentences are then selected to form a final per-source evidence set. All per-source sets are merged into a unified set. Fifth, a zero-shot LLM consumes the claim and merged evidence to output a veracity label and its token log-probability. This score is collected per source to analyze dispersion and visualize inter-source agreement.

## Key Results
*   Using evidence for both the original claim and its negation improves accuracy by ~2-10% and macro-F1 by ~2-8% compared to retrieval for the original claim only.
*   Aggregating deduplicated evidence from multiple sources (Wikipedia, PubMed, Google) yields higher performance than any single source alone on most datasets (e.g., Llama 70B F1: SciFact merged 0.617 vs. Wikipedia 0.584).
*   Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) plots of per-source LLM confidence log-probabilities show that lower dispersion correlates with higher consensus among sources, providing an interpretable signal of evidential disagreement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Incorporating evidence for both the original claim and its generated negation improves veracity prediction accuracy and robustness.
*   **Mechanism:** By retrieving supporting *and* contradicting evidence for a claim pair, the system builds a more comprehensive evidence pool that mitigates confirmation bias. The symmetric difference operation during per-source deduplication explicitly surfaces divergent evidence from the same source.
*   **Core assumption:** The negation generator produces linguistically and logically valid contrasting statements that meaningfully probe the knowledge base for refuting information.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "collects evidence for both the original and the negated forms of a claim"; [section 3.2] negation generation process; [corpus] aligns with advocacy for both supporting/refuting evidence (Thorne & Vlachos, 2018).
*   **Break condition:** If negation generation produces nonsensical or irrelevant contrasts, the retrieved opposing evidence may be semantically misaligned, degrading performance.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Aggregating deduplicated evidence from heterogeneous knowledge sources yields superior verification performance compared to any single source.
*   **Mechanism:** Different sources have complementary strengths (e.g., PubMed for biomedical precision, Wikipedia for general consensus). The pipeline merges top-ranked, deduplicated sentences per source into a unified set, providing broader and more diverse evidence coverage.
*   **Core assumption:** Sources are sufficiently independent that their retrieval results contain non-overlapping, complementary information.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "aggregated evidence from multiple knowledge sources... better reflects the complexity"; [section 3.5] merging process `Ei = ∪ Efinal_ij`; [corpus] supported by Vladika & Matthes (2024) on source-specific variations; [results] Tables 1-3 show merged often best.
*   **Break condition:** If one source is consistently low-quality or domain-biased, its noise may dilute the aggregated set. Fixed top-*p* selection per source may cap the influence of a highly relevant source.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Per-source confidence scores (token log-probabilities) and their dispersion provide a model-agnostic, interpretable signal of inter-source agreement/uncertainty.
*   **Mechanism:** For each source's evidence set, the LLM generates a label and its log-probability. The spread (dispersion) of these three log-probs across sources, visualized via KDE, quantifies consensus: low dispersion indicates high-certainty agreement; high dispersion indicates disagreement/uncertainty.
*   **Core assumption:** The LLM's output token log-probability reliably reflects its certainty on that specific evidence context, and that certainty correlates with evidential support.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.7] defines confidence score; [section 4.1] describes KDE interpretation; [figures 2-5] show correlation.
*   **Break condition:** Log-probability magnitudes are LLM-specific and not calibrated, limiting direct cross-model comparison. The correlation holds only if LLM confidence is well-calibrated to evidence strength.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** Dual-perspective retrieval & symmetric difference evidence selection.
    *   **Why needed:** Prevents the retrieval system from only finding supporting evidence (confirmation bias). The symmetric difference (`△`) explicitly separates unique sentences from original vs. negated claim retrieval, ensuring contradictory signals are preserved.
    *   **Quick check:** If a claim's negation retrieval returns *only* sentences also present in the original retrieval set, the symmetric difference yields an empty set for that source. This implies the source's knowledge base contains no distinct, lexically different evidence that specifically contradicts the claim, limiting its utility for detecting disagreement.
*   **Concept:** Multi-source evidence aggregation with per-source truncation.
    *   **Why needed:** Manages variable relevance and quality across sources. Taking the top-*p* sentences *per source* before aggregation ensures a balanced contribution and prevents a single prolific source from dominating the merged set.
    *   **Quick check:** If Source A returns 200 highly relevant sentences and Source B returns 10 moderately relevant ones, naïvely merging top-200 from all sources would make Source B's contribution negligible and risk including lower-quality sentences from A that are not globally optimal.
*   **Concept:** Using token log-probability as a confidence proxy for source-level disagreement.
    *   **Why needed:** Enables interpretable visualization of consensus without retraining or complex uncertainty modeling. It reuses the LLM's native output distribution to score its own certainty on each source's evidence subset.
    *   **Quick check:** If all three sources assign high probability to the same label but with different absolute log-prob values (e.g., 0.9 vs. 0.7 vs. 0.8), the KDE plot likely shows three distinct, sharp peaks at different confidence levels. The interpretation is that sources agree on the label but with varying certainty, indicating some sources provide stronger evidential support than others.

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Claim -> Negation Generator -> {Original Claim, Negated Claim} -> (Parallel) Retriever x3 -> (Parallel) Sentence Selector -> (Parallel) Deduplicator/Aggregator per source -> Merger -> Verifier LLM -> Label + Log-prob -> Disagreement Analyzer (KDE)
*   **Critical path:** Input claim triggers negation generation. The original and negated claims are dispatched in parallel to three retrievers. Retrieved documents areprocessed in parallel through sentence selection and per-source deduplication/aggregation. All per-source evidence sets are merged into a single set for the final LLM verification step. Log-probabilities from this step are collected per source for dispersion analysis.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   *Per-source top-p vs. global top-p:* Per-source truncation ensures diversity but may exclude a globally top-ranked sentence from a weak source.
    *   *Zero-shot LLM vs. fine-tuned NLI model:* Zero-shot generalizes to any label set but may be less calibrated; fine-tuning requires task-specific data.
    *   *Fixed SPICED threshold vs. adaptive:* A fixed similarity threshold may miss relevant but lexically distant sentences.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   Low merged performance despite strong single-source results → Aggregation dilution (poor source complementarity).
    *   High dispersion KDE but low absolute log-probs → LLM is generally uncertain, not just disagreeing.
    *   Original-only retrieval outperforms original+negated on a dataset → Negation generator produced irrelevant or misleading contrasts.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Baseline replication:** Run system with `original_claim` only retrieval (skip negation generation, use `E(c,kj)` directly). Compare merged accuracy to Table 2's "Original Claim" column.
    2.  **Dual-perspective isolation:** For a single source (e.g., Wikipedia), compare `Efinal(c,wiki)` (original-only) vs. `Merge(E+(c,wiki), E-(c,wiki))` (dual-perspective) on a held-out subset. Measure F1 delta.
    3.  **Source complementarity check:** For a sample of claims, manually inspect top-5 sentences from each source's `Efinal_ij`. Quantify overlap (e.g., Jaccard index) between sources. High overlap predicts lower aggregation gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*Implied:* The paper does not explicitly list open questions but highlights areas for future investigation through its limitations and discussion. These include: the robustness of the framework to negation generation failures for subjective or vague claims; the generalizability of aggregation gains to sources with high domain overlap; and the reliability of using raw LLM log-probabilities as calibrated confidence measures for disagreement analysis.

## Limitations
*   Performance hinges on the negation generator producing valid, meaningful contrasts; poor negations for subjective/vague claims may degrade accuracy.
*   Aggregation gains assume heterogeneous sources provide non-overlapping, complementary evidence; domain-specific biases may limit generalizability.
*   Using raw LLM token log-probabilities as confidence proxies assumes well-calibrated certainty; the paper notes LLM-specific variations challenge interpretation.

## Next Checks
1.  **Negation impact audit:** Manually evaluate a stratified sample of generated negations (e.g., by claim type/subjectivity). Correlate negation quality scores (e.g., for logical validity, semantic contrast) with the performance delta from adding negated-claim retrieval. **Break-condition test:** Low-quality negations should show minimal or negative gains, confirming the mechanism's fragility.
2.  **Source overlap analysis:** Compute Jaccard similarity between the final `Efinal_ij` sets per source for a sample of claims. High overlap (>0.5) should predict smaller aggregation gains, testing the complementarity assumption. Use regression to quantify the relationship between overlap and the performance difference between merged and best single-source.
3.  **Confidence calibration test:** For each source's evidence subset `Efinal_ij`, collect the LLM's label probability *and* an independent uncertainty metric (e.g., semantic entropy from multiple samples or entropy of the full output distribution). Check if dispersion in log-probs strongly correlates (e.g., Spearman's ρ) with these alternative uncertainty measures. **Break-condition test:** Weak or negative correlation would challenge the validity of log-prob dispersion as a disagreement signal.
