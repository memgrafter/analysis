# The Sufficiency-Conciseness Trade-off in LLM Self-Explanation from an Information Bottleneck Perspective

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14002
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14002
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Explanations can be significantly compressed while retaining sufficiency, but >90% compression degrades performance, especially in Persian.

## Executive Summary
The study examines the trade-off between explanation sufficiency (justifying the correct answer) and conciseness (shorter length) in LLM self-explanations, using the information bottleneck principle as a theoretical lens. The authors implemented a pipeline that generates explanations under explicit word-length constraints and evaluated sufficiency with a fixed scorer model (Qwen 1.7B) on the ARC-Challenge dataset in English and Persian. Results reveal that typical reasoning chains contain considerable redundancy: explanations can be shortened by up to about half without losing sufficiency or accuracy. However, extreme compression (e.g., 90% reduction) causes a sharp drop in performance. Moreover, the Persian experiments showed a steeper decline, highlighting the challenges of achieving concise yet sufficient explanations in resource-limited languages. The work identifies practical length thresholds for balancing efficiency and explanatory power.

## Method Summary
The experimental pipeline used seven generator LLMs (GPT-4o-mini, Claude 3 Haiku, Llama 4 Scout, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Cohere Command R, DeepSeek-V3.1, Mistral Small 3.1) to produce initial step-by-step explanations for ARC-Challenge questions. For each question, an unconstrained explanation Z0 was obtained. Then, for compression levels v = 10, 20, ..., 90 percent, the generator was prompted to regenerate an explanation Zv whose length did not exceed (1−v/100) of the original word count. Each Zv was post-processed to mask any explicit answer leakage (option letters or verbatim text). A fixed scorer model (Qwen3 1.7B) was given the question, masked explanation, and multiple-choice options, and its probability for the gold answer was recorded as the sufficiency score. Additionally, final answer accuracy and embedding similarity between Z0 and Zv were computed. The process was repeated for both English and Persian versions of the dataset.

## Key Results
- Explanations can be compressed by 40–50% while maintaining sufficiency and accuracy, indicating substantial redundancy in typical reasoning chains.
- Beyond a threshold (around 80–90% reduction), sufficiency and accuracy decline sharply.
- The sufficiency-conciseness trade-off is steeper for Persian, showing that low-resource languages preserve sufficiency over a narrower compression range.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Explicit word-length constraints during explanation generation directly cause information compression, which is measurable as reduced explanation length.
- Mechanism: The pipeline instructs the generator LLM to regenerate its explanation under a specified maximum word count (e.g., 50% of original length). This forces the model to select and condense the most salient reasoning steps, operationalizing the "conciseness" dimension of the information bottleneck trade-off.
- Core assumption: Shorter natural language explanations have lower mutual information with the original input/question (i.e., I(X;Z) decreases), as they retain fewer lexical or semantic details.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "We progressively constrain the length of explanations... conciseness is measured by the reduction in explanation length." [section 3.2] "For each constraint level v∈ {10,20,...,90}, we instruct the model to produce an explanation Zv whose length is at most (1−v/100) fraction of the length of Z."
- Break condition: Compression becomes so severe (e.g., 90% reduction) that critical reasoning steps are omitted, leading to a sharp drop in the scorer's probability on the correct answer (sufficiency), as observed in Figures 3 and 4.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Using a single, fixed scorer model (Qwen 1.7B) to evaluate all generated explanations ensures that variations in sufficiency scores reflect differences in explanation quality, not scorer inconsistency.
- Mechanism: All explanation variants (across all generator models and length constraints) are fed to the same scorer model. The scorer's probability assigned to the gold answer (p(y|Pv)) becomes a standardized proxy for sufficiency, isolating the effect of the explanation's content.
- Core assumption: The fixed scorer model provides a consistent and reliable measure of whether an explanation contains sufficient information to justify the correct answer, acting as an invariant "judge."
- Evidence anchors: [section 3.3] "Evaluation is performed using an asymmetric setup: while multiple models generate explanations, a single fixed scorer model M is employed to assess sufficiency across all conditions, ensuring comparability." [section 4.2] "A single fixed scorer model, Qwen3 1.7B... was used to evaluate explanation sufficiency."
- Break condition: The scorer model itself has inherent biases (e.g., towards certain linguistic patterns or reasoning structures) that systematically misjudge sufficiency, especially for non-English (Persian) explanations, confounding the measured trade-off.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The sufficiency-conciseness trade-off is language-dependent, with resource-limited languages (Persian) exhibiting steeper performance degradation under compression.
- Mechanism: Translating the ARC-Challenge dataset creates a setting with potentially less training data and lower model capability for Persian. This reduces the amount of "redundant" information that can be safely compressed, making concise explanations less robust.
- Core assumption: Models have less exposure to high-quality reasoning chains in low-resource languages, so their generated explanations have less inherent redundancy; thus, any removal of content more quickly damages sufficiency.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "The Persian (resource-limited) setting exhibited steeper declines, highlighting language-specific challenges." [section 5.4] "the degradation is steeper in Persian compared to English, reflecting the additional difficulty of generating effective concise explanations in a low-resource language."
- Break condition: The observed language effect is an artifact of the translation process (e.g., semantic drift, loss of nuance) rather than an intrinsic property of the language, meaning the Persian dataset does not fairly represent reasoning in that language.

## Foundational Learning
**Information Bottleneck (IB) Principle**  
Why needed here: Provides the theoretical framing that explanations (Z) should be compressed representations of the input/question (X) that maximize information about the answer (Y). It formally defines the trade-off between conciseness (minimizing I(X;Z)) and sufficiency (maximizing I(Z;Y)).  
Quick check: If mutual information I(X;Z) cannot be computed for LLM text outputs, what practical proxy metrics are used in this study to represent the two competing objectives?

**Explanation Sufficiency (Faithfulness)**  
Why needed here: The core dependent variable. It operationalizes whether an explanation genuinely supports the correct answer, moving beyond just the final answer's correctness. This distinguishes between a lucky guess and a justified reasoning process.

**Operationalizing Conciseness via Length Constraints**  
Why needed here: Conciseness is the independent variable manipulated in the experiment. The study moves beyond vague notions of "brevity" by enforcing explicit, progressive word-count limits during generation.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Generator Models (7 LLMs) → Constraint Applier (prompt injection) → Post-Processing/Masking Module → Fixed Scorer Model (Qwen 1.7B) → Metric Aggregator

### Critical path
1. For each question, obtain the initial unconstrained explanation Z0 from the generator.
2. For each compression level v (10%, 20%, ..., 90%), prompt the same generator with a length constraint to produce Zv.
3. Apply masking to Zv to remove explicit answer leakage (option letters, verbatim text).
4. Build the scorer prompt Pv containing the question, masked explanation, and options.
5. Query the fixed scorer model to obtain the probability assigned to the correct answer, p(y|Pv).
6. Aggregate sufficiency scores, accuracy, and embedding similarity across the dataset for each condition.

### Design tradeoffs
- Fixed Scorer vs. Multiple Scorers: A single scorer ensures consistency but may not generalize; using the generator as its own scorer would confound the measurement.
- Hard Length Constraint vs. Soft Guidance: Explicit word limits guarantee conciseness but may force awkward phrasing; softer instructions (e.g., "be brief") were avoided to make the trade-off measurable.
- Binary Masking vs. Nuanced Cleaning: Simple replacement of option text is crude but prevents leakage; more sophisticated cleaning might remove subtle cues but is error-prone.

### Failure signatures
- **Constraint violation**: Generator ignores length limits, producing Zv longer than allowed. Diagnostic: compute word count of Zv; if > limit, note discrepancy.
- **Incomplete masking**: Residual answer cues (e.g., paraphrased correct option) inflate sufficiency. Diagnostic: manually examine masked Zv samples; refine masking if needed.
- **Scorer baseline drift**: Scorer's no-explanation accuracy differs markedly from expected baseline (e.g., Table 1). Diagnostic: evaluate baseline on a held-out set; check model version and log-prob handling.
- **Translation-induced noise**: Persian results degrade due to mistranslation rather than language limitation. Diagnostic: back-translate Persian samples and compute similarity to original; low scores indicate poor translation quality.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline Scorer Capability (Table 1)** – Run the fixed scorer on the dataset without any explanations to establish its intrinsic accuracy and baseline sufficiency.
2. **Full-Explanation Performance (Table 2)** – Generate unconstrained explanations (Z0) for all seven generator models in both languages and evaluate their accuracy and sufficiency to establish peak performance.
3. **Progressive Compression Curves (Figures 3 & 4)** – For a selected model (e.g., DeepSeek V3.1), generate and evaluate all constraint levels (v = 10, 20, ..., 90). Plot sufficiency and accuracy versus constraint level to visualize the trade-off curve and identify the knee point before sharp decline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can the proposed evaluation framework be extended to open-ended generation tasks (e.g., creative writing, dialogue) where ground-truth answers are less defined?  
  *Basis in paper:* The conclusion states: "Future work will extend the framework to open-ended tasks..."  
  *Why unresolved:* The current pipeline relies on multiple-choice QA with a fixed scorer, which may not apply to tasks without clear correct answers or where sufficiency is less measurable.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Adapt the pipeline to tasks like summarization or story generation, defining task-specific sufficiency metrics (e.g., human evaluations, downstream task performance) and testing conciseness trade-offs.

- **Open Question 2:** Can automatic, dynamic conciseness control be achieved during explanation generation, rather than using fixed post-hoc length constraints?  
  *Basis in paper:* The conclusion mentions investigating "automatic conciseness control."  
  *Why unresolved:* The study uses manually set percentage constraints (e.g., 20%, 90% reduction), which require prior knowledge of original length and may not adapt to content.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Develop reinforcement learning or controllable decoding methods that learn optimal compression points based on a sufficiency reward, and compare performance against fixed constraints.

- **Open Question 3:** To what extent do the findings on sufficiency-conciseness trade-offs generalize beyond English and Persian to other languages, especially those with different script families or morphological complexity?  
  *Basis in paper:* The conclusion proposes to "explore sufficiency preservation under multilingual... conditions."  
  *Why unresolved:* The bilingual experiment only covers English (Indo-European, Latinate script) and Persian (Indo-European, Arabic script). Other language families (e.g., agglutinative, logographic) may exhibit different compression-sufficiency dynamics.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate the pipeline on typologically diverse languages (e.g., Finnish, Chinese, Arabic) and analyze whether the observed "steep decline" pattern in low-resource settings holds universally.

- **Open Question 4:** Does the scorer-based sufficiency metric (probability assigned to correct answer) faithfully approximate the Information Bottleneck's mutual information objective, or does it introduce biases?  
  *Basis in paper:* The methodology admits that direct mutual information computation is intractable, so they use a proxy: "We define Sufficiency as the probability the model assigns to the correct answer... This allows us to empirically evaluate the balance."  
  *Why unresolved:* The scorer's probability may be influenced by superficial cues, priors, or its own limitations (e.g., being a small 1.7B model), rather than capturing the true information-theoretic compression.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Compare scorer-based sufficiency with alternative metrics (e.g., human judgments, faithfulness probes, or model-internal information measures if accessible) and correlate with compression levels.

- **Open Question 5:** What specific linguistic or reasoning features in explanations are most critical for preserving sufficiency under compression, given that semantic similarity (embedding cosine) does not consistently predict sufficiency?  
  *Basis in paper:* The results note: "semantic similarity does not always align with sufficiency... structural and causal cues in the explanation are also essential." The Persian explanations degrade faster in similarity but the sufficiency drop is steeper, suggesting other factors.  
  *Why unresolved:* The study measures semantic similarity via embeddings but does not analyze what aspects (e.g., logical connectors, entity co-reference, causal verbs) are lost during compression and cause sufficiency loss.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct fine-grained linguistic analyses (e.g., POS tagging, dependency parsing, logical form extraction) on compressed vs. original explanations, and perform ablation studies to identify key sufficiency-preserving elements.

## Limitations
- Undisclosed prompt templates and translation details hinder exact replication.
- Fixed scorer's low baseline accuracy on Persian (47.85%) threatens cross-linguistic reliability.
- Findings are limited to the ARC-Challenge dataset and a narrow set of models, reducing generalizability.

## Confidence
- Effective compression: High
- Trade-off existence: High
- Fixed scorer consistency: Medium
- Language difference: Medium
- Optimal thresholds: High (existence) / Medium (precise values)
