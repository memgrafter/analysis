# Vocabulary shapes cross-lingual variation of word-order learnability in language models

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.19427
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19427
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** Cross-lingual variation in Transformer language model surprisal under word-order perturbation is predicted by vocabulary structure metrics (e.g., Zipfian coverage, morphological complexity proxies) rather than by traditional free/fixed word-order classification.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates why languages differ in how easily their word-order patterns can be learned by language models. Researchers created synthetic versions of ten European languages by deterministically shuffling words in sentences using the Mallows permutation model, which preserves sentence length distributions and overall text entropy while varying word-order irregularity.Autoregressive Transformer language models were trained from scratch on each shuffled variant and evaluated via token surprisal. Results showed that increasing word-order irregularity consistently raised model surprisal across languages. Crucially, the classic binary distinction between "free-word-order" and "fixed-word-order" languages failed to account for the observed differences in learnability. Instead, metrics describing the languages' vocabulary structure—such as the coverage of frequent words/subwords, average sentence length, and proxies for morphological complexity—explained over 97% of the variance in model performance. The findings highlight that a language's lexicon, not just its syntax, fundamentally shapes the computational learnability of its word order.

## Method Summary
The core experiment manipulates word order while controlling for other textual properties. For each of ten European languages from the Europarl corpus, the researchers preprocessed sentences (lowercasing, punctuation removal, length filtering 1–80 words) and split them into 650k train, 5k validation, and 5k test sets. Word-order variants were generated by applying a single, deterministic permutation to all sentences of a given length *n*, sampled from a Mallows(θ) model parameterized by θ (which controls deviation from canonical order). This process preserves global entropy and sentence length distribution. For each language and θ value, a fresh ByteLevel BPE tokenizer (vocab size 16,000) was trained on the shuffled training text. A 50.5M parameter autoregressive Transformer (PICOLM architecture) was then trained for 1000 steps from random initialization. Model performance was measured by average test-set surprisal. Language-specific vocabulary metrics were extracted from the unshuffled corpora to predict surprisal changes via Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression.

## Key Results
*   Increased word-order irregularity (lower θ) monotonically increased model surprisal for all languages, indicating reduced learnability.
*   The traditional binary classification into free-word-order (e.g., Czech) and fixed-word-order (e.g., English) languages did not correlate with the magnitude of surprisal increase.
*   Vocabulary structure metrics—including word/subword coverage at rank 100, coverage integral, sentence length, and morphological complexity proxies like fertility (subwords per word)—strongly predicted surprisal changes. A PLS regression model explained 97% of the variance across languages and perturbation levels.
*   Subword-level (token) shuffling, which breaks morphology, amplified language-specific differences more than word-level shuffling, underscoring the role of tokenization.
*   In vocabulary size experiments, surprisal patterns diverged between free- and fixed-word-order languages above a vocabulary size of ~8,000 tokens, with free-word-order languages utilizing more low-frequency subwords and showing less robustness to irregular order.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism is that the vocabulary's structural properties—captured by metrics like coverage curves, fertility, and type diversity—act as a proxy for the language's morphological complexity and the granularity of its subword representation. Languages with richer morphology (often free-word-order) produce vocabularies with steeper Zipf curves, higher fertility, and greater type diversity at a given token budget. These properties make subword representations less cohesive and more sensitive to disruption in word order. When word order is perturbed, models trained on such languages experience a larger increase in surprisal because their subword sequences are less redundant and the loss of canonical syntax removes more predictive signal. Conversely, languages with simpler morphology rely on more stable, frequent word forms and fixed syntax, making them more robust to the same perturbations. Thus, the vocabulary metrics effectively quantify a language's "buffer" against order disruption.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Mallows Permutation Model:** A probabilistic model for generating permutations where the probability decays exponentially with Kendall's τ distance from a central (canonical) order. *Why needed:* Provides a controlled, parametric way to generate syntactically plausible but increasingly irregular word orders while preserving sentence length distribution and entropy. *Quick check:* For a small *n* (e.g., 5) and a given θ, sample many permutations and verify their mean Kendall's τ matches the theoretical value `(1 - exp(-θ))/(1 + exp(-θ))`.
*   **Token Surprisal:** The negative log-likelihood assigned by a language model to a token, `-log p(w_i | context)`. *Why needed:* Primary metric for quantifying model uncertainty and processing difficulty under perturbation. *Quick check:* Train a small LM on a held-out set; average token surprisal should be near the model's cross-entropy loss.
*   **Vocabulary Coverage Metrics:** Measures like the number of types covered by the top *k* most frequent words/subwords, or the integral under the frequency-rank curve. *Why needed:* Quantify how efficiently a vocabulary captures a language's types, which relates to morphological synthesis. *Quick check:* For a corpus, plot rank vs. cumulative frequency; compute the fraction of total tokens accounted for by the top 100 words.
*   **Morphological Complexity Proxies:** Metrics such as fertility (average subwords per word) and average word length. *Why needed:* Complex morphology leads to longer words and more subword splits, increasing token sequence length and reducing predictability from local context. *Quick check:* Train a BPE tokenizer; compute average tokens per word across a sample.
*   **Partial Least Squares (PLS) Regression:** A dimensional reduction technique that finds latent components maximizing covariance between predictors and response. *Why needed:* To model high-dimensional, correlated vocabulary metrics as predictors of surprisal across many (language × θ) conditions. *Quick check:* Apply PLS to a synthetic dataset with known latent factors; verify component loadings recover the signal.

## Architecture Onboarding
The experimental pipeline is a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) of transformations applied per (language, θ) pair:
Raw Europarl Text -> Preprocessing (clean, lowercase, filter) -> Word-level Mallows Shuffling (deterministic per sentence length) -> Tokenizer Training (ByteLevel BPE on shuffled text) -> LM Training (PICOLM, 1000 steps) -> Evaluation (test-set surprisal). An offline branch extracts vocabulary metrics from the *unshuffled* training corpus for analysis.

**Critical Path:** The sequence Data Prep -> Shuffling -> Tokenization -> Training -> Evaluation is strictly linear and must be completed for each variant without skipping or reusing intermediate artifacts (e.g., tokenizer must be retrained on each shuffled corpus).

**Design Tradeoffs:**
*   *Word-level vs. Subword-level Shuffling:* Word-level preserves morphological integrity but may still disrupt syntax; subword-level breaks morphology entirely, creating a more severe perturbation but confounding syntax with morphology.
*   *Deterministic per-length vs. Per-sentence Shuffling:* Determinism across sentences of same length controls for global text entropy changes; random per-sentence shuffling would add uncontrolled noise.
*   *Fixed vs. Varied Vocabulary Size:* Fixed size isolates word-order effects; varying size reveals interaction between tokenization budget and language-specific subword utilization.

**Failure Signatures:**
*   *Shuffling Error:* For a given *n*, shuffled sentences do not share the same permutation pattern. Surprisal curves become noisy and inconsistent across seeds.
*   *Tokenizer Mismatch:* The tokenizer is trained on unshuffled text but applied to shuffled text, or vice versa. Fertility metrics will deviate from expected patterns (e.g., lower than reported for free-word-order languages).
*   *Mallows Distribution Error:* The mean Kendall's τ distance for sampled permutations does not follow the theoretical curve for the chosen θ. This directly invalidates the perturbation gradient.

**First Three Experiments:**
1.  Validate the Mallows implementation: For θ = {0, 3, 9}, generate 10k permutations of n=50. Compute mean Kendall's τ and compare to formula.
2.  Preprocessing fidelity check: Process one raw Europarl file with the exact code from the official repository (if available) or the described rules. Compare sentence count, average length, and token count to values reported or implied in the paper.
3.  PLS out-of-sample test: Using the reproduced surprisal dataset, perform strict leave-one-language-out cross-validation. Train PLS on 9 languages' data, predict the held-out language's surprisal across all θ. Report out-of-sample R²; expect a drop from the reported 97% if the model is overfit.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Architectural disentanglement:** How do prediction objective (autoregressive vs. masked), tokenization scheme, and positional encoding independently affect sensitivity to word-order perturbations? The study uses a single architecture, preventing isolation of these factors.
*   **Human alignment:** Does model surprisal under these perturbations correlate with human processing difficulty or acquisition data for comparable synthetic languages? The study evaluates only LMs, leaving cognitive plausibility untested.
*   **Evolutionary causality:** What is the causal direction between morphological complexity and word-order flexibility in language change? The perturbation experiment holds morphology constant, so it cannot address historical causation.
*   **Subword divergence threshold:** Why do subword coverage patterns diverge between free- and fixed-word-order languages specifically above a vocabulary size of 8,000 tokens? The vocabulary size experiment shows the effect but does not probe the internal lexicon structure causing the threshold.
*   **Beyond word-level:** Would morpheme-level or part-of-speech-dependent shuffling produce different learnability patterns, potentially disentangling morphology from syntax more cleanly? The authors propose these as future extensions.

## Limitations
*   **Preprocessing ambiguity:** Exact regex patterns and language-specific normalization rules (e.g., Finnish `EU: n` → `EU:n`) are not fully specified in the paper, risking token boundary discrepancies.
*   **θ specification gap:** The precise numeric values for the "roughly log-scaled" θ scan (11 points in Fig. 2) are not listed, hindering exact curve reproduction.
*   **Implementation opacity:** The PICOLM architecture defaults (initialization, dropout, layer norms) from the cited Diehl Martinez et al. (2025) are not detailed, leaving room for subtle training differences.
*   **PLS generalizability:** The 97% R² may reflect in-sample fit across the scanned θ spectrum; out-of-sample predictive power (e.g., leave-one-language-out) is not confirmed.
*   **Threshold mechanism unclear:** The specific reason for the 8,000-token vocabulary divergence threshold between language groups remains speculative without micro-structural analysis of subword lexicons.

## Confidence
*   **Word-order irregularity increases surprisal:** High – Direction and general shape are robust across languages and clearly illustrated.
*   **Binary free/fixed typology fails to explain variance:** Medium – Claim depends on exact language grouping and θ range; plausible but requires exact replication with the same languages and perturbation spectrum.
*   **Vocabulary metrics strongly predict surprisal:** Medium – High R² is striking, but confidence is tempered by uncertainties in predictor computation and regression validation procedure.
*   **Morphological complexity confers robustness:** Medium – Supported by fertility correlations and PLS loadings, but the perturbation design only shows association, not causation in language evolution.

## Next Checks
1.  **Mallows distribution validation:** For each claimed θ value, generate 10,000 permutations of a fixed *n* (e.g., 50). Compute mean Kendall's τ distance and compare to the analytical mean `(1 - exp(-θ))/(1 + exp(-θ))`. A discrepancy >0.02 indicates a sampling or distance implementation error.
2.  **Preprocessing fidelity audit:** Obtain the official preprocessing script (from code repo if possible). Apply it to an identical raw Europarl sample for a language like Czech. Compare resulting sentence counts, average lengths, and token counts to the paper's reported statistics. A divergence >2% requires rule reconciliation.
3.  **PLS out-of-sample test:** With the reproduced (language, θ) surprisal matrix, perform leave-one-language-out cross-validation: train PLS on data from 9 languages, predict the held-out language's surprisal for all θ values. Compute mean squared error and R² for each held-out language. If average out-of-sample R² falls significantly below 0.8, the 97% figure is likely in-sample optimism.
