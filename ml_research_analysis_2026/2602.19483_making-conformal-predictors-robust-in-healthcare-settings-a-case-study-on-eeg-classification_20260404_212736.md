# Making Conformal Predictors Robust in Healthcare Settings: a Case Study on EEG Classification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19483
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19483
- **Reference count**: 17
- **Primary result**: Neighborhood Conformal Prediction (NCP) improves empirical coverage by over 20 percentage points compared to naive split conformal prediction under patient distribution shifts in EEG seizure classification, while maintaining comparable prediction set sizes.

## Executive Summary
Standard conformal prediction assumes i.i.d. data, but in healthcare settings like EEG seizure classification, patient-level distribution shifts between calibration and test sets violate this assumption, leading to poor coverage. This paper introduces Neighborhood Conformal Prediction (NCP), a personalized calibration method that uses k-nearest neighbors in the embedding space of a pre-trained classifier (ContraWR) to compute test-point-specific nonconformity thresholds. NCP improves empirical coverage by over 20 percentage points (e.g., 25% higher at α=0.2) compared to naive split conformal prediction, while keeping prediction set sizes similar. Theoretical analysis under covariate shift shows that NCP reduces coverage error by decreasing the variability of conditional coverage. In contrast, non‑personalized covariate shift correction via density estimation (KDE) fails to improve coverage, suggesting that EEG distribution shifts are more complex than simple covariate shift. The authors also release the PyHealth library to lower barriers to adoption.

## Method Summary
The study uses two EEG datasets from the Temple University Hospital EEG Corpus: TUAB (binary abnormal/normal) and TUEV (multiclass). EEG segments are preprocessed into multi‑channel spectrograms. A CNN‑based classifier, ContraWR, is trained on a 60% training split and validated on 10%. For conformal prediction, a 15% calibration set provides nonconformity scores V(x,y) = 1 − p_θ(y|x), where p_θ is the classifier’s predicted probability. Neighborhood Conformal Prediction (NCP) operates as follows: for a test sample, its embedding Φ(x) (from ContraWR’s penultimate layer) is used to retrieve k nearest neighbors from the calibration store (which holds precomputed Φ(x_i) and V_i). NCP weights the neighbors’ scores (e.g., by inverse distance) and computes the (1−α) weighted quantile as the threshold. The prediction set includes all classes with V(x,y) below that threshold. Baselines include naive split CP and weighted CP using KDE‑estimated density ratios.

## Key Results
- NCP improves empirical coverage by over 20 percentage points compared to naive split CP, with a 25% improvement at α=0.2.
- NCP achieves these coverage gains while maintaining comparable average prediction set sizes.
- Covariate shift correction via KDE‑based importance weighting does not significantly improve coverage over naive CP.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Local calibration via k‑NN in embedding space
NCP uses test‑point‑specific thresholds by finding k‑nearest neighbors in the calibration set within the model’s embedding space Φ(x). It then computes a weighted (1−α) quantile from their nonconformity scores, rather than a global quantile. This localization assumes that patients with similar embeddings have similar conditional distributions P(Y|X) and thus similar nonconformity score distributions. Evidence is given in Section 2.2 and the abstract’s claim of >20% coverage improvement. The method may fail if the embedding does not encode relevant patient similarity (e.g., if it clusters by artifacts instead of physiology).

### Mechanism 2: Reducing coverage error via variance reduction under covariate shift
Theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) decomposes test‑domain coverage error into Cov(w(X), s(X)), where w is the density ratio and s(x)=c(x)−(1−α) is the conditional coverage deviation. Standard CP’s global threshold can yield high Var(s). NCP’s local calibration makes s(x) more stable, reducing Var(s). By Cauchy‑Schwarz, a smaller Var(s) tightly bounds |Cov(w,s)|, thus minimizing coverage error for a given shift magnitude Var(w). Evidence: Section 2.4/2.5 and abstract. This relies on the covariate shift assumption and that local thresholds are conditionally valid; if neighborhoods are too small (high variance) or too large (loss of locality), the reduction may not occur.

### Mechanism 3: Failure of non‑personalized density ratio correction
Weighted Conformal Prediction (Covariate CP) uses importance weights w(x)=p_test(x)/p_cal(x) estimated via KDE to adjust the global threshold. In the EEG experiments, this approach does not improve coverage over naive CP. This indicates that either the covariate shift assumption is violated (e.g., label or concept shift dominates) or that KDE is too inaccurate in high dimensions to estimate useful density ratios. Evidence: Section 3 and abstract. The method would fail if the true density ratio is too complex for KDE or if the representation used for KDE does not align with the shift.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map** (data flow):  
Raw EEG segment → ContraWR (base model) → embedding Φ(x) and class probabilities p(y|x) → Neighborhood Sampler queries Calibration Store (precomputed Φ(x_i), V_i) → weighted nonconformity scores → Weighted Quantile Calculator → threshold q̂_{1−α}(x) → Prediction Set Generator → final prediction set.

**Critical path**:
1. Compute Φ(x) and p(y|x) for test sample using ContraWR.
2. Find k‑nearest neighbors in calibration set based on distance in Φ‑space.
3. Compute weighted (1−α) quantile of neighbors’ nonconformity scores.
4. Form prediction set by including classes with V(x,y) ≤ threshold.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **k selection**: Small k increases variance in quantile estimate; large k dilutes locality. Requires validation.
- **Embedding choice**: Using the classifier’s penultimate layer is convenient but may not optimally encode patient similarity; a dedicated metric learning layer could improve neighborhoods.
- **Computational cost**: k‑NN search per test sample is O(n_cal·d) without indexing; approximate nearest neighbor methods are needed for large calibration sets.

**Failure signatures**:
- Systematic undercoverage at very low α (high confidence), as seen at α=0.01 in experiments.
- Covariate CP performs no better than Naive CP, indicating density ratio estimation failure.
- Prediction set sizes explode for test points with no close neighbors (“orphan” points).

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Replicate core coverage result: On TUAB/TUEV with fixed k, compare empirical coverage of NCP vs. Naive CP across α ∈ [0.01, 0.2]. Confirm >20% improvement at α=0.2.
2. Sensitivity to k and embedding quality: Sweep k ∈ {5,20,50,100} and compare embeddings from ContraWR, random features, and contrastive learning; measure coverage and average set size.
3. Diagnose Covariate CP failure: Visualize estimated density ratio w(x) via UMAP, correlate with patient metadata or recording sites; test whether a flexible density estimator (e.g., normalizing flows) can recover coverage.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Do personalized conformal predictors generalize beyond EEG?**  
   *Basis*: Future work suggests extending to other healthcare domains.  
   *Why unresolved*: Only two EEG datasets were studied; other domains (e.g., medical imaging, EHR) may exhibit different shift characteristics.  
   *Evidence needed*: Systematic evaluation of NCP on multiple benchmarks (e.g., MIMIC‑III, ISIC) with quantified distribution shifts.

2. **Can specialized EEG foundation models close the coverage gap at low α?**  
   *Basis*: Future work mentions incorporating more powerful EEG pre‑training (e.g., TFM‑Tokenizer).  
   *Why unresolved*: NCP underperforms at α=0.01, limiting high‑stakes use; better embeddings might produce more discriminative neighborhoods.  
   *Evidence needed*: Replace ContraWR with state‑of‑the‑art EEG foundation models, re‑run experiments at α=0.01, and compare coverage/set size.

3. **What barriers impede clinical adoption, and can PyHealth mitigate them?**  
   *Basis*: Future work aims to lower entry barriers via open‑source PyHealth.  
   *Why unresolved*: Real‑world deployment requires integration with clinical systems, clinician training, and regulatory validation—factors not studied.  
   *Evidence needed*: Mixed‑methods research with clinicians and ML engineers to identify hurdles; design and release PyHealth modules for workflow integration; pilot deployments measuring usability and impact.

4. **Why does NCP fail to achieve nominal coverage at very low α (e.g., 0.01)?**  
   *Basis*: Inferred from experimental results showing undercoverage at α=0.01.  
   *Why unresolved*: High‑risk applications demand stringent coverage; understanding failure modes (e.g., neighbor sparsity, noise sensitivity) is critical for safe use.  
   *Evidence needed*: Ablation over k at α=0.01; coverage analysis conditioned on patient subgroups or EEG signal quality; theoretical examination of weighted quantile estimation under small‑neighborhood asymptotics; alternative nonconformity scores.

5. **What shift mechanisms make covariate shift correction via density estimation ineffective?**  
   *Basis*: Covariate CP fails; paper suggests EEG shifts are more complex than simple covariate shift.  
   *Why unresolved*: Misdiagnosing shift type leads to ineffective corrections; EEG shifts may involve label or concept shift, invalidating importance weighting assumptions.  
   *Evidence needed*: Apply shift diagnosis tests (e.g., for label shift, concept shift) on calibration vs. test cohorts; evaluate conformal methods under different shift assumptions; characterize shift via clinical metadata (age, device, site).

## Limitations
- Exact NCP hyperparameters (k, kernel bandwidth) are unreported, affecting reproducibility and sensitivity analysis.
- The method relies on the quality of the model’s embedding Φ(x); if patient similarity is not encoded, local calibration may degrade coverage.
- Covariate CP’s failure root cause remains unclear—it could stem from inaccurate density ratio estimation or non‑covariate shift (label/concept shift).

## Confidence
- **NCP improves coverage via local calibration**: Medium–High confidence. Strong empirical gains are reported, but sensitivity to k and embedding choice is uncertain.
- **Variance reduction under covariate shift explains NCP’s theoretical advantage**: Medium confidence. The decomposition is plausible but assumes idealized conditions (e.g., E[s]≈0) that may not hold.
- **Non‑personalized density correction fails**: Medium confidence. Results are consistent, yet the precise failure mode (estimation vs. shift type) is not definitively diagnosed.
