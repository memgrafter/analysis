# Mind the Shift: Decoding Monetary Policy Stance from FOMC Statements with Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.14313
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14313
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: DCS achieves up to 71.1% sentence-level accuracy on hawkish-dovish classification and shows strong economic validity with Spearman correlations up to 0.62 with CPI and 0.55 with PPI, predicting Treasury yields.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Delta-Consistent Scoring (DCS), a framework that leverages frozen large language models (LLMs) to produce continuous monetary‑policy stance scores from FOMC statements without manual labeling. DCS treats the chronological sequence of statements as a source of self‑supervision: it learns to align absolute stance per meeting with relative shifts between consecutive meetings via a delta‑consistency objective. The method uses two prompt views (absolute and relative), a dual‑axis linear projection, and a confidence regularizer, followed by a post‑hoc polarity anchoring step. DCS achieves state‑of‑the‑art sentence‑level classification accuracy (up to 71.1%) and meeting‑level scores that robustly correlate with inflation metrics and Treasury yields across maturities.

## Method Summary
DCS processes a sequence of FOMC statements. For each meeting, an *absolute* prompt (“Consider the following FOMC statement. What is the stance?”) is fed to a frozen LLM to extract the final‑layer hidden state \(h_{\text{abs}}^t\). For every consecutive pair \((t-1, t)\), a *relative* prompt (“Compare the following two FOMC statements. Which is more hawkish?”) yields \(h_{\text{rel}}^t\). Two independent linear projections then produce a raw absolute logit \(z_{\text{abs}}^t = \theta_{\text{abs}}^\top h_{\text{abs}}^t + b_{\text{abs}}\) and a raw relative shift logit \(z_{\text{rel}}^t = \theta_{\text{rel}}^\top h_{\text{rel}}^t + b_{\text{rel}}\). The absolute stance score is \(s_t = \sigma(z_{\text{abs}}^t)\); the estimated shift is \(\delta_t = \tanh(z_{\text{rel}}^t / \tau)\). Training minimizes the **delta‑consistency loss** \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{delta}} = \mathbb{E}\big[(z_{\text{abs}}^t - z_{\text{abs}}^{t-1} - \alpha \cdot \delta_t)^2\big]\), which forces the change in absolute scores to match the predicted relative shift (scale factor \(\alpha\) is learned). A **confidence regularizer** \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{conf}} = -\lambda \cdot \text{Entropy}(s_t)\) encourages scores to move away from the ambiguous 0.5 region. After convergence, a set of hand‑picked hawkish and dovish anchor sentences is used to fix polarity: if the mean absolute score for hawkish anchors is lower than for dovish anchors, both projection heads are sign‑flipped so that higher scores correspond to more hawkish language.

## Key Results
- **Sentence‑level accuracy**: Up to 71.1% on a hawkish‑dovish classification benchmark, outperforming supervised probes and LLM‑as‑judge baselines across four LLM backbones.
- **Economic validity**: Meeting‑level scores correlate with CPI (Spearman \(\rho = 0.62\)) and PPI (\(\rho = 0.55\)).
- **Predictive power**: Regression analysis shows significant associations with Treasury yields (2‑year, 10‑year, 20‑year) with robust Newey–West standard errors; results hold across different policy regimes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: DCS learns a temporally coherent stance trajectory by aligning absolute stance score changes with relative shift estimates between consecutive meetings, using temporal ordering as implicit supervision without manual labels.
- **Mechanism**: For each consecutive pair of statements, DCS extracts an absolute stance score \((s_{t-1}, s_t)\) via a linear projection on the LLM's absolute representation (\(h_{\text{abs}}\)), and a relative shift score \(\delta_t\) via a separate projection on the relative representation (\(h_{\text{rel}}\)). The delta‑consistency loss penalizes squared deviation between the actual score difference and a scaled tanh‑bounded \(\delta_t\): \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{delta}} = \mathbb{E}\big[(z_{\text{abs}}^t - z_{\text{abs}}^{t-1} - \alpha\cdot\tanh(z_{\text{rel}}^t/\tau))^2\big]\). This enforces that the direction and magnitude of score changes match the estimated shifts.
- **Core assumption**: The change in the LLM's absolute hidden state between consecutive statements reflects the true policy shift, and the relative prompt captures this shift in a scale that can be aligned via the tanh transformation.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "DCS uses consecutive meetings as a source of self‑supervision… a delta‑consistency objective encourages changes in absolute scores to align with the relative shifts."
  - Section 3.4: Explicit loss definition \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{delta}} = \mathbb{E}\big[(z_{\text{abs}}^t - z_{\text{abs}}^{t-1} - \alpha\cdot\tanh(z_{\text{rel}}^t/\tau))^2\big]\).
- **Break condition**: If the relative representation does not correlate with the true difference in absolute stances, the loss cannot provide useful gradients, leading to arbitrary absolute scores.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: DCS separates absolute and relative stance signals via distinct prompt views and projection heads, allowing each to specialize and preventing interference.
- **Mechanism**: Two prompt templates are used: an absolute prompt asks for the stance of a single statement; a relative prompt compares the current statement to the previous one. Hidden states from the final layer are projected separately: \(z_{\text{abs}} = \theta_{\text{abs}}^\top h_{\text{abs}} + b_{\text{abs}}\) and \(z_{\text{rel}} = \theta_{\text{rel}}^\top h_{\text{rel}} + b_{\text{rel}}\). The relative output is bounded by \(\tanh(z_{\text{rel}}/\tau)\) to keep shift magnitudes comparable to score differences.
- **Core assumption**: The two prompts elicit representations that predominantly encode the respective concepts (absolute stance vs. relative shift) and these concepts occupy separable directions in the frozen LLM's representation space.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Section 3.2: Describes the two prompt views and the extraction of \(h_{\text{abs}}\) and \(h_{\text{rel}}\).
  - Section 3.3: Details the dual‑axis projection with separate parameters.
- **Break condition**: If the absolute and relative representations are highly correlated or lie in overlapping subspaces, the separate projections may not yield distinct signals, causing the delta‑consistency constraint to be ineffective or leading to degenerate solutions.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: DCS resolves the polarity ambiguity inherent in the self‑supervised objective by post‑hoc alignment with a small set of anchor sentences, ensuring interpretability (higher scores = more hawkish).
- **Mechanism**: After training, compute mean absolute logits \(\bar{z}_{\text{hawk}}\) for a set of hawkish anchor sentences and \(\bar{z}_{\text{dove}}\) for dovish anchors. If \(\bar{z}_{\text{hawk}} < \bar{z}_{\text{dove}}\), flip the signs of both \(\theta_{\text{abs}}, b_{\text{abs}}\) and \(\theta_{\text{rel}}, b_{\text{rel}}\). This flips the entire stance axis while preserving the relative ordering learned.
- **Core assumption**: The anchor sentences are unambiguous exemplars of hawkish and dovish language, and flipping both axes maintains internal consistency between absolute and relative scores.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Section 3.5: "We flip both axes so that the sign convention remains consistent between absolute stance scores and relative shifts."
  - Appendix C: Lists the 15 hawkish and 15 dovish anchor sentences used.
- **Break condition**: If the anchor sentences are not representative (e.g., from a different policy regime or containing mixed signals), the polarity flip could invert the true economic meaning.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
- **Input**: Chronological sequence of FOMC post‑meeting statements (pre‑filtered).
- **Prompt generation**: Two templates (absolute and relative) produce text inputs.
- **Frozen LLM**: Encodes prompts into hidden states (last token, final layer).
- **Dual‑axis module**: Two linear layers projecting \(h_{\text{abs}} \rightarrow z_{\text{abs}}\) and \(h_{\text{rel}} \rightarrow z_{\text{rel}}\).
- **Loss computation**: Delta‑loss (matches \((z_{\text{abs}}^t - z_{\text{abs}}^{t-1})\) to \(\tanh(z_{\text{rel}}^t/\tau)\)) and confidence regularizer (entropy of \(\sigma(z_{\text{abs}})\)).
- **Training**: Optimize projection parameters with frozen LLM.
- **Post‑hoc anchoring**: Flip sign based on anchor sentence means.
- **Output**: Continuous stance score \(s_t = \sigma(z_{\text{abs}}^t)\) per meeting.

### Critical path
1. **Preprocess**: Apply rule‑based sentence filter (dictionary matching) to each statement to retain policy‑relevant content.
2. **Encode**: For each meeting, run absolute prompt through frozen LLM to get \(h_{\text{abs}}^t\); for each consecutive pair \((t-1, t)\), run relative prompt to get \(h_{\text{rel}}^t\).
3. **Train**: Initialize dual‑axis projections; minimize \(\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{delta}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{conf}}\) over all consecutive pairs. Use delayed warm‑up for \(\lambda\).
4. **Anchor**: After convergence, compute mean \(z_{\text{abs}}\) for hawk/dove anchors; if \(\bar{z}_{\text{hawk}} < \bar{z}_{\text{dove}}\), flip signs of both projection heads.
5. **Inference**: For a new meeting, compute \(h_{\text{abs}}\), apply trained projection, sigmoid, and apply sign flip (same as training).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Annotation‑free vs. accuracy**: DCS eliminates costly labeling but may be less accurate than supervised methods if temporal consistency is a weak signal; however, it outperforms supervised probes in experiments.
- **Frozen vs. fine‑tuned**: Using frozen representations preserves general knowledge and avoids overfitting, but limits adaptation to domain specifics.
- **Dual‑axis vs. single‑axis**: Separate prompts add complexity but empirically improve performance by disentangling absolute and relative aspects (ablation shows single‑axis underperforms).
- **Continuous vs. discrete**: Continuous scores allow finer analysis and economic validation, but require post‑hoc anchoring for polarity.

### Failure signatures
- **Delta‑loss fails to decrease**: Might indicate that the relative prompt does not capture meaningful shift, or that model capacity is insufficient.
- **Absolute scores collapse to ~0.5**: Could be due to too low \(\lambda\) or missing confidence regularizer; scores remain uncertain.
- **Polarity flip reverses meaning**: Check anchor sentence content; if anchors are not truly hawkish/dovish, the flip may be incorrect.
- **Poor correlation with economic indicators**: May result from inadequate prompt design, insufficient training data, or mismatch between training period and evaluation period.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Loss convergence sanity check**: Train DCS on a small subset (e.g., 20 meetings) and plot \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{delta}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{conf}}\) over epochs. Ensure \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{delta}}\) decreases and absolute score differences correlate with relative shift predictions (Spearman between \((s_t - s_{t-1})\) and \(\delta_t\) should be positive).
2. **Sentence‑level classification benchmark**: Apply trained DCS to the Shah et al. (2023) sentence dataset, threshold scores at 0.5, and compute accuracy/F1. Compare against linear probe baseline; expect >55% accuracy on at least one backbone.
3. **Economic validation replication**: Compute meeting‑level DCS scores for the full period, standardize, and run univariate regression of 10‑year Treasury yield on score. Verify \(\beta > 0\) and \(p < 0.01\) (as in Table 3 for DeepSeek‑R1‑14B). Also compute Spearman with CPI/PPI; expect \(\rho > 0.5\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can DCS be extended to measure monetary policy stance for central banks with different communication structures or in languages other than English?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper focuses exclusively on U.S. FOMC statements in English. The authors state, "Our analysis is limited to FOMC statements" and suggest future work could apply the framework to "other central banks and communication forms."
- **Why unresolved**: The method's reliance on consecutive meeting ordering and specific prompt templates may not generalize to institutions with irregular meeting schedules, different rhetorical conventions, or multilingual contexts.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Apply DCS to a diverse set of central bank communications (e.g., ECB, BOJ) with translated or native‑language texts, and validate the resulting scores against market reactions or expert annotations for those institutions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How does DCS perform when the temporal sequence of statements is sparse or irregular, such as during unscheduled policy announcements or periods of meeting cancellations?
- **Basis in paper**: The method explicitly uses the consecutive ordering of meetings as a self‑supervision signal. The paper notes the approach "presumes a regular meeting cadence."
- **Why unresolved**: Financial markets react to any policy signal, including unscheduled communications. A method dependent on strict pairwise consecutive comparisons may fail or produce unreliable scores when the sequence is broken.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematically introduce gaps (e.g., removing meetings, adding unscheduled statements) into the FOMC timeline and measure the degradation in DCS's economic validity (correlations with yields/inflation) and internal consistency (delta‑consistency loss).

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Is the dual‑axis projection (separate absolute and relative prompts) necessary, or could a single, more powerful representation architecture achieve comparable performance?
- **Basis in paper**: The ablation study shows the dual‑axis design improves performance over a single‑axis variant, but the paper does not explore more sophisticated alternatives like attention mechanisms or separate fine‑tuned encoders for each signal.
- **Why unresolved**: The dual‑axis design introduces extra parameters and prompt engineering complexity. It is unclear if the observed gains are fundamental to separating the two concepts or an artifact of the simple linear probes used.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Compare DCS's dual‑axis linear probes against alternative architectures: (1) a single encoder with multi‑head outputs, (2) a model that directly predicts both score and shift from the same representation, or (3) learning separate prompt embeddings rather than fixed templates, while controlling for model size.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Does the delta‑consistency objective's assumption that the change in absolute scores should align linearly with the relative shift score hold universally across all policy regimes?
- **Basis in paper**: The delta‑consistency loss uses a scaled hyperbolic tangent (\(\alpha\cdot\tanh(z_{\text{rel}}/\tau)\)) to bound the relative output and aligns it with \((z_{\text{abs}}^t - z_{\text{abs}}^{t-1})\). This assumes a roughly linear, monotonic relationship between the latent shift signal and the change in absolute stance.
- **Why unresolved**: In extreme policy regimes (e.g., near‑zero lower bound, quantitative easing), the linguistic cues for "shift" might not map linearly to changes in a continuous hawkish‑dovish score. The paper's robustness test shows DCS correlates well across periods, but this does not validate the specific functional form of the loss.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Analyze the distribution of \((z_{\text{abs}}^t - z_{\text{abs}}^{t-1})\) vs. \(\delta_t\) across different policy periods. If systematic nonlinearities are found (e.g., compressed score changes during "lower for longer" periods), test alternative loss functions (e.g., with learned warping functions) and check for improved economic validity.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: How stable and model‑agnostic is the post‑hoc directional anchoring step? Could the polarity ambiguity be resolved during training instead of via a small, fixed set of anchor sentences?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper states the self‑supervised objective is "symmetric with respect to polarity" and resolves it post‑hoc using "a small set of hawkish and dovish exemplar sentences."
- **Why unresolved**: The anchoring relies on manually chosen exemplars. The choice of anchors could introduce bias, and the method's correctness depends on these few samples. The paper does not analyze sensitivity to anchor selection or explore end‑to‑end polarity resolution.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: (1) Test DCS with different sets of anchor sentences (e.g., varying number, style, source) and measure variance in final economic correlations. (2) Propose and evaluate a training‑time method to break symmetry, such as incorporating a tiny amount of labeled data, using a directional prior from dictionary scores, or enforcing monotonicity against an external time‑series like the Fed Funds Rate.

## Limitations
- **Filter specifics**: Exact sentence‑filtering rule (case sensitivity, stemming, co‑occurrence) is not fully detailed, which may alter the input corpus.
- **Thresholding & anchoring**: Binary classification threshold (likely 0.5) and the 30 anchor sentences' provenance affect polarity and score calibration.
- **Training details**: Batching, negative sampling, and warm‑up schedule are omitted, risking non‑reproducible optimization.

## Confidence
- **High**: DCS achieves state‑of‑the‑art sentence classification (71.1% accuracy).; Meeting‑level scores correlate with macro indicators (CPI \(\rho=0.62\), PPI \(\rho=0.55\)) and predict Treasury yields.
- **Medium**: Delta‑consistency objective is the key to learning shifts without labels.; Dual‑axis prompts disentangle absolute and relative signals.; Post‑hoc anchoring ensures interpretable polarity.

## Next Checks
1. **Filter reproduction**: Implement the exact sentence‑filtering rule, report the number of meetings with zero retained sentences, and verify against any provided statistics.
2. **Loss ablation**: Remove the delta‑consistency term (set \(\lambda=0\)) and compare both sentence accuracy and meeting‑level correlations to the full model; a substantial drop would confirm its importance.
3. **Anchoring robustness**: Substitute an alternative set of anchor sentences (e.g., from a different policy era) and check whether the resulting stance scores retain positive correlation with CPI/PPI; a sign flip would indicate anchoring fragility.
