# Linking Perception, Confidence and Accuracy in MLLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12149
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12149
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Multi-modal LLMs suffer from severe confidence miscalibration; the proposed CDRL+CA-TTS framework achieves state-of-the-art results on four benchmarks with an average 8.8% gain and significantly improves calibration and test-time scaling efficiency.

## Executive Summary
Multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) often exhibit high confidence even when their visual perception degrades, indicating poor self-awareness. This paper introduces two complementary components: Confidence-Driven Reinforcement Learning (CDRL) and Confidence-Aware Test-Time Scaling (CA-TTS). CDRL uses original/noised image pairs and a novel confidence-based reward to align the model's confidence with its actual accuracy under visual perturbations. The calibrated confidence then enables CA-TTS, where an expert model dynamically orchestrates three modules—Self-Consistency, Self-Reflection, and Self-Check—based on the confidence signal. Together, they yield substantial improvements on math- and vision-heavy benchmarks, with an average gain of 8.8%, and demonstrate superior scaling efficiency during inference.

## Method Summary
The proposed system consists of a training stage (CDRL) and an inference stage (CA-TTS). In CDRL, a base MLLM (Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct) is fine-tuned via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on pairs of original and noised images. The reward function combines a perception term that encourages a large confidence drop (ΔC) between the two outputs and a calibration term that ties normalized confidence to binary correctness. After training, the model's confidence becomes sensitive to visual degradation. At test time, an expert model (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro) acts as a Planner to schedule three reasoning modules: Self-Consistency (confidence-weighted voting over multiple samples), Self-Reflection (critique-based refinement), and Self-Check (visual contrastive decoding using original/noised images). The Expert Voter externally calibrates the final vote, and the answer with the highest aggregated score is selected. This framework leverages calibrated confidence as a free lunch to allocate computational effort adaptively.

## Key Results
- SOTA accuracy on four benchmarks: Math-Vista 79.5%, Math-Vision 42.4%, MMStar 71.3%, MMMU 66.3% (average +8.8%).
- CDRL increases confidence drop under perturbations by 4–8×, indicating improved perceptual sensitivity and calibration.
- CA-TTS achieves a steeper scaling slope (3.65) compared to baselines (2.2–3.1×), demonstrating superior test-time compute efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Confidence-Driven RL (CDRL) calibrates the model's holistic response confidence to align with actual accuracy under visual degradation.
- **Mechanism:** CDRL uses original/noised image pairs and a composite reward during GRPO training. The reward combines a *perception term* (α·tanh(β·ΔC)) that rewards a large confidence drop (ΔC) between outputs on original vs. noised images, and a *calibration term* ((2·R_output−1)·C_norm) that directly links binary correctness to normalized confidence. The perception term forces sensitivity to visual perturbations; the calibration term pushes the model to be confident only when correct.
- **Core assumption:** The model's holistic confidence (mean NMLP over all tokens) can be steered via RL to reflect the integrity of its visual grounding, and this learned sensitivity transfers to unseen perturbations.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and section 3.2.2 detail the reward function; neighboring work addresses perception and calibration separately but not this exact RL formulation.
- **Break condition:** The ΔC gradient may incentivize the model to artificially lower confidence on *all* noised inputs, even when the answer remains correct, harming overall calibration if α is misweighted or if the noising function destroys critical information entirely.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Confidence-Aware Test-Time Scaling (CA-TTS) dynamically orchestrates decoupled reasoning modules (Self-Consistency, Self-Reflection, Self-Check) using a calibrated confidence signal, achieving superior scaling efficiency.
- **Mechanism:** A high-confidence prediction triggers lightweight Self-Consistency (confidence-weighted voting). A low-confidence prediction invokes the Expert *Critic* to generate a critique for Self-Reflection and uses Visual Contrastive Decoding (original/noised pair) for Self-Check. An Expert *Planner* schedules the order of these independent modules. The Expert *Voter* externally calibrates the final vote. The confidence signal acts as an efficient router that allocates more expensive verification steps only where needed.
- **Core assumption:** Calibrated confidence from CDRL is a reliable, low-cost indicator of perception quality that can gate the deployment of more computationally intensive reasoning modules without a separate confidence-prediction head.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and section 3.3 describe the modules and expert roles; section 4.4d shows CDRL enables confidence drops that trigger CA-TTS.
- **Break condition:** If the confidence signal is noisy or over-trusted, CA-TTS may skip necessary checks on borderline cases (false negatives) or waste computation on high-confidence but incorrect predictions (false positives). The system's robustness relies on the Expert model's capability to correct base model errors.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The synergy between CDRL's perceptual calibration and CA-TTS's adaptive routing creates a "Perceive-then-Reason" virtuous cycle, where improved visual awareness during training yields more reliable confidence for efficient inference-time verification.
- **Mechanism:** CDRL trains the base model to have confidence that accurately mirrors its perceptual certainty. This trained model then serves as a better "oracle" for CA-TTS: its confidence scores more reliably distinguish between "I see it clearly" and "I'm guessing." CA-TTS leverages this distinction to apply Self-Check (visual verification) precisely when the model's perception is weak. The high-quality reasoning paths from successful CA-TTS executions can theoretically be fed back to improve the policy, though the paper presents this as a one-way benefit.
- **Core assumption:** The confidence calibration learned in the static RL training setting generalizes to the dynamic, multi-module context of test-time inference, maintaining its predictive power for perception quality.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states calibrated confidence enables more effective test-time scaling; section 4.4 ablation shows best results with combination; conclusion mentions CDRL creates the necessary foundation for CA-TTS.
- **Break condition:** If the distribution of test-time visual perturbations differs significantly from the synthetic noising used in CDRL training, the confidence signal may not generalize, causing CA-TTS's routing to misfire.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Reinforcement Learning from Human/Artificial Feedback (RLHF/RLAIF) & Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).**
- **Why needed:** CDRL uses GRPO, an on-policy RL algorithm, to optimize the model based on a reward combining accuracy, format, and the confidence calibration term. Understanding advantage estimation and group-relative scoring is key to grasping how the policy learns from the rollout pairs.
- **Quick check:** In GRPO, advantages are computed relative to the group mean (r_j - mean(r_group)). How does this encourage outputs with larger confidence drop ΔC and better calibration? It makes the reward comparative within the group, so an output with higher ΔC or better calibration gets a positive advantage relative to others in the same group, biasing the policy update towards such outputs.

**Concept: Confidence Calibration Metrics (ECE, AUC).**
- **Why needed:** The paper measures miscalibration (Expected Calibration Error - ECE) and uncertainty discrimination (AUC) under visual degradation to prove CDRL's effect. ECE quantifies the gap between confidence and accuracy.
- **Quick check:** If a model is perfectly calibrated, its ECE would be 0. What does a large drop in confidence (ΔC) under noising combined with improved AUC indicate? It indicates that the model's confidence reliably decreases when visual information is degraded (sensitivity) and that the confidence scores better separate correct from incorrect answers (discrimination).

**Concept: Test-Time Scaling (TTS) & Self-Consistency.**
- **Why needed:** CA-TTS is a form of TTS. Understanding the baseline paradigm of generating multiple outputs (self-consistency) and voting is essential to appreciate how CA-TTS improves it with confidence-weighting and module orchestration.
- **Quick check:** What distinguishes parallel scaling (majority voting) from sequential scaling (self-reflection), and how does CA-TTS hybridize them? Parallel scaling generates independent samples and aggregates in one step; sequential scaling iteratively refines a single output. CA-TTS hybridizes by scheduling modules (both parallel and sequential) based on confidence, allowing adaptive use of compute.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**
Training Stage (CDRL): Data Filtering Pipeline -> Noised Image Generator (CLIP-attention guided) -> GRPO Trainer with Confidence-based Calibration Reward -> Calibrated Base Model (π_θ)
Inference Stage (CA-TTS): Calibrated Base Model + Expert Model (M_expert)
- Planner (Expert): Schedules module order
- Self-Consistency: N parallel samples -> Internal confidence-weighted voting (V_internal) -> Voter (Expert) external calibration -> V_final
- Self-Reflection: Critic (Expert) generates critique -> Base model re-answers
- Self-Check: Visual Contrastive Decoding (original vs. noised logits)
Aggregator: Final answer from V_final vote dictionary

**Critical Path:**
1. Train base model with CDRL on (original, noised) pairs.
2. At inference, for a new (image, question), Planner decides module order.
3. Execute modules in order, each contributing to V_final.
4. Select argmax from V_final as the final answer.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- *Expert Model Choice vs. Cost:* A more capable expert (Gemini-2.5-Pro) yields better results but adds API latency/cost. A weaker expert may bottleneck performance.
- *Module Weighting (τ1, τ2, τ3):* Equal weights are used, but optimal weights may be task-dependent. Over-weighting Self-Check increases cost but may help perception-heavy tasks.
- *Noising Function:* CLIP-attention guided noise targets salient regions. An overly aggressive noiser may destroy all information, making the perception term trivial.

**Failure Signatures:**
- *CDRL failure:* Model becomes under-confident overall (high accuracy, low confidence). Confidence drops (ΔC) are insignificant under perturbation. ECE remains high.
- *CA-TTS failure:* No significant scaling slope improvement over Majority Voting. The Expert Planner frequently selects inefficient orders. The Voter's external confidence does not change V_final much.
- *Module-specific:* Self-Reflection critique is ignored or makes answers worse. Self-Check (VCD) produces garbled outputs.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Probe Confidence Miscalibration:** Add incremental Gaussian/blur/occlusion noise to a validation set. Plot model accuracy vs. mean confidence (NMLP). Expect: accuracy drops steeply while confidence remains flat (high miscalibration).
2. **Validate CDRL Reward Terms:** Ablate the R_Conf reward. Train three models: (a) full R_Conf, (b) only perception term (ΔC), (c) only calibration term ((2·R_out−1)·C_norm). Evaluate on clean and noised sets. Expect: (a) best balance of accuracy on clean and large ΔC on noised; (b) sensitive but poorly calibrated; (c) calibrated but insensitive.
3. **Test CA-TTS Scaling Law:** Run inference with N=1,2,4,8,16,32 samples for Majority Voting, DeepConf, and CA-TTS (with CDRL-trained model). Plot accuracy vs. N. Expect: CA-TTS has a significantly steeper slope (β) and higher asymptotic accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Do multi-modal LLMs possess accurate self-awareness of knowledge boundaries?**  
   The paper explicitly asks if models can know when they do not know. While CDRL and CA-TTS improve calibration, the solution is validated on limited models and benchmarks; reliable self-awareness across diverse tasks and architectures remains an open challenge. A comprehensive study across various MLLMs and systematic uncertainty probes would be needed.

2. **What is the optimal method for aggregating token-level confidences into sequence-level scores, and does it generalize across model families?**  
   The paper uses mean negative log-probability (NMLP) and briefly experiments with tail and bottom-group confidence, finding mean NMLP most effective for their base model. However, the optimal aggregation could affect calibration and scaling; findings are based on a single model and narrow tasks. A systematic benchmark comparing aggregation functions (mean, min, max, entropy-based, learned) across diverse MLLMs and tasks would help.

3. **Can CA-TTS benefits be realized without an external expert model, making the system fully self-contained?**  
   CA-TTS relies on an external expert (e.g., Gemini) as Planner, Voter, and Critic. While it works with weaker experts, best performance requires a stronger external model. Dependence introduces cost and reproducibility issues. It is unclear whether the base model can be trained to perform expert roles itself or if the expert's contributions can be internalized. Fine-tuning the base model to imitate expert functions or distilling expert knowledge would test this.

4. **How does the choice of visual perturbations during CDRL training affect calibration and robustness to unseen degradations?**  
   CDRL uses only noising (Gaussian) guided by CLIP attention, but evaluation includes other corruptions (occlusion, blur, etc.). The transfer of sensitivity to unseen perturbations is unknown. A study training with diverse augmentation policies and evaluating on a comprehensive corruption benchmark (e.g., ImageNet-C) would reveal the optimal strategy and generalization limits.

## Limitations
- Critical implementation details for the Expert model's prompts (Planner, Voter, Critic) are missing, relying on non-included supplementary figures.
- The precise noise generation function (type, magnitude, CLIP-attention masking) is not fully specified, making training conditions ambiguous.
- CA-TTS's performance strongly depends on the choice of external Expert model (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro); gains may not transfer with weaker experts or in low-resource settings.
- The virtuous cycle between CDRL and CA-TTS is asserted but not explicitly verified; the paper does not feed back high-quality reasoning paths to improve training further.

## Confidence
- **Calibration improvement (4–8× confidence drop under perturbations):** Medium (based on internal benchmark ablation; depends on specific noising implementation)
- **Accuracy gains (average 8.8% on four benchmarks):** Medium (reported on a filtered training set; generalization to full benchmarks unverified)
- **CA-TTS scaling efficiency (slope 3.65, 2.2–3.1× steeper than baselines):** Low (highly sensitive to expert capability and module hyperparameters)
- **Synergy between CDRL and CA-TTS:** Medium (ablation suggests complementarity, but causality rests on unverified robustness of confidence signal)
