# Dissecting Model Failures in Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm Segmentation through Explainability-Driven Analysis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.24801
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24801
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: XAI-SAM achieves up to 94.95% IoU on complex AAA outer wall segmentation, dwarfing SAM's 78.43%.

## Executive Summary
Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) segmentation models often fail on clinically challenging cases with thin boundaries, adjacent distracting structures, or slice-to-slice inconsistencies. This paper introduces XAI-SAM, an explainability-driven framework built upon the Segment Anything Model (SAM) to correct such failures. The method computes a gradient-based “XAI field” from the encoder to capture the model’s focus, then aligns the predicted probability mass with this field via dedicated losses. A confidence prior derived from the XAI field modulates the decoder logits at inference to suppress distractors and preserve thin walls. A pairwise consistency classifier regularizes transitions between adjacent slices. Evaluated on 9,037 CTA slices from 147 patients, XAI-SAM dramatically outperforms vanilla SAM, especially on a curated complex subclass. For outer wall segmentation, it reaches 94.95% IoU versus 78.43% for SAM, and reduces the 95th percentile Hausdorff distance from 5.49 mm to 0.31 mm. The work demonstrates how explicit focus alignment and anatomy-aware regularization can make SAM-based segmentation robust in demanding medical scenarios.

## Method Summary
The authors extend SAM (ViT-B encoder) with an explainability-guided encoder shaping pipeline. From the final encoder block, they derive an attribution-based “XAI field” Φ: activations are channel‑wise weighted by gradients, summed (with ReLU), upsampled, and L₁‑normalized. To force the decoder to focus where the encoder “looks”, they add two alignment penalties: L_ovlp (overlap between mass under the prediction p and Φ) and L_div (symmetrized KL divergence between the normalized distributions). An auxiliary decoder G processes refined encoder features to produce an auxiliary probability map p_aux; a confidence head A yields a spatial prior m_c. During inference, the main decoder’s logits are modulated by m_c (e.g., multiplicatively) to down‑weight low‑confidence regions. Additional anatomy‑aware losses (L_edge for boundary gradient alignment, L_cline for skeleton coherence) target thin structures. A pre‑trained pairwise consistency classifier C distinguishes between consecutive versus non‑consecutive ground‑truth mask pairs; its output on predicted neighboring slices contributes L_pair to penalize anatomically implausible transitions. Training follows a two‑phase curriculum: an early stage (segmentation loss + confidence + consistency) stabilizes basic learning, then a late stage adds anatomy and divergence terms. The model is trained on 9,037 axial CTA slices (1–3 mm thickness) from 147 patients, split 80/20 at patient level; the “complex” test subset is curated from baseline SAM failures.

## Key Results
- **Outer wall segmentation (complex subset)**: IoU 94.95% (↑ vs SAM 78.43%), Dice 97.30% (↑ vs 86.83%), HD95 0.31 mm (↓ vs 5.49 mm).
- **Lumen segmentation (complex subset)**: IoU 94.55% (↑ vs 82.11%), Dice 97.11% (↑ vs 89.57%).
- XAI-SAM consistently outperforms SAM and other baselines, with particularly strong gains in boundary accuracy and slice‑wise consistency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Focus Alignment via XAI Field
- **Claim:** Aligning predicted probability mass with a gradient‑based attribution map corrects attention misallocation to irrelevant structures.
- **Mechanism:** The XAI field Φ, computed from encoder activations and gradients, serves as a spatial focus map. Losses L_ovlp (mass overlap) and L_div (distribution divergence) encourage the segmentation probability p to concentrate in the same regions as Φ, shaping the decoder to respect the encoder’s explanatory signal.
- **Core assumption:** The spatial pattern of encoder gradients reliably indicates where the model is internally “looking” for the decision.
- **Evidence anchors:** Eq. 7–8 define Φ; Eq. 10–11 define alignment losses; Section 3.4 describes the implementation.
- **Break condition:** If Φ does not correlate with the true target (e.g., on very thin walls), the alignment could enforce a misleading focus.

### Mechanism 2: Confidence Prior Modulation
- **Claim:** Injecting the XAI field into a refinement pathway yields a confidence prior that suppresses distractors and reinforces subtle structures.
- **Mechanism:** An auxiliary decoder processes encoder features to produce p_aux; a confidence head converts it into m_c. At inference, the main decoder’s logits are modulated by m_c (often multiplicatively), attenuating predictions in low‑confidence (likely distractor) regions while preserving high‑confidence (likely thin boundary) regions.
- **Core assumption:** High attribution mass in Φ indicates spatially reliable prediction areas.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2 and 3.5 detail the architecture; ablation studies show benefit.
- **Break condition:** Noisy or diffuse XAI fields might cause erroneous suppression of true positives on delicate boundaries.

### Mechanism 3: Pairwise Slice Consistency
- **Claim:** A learned classifier regularizing predictions across adjacent slices prevents anatomically implausible discontinuities.
- **Mechanism:** A lightweight CNN is pre‑trained to distinguish between consecutive (positive) and non‑consecutive (negative) ground‑truth mask pairs. During segmentation training, the binarized predictions of neighboring slices are fed to this fixed classifier; the loss L_pair = λ_c · E[1−C(·)] penalizes pairs that the classifier deems inconsistent.
- **Core assumption:** Anatomically plausible slice‑to‑slice transitions follow a learnable manifold; deviations indicate segmentation errors.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.3 describes classifier construction and loss; Fig. 4 shows visibly more stable masks when the regularizer is active.
- **Break condition:** If the classifier overfits to smooth transitions (e.g., all training aneurysms are simple), it may penalize valid complex anatomical variations.

## Foundational Learning
### Gradient-based Attribution
- **Why needed:** The XAI field relies on computing meaningful spatial gradients from the encoder; a solid grasp of gradient‑based attribution (e.g., Grad‑CAM) is prerequisite.
- **Quick check:** Why might averaging spatial gradients over channels produce a more robust attribution map than using a single channel's gradient?

### Dice Loss and its Limitations for Thin Structures
- **Why needed:** The paper contrasts its approach with standard region‑based losses like Dice, which optimize volumetric overlap but are insensitive to precise boundary placement, motivating the anatomy‑aware components.
- **Quick check:** For a very thin target, why might a high Dice score still coexist with a poor Hausdorff distance?

### 3D Anatomical Continuity in Medical Imaging
- **Why needed:** The pairwise consistency mechanism assumes that consecutive slices in a CT volume should change gradually; understanding this clinical expectation validates the regularizer.
- **Quick check:** What clinical or anatomical rationale justifies enforcing consistency between adjacent axial slices of an abdominal CT?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
SAM Encoder (Enc) → Shape Projection Head (P) → SAM Mask Decoder (Dec) → logits s.  
XAI Field Extractor hooks into Enc's final block to compute Φ.  
E' (shaped features) → Auxiliary Decoder (G) → p_aux → Confidence Head (A) → m_c.  
Pairwise Consistency Classifier (C) (pre‑trained).

### Critical Path
1. Input image → SAM Encoder → features E.
2. Compute XAI field Φ from E (activations A, gradients G).
3. Primary path: P(E) → SAM Decoder → logits s → p = σ(s).
4. Auxiliary path: E' → G → p_aux → A(p_aux) → m_c.
5. Inference: modulate s using m_c (e.g., s_mod = s * m_c).
6. Training: minimize combined loss L_seg + L_align + L_anat + L_pair with curriculum (early: L_seg + α₁L_conf + α₂L_pair; late: L_seg + β₁L_anat + β₂L_conf + β₃L_div; λ_kl=0.2 mentioned).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Attribution method:** Simple gradient‑based XAI field is computationally cheap but potentially noisy; more sophisticated methods (Integrated Gradients) may be more accurate but heavier.
- **Loss weighting:** Hyperparameters (α₁, α₂, β₁, β₂, β₃, λ_c) require careful tuning; over‑weighting alignment could enforce a wrong focus if Φ is unreliable.
- **Auxiliary branch:** Adds parameters and memory; the “lightweight” design trades off some efficacy for efficiency.
- **Classifier:** Fixed after pre‑training; too weak → ineffective regularization, too strong → over‑constrain valid anatomical variations.

### Failure Signatures
- High JSD(˜p,˜ϕ) and FMI: prediction mass scattered or focused on irrelevant areas.
- Low BCov_r (r=1–3 px): attribution fails to cover true boundary, leading to missing thin walls.
- High E_cons: abrupt slice‑to‑slice mask changes, indicating inconsistency.
- Qualitative: leakage into adjacent vessels/calcifications; mask collapse on thin sections; flickering across slices.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Failure Mode Diagnosis:** Run baseline SAM on complex AAA slices; extract XAI fields; compute diagnostics (JSD, FMI, BCov_r, E_cons); overlay Φ on error maps to confirm correlation between misalignment and segmentation error.
2. **Component Ablation:** Train models with (S)egmentation refinement only, (X)AI guidance only, (A)natomy‑aware only, and all combined (XAI‑SAM); compare IoU/Dice/HD95 on complex test set (Table 3) to isolate contributions.
3. **XAI Field Visualization:** For hard cases (thin wall, adjacent vessel), visualize Φ from baseline SAM vs. XAI‑SAM; check for sharper, contiguous focus on aneurysm wall/lumen and reduced attribution on distractors.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Alternative XAI Methods:** How do other attribution techniques (e.g., integrated gradients, attention maps) affect performance and interpretability? The current XAI field uses a specific gradient‑based approach; exploring alternatives is suggested as future work.
- **Multi‑task Formulation:** Does jointly modeling outer wall and lumen segmentation yield gains and better anatomical consistency? Separate models ignore potential synergies; a multi‑task architecture could exploit structural dependencies.
- **Generalizability:** To what extent is the framework transferable to other thin‑structure segmentation tasks in different organs? While AAA results are strong, generic applicability needs validation on diverse datasets.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter values (α₁, α₂, β₁, β₂, β₃, λ_c) and exact training schedule are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- The XAI field relies on gradient‑based attribution, which can be noisy and may not reliably capture clinically relevant features for thin boundaries.
- External corroboration is weak; neighboring literature focuses on aneurysm growth prediction or general vessel segmentation, not XAI‑guided methods, raising questions about broader impact.
