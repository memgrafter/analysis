# DMamba: Decomposition-enhanced Mamba for Time Series Forecasting

## Quick Facts

- arXiv ID: 2602.09081
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09081
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: Reduces average MSE by 16.5% on ETTh2 vs S-Mamba, achieves 0.099 on PEMS08, and is 9x faster than PatchTST on PEMS03 with fewer FLOPs.

## Executive Summary

DMamba addresses the poor performance of existing Mamba-based time series forecasting models on non-stationary data, which is caused by entangled multi-scale temporal patterns. The core method employs exponential moving average (EMA) decomposition to separate the input into trend and seasonal components. It then processes these with differentially complex modules: a bidirectional, variable-direction Mamba encoder for the high-dimensional, dynamic seasonal residuals, and a lightweight channel-independent MLP for the low-dimensional, stable trend component. This architectural parsimony aligns model capacity with component-specific data complexity. Extensive experiments demonstrate state-of-the-art results, notably reducing average MSE by 16.5% on ETTh2 compared to S-Mamba and achieving an average MSE of 0.099 on PEMS08. The model also offers superior efficiency, being nearly 9x faster than PatchTST on PEMS03 while using significantly fewer FLOPs.

## Method Summary

DMamba begins with a RevIN layer to instance-normalize each channel, mitigating distribution shift. It then applies EMA decomposition: a recursive smoothing filter separates the input into a low-frequency trend component and high-frequency seasonal residuals. These two streams are processed in parallel: the trend stream uses a channel-independent MLP with average pooling to capture stable, low-dimensional intra-variable patterns; the seasonal stream first inverts the embedding to treat variates as tokens, then passes through a bidirectional Mamba block that scans across variables to model complex, dynamic cross-variable interactions. The outputs from both streams are concatenated, projected linearly to the forecast horizon, and finally denormalized via the inverse RevIN transformation. The design deliberately assigns Mamba’s selective state mechanism to the purified seasonal residuals, where high-dimensional interactions dominate, while the trend’s simpler manifold is handled by a lightweight MLP.

## Key Results

- 16.5% average MSE reduction on the non-stationary ETTh2 dataset compared to the previous SOTA Mamba-based model (S-Mamba).
- Achieves an average MSE of 0.099 on the PEMS08 traffic dataset, setting a new state of the art.
- Inference is nearly 9× faster than PatchTST on PEMS03 while using significantly fewer FLOPs, demonstrating high computational efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: EMA Decomposition Disentangles Multi-Scale Patterns
Exponential Moving Average decomposition effectively separates stable trend and dynamic seasonal components, reducing input entanglement that overwhelms standard Mamba blocks. The recursive smoothing filter (`X_trend[t] = α * X'[t] + (1-α) * X_trend[t-1]`) creates two streams with distinct statistical properties: the trend is low-frequency and often driven by few common factors (low-dimensional manifold), while the seasonal residuals are high-frequency and exhibit dynamic, high-dimensional cross-variable interactions (e.g., phase and amplitude co-movements). This disentanglement prevents the Mamba’s selective scan from receiving conflicting signals across scales.

Evidence: The abstract states that “the statistical nature of inter-variable relationships differs fundamentally between the trend and seasonal components”. Section 4.2 formally describes EMA decomposition. Neighboring works like KARMA and ms-Mamba also use decomposition, indirectly supporting the premise, though DMamba’s specific design is unique.

Break condition: If the time series is purely stochastic with no discernible trend or seasonality, EMA may produce components lacking meaningful statistical distinction, negating the architectural advantage.

### Mechanism 2: Differential Complexity Aligns Capacity with Data Complexity
DMamba assigns architectural complexity specifically: a bidirectional Mamba encoder for the high-dimensional seasonal residuals and a lightweight channel-independent MLP for the low-dimensional trend. The seasonal stream inverts embedding so that variates become tokens, allowing Mamba’s selective scan to model complex cross-variable dynamics by scanning across the variate sequence. The trend stream uses a simple MLP with pooling, assuming the trend relationships lie on a lower-dimensional manifold that can be captured by linear projections.

Evidence: The abstract explicitly describes “specialized, differentially complex modules”. Sections 4.3.1 and 4.3.2 detail the seasonal Mamba and trend MLP architectures. Ablation results (Table 5.4.1) show that swapping modules—especially using MLP for seasonal (`All-MLP`) or Mamba for trend (`Mamba-Trend`)—causes severe performance drops, confirming the importance of the specific assignment.

Break condition: If the trend component is actually highly non-linear and high-dimensional (e.g., chaotic), the MLP will underfit; if the seasonal component is low-dimensional, the Mamba becomes over-parameterized.

### Mechanism 3: Purified Seasonal Stream Enables Effective Mamba Modeling
Applying Mamba’s content-aware selective scan to the seasonal residuals (rather than raw entangled input) prevents the state mechanism from being confused by conflicting multi-scale signals. In the purified seasonal stream, the scan can focus on modeling long-range dependencies in the high-frequency dynamics without low-frequency trend interference. The trend stream, being stable, does not require such complex temporal reasoning.

Evidence: The introduction hypothesizes that Mamba’s degradation on non-stationary data results from being overwhelmed by entanglement. Table 1 shows existing Mamba models (S-Mamba) lag on non-stationary ETT data, while DMamba’s decomposition+Mamba-on-seasonal design resolves this. Ablation with `T-Mamba` (temporal scan on seasonal) performs worse than DMamba’s variate-wise scan, indicating the importance of modeling cross-variable dynamics in the purified seasonal component.

Break condition: On highly stationary data with minimal multi-scale entanglement, the decomposition step may be unnecessary; a monolithic Mamba on raw input could perform equally well or better due to fewer processing steps.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**:  
  RevIN → EMA Decomposition → Parallel streams (Trend: channel-independent MLP with avg-pooling; Seasonal: inverted embedding → bidirectional Mamba block → projection) → Concat → Linear → Denorm

- **Critical path**: The quality of EMA decomposition is paramount. The Mamba block must operate on the purified `X_seasonal`, not the raw input. Any error in decomposition propagates to both streams.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Parsimony vs. capacity: Using a simple MLP for trend reduces parameters and prevents overfitting on stable patterns, but risks underfitting if the trend is complex.
  - Directionality: Bidirectional Mamba (forward+backward on variate sequence) mitigates causality artifacts from variable ordering but doubles computation for that stream.
  - Decomposition choice: EMA avoids moving average padding bias but introduces a smoothing hyperparameter α that requires tuning per dataset.
  - Loss function: The Arctan Weighted Loss prioritizes near-term accuracy, complementing the architecture but not substituting for it.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Poor decomposition: α too low → trend too noisy; α too high → trend lags; both yield suboptimal inputs.
  - Mismatched capacity: If the seasonal component is actually simple, the Mamba stream may overfit; if the trend is complex, the MLP will underfit.
  - Variable ordering bias: The Mamba block scans variates in fixed order; strong asymmetric inter-variable relationships may be sensitive to order—bidirectional design helps but does not eliminate this.
  - Distribution shift beyond RevIN: RevIN assumes stationarity within the look-back window; a sudden non-linear shift within the window may not be corrected.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline Comparison**: Run DMamba against S-Mamba, PatchTST, iTransformer, etc., on ETT (non-stationary) and PEMS (spatial-temporal). Expect SOTA on ETT and strong gains on PEMS, confirming the decomposition+Mamba benefit.
  2. **Architectural Ablation**: Compare DMamba (Mamba on seasonal, MLP on trend) against `All-MLP`, `Mamba-Trend` (swapped), `All-Mamba`, and `T-Mamba`. Expect DMamba to win and `Mamba-Trend` to perform poorly, validating the specific assignment of Mamba to seasonal.
  3. **Component Isolation & Loss**: Replicate DMamba* and baselines with the same Arctan Weighted Loss. Expect DMamba* to still outperform, proving gains are architectural, not merely loss-function-dependent.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

The paper does not explicitly list open questions; it focuses on presenting the method and results. However, based on its identified limitations and proposed next checks, the following questions emerge for future investigation:

- How does EMA decomposition perform on time series with no clear trend/seasonal separation (e.g., purely stochastic or chaotic signals)?
- Is the fixed assignment of Mamba to seasonal residuals and MLP to trend optimal across diverse datasets, or could a dynamic, data-driven routing improve robustness?
- Can the smoothing factor α in EMA be learned adaptively (e.g., via hypernetworks) to avoid manual tuning and better handle varying dataset characteristics?
- How does RevIN handle non-stationarities that occur within the look-back window, and could complementary normalization techniques improve robustness?
- Does the bidirectional Mamba's assumption of fixed variable ordering introduce bias in datasets with asymmetric inter-variable dependencies, and how does it scale to very high-dimensional variate sets?

These questions are inferred from the paper's own discussion and suggest natural extensions of the work.

## Limitations

- **Decomposition generalizability**: EMA’s effectiveness assumes separable trend/seasonal statistics; on purely stochastic or chaotic series, the split may be artificial, harming both streams.
- **Architectural rigidity**: The fixed assignment (Mamba→seasonal, MLP→trend) presumes stable dimensionality bounds. If a dataset’s trend is highly non-linear or the seasonal component is simple, capacity mismatch occurs.
- **External validation gap**: Neighboring works (e.g., KARMA, ms-Mamba) also use decomposition but differ architecturally; no direct comparative study isolates why DMamba’s specific design excels.

## Confidence

- **EMA disentanglement**: Medium (supported by ablation and theory, but decomposition quality not quantitatively validated)
- **Differential capacity**: High (ablations show severe degradation when modules are swapped, confirming the specific assignment)
- **Mamba on purified seasonal**: Medium (clear gains on non-stationary ETT data, but causality needs controlled synthetic experiments)
