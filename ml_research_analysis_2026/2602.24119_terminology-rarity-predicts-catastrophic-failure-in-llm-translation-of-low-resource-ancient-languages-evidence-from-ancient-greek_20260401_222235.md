# Terminology Rarity Predicts Catastrophic Failure in LLM Translation of Low-Resource Ancient Languages: Evidence from Ancient Greek
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.24119  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24119  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** LLM translation quality for Ancient Greek technical prose catastrophically fails (near 0/100) when passages contain a high ratio of terminology rare in a literary corpus (threshold ~0.30), despite high quality on previously translated texts.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates commercial LLMs on translating low-resource Ancient Greek technical prose from Galen, using expert human evaluation (MQM) and automated metrics. LLMs performed exceptionally well on previously translated expository text (mean MQM 95.2/100) but significantly worse on untranslated pharmacological text (mean 79.9/100) with high variance. A strong negative correlation (r = –.97) was found between translation quality and the proportion of terms rare in a literary corpus; passages exceeding a 30% rare-term ratio produced near-total failures. Standard automated metrics showed only moderate correlation with human judgment and failed to detect catastrophic errors or discriminate among high-quality translations, highlighting their unreliability for domain-specific quality assurance.

## Method Summary
The authors assembled a dataset of 20 paragraph-length passages (~215 words) from two Galenic works: *On Mixtures* (previously translated, 10 passages) and *On the Composition of Drugs* (untranslated, 10 passages). Three commercial LLMs (Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT) translated each passage using a standardized prompt. Translation quality was assessed via a modified MQM framework by domain experts, yielding a Translation Quality Score (TQS). Terminology rarity was quantified by lemmatizing passages and querying lemma frequencies in the 10M-word Diorisis literary corpus, computing the "rare term ratio" (lemmas with frequency <50 or absent). Correlations between TQS and rarity metrics, and between TQS and a suite of automated metrics (BLEU, BERTScore, COMET, etc.), were analyzed separately for each text type.

## Key Results
- LLMs achieved near-perfect quality on *On Mixtures* (mean TQS 95.2) but showed a large quality drop and high variance on *On Composition* (mean TQS 79.9).
- Terminology rarity predicted failure catastrophically: the two passages with rare term ratios > 0.40 scored near 0/100; the correlation across *Composition* passages was r = –.97.
- When excluding these two catastrophic failures, TQS scores for *Composition* converged to within 4 points of *Mixtures*, demonstrating a sharp threshold effect.
- Automated metrics correlated moderately with TQS only on the high-variance *Composition* set (BERTScore r = .75); on the high-quality, low-variance *Mixtures* set, no metric correlated significantly with human judgment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Terminology rarity (low corpus frequency) directly causes catastrophic translation failure in LLMs for low-resource ancient languages.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs rely on statistical patterns in training data. Terms rare in the dominant literary corpus (Diorisis) are statistically marginal, forcing models to operate at the edge of learned representations where they generate incorrect or nonsensical outputs instead of accurate translations.
- **Core assumption:** Training data distribution (literary AG) does not adequately cover specialized technical terminology (pharmacological terms), creating a competence barrier.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Terminology rarity emerged as a strong predictor of translation failure (r = –.97).
  - Passages with rare term ratio > .30 produced catastrophic failures (scores near 0).
  - Catastrophic passages (Comp. 8, ratio .407; Comp. 10, .434) contained clusters of extremely rare pharmacological terms.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** High performance on previously translated texts is partially due to training-data contamination (memorization), not purely de novo translation capability.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs may parrot or paraphrase human translations present in training data for well-translated texts, inflating apparent competence. This creates an illusion of reliability that does not generalize to untranslated texts with similar structure but rare terminology.
- **Core assumption:** Performance gaps between translated and untranslated texts reflect both contamination and genuine difficulty.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - LLMs achieved high quality on previously translated expository text but lower on untranslated pharmacological text.
  - The persistence of the BLEU gap even when the MQM gap disappears (after removing catastrophic failures) provides partial support for memorization.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Standard automated evaluation metrics fail to discriminate among high-quality translations and severely underrate catastrophic failures, making them unreliable for quality assurance in specialized domains.
- **Mechanism:** Metrics trained on modern language pairs are domain-mismatched. They penalize valid paraphrasing in morphologically rich languages and compress extreme score ranges, missing critical errors that experts detect via domain knowledge.
- **Core assumption:** Human expert judgment (MQM) is the gold standard for domain-specific translation quality.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Automated metrics correlated moderately (BERTScore r = .75) but failed to discriminate among good translations or detect catastrophic errors.
  - On *Mixtures* (high-quality, low-variance), no automated metric correlated significantly with TQS.
  - COMET compressed a 56.7-point human TQS drop into a 4.8% dip for catastrophic passages.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multidimensional Quality Metrics (MQM) framework**
  - *Why needed here:* Provides a structured, error-based, reference-free evaluation method adaptable to ancient languages where reference translations may be unstable or nonexistent. Allows domain experts to judge accuracy against source text directly.
  - *Quick check question:* Why is MQM preferred over BLEU for evaluating translations of texts with multiple valid interpretations (like Ancient Greek technical prose)?

- **Concept: Terminology rarity as a corpus-derived feature**
  - *Why needed here:* Offers an objective, scalable predictor of translation difficulty by quantifying how uncommon words are in a representative corpus (Diorisis). Bridges textual properties to model performance.
  - *Quick check question:* How might the choice of corpus (literary vs. technical) affect the "rarity" measurement and its predictive power?

- **Concept: Training-data contamination in LLMs**
  - *Why needed here:* Explains why LLMs might appear competent on texts with existing translations, raising questions about the generalizability of their "skill." Critical for trust calibration.
  - *Quick check question:* What evidence would definitively separate contamination effects from genuine translation ability in an LLM's output?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Source Text Corpus -> Translation Engine -> Human Evaluation Pipeline -> Automated Metrics Suite -> Terminology Analyzer -> Statistical Correlation Engine
- **Critical path:** Passage selection (from two Galen works) -> MT generation (three LLMs, standardized prompt) -> MQM human evaluation (blinded, consensus) -> terminology rarity computation (Diorisis + lemmatizer) -> correlation analysis -> stratification (by passage difficulty) -> metric comparison.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - *Human vs. automated evaluation:* MQM provides validity for domain-specific quality but is prohibitively expensive; automated metrics are scalable but domain-fragile.
  - *Single vs. multiple references:* For untranslated *Composition*, a single expert reference was used, introducing potential bias; *Mixtures* used two references.
  - *Passage length:* Paragraph-length inputs model real use but introduce context-window sensitivity not tested.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. **Catastrophic failure mode:** TQS near 0, multiple critical terminology errors, rare term ratio > 0.30.
  2. **Metric mismatch mode:** High BLEU/COMET score paired with low MQM (fluent but inaccurate output).
  3. **Contamination suspicion:** High lexical metric scores on translated text despite no human reference for untranslated text of similar difficulty.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replication with new text:** Apply identical pipeline to another low-resource ancient technical author (e.g., Hippocratic Corpus) to test if the rare term threshold (~0.30) holds.
  2. **Prompt perturbation:** Vary system prompts (e.g., "transliterate unknown terms" vs. "translate") to see if catastrophic failures on rare terms can be mitigated.
  3. **Metric calibration study:** On a held-out set of passages with known rare-term density, test whether adding a simple rare-term ratio feature improves automated metric correlation with MQM.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What specific failure patterns (e.g., hallucination types) do LLMs exhibit when translating passages with high terminology rarity, and how can these be systematically characterized?
- **Open Question 2:** Does the strong negative correlation between terminology rarity (as measured by literary corpus frequency) and translation quality hold across a larger and more diverse corpus of Ancient Greek texts, and what is the critical rare term ratio threshold that precipitates catastrophic failure?
- **Open Question 3:** Can prompt modifications—such as instructing the model to transliterate rather than translate uncertain terms—reduce the frequency of critical errors on terminologically dense passages?
- **Open Question 4:** How do blind expert comparisons between human translations and LLM outputs differ from the MQM error‑based assessment in capturing aspects like fluency, stylistic appropriateness, or interpretive richness?

## Limitations
- Exact LLM model identifiers/versions used may be inaccessible at reproduction.
- Diorisis corpus access procedure and exact snapshot version are not provided; alternate corpuses change rarity metrics.
- MQM adjudication rules for Ancient Greek technical terms are not fully documented; human evaluation is not perfectly replicable.
- The strong rarity–failure correlation (r = –.97) is driven by two extreme passages; relationship weakens when excluded (r = –.71).
- For untranslated *Composition*, only one expert reference was used, risking bias.
- Automated metrics show a ceiling effect, failing to discriminate among high-quality translations.

## Confidence
- **Terminology rarity as failure predictor (Mechanism 1):** Medium. Strong correlation evidence but concentrated in two passages; threshold (~0.30) may not generalize.
- **Training-data contamination (Mechanism 2):** Low. Inferred from BLEU/MQM divergence; no direct contamination proof.
- **Automated metrics unreliability (Mechanism 3):** Medium. Clear failure on *Mixtures* texts, but metric performance on *Composition* is mixed and variance-dependent.

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate with new domain/text:** Apply identical pipeline to another low-resource ancient technical author (e.g., Hippocratic Corpus) to test if the rare-term ratio threshold (~0.30) holds.
2. **Prompt perturbation experiment:** Vary system prompts (e.g., “transliterate unknown terms” vs. “translate”) to test if catastrophic failures on rare terms can be mitigated, isolating model vs. prompt effects.
3. **Metric calibration study:** On a held-out set with known rare-term density, test whether adding rare-term ratio as a feature improves automated metrics’ correlation with MQM scores.
