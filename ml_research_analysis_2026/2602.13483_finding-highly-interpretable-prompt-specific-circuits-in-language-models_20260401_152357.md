# Finding Highly Interpretable Prompt-Specific Circuits in Language Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13483
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13483
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Prompt-specific circuits exist; ACC++ identifies lower-dimensional interpretable signals; for IOI, different templates yield distinct circuit families and 63% of signals in GPT-2 Small are interpretable.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the prevalent assumption that language models employ a single stable circuit for a given task, which can obscure prompt-specific mechanisms even for fixed tasks. To address this, the authors extend Attention Causal Communication (ACC) to ACC++, a method that uses Integrated Gradients to attribute changes in the true nonlinear attention weight (after Softmax), thereby accounting for Softmax competition across all source tokens. ACC++ yields lower-dimensional causal signals from a single forward pass, avoiding the need for activation patching or sparse autoencoders.

Applied to the Indirect Object Identification (IOI) task in GPT-2 Small, Pythia-160M, and Gemma-2 2B, ACC++ reveals that no single IOI circuit exists: different prompt templates induce systematically different internal attention mechanisms. Circuits cluster into a few prompt families (e.g., GPT-2 splits by ABBA vs. BABA patterns; Pythia by lower-level templates). Representative circuits for each family facilitate comparison. An automated interpretability pipeline shows that a substantial fraction of signals are human-interpretable (63% in GPT-2 Small), exposing mechanistic differences such as distinct parallel-structure detection strategies. These findings underscore the necessity of prompt-level analysis for mechanistic interpretability.

## Method Summary
ACC++ traces a circuit for a given attention head and destination–source token pair. It computes the QK matrix Ω and its singular value decomposition (SVD) to obtain left and right singular vectors. The residual streams are decomposed into upstream component outputs, which are projected onto the singular vectors to generate candidate signals (both destination- and source-side). A contribution matrix C captures the influence of each candidate on the raw attention scores. Integrated Gradients (with a baseline of zeroed signals) then attributes changes in the attention weight to each candidate, properly accounting for the nonlinear Softmax competition among all source tokens. A greedy removal algorithm iteratively discards the highest-attribution candidates until the residual attention weight drops below a threshold τ(d)=2.5/d (determined from the empirical knee of the ECDF). The retained candidates form a sparse set of causal edges, each labeled with the component and singular vector index. This recursion continues upstream until a termination condition.

The authors apply ACC++ to 3000 IOI prompts (15 low-level templates, role orders ABBA/BABA, 100 examples each) across three models. Per-prompt circuits are encoded as binary vectors of edge–singular-vector pairs. Pairwise Jaccard distances and average linkage clustering identify prompt families; the representative circuit for a cluster minimizes average within-cluster Jaccard distance. An autointerpretation pipeline assesses interpretability: for each signal pair, it retrieves top-activation token-pair contexts from the Pile-10k, prompts an LLM (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B) to generate a short label, and evaluates with a fuzzing judge (Gemma-3). Signals with false discovery rate ≤5% are considered interpretable.

## Key Results
- **No single IOI circuit exists:** Across GPT-2, Pythia, and Gemma, different prompt templates induce systematically different internal attention structures.
- **Prompt-family clustering:** Circuits group into a few families; GPT-2 splits by high-level ABBA vs. BABA, Pythia by lower-level templates, and Gemma shows mixed/weak clustering. Representative circuits summarize each family.
- **High autointerpretability:** A majority of signals are human-interpretable: 63% (62–64%) in GPT-2 Small, 50% in Pythia-160M, and 31% in Gemma-2 2B meet FDR≤5%. This reveals mechanistic differences, such as GPT-2 using a “second item” detection for BABA but a positional mechanism for ABBA.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: ACC++ identifies lower-dimensional, cleaner causal signals for attention decisions by using Integrated Gradients on the true nonlinear attention weight (Softmax output), accounting for competition across all source tokens.
- Mechanism: Given a destination–source pair, candidates are signal projections of upstream component outputs onto singular vectors of the head’s QK matrix. A contribution matrix is built; Integrated Gradients attributes each candidate’s effect on the attention weight. A greedy removal selects the minimal set whose deletion reduces the attention weight below a threshold τ.
- Core assumption: The Softmax normalization introduces nonlinear competition; a linear proxy (as in ACC) cannot correctly isolate the necessary signals.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "using Integrated Gradients to attribute true nonlinear attention weight changes, accounting for Softmax competition across all tokens, yielding lower-dimensional causal signals"
  - [section 2]: Provides full derivation of the counterfactual objective and IG attribution; shows empirically that ACC++ signals are lower-dimensional (Fig 11).
  - [corpus]: No direct mention of ACC++; neighbors discuss related circuit analysis methods.
- Break condition: If the attention distribution is extremely peaked (e.g., one token dominates), IG computations may become numerically unstable or the threshold τ might not cleanly separate signal from noise.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Circuits are prompt-specific; different prompt templates within the same task (IOI) systematically induce distinct circuits, contrary to the assumption of a single stable task-level circuit.
- Mechanism: The authors trace a full circuit for each prompt that the model answers correctly, using ACC++. Circuits are represented as sets of edge–singular-vector pairs. Pairwise Jaccard distances reveal clustering aligned with high-level (ABBA vs BABA) or low-level templates. Representative circuits summarize each cluster.
- Core assumption: Lexical and syntactic variations change the internal attention patterns enough to produce differing causal structures; averaging across prompts would conflate these distinct mechanisms.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "find there is no single circuit for IOI in any model: different prompt templates induce systematically different mechanisms"
  - [section 3]: Presents clustermaps (Fig 1) showing template-dependent families; GPT-2 split by ABBA/BABA, Pythia by low-level templates.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors such as "Adaptive Circuit Behavior..." report similar prompt-level variations, lending indirect support.
- Break condition: If nearly all prompts traced yielded identical component sets, the clustering would show minimal structure; the method would still function but would not reveal the reported heterogeneity.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Many ACC++ signals are human-interpretable, and their automatic interpretation explains mechanistic differences between prompt families (e.g., GPT-2 uses "second item" vs. "positional" detection for ABBA/BABA).
- Mechanism: An automated pipeline retrieves top-activation contexts for each signal pair, prompts an LLM to generate a short interpretation, and evaluates it with a fuzzing-style judge (top vs random contexts). Signals with FDR ≤5% are considered interpretable. Signal summaries (destination/source) are compared between representative circuits.
- Core assumption: High activation across contexts consistently corresponds to a coherent semantic or syntactic feature that can be captured in natural language.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "autointerpretation shows many signals are human-interpretable (e.g., 63% (62–64%) in GPT-2 Small), exposing mechanistic differences"
  - [section 4]: Details pipeline and shows Figure 3 where interpretable labels reveal different parallel-structure detection in ABBA vs BABA.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors like "Transcoders Find Interpretable LLM Feature Circuits" and "Automated Circuit Interpretation via Probe Prompting" pursue similar automated interpretation; coherence supports feasibility.
- Break condition: If fuzzing scores cluster near chance and most signals fail the FDR threshold, the interpretability claim fails; the pipeline would not produce reliable descriptions.

## Foundational Learning
- Concept: Attention-Causal Communication (ACC)
  - Why needed here: ACC++ extends the ACC framework; understanding ACC’s goal (locating low-dimensional subspaces and minimal components causal for attention) is prerequisite to appreciating the refinements.
  - Quick check question: What is the output of ACC for a given attention head and token pair, and why does it use SVD of the QK matrix?

- Concept: Integrated Gradients (IG) for nonlinear attribution
  - Why needed: ACC++ replaces ACC’s linear proxy with IG to correctly measure each candidate’s effect on the final attention weight under Softmax competition.
  - Quick check question: How does Integrated Gradients account for the influence of all source tokens when attributing change to a single candidate?

- Concept: Circuit representation as directed graphs with signal-level edges
  - Why needed: The paper’s unit of analysis is the prompt-specific circuit where edges include the specific singular vector direction; this granularity enables clustering and signal comparison.
  - Quick check question: In an ACC++ circuit graph, what does an edge (upstream component c, singular vector index k) represent, and why is the index part of the edge identity?

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map: QK matrix Ω and its SVD (u_k, σ_k, v_k) -> residual decomposition into upstream component outputs o_d^c -> candidate signal generation via projections onto singular vectors (destination P_U^k o_d^c, source P_V^k o_j^c) -> contribution matrix C (rows=candidates, columns=source positions) -> Integrated Gradients attribution for Ads -> greedy removal iteratively deleting highest-attribution candidates until Ads < τ -> resulting edges (component, singular vector index) compose the circuit graph -> clustering via Jaccard distance on edge–singular-vector sets -> representative circuit selection -> autointerpretation pipeline (top-activation retrieval, LLM interpreter, fuzzing judge, FDR control).

- Critical path: For a specific attention head and destination–source pair:
  1. Compute Ω and its SVD; obtain orthonormal bases u_k, σ_k, v_k.
  2. Decompose input residuals at each token into upstream component outputs.
  3. Build candidate sets (destination and/or source) by projecting onto singular vectors.
  4. Assemble contribution matrix C according to chosen view (destination or source).
  5. Compute IG attributions for all candidates by integrating along the path from zero to full scores.
  6. Run greedy removal: repeatedly remove highest-attribution candidate and recompute Ads until Ads < τ.
  7. The removed candidates form the causal signal set; add edges to the circuit graph.
  8. Recurse on each upstream component that contributed a selected signal until termination condition (no upstream attention dependence).
  9. After tracing many prompts, cluster circuits, pick representatives, and compute signal summaries for comparison.
  10. For each signal pair, run autointerpretation: retrieve top contexts, ask LLM for interpretation, evaluate with fuzzing.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Single forward pass vs. activation patching: ACC++ is much faster and avoids counterfactual data construction, but may miss longer-range counterfactual dependencies that patching could reveal.
  - Using SVD projections: Strongly reduces dimensionality and noise, yet might discard weaker but relevant modes; authors found most signals are rank-1.
  - Dynamic τ via ECDF knee: Data-driven and generalizes across models, but could be sensitive to dataset statistics; alternative fixed thresholds exist.
  - Greedy removal: Heuristic, not guaranteed to find globally minimal set, but works well in practice.
  - Automated interpretability: Scalable but relies on LLM judge quality; susceptible to judge bias and may miss signals not aligned with natural language.

- Failure signatures:
  - High proportion of signals with >1 singular vector used: indicates attribution noise or poor separation (Appendix E).
  - Autointerpretation FDR rates near 1 for most signals: pipeline failing to find coherent patterns.
  - Clustermap shows no block structure: within-template Jaccard distances ≈ dataset baseline (as in Gemma), suggesting circuits are not template-stable.
  - ECDF of d·Ads shows no clear knee: τ selection becomes arbitrary.
  - Recursive tracing does not terminate (e.g., keeps finding causal signals indefinitely): probably τ too low or residual contamination.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Reproduce signal rank distribution: On GPT-2 Small (same setup as Appendix E), compute fraction of ACC++ signals that are rank-1; expect >80% (Fig 11).
  2. Validate prompt family clustering: Using the provided 3000-prompt IOI dataset, run ACC++ on a subset, compute component sets (edge–sv pairs), perform average linkage clustering, and confirm GPT-2 yields two clear high-level template families (Fig 1).
  3. Check autointerpretability rate: Randomly sample 100 signals from GPT-2 Small IOI circuits, run the full pipeline, verify that the fraction of signals with FDR ≤5% falls near 63% (Table 1).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question:** How does the autointerpretation pipeline's focus on direct positive evidence for attention interactions bias the discovered signal interpretations, and what complementary methods (e.g., targeted causal interventions) are needed to capture signals that act primarily through relative effects like suppression or redistribution?
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The limitations subsection (Section 4) states that the pipeline favors interpretations corresponding to direct positive evidence and may miss signals that matter through relative effects, even though ACC++ accounts for them. It suggests complementary analyses with clustering and targeted causal interventions.
   **Why unresolved:** The current method may systematically underrepresent a class of functionally important signals (e.g., inhibitory ones), limiting the completeness of circuit explanations. Without validated alternatives, the full mechanistic picture remains obscured.
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop and apply intervention-based probing methods specifically designed to detect and characterize suppression/redistribution effects within ACC++-traced circuits, then compare the resulting signal sets and interpretations against the current autointerpretation outputs across tasks and models.

2. **Question:** What is a principled, data-driven method for setting the attention weight threshold τ in ACC++ that generalizes beyond the empirical knee observed in the current IOI datasets, ensuring robustness across different models, tasks, and prompt distributions?
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Appendix D describes the current heuristic (τ(d) = 2.5/d) based on an empirical knee in the ECDF for the IOI dataset, but explicitly states that the impact of this strategy is unclear in other scenarios and that better threshold-setting methods are left for future work.
   **Why unresolved:** The choice of τ directly controls which attention interactions are deemed causal and thus shapes the traced circuits. An arbitrary or dataset-specific threshold risks missing important edges or including noise, undermining comparability and reliability.
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Propose and evaluate a τ-setting method (e.g., based on statistical outlier detection, model-specific calibration, or learning from counterfactual patches) across a diverse suite of tasks and models, demonstrating that resulting circuits are more stable and faithful than those using the current heuristic.

3. **Question:** What architectural or training factors cause different language models to cluster prompt-level IOI circuits into distinct families (e.g., GPT-2 by high-level template, Pythia by low-level template, Gemma with mixed clustering), and how general is this pattern across other tasks?
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 3 and Figure 1 show that the dominant split in clustering varies by model (high-level for GPT-2, low-level for Pythia, weak/mixed for Gemma). The paper notes this contrast but does not explain the underlying cause.
   **Why unresolved:** Understanding why models organize circuits differently is crucial for predicting circuit variability and for developing model-agnostic interpretability methods. The current observation is descriptive, not explanatory.
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct controlled experiments varying model size, architecture (e.g., attention patterns, positional encoding), and training data/dynamics, then measure how prompt-family clustering changes. Correlate clustering patterns with internal representations or training dynamics to identify predictive factors.

4. **Question:** Why does the fraction of ACC++ signals deemed interpretable under the fuzzing criterion vary significantly across models (63% GPT-2 Small, 50% Pythia-160M, 31% Gemma-2 2B), and what does this imply about the inherent interpretability of circuits in different models?
   **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Table 1 reports these fractions. The paper notes the result but does not analyze the discrepancy. Given that ACC++ signals are causal for attention, the variance suggests model-dependent differences in how neatly internal computations align with human-interpretable features.
   **Why unresolved:** If interpretability varies systematically with model properties (scale, architecture, training), it affects the feasibility of automated circuit analysis. The cause of the variance is unknown: is it noise, true conceptual differences, or limitations of the autointerpretation protocol?
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform a finer-grained analysis of the non-interpretable signals (e.g., by clustering their activation contexts or testing alternative interpreter prompts) to determine whether they are genuinely opaque or just mismatched to the evaluation. Correlate interpretability rates with model characteristics and with independent measures of feature granularity (e.g., SAE sparsity).

5. **Question:** How transferable are prompt-family representative circuits—summaries of clustered prompt-level traces—to other datasets or tasks? Do they capture stable computational motifs, or are they artifacts of the specific prompt distribution used?
   **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper introduces representatives as a practical unit of analysis (Section 3) and applies them only within the IOI task. It does not test whether these representatives generalize to unseen IOI prompts or to entirely different tasks, leaving open whether the clustering is robust or overfitted to the dataset.
   **Why unresolved:** If representatives are not generalizable, the concept of a "circuit family" may not be a useful abstraction beyond the studied prompts. Stability across distributions is key for scaling mechanistic interpretability.
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Test representative circuits from one IOI dataset (e.g., the paper's 3000-prompt set) on held-out IOI prompts from different templates or sources. Also, extract circuits for a new task (e.g., subject-verb agreement) and see if analogous family structures and representatives emerge, using signal-level similarity to assess motif reuse.

## Limitations
- Reliance on low-rank SVD approximation may miss multi-dimensional causal factors.
- Threshold τ is dataset-specific and may not generalize to other tasks or data distributions.
- Autointerpretation depends on an external LLM judge, whose biases and limitations can affect interpretability scores.

## Confidence
- Method effectiveness (ACC++ yields low-dimensional causal signals): High
- Prompt-specific circuits (no single task circuit, families exist): Medium
- Automated interpretability (63% FDR≤5%): Medium
