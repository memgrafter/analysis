# LIBERO-X: Robustness Litmus for Vision-Language-Action Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.06556
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06556
- **Reference Count**: 40
- **Primary Result**: The best VLA model achieves only 65.2% success at Level 1; average across models is 39.4%, with an average 31.2% drop to Level 5 and near-zero performance on tasks requiring ≥3 steps.

## Executive Summary
LIBERO-X addresses misleading evaluations of Vision‑Language‑Action (VLA) models caused by insufficient testing protocols and homogeneous training data. It introduces a hierarchical evaluation with five progressive difficulty levels and collects a high‑diversity training dataset of 2,520 human‑teleoperated demonstrations across 600 tasks and 100 scenes. Evaluating five representative VLA models reveals severe robustness gaps: even the best model attains 65.2% success at the easiest level, average success is 39.4%, and performance declines by 31.2% to the hardest level. Multi‑label analysis uncovers near‑zero success on tasks with three or more steps, highlighting a critical long‑horizon planning deficit. Relaxing the time limit to 1.1× human duration improves outcomes but plateaus beyond 1.5×, indicating inherent limitations beyond speed.

## Method Summary
LIBERO-X provides a diverse training dataset: 2,520 human‑teleoperated demonstrations covering 600 tasks across 100 scenes in simulated kitchen and study environments. The dataset includes multi‑view images, proprioceptive states, actions, and language instructions in LeRobot/RLDS format.

The evaluation suite defines five progressive levels of perturbation: Level 1 (local spatial jitter), Level 2 (extended spatial shifts), Level 3 (topological changes), Level 4 (visual attribute variations), and Level 5 (semantic instruction reformulations). Tasks are annotated with multi‑label metadata (interaction type, subtask count, spatial relation, object attribute). Five VLA models (OpenVLA‑OFT, π0, X‑VLA, GR00T1.5, π0.5) are fine‑tuned on the training set and assessed via PDDL predicate‑based success with a time limit of 1.1× the human average.

## Key Results
- Best model (π0.5) achieves 65.2% success at Level 1; average across models is 39.4%.
- From Level 1 to Level 5, success drops by an average of 31.2%, with larger drops from extended spatial shifts (9.8%) and topological changes (12.7%).
- Near‑zero success on tasks requiring three or more steps, revealing a fundamental long‑horizon planning deficit.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hierarchical Evaluation Framework
- **Claim**: The hierarchical evaluation systematically exposes model weaknesses by introducing cumulative perturbations.
- **How it works**: Five progressive difficulty levels—Level 1 (local spatial jitter), Level 2 (extended spatial shifts), Level 3 (topological changes), Level 4 (visual attribute variation), Level 5 (semantic instruction reformulation)—incrementally challenge spatial reasoning, object recognition, and language grounding.
- **Core assumption**: Models overfitting to superficial cues will show sharp performance declines as test conditions diverge; robust models maintain higher success.
- **Evidence**: Average 31.2% drop from Level 1 to 5 across all five VLA models (Table II, Figure 5). Neighboring benchmarks used isolated perturbations, limiting diagnostic insight; LIBERO‑X’s progressive design yields finer failure isolation.
- **Break condition**: Consistent declines confirm discriminative power; if models maintained or improved success at higher levels, perturbations would be insufficient or models exceptionally robust.

### Mechanism 2: High‑Diversity Training Data
- **Claim**: High‑diversity training data prevents memorization of template trajectories and yields a more faithful assessment of generalization.
- **How it works**: The dataset collects 2,520 human‑teleoperated demonstrations across 100 scenes and 600 tasks, with each scene supporting multiple fine‑grained objectives (attribute‑conditioned selection, spatial relationship reasoning). This forces models to learn compositional semantics rather than fixed action sequences.
- **Core assumption**: Varied visual and task distributions reduce overfitting to spurious correlations (e.g., fixed object positions) and improve transfer to unseen configurations.
- **Evidence**: Compared to the original LIBERO (narrower train‑test gap), LIBERO‑X’s diversity leads to more realistic performance ranges (Section III.A, Figure 3).
- **Break condition**: If models trained on LIBERO‑X performed similarly to those trained on homogeneous data under perturbations, the diversity benefit would be questionable; the paper shows clear advantages.

### Mechanism 3: Multi‑Label Annotation for Fine‑Grained Diagnosis
- **Claim**: Multi‑label annotation enables fine‑grained diagnosis of failure modes, revealing specific bottlenecks such as long‑horizon planning deficits.
- **How it works**: Tasks are tagged with interaction type (placement, state change, composite), subtask count (1, 2, 3+), spatial relation (surface, container, left‑right, ordinal, distance), and object attribute (color, size, pattern, category). Success rates are aggregated per label.
- **Core assumption**: Different task dimensions tax distinct capabilities; isolating them pinpoints weaknesses more effectively than overall scores.
- **Evidence**: Near‑zero success on tasks with three or more steps (Finding 5, Figure 6, Tables V–IX). Prior benchmarks lacked such breakdowns.
- **Break condition**: Uniformly high or low performance across all labels would render the annotation uninformative; the observed variance confirms its diagnostic value.

## Foundational Learning

- **Vision‑Language‑Action (VLA) Models**:  
  *Why needed*: LIBERO‑X evaluates VLA models that map visual observations and language instructions to robot actions. Understanding their architecture (VLM backbone + action head) is essential to interpret evaluation outcomes.  
  *Quick check*: What are the two main action modeling strategies in VLA models (discrete tokenization vs. continuous regression), and how do they differ in output representation?

- **Behavioral Cloning (Imitation Learning)**:  
  *Why needed*: LIBERO‑X provides demonstration trajectories; models are trained via supervised learning on state‑action pairs. Recognizing the limitations of behavioral cloning (e.g., compounding errors, distribution shift) explains why models fail under perturbations.  
  *Quick check*: Why does pure imitation learning often lead to poor generalization when test environments differ from training?

- **Robustness Evaluation via Controlled Perturbations**:  
  *Why needed*: The core of LIBERO‑X is to test model robustness under systematic distribution shifts. Knowing the rationale behind perturbation design (spatial, topological, attribute, semantic) is key to proper benchmark use.  
  *Quick check*: How does progressively increasing perturbation severity from Level 1 to 5 help identify the breaking point of a VLA model?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
- Simulation core: MuJoCo physics engine with LIBERO‑X scenes (kitchen/study) and 3D assets.
- Training dataset: 2,520 human‑teleoperated trajectories (RLDS/LeRobot format) spanning 100 scenes and 600 tasks; supports multi‑task, attribute‑conditioned, and spatial‑reasoning tasks.
- Evaluation suite: Five‑level perturbation generator (Level 1–5) plus multi‑label metadata (interaction type, subtask count, spatial relation, object attribute).
- Success evaluator: PDDL predicates (e.g., ExactIn, UprightOn) for precise task completion checks.
- Model interface: Standardized inputs (multi‑view images, language instruction) and outputs (action chunk/sequence) compatible with major VLA checkpoints.

### Critical Path
1. Set up environment: Install MuJoCo, LeRobot, and download LIBERO‑X scene assets.
2. Load a VLA model (official checkpoint) and optionally fine‑tune on the LIBERO‑X training set.
3. For each evaluation task (scene, instruction, level), instantiate the simulation, apply the prescribed perturbations, and run the model for an episode (time limit = 1.1× human average).
4. Record task success via predicate evaluation; collect step‑wise data for multi‑label breakdown.
5. Aggregate results over multiple seeds (paper uses 10 trials) and compute success rates per level and label.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Simulation vs. real**: LIBERO‑X uses simulation for control and reproducibility, sacrificing some physical realism; this trade‑off enables large‑scale evaluation but introduces a reality gap.
- **Diversity vs. collection cost**: Human teleoperation yields high‑quality, varied data but is labor‑intensive; automated data generation could scale but may lack natural variation.
- **Progressive difficulty**: Cumulative perturbations allow precise failure isolation, but designing non‑overlapping, independent perturbations is non‑trivial; effects might confound.
- **Time limit**: A strict limit enhances discriminative power but may penalize slower policies; a relaxed limit (1.1×) balances fairness and realism, yet further relaxation permits brute‑force success.

### Failure Signatures
- **Model‑specific**: Low Level 1 success → failure to learn basic manipulation; sharp drop Level 1→2 → poor spatial generalization; near‑zero on three‑or‑more‑step tasks → long‑horizon planning deficit.
- **Benchmark‑specific**: All models near 100% on Level 1 → perturbations too small; huge variance across seeds → protocol irreproducible; uniform label performance → labeling not discriminative.
- **Implementation**: Success predicate bugs (e.g., ExactIn threshold mis‑set) cause false positives/negatives; missing assets or incorrect scene initialization lead to crashes or invalid states.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Baseline evaluation: Fine‑tune π0.5 on LIBERO‑X, evaluate across Levels 1–5, and reproduce Table II and Figure 5.
2. Training diversity ablation: Train a model on the original LIBERO dataset (homogeneous) and compare its performance on LIBERO‑X; expect larger drops, highlighting diversity’s benefit.
3. Multi‑label breakdown: Compute success rates per label category (subtask count, object attribute, etc.) for all models; replicate Figure 6 and Tables V–IX to expose patterns like the near‑zero success for three‑or‑more‑step tasks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

#### Open Question 1: Long‑Horizon Planning
**Question**: How can VLA model architectures be fundamentally redesigned to overcome the catastrophic failure on long‑horizon tasks requiring three or more sequential steps, as opposed to merely mitigating error accumulation?

**Basis**: Finding 5 states that success rates drop to near zero for tasks with three or more steps, indicating a "fundamental deficiency in long‑horizon reasoning and temporal consistency." The paper concludes that "future research must prioritize error mitigation and long‑term planning."

**Why unresolved**: Current models, even the best (π0.5), show near‑zero success on such tasks despite diverse training data. This suggests existing architectural paradigms (e.g., transformer‑based action chunking, diffusion policies) are inherently insufficient for maintaining coherent multi‑step plans without external orchestration.

**What would resolve**: A new model introduced on LIBERO‑X that demonstrates non‑zero, statistically significant success rates (e.g., >10%) on "Three or More Actions" tasks at Level 1, while matching or exceeding state‑of‑the‑art performance on shorter tasks. Crucially, ablation studies must show the improvement stems from a novel architectural component for planning (e.g., an explicit world model, hierarchical policy, or learned subtask scheduler) rather than increased model scale or longer time limits.

#### Open Question 2: Spatial Relationship Reasoning
**Question**: What specific inductive biases or training objectives can teach VLA models to reason about spatial relationships (e.g., left/right, container relations) rather than memorizing absolute object positions, thereby improving robustness to topological changes (Level 3)?

**Basis**: Finding 2 identifies a lack of "structural invariance" and states that models struggle when "semantic relationships between objects remain valid but their topological organization has been reconstructed," causing the largest performance drop (12.7%).

**Why unresolved**: The paper attributes this to overfitting on training configurations but does not hypothesize *why* the models fail to learn relational concepts. It is unclear if this is a limitation of the VLM's visual representations, the policy's action space, or the training data's lack of relational diversity.

**What would resolve**: A controlled study where models are trained on a variant of the LIBERO‑X dataset that systematically removes absolute coordinate information (e.g., using only relational descriptors in instructions and augmenting scenes to decouple object positions from their relations). If such training significantly reduces the Level 2→3 performance gap (from 12.7% to, say, <5%), it would confirm that relational reasoning can be incentivized via data design. Conversely, if the gap persists, it would implicate architectural limitations in the VLM or policy head.

#### Open Question 3: Visual Concept Grounding for Unseen Objects
**Question**: To what extent does the performance gap between "Confounding Objects" (CO) and "Unseen Objects" (UO) in Level 4 stem from a failure in the VLM's *concept grounding* versus a failure in the *policy's ability to act on novel visual features*?

**Basis**: Finding 3 notes that success rates are "notably higher" on CO than on UO, indicating that while models gain robustness against visual distractors, they "still struggle to ground instructions to novel visual concepts." This implies a specific breakdown when the target object lacks any trained visual representation.

**Why unresolved**: The paper uses a fine‑tuned VLA model where the VLM backbone is typically frozen or lightly adapted. The observed gap could be due to (a) the VLM's frozen representations not being sufficiently rich to bootstrap novel category recognition, or (b) the policy network failing to map novel visual features to correct actions even if they are recognized. Disentangling these requires diagnostic probing not performed in the paper.

**What would resolve**: An experiment that freezes the policy head and only fine‑tunes the VLM backbone on the LIBERO‑X dataset. If the UO success rate improves significantly while CO remains stable, it would indicate the bottleneck is in VLM concept grounding. If both UO and CO improve similarly, it suggests the policy's action mapping is the primary limiter. Alternatively, probing the VLM's internal representations (e.g., via linear probes) for unseen vs. seen object categories before and after fine‑tuning would provide direct evidence.

#### Open Question 4: Instruction Paraphrase Robustness
**Question**: Are the observed sensitivities to instruction reformulation (Level 5) primarily a failure of the language encoder to achieve *semantic equivalence* under paraphrase, or a failure of the policy to maintain consistent goal conditions despite linguistic variation?

**Basis**: Finding 4 reports a 3.26% drop from Level 4 to 5 and notes that "voice conversion proved to be the least disruptive." This suggests the model's understanding is brittle to syntactic changes, even when semantics are preserved.

**Why unresolved**: The paper documents the effect but does not analyze its source. VLA models use frozen VLMs (e.g., Llama, PALI) known for strong paraphrase robustness. The degradation could arise from (a) the instruction tokenizer or prompt engineering within the VLA framework introducing bias, (b) the policy overfitting to surface form patterns in the training data, or (c) a genuine weakness in the model's compositional linguistic generalization.

**What would resolve**: A controlled test where the same VLM backbone (without any policy head) is used as a zero‑shot classifier to select the correct target object from a static image given original vs. reformulated instructions. If the VLM itself shows high consistency (>90%) on this probe, the fault lies in the VLA policy's integration of linguistic variation. If the VLM is inconsistent, the issue is upstream in language understanding. Additionally, analyzing attention patterns in the language encoder under different reformulations could pinpoint whether semantic layers or positional embeddings are affected.

#### Open Question 5: Theoretical Performance Ceiling of VLM‑Based VLA
**Question**: What is the theoretical upper bound on VLA model performance on LIBERO‑X's hierarchical evaluation when trained on its diverse dataset, and does this bound suggest fundamental limitations of the VLM‑as‑backbone paradigm for robotic manipulation?

**Basis**: The paper reveals that even with a high‑diversity training set (2,520 demos, 100 scenes), the best model (π0.5) achieves only 65.2% at Level 1 and plummets to 18.0% at Level 5. This gap between training diversity and test robustness implies that either (a) the dataset diversity is still insufficient to cover real‑world distribution shifts, or (b) the VLM‑based architecture has a ceiling in its ability to learn robust, generalizable manipulation policies from demonstration data.

**Why unresolved**: The paper proposes LIBERO‑X as a step toward a more reliable benchmark but does not speculate on a performance ceiling. The steep decline across levels, especially the near‑zero rates on complex spatial and multi‑step tasks, suggests current approaches may be hitting a wall. Is this due to the imitation learning paradigm itself (compounding errors, lack of temporal abstraction) or the representational capacity of VLMs for control?

**What would resolve**: A scaling experiment where models of varying capacities (e.g., VLM backbone size, policy network depth) are trained to convergence on the full LIBERO‑X dataset. Plotting performance across all five levels against model size would reveal if there is a plateau. If even the largest feasible models show similar degradation patterns, it would suggest a paradigm‑level limitation. Alternatively, if performance scales indefinitely (but slowly), it would indicate that current models are simply under‑parameterized or under‑trained. This would guide whether future work should pursue architectural innovation or simply larger‑scale data and compute.

## Limitations
- Simulation‑only evaluation introduces a reality gap; results may not transfer to physical robots.
- The training dataset and exact perturbation parameters are not publicly released, hindering exact replication.
- Benchmark domains are limited to kitchen and study scenes, reducing generalizability to other robotic tasks.
