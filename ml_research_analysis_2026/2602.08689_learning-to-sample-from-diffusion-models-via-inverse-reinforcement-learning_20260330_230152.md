# Learning To Sample From Diffusion Models Via Inverse Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08689
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08689
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Diffusion sampling quality and efficiency are improved at inference without denoiser retraining by learning an optimal policy via f-divergence inverse reinforcement learning.

## Executive Summary
The paper frames diffusion sampling as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where actions modify dynamics (e.g., guidance scale, restarts, stochasticity). Using inverse reinforcement learning (IRL), it learns a stochastic policy that matches a desired expert state distribution—constructed from corrupted data at various noise levels—without requiring an explicit reward function. The method minimizes an f-divergence between the policy’s state occupancy and the expert’s, estimating density ratios via a discriminator and optimizing the policy with a PPO-style objective. Experiments across CIFAR-10, FFHQ, and ImageNet demonstrate consistent gains: adaptive guidance reduces FID while controlling precision/recall trade-offs via divergence choice; renoising with KL divergence improves FID at lower function evaluations; and learned stochasticity injection matches or exceeds fixed-baseline performance. The approach also mitigates class imbalances and shows robustness to temperature scaling.

## Method Summary
The core method treats the diffusion sampler as a finite-horizon MDP. States combine the current sample `x_t` and noise level `σ_t`. A base deterministic operator (e.g., Heun solver) is perturbed by discrete actions: selecting a guidance scale from a discretized set, deciding whether to “re-noise” to a previous `σ`, or adjusting EDM-specific parameters like `γ`. The expert occupancy measure is defined by state marginals at each noise level, derived by corrupting training data. The learning objective minimizes an f-divergence `D_f(μ_E || μ_θ)` between expert and policy state occupancies. A discriminator `D_φ` is trained to distinguish expert from policy states at each noise level; its output yields a density ratio estimate. A policy gradient method, inspired by PPO with importance sampling and reward normalization, updates the stochastic policy `π_θ`. Occupancy weights `w_θ(σ)` are estimated empirically from trajectories and combined with the expert weighting `w_E(σ)` to form advantages. The policy network is a dual-head CNN based on the EDM UNet backbone, conditioned on normalized noise level `τ` and (for non-stationary policies) the timestep index `t`.

## Key Results
- Adaptive guidance policies reduce FID while precision/recall trade-offs are controlled by choice of f-divergence (KL vs. reverse-KL).
- Learning when to re-noise (with KL divergence) improves FID while lowering the average number of function evaluations (NFE).
- Learned stochasticity-injection profiles match or beat fixed stochasticity baselines.
- Policies correct class-imbalance artifacts in class-conditional generation.
- Inference-time temperature scaling maintains robustness without retraining.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The IRL formulation bypasses the need for a handcrafted reward by directly matching a target state distribution (the expert). The expert distribution itself is a natural “optimal” sampling trajectory—clean data at `σ=0` and appropriately corrupted data at intermediate `σ`. By minimizing an f-divergence, the method flexibly optimizes for different trade-offs (e.g., mode coverage vs. sample quality). The discriminator provides a scalable way to estimate the density ratio needed for the occupancy matching objective. The policy gradient then optimizes actions that push the sampler’s visited states toward the expert states across all noise levels, effectively learning adaptive strategies such as when to use stronger guidance or when to re-inject noise to recover lost modes.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Expert Data Generator → Expert State Marginals (per σ) → Discriminator D_φ (per σ) → Density Ratios → Occupancy Weights → Advantage Estimation → Policy π_θ (dual-head UNet) → Actions → Modified Sampler Trajectories → Occupancy Statistics (w_θ)

### Critical Path
The training loop alternates between (1) generating trajectories with the current policy, (2) training the discriminator on expert and policy states, (3) computing density ratios and occupancy-based advantages, and (4) updating the policy via a clipped policy gradient. Evaluation uses an EMA-smoothed policy.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Discrete vs. Continuous Actions:** Discrete actions (e.g., bins for guidance) simplify the policy but limit granularity; continuous actions could be more expressive but may destabilize training.
- **Discriminator Capacity vs. Overfitting:** A powerful discriminator yields accurate density ratios but risks overfitting to the finite sample of policy states, biasing the gradient.
- **Expert Weighting `w_E`:** Biasing `w_E` toward lower `σ` prioritizes final sample quality but may hurt early-stage exploration; uniform weighting balances all noise levels but may not optimize for speed.

### Failure Signatures
- **Policy Collapse:** Policy entropy drops to near zero early, yielding deterministic but suboptimal actions (e.g., always continue).
- **Discriminator Saturation:** Discriminator accuracy exceeds 90%, indicating the density ratio estimates are extreme and likely biased.
- **Training Instability:** Large variance in FID across evaluation steps suggests poor gradient estimation or occupancy weight mismatch.

### First Three Experiments
1. **CIFAR-10 Replication:** Train a policy with KL divergence on the EDM model, generate 50k samples, and report FID, Precision, Recall, and NFE. Target: within 5% of published numbers.
2. **Discriminator Ablation:** Vary discriminator width (e.g., 0.5x, 1x, 2x) and update frequency (per iteration vs. per epoch). Monitor policy entropy, discriminator accuracy, and final FID to identify stable regimes.
3. **Cross-Model Transfer:** Apply the policy trained on EDM (CIFAR-10) to a DDPM checkpoint without retraining. Measure FID change to assess generalization across diffusion architectures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can sensitivity to policy initialization be mitigated to avoid suboptimal local minima?  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper identifies this as a limitation but offers no solution; it suggests better heuristics or pretraining as future work.

- **Open Question 2:** Can continuous action spaces be incorporated into the IRL framework for diffusion sampling, and how do they compare to discrete actions?  
  *Why unresolved:* Only discrete actions are implemented; continuous parameterization (e.g., guidance scale) is proposed but not evaluated.

- **Open Question 3:** What is the optimal way to choose the expert occupancy weight function `w_E` to balance quality and speed for different objectives?  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper uses simple heuristics (uniform over non-terminal levels) and does not analyze the impact of different weightings.

## Limitations
- Missing detailed specifications for policy/discriminator network architectures and conditioning injection methods.
- Critical hyperparameters (expert weight distribution `w_E`, occupancy horizon `T`, expert sample counts per noise level) are not reported, hindering exact replication.
- Discriminator training is noted as unstable; no ablations on capacity or update ratio are provided.
- High computational cost: generating expert marginals and rolling out many-step trajectories is expensive.
- No analysis of how denoiser errors propagate to policy decisions.
