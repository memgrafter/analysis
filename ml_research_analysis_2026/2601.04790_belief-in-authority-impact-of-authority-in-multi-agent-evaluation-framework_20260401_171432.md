# Belief in Authority: Impact of Authority in Multi-Agent Evaluation Framework
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04790
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04790
- **Reference Count:** 11
- **Primary Result:** In multi-agent LLM evaluations, roles with Expert or Referent power (e.g., Specialist, Supervisor) exert stronger influence than Legitimate power roles (e.g., Judge), with authority bias stemming from authoritative roles' consistent position-holding and general agents' flexible conformity.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates role-based authority bias in multi-agent LLM systems, a previously underexplored source of systematic error. Using the ChatEval framework, it assigns nine authoritative roles derived from French and Raven’s social power theory (Legitimate, Referent, Expert) to one agent in a 12-turn dialogue with a "General Public" agent. The task involves subjective writing evaluation, where both agents score options after each turn. Key findings across GPT-4o and DeepSeek R1 reveal that Expert and Referent roles induce significantly higher agreement from the general agent over time compared to Legitimate roles. The bias mechanism is asymmetric: authoritative roles rarely change their initial stance, while general agents adjust toward them. Crucially, when authorities select neutral options, the bias effect disappears. Content-controlled experiments confirm the effect is driven by role labels, not conversational content.

## Method Summary
The study employs the ChatEval multi-agent debate framework. Two writing evaluation datasets (FairEval, Topical-Chat) are used. Each dialogue involves a "General Public" agent and one of nine authoritative role agents (e.g., Judge, Specialist, Supervisor) across 12 alternating turns. After every turn, both agents score two writing options (1–10) and select a preferred option (or neutral if tied). The primary metrics are agreement ($A_t$) and Cohen’s Kappa ($\kappa_t$) between the General Public’s choice and the authoritative role’s turn-2 choice at turns $t=1,4,8,12$. Two experiments are run: (1) Free-form dialogues with role prompts; (2) Content-controlled replication where identical dialogue utterances are replayed with swapped role labels to isolate label effects. Only GPT-4o and DeepSeek R1 passed a screening for coherent 12-turn non-repetitive dialogue.

## Key Results
- Expert and Referent power roles produce significantly larger increases in agreement ($\Delta A$) over turns than Legitimate power roles.
- Authority bias manifests through low conformity from authoritative roles (low $A_1$ in Public-Authoritative vs. Public-Public dialogues) and flexible adjustment by general agents.
- When authoritative roles select neutral options frequently, as with GPT-4o, measured agreement increases are muted or eliminated; DeepSeek R1, with fewer neutrals, shows near-perfect agreement ($A_t$=100%) for Expert roles.
- Content-controlled experiments, which hold conversation content constant while swapping only the second agent’s role label, replicate the agreement pattern, confirming the role-label as the causal factor.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Expert and Referent power roles exert stronger influence on general agents than Legitimate power roles in multi-agent evaluation.
- **Mechanism:** Roles that signal perceived expertise (Specialist, Expert) or personal trust (Supervisor, Leader, Mentor) are more persuasive in evaluation contexts than roles based solely on formal position (Judge, Foreman, Management). LLMs internalize these social power cues from role labels and weight influence accordingly, with knowledge/trust signals outweighing formal authority cues.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs parse role semantics and adjust conformity behavior based on the implied power base.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Expert and Referent power roles exert stronger influence than Legitimate power roles."
  - [section 3.6] Role group analysis shows Legitimate Power roles exhibit lower agreement increases (e.g., ~2.8% $\Delta A_4$) versus Referent (~7.5%) and Expert (~7.2%) in GPT-4o/FairEval.
- **Break condition:** If controlled experiments show no agreement differential between power types when content is identical (Experiment 2), the effect is attributed to role-label perception.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Authority bias manifests not through general agents actively conforming, but through authoritative roles consistently maintaining their initial positions while general agents flexibly adjust.
- **Mechanism:** Authoritative roles exhibit low conformity to general agents' opinions (low $A_1$ in Public-Authoritative vs. Public-Public dialogues), creating an asymmetric influence dynamic where general agents' shifts toward the authority's fixed position drive measured bias.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs in authoritative roles show higher positional stability (lower susceptibility to influence) than unmarked "General Public" agents.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "authority bias emerges not through active conformity by general agents, but through authoritative roles consistently maintaining their positions while general agents demonstrate flexibility."
  - [section 4.2] "Public-Public conversations show high initial agreement $A_1$, whereas conversations including Authoritative roles exhibit markedly lower $A_1$ values... Authoritative roles tend to show less conformity to General Public opinions."
- **Break condition:** If authoritative roles show similar $A_1$ to general agents in baseline dialogues, the stability mechanism is not supported.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Authority influence requires clear, non-neutral position statements from the authority; neutral responses fail to generate measurable bias.
- **Mechanism:** When authoritative agents select neutral options (equal scores for both choices), the directional signal for general agents to conform disappears, resulting in stagnant or decreased agreement over turns.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs require a clear, unambiguous stance from an authority to trigger alignment behavior; ambiguity (neutrality) provides no consistent anchor for conformity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "authority influence requires clear position statements, as neutral responses fail to generate bias."
  - [section 3.6] "In the group where authoritative roles selected only neutral options... all roles showed notably decreased $A_t$ values... Conversely, in the group excluding neutral options... sharp increases in $A_t$ and $\kappa_t$ values were observed."
- **Break condition:** If neutral authority responses still produce significant agreement increases, the necessity of a clear position is disproven.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: French & Raven's Bases of Social Power**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's entire role design and hypothesis about power-type differences (Mechanism 1) directly map to Legitimate, Referent, and Expert power. Understanding this theory is prerequisite to interpreting *why* role labels might differentially influence LLMs.
  - *Quick check:* How might "referent power" (admiration/identification) differ in persuasive impact from "legitimate power" (formal right) when an LLM is told it is a "Leader" versus a "Judge"?
- **Concept: Multi-Agent Debate Frameworks (e.g., ChatEval)**
  - *Why needed here:* The experimental infrastructure—12-turn debates, per-turn evaluation, role-prompt engineering—is specific to the ChatEval paradigm. Replicating or extending this work requires understanding its dialogue structure and decision-tracking mechanism.
  - *Quick check:* In a per-turn evaluation setup, why is it critical that evaluation scores are *not* recorded in the chat log, and how does this affect observed bias dynamics?
- **Concept: Bias Measurement via Agreement & Cohen's Kappa**
  - *Why needed here:* The core dependent variables ($A_t$, $\kappa_t$) operationalize "authority bias" as increasing agreement with a fixed authority reference point (turn 2). Misunderstanding these metrics would corrupt all result interpretation.
  - *Quick check:* If $A_1$ (initial agreement) is already high, can subsequent increases in $A_4/A_8/A_{12}$ still indicate authority bias, or could they reflect mutual convergence?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Role Prompt -> Dialogue Engine (12-turn) -> Decision Tracker (per-turn scores/choices) -> Analysis Pipeline (compute $A_t$, $\kappa_t$, split by role/power/neutrality).
- **Critical path:**
  Inject Role Prompt -> Turn 1 (GP utterance & decision) -> Turn 2 (Auth utterance & decision, fixed as reference) -> Continue dialogue to turns 4,8,12 -> Track GP decisions at each turn -> Calculate $\Delta A$ ($A_1$ vs. $A_{4,8,12}$) & $\kappa_t$ -> Compare across power types, models, neutrality conditions.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Task choice:* Used subjective writing evaluation (no ground truth) to isolate authority bias from truth-convergence. This limits generalizability to fact-based domains.
  - *Model selection:* Required models that avoid repetition over 12 turns, severely narrowing candidate models (only GPT-4o, DeepSeek R1 passed). Tradeoff: ecological validity vs. feasibility.
  - *Content control:* Experiment 2 re-uses Public-Public dialogues, swapping only role labels. Assumption: identical content isolates label effect. Risk: role label may implicitly alter content generation.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High repetition or early dialogue collapse in >4-turn conversations (failed model capability check).
  - Authority role selecting neutral >40% of time (as with GPT-4o), which can mask bias unless analyzed separately.
  - $A_1$ already near 100% in control condition, leaving no room for observed increase (ceiling effect).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Pilot Model Screening:** Test candidate LLMs on 5 multi-turn ChatEval dialogues to filter for non-repetitive, coherent 12-turn completion.
  2. **Free-Form Authority Bias Run (Exp1):** Execute full 9-role × 2-model × 2-dataset matrix, collect decisions at turns 1,2,4,8,12. Primary analysis: $\Delta A$ ($A_4-A_1$) by power type.
  3. **Content-Controlled Replication (Exp2):** For all dialogues where Auth role was non-neutral in Exp1, replay identical utterances with second agent's role label changed from "General Public" to "Authority". Compare $A_1$ values to Exp1's Public-Authoritative $A_1$ to isolate label effect.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Why do different LLMs exhibit varying susceptibility to authority bias, and what model-intrinsic factors (e.g., architecture, training) explain these differences?
  - **Basis:** The paper's limitation explicitly states that only two models (GPT-4o, DeepSeek R1) were tested due to fluency requirements, constraining the diversity of examined models.
  - **Why unresolved:** The observed inter-model differences (e.g., DeepSeek R1's higher agreement rates) are noted but not causally linked to model properties; no analysis of internal representations or training data is provided.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct cross-model comparisons across a wider range of LLMs (e.g., varying sizes, open vs. proprietary) while probing decision-making processes (e.g., attention patterns, confidence scores) during role-based interactions.
- **Question:** How do authority bias patterns generalize to non-evaluation domains (e.g., creative collaboration, technical problem-solving), and do domain-specific roles alter the influence hierarchy?
  - **Basis:** The authors acknowledge their ChatEval-based framework is limited to evaluation tasks, noting that other domains may exhibit different bias patterns.
  - **Why unresolved:** The study uses only writing evaluation tasks; domains with objective ground truths or different social structures (e.g., coding) may change how authority types exert influence or whether bias manifests.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the free-form and content-controlled experiments in diverse domains with tailored authoritative roles and measure agreement dynamics to see if Expert/Referent dominance persists.
- **Question:** What specific mechanisms cause Expert and Referent power roles to exert stronger influence than Legitimate power roles in LLM-based MAS, and is this driven by role semantics or task context?
  - **Basis:** The paper states "further research is needed to draw clear conclusions" about why Expert/Referent roles showed stronger influence, despite aligning with human psychology theory.
  - **Why unresolved:** While the pattern replicates Carson et al. (1993), it's unclear if LLMs inherently mimic these power dynamics or if the evaluation task's emphasis on expertise/trust amplifies the effect; role-label content vs. interactive behavior is not disentangled.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Run controlled studies varying role descriptions (e.g., stripping expertise cues) and task types (e.g., factual vs. subjective) to isolate which factors trigger stronger conformity from general agents.
- **Question:** Why does GPT-4o exhibit a higher propensity for neutral responses from authoritative roles, and how does this behavior distort the measurement of authority bias in MAS?
  - **Basis:** The paper identifies GPT-4o's neutral response rate (12%-45%) as dampening measured bias, but does not investigate its cause; this is inferred as an unstated assumption affecting results.
  - **Why unresolved:** The neutral response effect is documented but not explained; it may stem from model calibration, safety fine-tuning, or ambiguity tolerance, potentially masking true bias levels.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyze GPT-4o's output distributions (e.g., probability entropy) and test prompt variations to see if neutrality is triggered by role ambiguity; compare bias metrics after filtering neutrals across models with different baseline neutrality rates.

## Limitations
- Replication details missing: ChatEval's prompt structure and evaluation timing are underspecified, risking implementation drift.
- Narrow model coverage: Only GPT-4o and DeepSeek R1 were tested; findings may not generalize.
- Subjective task only: Writing evaluation lacks ground truth; biases might differ in fact-based settings.
- Neutral response interference: High neutrality rates, especially in GPT-4o, complicate measurement and may mask effects.
- Content control assumption: Role-label change during replay could alter interpretation of identical utterances.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1** (Expert/Referent > Legitimate): **Medium**. Evidence from within-study analyses supports the difference, but limited to two models and one task; theoretical backing is indirect.
- **Mechanism 2** (Asymmetric stability): **Medium-High**. Consistent $A_1$ patterns and low authority conformity are robust, though alternative explanations (e.g., positional extremity) remain.
- **Mechanism 3** (Clear position needed): **Medium**. Agreement drops with neutral authorities, but results are confounded by model-specific neutrality rates and sparse data for some roles.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-model replication:** Test with additional models (Claude 3.5, Llama 3 70B) to assess generality of power-type effects and model susceptibility differences.
2. **Domain extension:** Conduct similar experiments on a factual QA benchmark (e.g., MMLU) to determine if bias persists with objective correct answers.
3. **Prompt ablation:** Vary role description granularity (title only vs. full description) to pinpoint which aspects of role labels trigger the bias.
