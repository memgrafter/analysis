# Amortized Reasoning Tree Search: Decoupling Proposal and Decision in Large Language Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12846
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12846
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** ARTS achieves 74.6% Best-of-16 accuracy on MATH-500, matching a fully fine-tuned GRPO policy (74.7%) while using a frozen backbone and only 0.3% additional parameters, and uniquely recovers 6.9% accuracy on long-tail problems where RLVR collapses to 0%.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for large language models suffers from a "Normalization Squeeze" that systematically suppresses rare but correct reasoning paths due to low-temperature sampling and finite rollouts. This paper proposes Amortized Reasoning Tree Search (ARTS) to address this issue by decoupling the generation of reasoning traces (Proposer) from their verification (Verifier). The Verifier is trained with a Flow Matching objective, which estimates the conservation of probability flow across a sparse reasoning graph, enabling robust ranking of trajectories even when correct paths are infrequently sampled. On the MATH-500 benchmark, ARTS achieves 74.6% Best-of-16 accuracy, matching a fully fine-tuned GRPO policy while using a frozen backbone and only 0.3% additional parameters. Most notably, on the hardest long-tail problems where RLVR fails completely (0% pass@16), ARTS recovers 6.9% accuracy, demonstrating its ability to preserve rare reasoning capabilities.

## Method Summary
ARTS employs a frozen pretrained LLM as the Proposer to generate diverse chain-of-thought trajectories. For training, it constructs a Sparse Deep Fork (SDF) graph from a small set of sampled trajectories by stochastically branching at intermediate states. A separate Verifier network, built from a small instruction-tuned LLM with LoRA adapters, is trained using a Reasoning Flow Matching (RFM) loss. This loss enforces that the predicted flow at each non-terminal node approximates the sum of flows of its observed children, while terminal nodes are regressed to the reward (10 for correct, 0.1 otherwise). At inference, the Proposer generates multiple candidate trajectories (Best-of-N), each terminal state is scored by the Verifier's flow estimate, and the highest-scoring answer is selected. By training the Verifier to estimate global flow conservation rather than local correctness, ARTS maintains sensitivity to rare correct paths that would be ignored by discriminative objectives.

## Key Results
- ARTS achieves 74.6% Best-of-16 accuracy on MATH-500, matching the performance of a fully fine-tuned GRPO policy (74.7%) while freezing the backbone and adding only 0.3% parameters.
- On problems where GRPO collapses to 0% pass@16 (the "Extinction Set"), ARTS recovers 6.9% accuracy, closing a 25.8% gap.
- ARTS attains 27.0% accuracy on rare solutions (those with base probability ≤12.5%), significantly outperforming standard RLVR.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Normalization Squeeze in RLVR
Standard RLVR with verifiable rewards suppresses valid but low-likelihood reasoning traces due to a "Normalization Squeeze". Low-temperature sampling and finite rollouts create a sparse empirical support set. The policy gradient update only boosts logits of sampled (high-likelihood) traces, shifting the partition function by a factor α_k > 0. Consequently, the probability of any unsampled trace—even if correct—decays multiplicatively by 1/(1+α_k) at each iteration. This acts as a high-pass likelihood filter, driving rare correct paths to extinction. The authors validate this via controlled experiments showing consistent negative drift in log-likelihood for rare and endangered traces.

### Mechanism 2: Flow Matching Verifier
ARTS decouples verification from generation and trains the Verifier with a Flow Matching objective on the SDF graph. The Verifier F_θ(s) is trained to satisfy local flow conservation: log F(s) ≈ log Σ_{s'∈C_obs} F(s'). Because correct paths carry a high reward (10 vs. 0.1), observing any one correct child in the sparse graph pushes the parent's flow estimate high, creating a robust ranking signal: F(s_good) >> F(s_bad) even if s_good was rarely sampled. This global, subtree-aggregated value estimate remains sensitive to rare correct paths that discriminative objectives would miss.

### Mechanism 3: Search-Based Probability Amplification
During inference, Best-of-N search with the flow guide amplifies the discovery probability of rare correct trajectories. For a trajectory τ* with base probability π_0(τ*) ≪ 1, the probability that it appears among N proposals is approximately N · π_0(τ*). For a full trajectory of length H requiring H rare steps, the discovery probability scales as K^H · π_0(τ*) (where K is the proposal width). This geometric amplification allows ARTS to recover traces whose base probability would make them statistically invisible to RL update mechanisms.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Problem -> Proposer (frozen LLM) -> Trajectories -> SDF Constructor (stochastic forking) -> Sparse DAG G_sdf -> Training: Verifier (LLM+LoRA + Value Head) with RFM loss -> Trained Verifier F_θ -> Inference: Proposer generates N candidates -> Verifier scores terminal states -> Selection of highest F(s_T)

**Critical path:**  
1. Generate N base trajectories per problem from the frozen Proposer.  
2. Build SDF graph: for each intermediate node with probability 0.5, perform one additional rollout (fork), adding observed children.  
3. Train Verifier with RFM loss: for non-terminal nodes, minimize (log F(s) - log Σ_obs F(s'))²; for terminals, minimize (log F(s_T) - log R)².  
4. At inference, sample N full trajectories, score each terminal with F_θ, and output the answer from the highest-scoring trajectory.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Deliberation vs. Internalization: ARTS trades training-time parameter updates for inference-time compute, preserving base model diversity but increasing latency.  
- Sparse Graph Construction: SDF provides O(1.5N) node growth instead of exponential, but may miss critical branches, weakening the flow lower bound.  
- Flow Matching vs. Discriminative: RFM yields a ranking-preserving lower bound that is robust to sparse sampling, whereas discriminative objectives (PRM/RM) may overfit to local patterns and penalize rare-seeming correct steps.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Proposer Coverage Gap: Base model generates no correct path in N samples → Verifier has no positive signal → ARTS fails (0% accuracy).  
- Flow Calibration Collapse: Sparse graph or insufficient reward margin leads to underestimation of all flows → no separation between correct and incorrect → Best-of-N no better than random.  
- Search Budget Exhaustion: Even with amplification, if rare trajectory probability is astronomically small, K^H factor may still be insufficient for discovery.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Validate Normalization Squeeze: Run GRPO with fixed trajectory pool; track log-likelihood changes for Mode, Rare, Endangered traces. Expect rare/endangered to decay.  
2. Benchmark Verifier Objectives: Train Pairwise RM, Pointwise PRM, and ARTS RFM on identical SDF data; evaluate Best-of-N on MATH-500. Expect ARTS to match/surpass on hardest subsets.  
3. Stress Test Long-Tail: Evaluate BoN@16 on (A) problems with ≤2/16 correct base samples, and (B) problems where GRPO has 0% pass@16. Expect ARTS to show positive gain on both while others at/below random.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How can the reward shaping parameters in the Flow Matching objective (e.g., the 10 vs. 0.1 reward values) be determined from a principled probabilistic framework instead of manual tuning?  
*Basis:* Section 6.2 explicitly states reliance on manually designed soft reward scheme.  
*Why unresolved:* Manual hyperparameter selection may not generalize across diverse benchmarks or model scales.  
*What evidence would resolve:* A theoretical derivation linking reward magnitudes to properties like entropy of the reasoning space, supported by empirical validation showing consistent performance without manual adjustment.

**Open Question 2:** To what extent does ARTS's dependency on a frozen proposer constrain its ability to solve problems where correct reasoning paths are not present in the base model's initial support?  
*Basis:* Section 6.2 notes ARTS is a selector, not a creator, and cannot generate solutions structurally absent from the base model’s support.  
*Why unresolved:* If the base model lacks fundamental strategies for certain problem classes, ARTS cannot compensate, leaving a coverage gap.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Controlled experiments where proposer support is artificially constrained (e.g., fine-tuned to suppress specific patterns) and measuring ARTS's failure rate, or hybrid approaches that iteratively update the proposer using ARTS-filtered trajectories.

**Open Question 3:** Can the inference latency of ARTS be reduced to be competitive with greedy decoding policies while preserving its advantage on long-tail reasoning problems?  
*Basis:* Section 6.2 admits higher latency due to Best-of-N or tree search.  
*Why unresolved:* Computational overhead hinders real-world deployment; efficiency improvements without sacrificing robustness are needed.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Implementation optimizations (parallel verification, early exiting) or architectural modifications that achieve comparable accuracy with lower search budgets, benchmarked on latency metrics.

**Open Question 4:** Does the Flow Matching objective in ARTS maintain its effectiveness for sparse reasoning tasks beyond mathematics, such as code generation or agent-based planning in open-world environments?  
*Basis:* Section 6.3 suggests applicability to code generation and agentic planning but leaves it untested.  
*Why unresolved:* Transferability to domains with different structure, noise, and reward signals remains unknown.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Replication on code generation benchmarks (e.g., HumanEval) and agent planning benchmarks (e.g., WebShop), comparing against domain-specific verifiers and analyzing performance on high-branching-entropy tasks.

## Limitations
- The Normalization Squeeze analysis assumes trace orthogonality; real LLMs' shared representations may alter the dynamics, potentially weakening the effect.  
- ARTS relies on the frozen Proposer having non-zero coverage of correct reasoning paths; if the base model never produces a correct solution for a problem, the Verifier cannot recover it.  
- The Sparse Deep Fork graph construction may omit critical branching points, reducing the fidelity of the flow lower bound and limiting Verifier accuracy.

## Confidence
- Normalization Squeeze: Medium (theoretically derived with empirical support, but orthogonality assumption may not hold universally).  
- Flow Matching Verifier: High (strong empirical results matching GRPO with minimal extra parameters, robust recovery on long-tail).  
- Probability Amplification: Medium (derivation assumes conditional independence; scaling not directly measured).

## Next Checks
1. **Controlled squeeze test:** On a synthetic task with known trace probabilities, run RLVR with low temperature and monitor rare trace frequencies to confirm the multiplicative decay predicted by the Normalization Squeeze equation.  
2. **Coverage gap ablation:** Vary the number of base samples per problem (N) and measure ARTS accuracy on problems with zero correct samples in the initial pool to pinpoint the break point of proposer coverage.  
3. **Amplification scaling measurement:** On a task with a rare action of known probability p, sweep beam width K and trajectory length H; compare observed rare-trajectory discovery rate to the theoretical K^H · p bound.
