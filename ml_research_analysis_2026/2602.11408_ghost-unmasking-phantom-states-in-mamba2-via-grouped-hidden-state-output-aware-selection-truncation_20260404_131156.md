# GHOST: Unmasking Phantom States in Mamba2 via Grouped Hidden-state Output-aware Selection & Truncation

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.11408
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11408
- **Reference count**: 25
- **Primary result**: GHOST reduces Mamba2's state dimension by 50% with only ~1 point perplexity increase on WikiText-2, matches/exceeds gradient-based pruning without backpropagation, and remains stable at >70% sparsity.

## Executive Summary
Mamba2, a selective state-space model, suffers from high inference memory bandwidth due to its large state dimension. Existing pruning methods either fail to reduce this memory (unstructured sparsity) or are impractical (gradient-based, high cost). GHOST introduces a structured pruning approach that approximates control-theoretic balanced truncation using only forward-pass statistics. It computes a saliency score for each state channel as the product of controllability (hidden state covariance proxy) and observability (output Hessian proxy), then prunes across grouped heads per layer via pooled thresholding. Across models from 130M to 2.7B parameters, GHOST halves the state dimension with minimal perplexity degradation, outperforms Taylor pruning, and generalizes robustly across sequence lengths and out-of-distribution tasks—all with linear memory overhead and without masked distribution shift.

## Method Summary
GHOST prunes Mamba2's state dimension \(N\) in a structured, layerwise manner. For each layer, it runs a forward pass on a calibration set to collect per-channel statistics: the squared hidden state magnitude (controllability proxy) and the squared output projection magnitude after the depthwise convolution (observability proxy). Their product, averaged over time and heads, yields a saliency score. All scores from the layer's groups are pooled into a single list, and the lowest-scoring channels up to the target sparsity are pruned by masking corresponding columns in the \(B'\) and \(C'\) projection matrices and their associated convolution filters. To mitigate distribution shift, after pruning a layer, a second forward pass through subsequent layers uses the sparsified activations to recompute their saliency scores before they are pruned. This sequential process requires only two forward passes per layer and no gradient computation.

## Key Results
- Achieves 50% state reduction with only ~1.0 perplexity point increase on WikiText-2.
- Outperforms gradient-based Taylor pruning, particularly at high sparsity (>70%) and on small models (130M–370M).
- Generalizes across evaluation sequence lengths and shows small in-domain vs. out-of-distribution performance gaps (HumanEval, MMLU).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**1. Forward-pass approximation of balanced truncation**  
GHOST approximates the Hankel singular values from control-theoretic balanced truncation by using the instantaneous product \(P_i(t) = H_{t,i}^2\) (controllability proxy) and \(Q_i(t) = (C'_{g,i})^2\) (observability proxy). Aggregating \(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[P_i Q_i]}\) over a calibration set rank-orders state channels by their combined ability to be influenced by inputs and affect outputs, avoiding expensive Lyapunov solves or Hessian computations.

**2. Inter-group pooled thresholding**  
Mamba2's Grouped Query Attention shares dynamics within a group. Instead of pruning uniformly per group, GHOST pools saliency scores across all groups in a layer and applies a single threshold. This permits uneven sparsity: groups with higher dynamic energy retain more channels, aligning capacity allocation with actual modeling needs.

**3. Sequential layer-by-layer processing with activation updates**  
After pruning layer \(j\), GHOST propagates the modified activations forward and recomputes saliency for layers \(j+1..L\). This approximates the effect of a fully sparse network and mitigates masked distribution shift, all with only two forward passes per layer.

Each mechanism relies on assumptions: the local proxy sufficiently correlates with true time-varying Gramians; cross-group score comparability holds; and pruning effects are mostly local. Break conditions include severe calibration mismatch or highly nonlinear dynamics where the proxy fails.

## Foundational Learning
**State-Space Models (SSMs) & Selective SSMs**  
*Why needed*: GHOST prunes the state dimension \(N\) of Mamba2. The hidden state \(H_t\) is a compressed memory; its size directly controls memory bandwidth.  
*Quick check*: In \(H_t = A H_{t-1} + x' \otimes B'\), what are the computational and memory implications of reducing \(N\)?

**Control-Theoretic Balanced Truncation**  
*Why needed*: GHOST’s saliency score is an analog of Hankel singular values, which balance controllability and observability.  
*Quick check*: Why can’t classical balanced truncation (solving Lyapunov equations) be directly applied to Mamba2’s input-dependent system?

**Structured vs. Unstructured Pruning**  
*Why needed*: Only structured pruning of the state dimension reduces the recurrent state’s memory footprint. Unstructured weight sparsity leaves activations dense.  
*Quick check*: If you prune 50% of individual weights in a matrix \(W\) randomly, why might the activation memory remain unchanged?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input projection → Depthwise Conv + SiLU → GQA structure → Discretization → Recurrent update → Output → Gating & Projection. GHOST intervenes by pruning columns of \(B'\) and \(C'\) (state dimension \(N\)) based on statistics from the recurrent update and output steps.

**Critical path**: For each layer, first forward pass accumulates saliency \(S_i \propto \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[H_{t,i}^2 \cdot (C'_{g,i})^2]}\). Scores from all groups are pooled; lowest-ranked channels up to sparsity \(\kappa\) are masked out in the \(B'\) and \(C'\) projection weights and convolution kernels. A second forward pass (or continued propagation) with the pruned layer updates activations for subsequent layers, whose scores are then computed similarly.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Memory vs. fidelity: Uses \(O(G \cdot N)\) space for scores, avoiding \(O(N^2)\) Gramians/Hessians, at the cost of a local Hessian proxy.
- Per-layer adaptivity: Pooling yields a single threshold per layer, enabling uneven sparsity across groups but simplifying decisions relative to per-group thresholds.
- Sequential vs. joint: Sequential pruning is cheap but risks error accumulation; joint re-optimization would be accurate but computationally prohibitive.

**Failure signatures**:
- Perplexity explosion at very high sparsity (>90%) or when skipping sequential activation updates.
- Gradient-based baselines (Taylor) degrade with longer evaluation contexts or on small models, indicating metric instability.
- Out-of-distribution performance drops for magnitude/random pruning, showing overfitting to calibration distribution.

**First three experiments**:
1. **Sparsity sweep** (10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 90%): Check that GHOST’s perplexity degrades more gracefully than baselines, especially at high sparsity.
2. **Sequence length generalization**: Calibrate on short sequences (e.g., length 128) and evaluate on long contexts (up to 2048+). GHOST should maintain stable or improving perplexity with length, unlike Taylor.
3. **Saliency ablation**: Compare using only controllability (\(P\)), only observability (\(Q\)), and their product (\(P \cdot Q\)). The product should yield the lowest perplexity, confirming the balanced truncation principle.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Essential dynamics at extreme sparsity**: Which specific state functionalities are irrevocably lost when pruning beyond ~90%, causing abrupt performance drops? The paper notes an “elbow” but does not characterize the pruned channels.
2. **Gradient-based failure on small models**: Why do Taylor scores become catastrophically unreliable for Mamba2 models of 130M–370M parameters while GHOST remains robust? The discussion hypothesizes noisy gradients but provides no analysis.
3. **Extension to other SSMs**: How can GHOST’s controllability-observability proxy be adapted to SSMs with different transition matrices (e.g., S4, Mamba1) that lack Mamba2’s scalar-identity and GQA structure?
4. **Complementarity of P and Q**: What distinct information do the two saliency components capture, and why is their product superior to either alone, particularly at high sparsity? A correlation or ablation study per cluster is missing.

## Limitations
- **Masking ambiguity**: Unclear exactly how the binary mask is applied to depthwise convolution filters and whether biases are included, hindering exact reproduction.
- **Sequential update details**: The precise number of forward passes per layer and the extent of re-calibration are not fully specified, leaving uncertainty about the claimed mitigation of distribution shift.
- **Calibration sensitivity**: The dependence of the empirical Gramian proxy on calibration sequence length and distribution is insufficiently ablated; extreme mismatches may degrade saliency rankings.

## Confidence
- **Empirical pruning efficacy**: High (demonstrated across model scales and sparsity levels).
- **Superiority over gradient-based Taylor pruning without backprop**: Medium (supported by experiments but potential implementation differences).
- **Inter-group pooling adaptive capacity allocation**: Low (conceptual rationale provided but no direct ablation against per-group thresholding).
- **Mitigation of masked distribution shift via sequential updates**: Medium (contrast with Taylor and algorithmic description support it, but cumulative error risk not quantified).

## Next Checks
1. **Inspect masking on convolution parameters**: In any released implementation, verify exactly how the mask \(m\) is broadcast to \(W_B\), \(W_C\), and the depthwise convolution kernels, and whether biases are affected.
2. **Calibration length sensitivity test**: Fix a model (e.g., 1.3B) and vary calibration sequence lengths (128, 512, 2048). Evaluate perplexity on a long context (e.g., 4096). If GHOST’s advantage over Taylor diminishes with longer calibration, the forward-pass proxy may still be horizon-dependent.
3. **Correlation with exact Hankel singular values**: On a small linear SSM (e.g., N=16, random A, B, C), compute true time-varying controllability/observability Gramians and Hankel singular values over a sequence. Correlate with GHOST’s aggregated \(\sqrt{\text{mean}(H_t^2 C_t^2)}\) scores; a Pearson correlation >0.8 would validate the proxy.
