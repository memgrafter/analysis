# Implicit Grading Bias in Large Language Models: How Writing Style Affects Automated Assessment Across Math, Programming, and Essay Tasks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.18765  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18765  
- **Reference count**: 11  
- **Primary result**: LLM graders exhibit large, statistically significant bias against informal language and grammatical errors in essay grading, but not in math or programming tasks, even when explicitly instructed to ignore style.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates bias in large language models (LLMs) used for automated grading. Using a controlled dataset of 180 student responses across math, programming, and essay tasks, two state-of-the-art open-source models (LLaMA 3.3 70B, Qwen 2.5 72B) were prompted to grade on a 1–10 scale while ignoring writing style. Results show a pronounced and statistically significant bias in essay grading, where informal language and errors led to substantial score reductions—equivalent to a full letter grade drop—despite correct content. In contrast, math and programming tasks showed minimal, mostly non-significant bias. The bias persists even with anti-bias prompting, indicating that LLM grading fairness is highly task-dependent and not easily mitigated by simple instructions.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed a curated dataset of 180 student answers spanning three subjects: mathematics, programming, and essay writing. Responses were manipulated to include variations in writing style (e.g., grammar errors, informal language, non-native phrasing) while keeping content correct. Two large open-source LLMs (LLaMA 3.3 70B and Qwen 2.5 72B) were prompted with explicit instructions to evaluate only content and ignore stylistic elements. Grades were assigned on a 1–10 scale, and statistical analysis (including Cohen’s d and p-values) measured score differences between standard and non-standard style variants.

## Key Results
- Essay tasks showed large-effect-size bias (Cohen’s d = 0.64 to 4.25, p < 0.05), with informal language incurring the severest penalties (LLaMA: -1.90, Qwen: -1.20 points).
- Mathematics and programming tasks exhibited minimal, mostly non-significant bias.
- Anti-bias prompting failed to eliminate the stylistic penalty in essays.
- Bias magnitude varied by model and style type, but was consistent and substantial for informal language.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The bias likely arises from LLMs’ training on vast corpora of polished, formal text, leading to implicit associations between standard writing style and correctness or quality. In essay tasks—where style and content are often intertwined in human evaluation—the models may overgeneralize stylistic cues as proxies for content quality. In contrast, math and programming tasks have more rigid, syntax-bound correctness criteria, making them less susceptible to stylistic variation. Explicit instructions to ignore style are insufficient because the models’ internal representations strongly correlate formal language with higher-quality outputs.

## Foundational Learning
**1. Prompt Engineering for Evaluation**  
*Why needed*: Directs the model’s focus to specific rubric dimensions (e.g., “ignore grammar”).  
*Quick check*: Does varying prompt phrasing change bias magnitude?

**2. Bias Metrics in NLP**  
*Why needed*: Quantifies disparate impact (e.g., Cohen’s d) on protected/grouped outputs.  
*Quick check*: Are effect sizes consistent across different bias definitions (e.g., error type)?

**3. Subject-Specific Rubric Design**  
*Why needed*: Defines what constitutes “content” vs. “style” differently per domain.  
*Quick check*: Do human instructors agree on the content/style boundary in essays?

**4. LLM Calibration**  
*Why needed*: Ensures model outputs (grades) are reliable and aligned with intended scales.  
*Quick check*: Are grade distributions similar across style variants for correct content?

**5. Training Data Provenance**  
*Why needed*: Explains model biases inherited from pre-training corpora.  
*Quick check*: Does model bias correlate with formality of its training data?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Input (Student Response + Prompt) -> Prompt Formatter -> LLM Inference Engine -> Output (Score)

**Critical Path**  
The score is generated by the LLM inference engine conditioned on the prompt. The prompt’s instructional clause (“ignore style”) is processed alongside the response text, but the model’s internal weights dominate the final output.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Generality vs. Specificity*: A single prompt for all subjects trades off subject-specific nuance.  
- *Interpretability vs. Performance*: Larger models (70B+) reduce randomness but obscure bias mechanisms.  
- *Instruction Following vs. Training Bias*: Models are trained to mimic high-quality text, which conflicts with “ignore style” instructions in style-sensitive tasks.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Score disparities between style variants despite identical content.  
- Anti-bias prompts failing to reduce disparities.  
- Bias isolated to tasks where human rubrics include style (essays).

**First Three Experiments**  
1. Test bias persistence with chain-of-thought prompting to see if intermediate reasoning reduces penalty.  
2. Replace LLM grading with a fine-tributed classifier on style features to isolate content scoring.  
3. Introduce adversarial examples with extreme style violations but correct content to probe bias thresholds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
None explicitly raised; the paper’s “Next Validation Checks” imply future work but no formal open questions are listed.

## Limitations
- Sample limited to 180 answers and two specific open-source models; generalizability unknown.  
- Dataset curated; real-world noise (diverse errors, topics) may change bias magnitude.  
- Ambiguous boundary between content and style in essay rubrics may confound “bias” vs. legitimate scoring.

## Confidence
| Claim                                      | Confidence |
|--------------------------------------------|------------|
| Bias exists in essay grading               | High       |
| No meaningful bias in math/programming    | Medium     |
| Bias resists anti-bias prompting          | High       |
| Bias generalizes beyond studied models/tasks | Low       |

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-model replication**: Test the same dataset on at least three additional LLM families (e.g., GPT-4, Claude 3, Mistral).  
2. **Human-LLM benchmarking**: Compare LLM grades against human instructors on identical responses to quantify bias divergence.  
3. **Rubric decomposition**: Re-run essay grading with fine-grained scores (content, organization, style) to isolate the penalized dimension.
