# Excited Pfaffians: Generalized Neural Wave Functions Across Structure and State

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.14515
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14515
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Combination of Multi‑State Importance Sampling (MSIS) and Excited Pfaffians yields near‑constant scaling O(Ns^0.14) for neural excited‑state VMC, enabling all 33 excited states of beryllium and chemical accuracy on most of 12 test molecules.

## Executive Summary
Neural‑network variational Monte Carlo (VMC) for excited states traditionally suffers from superlinear scaling with the number of states due to expensive overlap estimation and separate wave functions. This work introduces two innovations: Multi‑State Importance Sampling (MSIS) and Excited Pfaffians. MSIS pools samples from all target states to estimate overlaps with variance that does not grow with the number of states for a fixed batch size, stabilizing training. Excited Pfaffians adopt a Hartree‑Fock‑inspired architecture with a single shared neural backbone and state‑specific orbital selectors, allowing many states to be modeled with minimal extra parameters. The combination reduces computational scaling from up to O(Ns^4) to O(Ns^0.14) on the carbon dimer, matches the accuracy of prior high‑cost methods while training >200× faster, and models 12 states instead of 8. It is the first neural‑network application to compute all 33 excited states of beryllium, and a single model attains chemical accuracy (<1.6 mHa) on most of 12 diverse molecules, demonstrating generalized wave functions across compounds.

## Method Summary
The authors propose Multi‑State Importance Sampling (MSIS) and Excited Pfaffians. MSIS collects configurations from all target states during the sampling process and uses them to estimate the overlap matrix with variance independent of the number of states when the batch size is fixed. This replaces previous high‑variance estimators that limited scalability. Excited Pfaffians define a neural wave function where a single backbone network generates a set of orbitals; each state then uses a specific orbital selector (e.g., a learned mask or attention) to pick a subset, and a Pfaffian of the selected orbitals yields the antisymmetric wave function for that state. The Pfaffian ansatz captures correlation beyond a single Slater determinant at O(N^3) cost. Both components are trained jointly with a loss that minimizes each state’s energy and enforces orthogonality via the overlap matrix. By sharing the backbone, the total number of parameters grows only modestly with the number of states, while MSIS keeps the sampling overhead manageable.

## Key Results
- Scaling on carbon dimer improves from up to O(Ns^4) to near‑constant O(Ns^0.14) with the proposed combination.
- Matches the accuracy of the O(Ns^4) natural excited states method while training >200× faster and increasing the number of modeled states from 8 to 12.
- First neural‑network computation of all 33 excited states of beryllium.
- A single model achieves chemical accuracy (<1.6 mHa) on most of 12 tested molecules, indicating cross‑molecule generalization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MSIS drastically reduces the variance of overlap estimates by using the same set of sampled configurations for all states; for a fixed batch size the variance becomes independent of the number of states, avoiding the explosion that previously made scaling superlinear. Excited Pfaffians amortize representation learning: the shared backbone learns a rich, reusable set of orbital‑like features, while light‑weight state‑specific selectors tailor these features to each state without duplicating the heavy neural network. This keeps the parameter count low and allows the model to capture both common and state‑specific correlation patterns. The Pfaffian provides an expressive yet tractable antisymmetric ansatz. Together, these elements remove the two main bottlenecks—overlap estimation and per‑state model size—yielding dramatically better scaling and enabling the simultaneous treatment of dozens of states.

## Foundational Learning
- **Variational Monte Carlo (VMC)**: Stochastic energy evaluation by sampling configurations from |Ψ|^2. *Why needed*: Provides the optimization framework for neural wave functions. *Quick check*: Verify that the local energy estimator is unbiased under the correct distribution.
- **Neural Quantum States (NQS)**: Parametrize Ψ with a neural network (often with a positive output and antisymmetry via determinant/Pfaffian). *Why needed*: Offers a flexible, powerful representation for many‑electron wave functions. *Quick check*: Ensure antisymmetry is enforced (e.g., sign change under particle swap).
- **Excited States via Orthogonalization**: Enforce linear independence of multiple wave functions, usually by overlap penalties or diagonalisation. *Why needed*: Prevents all states from collapsing to the ground state during training. *Quick check*: After training, the overlap matrix should be approximately diagonal (entries near zero off‑diagonal).
- **Importance Sampling**: Reduce Monte Carlo variance by sampling more frequently where the integrand is large. *Why needed*: Makes VMC practical by lowering the number of required samples. *Quick check*: Metropolis acceptance ratio should lie in a reasonable range (e.g., 30‑60%).
- **Pfaffian Wave Functions**: Use a Pfaffian (generalized determinant) to build an antisymmetric wave function. *Why needed*: Captures pairing correlations and dynamic effects beyond a single Slater determinant while remaining O(N^3) to evaluate. *Quick check*: Swap two particle coordinates and confirm the wave function changes sign.
- **Hartree‑Fock Orbitals**: Self‑consistent single‑particle orbitals from mean‑field theory. *Why needed*: Provide a physically motivated starting point and structure for the neural backbone. *Quick check*: In the non‑interacting limit, learned orbitals should converge to Hartree‑Fock solutions.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Input (molecular geometry, basis set) → Shared Neural Backbone → State‑Specific Orbital Selectors → Pfaffian Matrix Construction → Wavefunction Evaluation (log|Ψ|) → Local Energy & Variance → MSIS Overlap Estimator → Loss (Energy + Orthogonality penalties) → Optimizer (Adam/SR)

**Critical path**  
The dominant computations are: (1) forward pass through the shared backbone, (2) Pfaffian evaluation per state and configuration (O(N^3) with N electrons), and (3) overlap matrix estimation via MSIS, which requires simultaneous evaluation of all states. The backbone cost is amortized across states, while Pfaffian and overlap steps scale with the number of states.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Sharing vs. expressivity*: A fully shared backbone reduces parameters and encourages transfer learning but might limit modeling of highly dissimilar or strongly correlated states.
- *Selector design*: Hard masks (discrete selection) are interpretable but non‑differentiable; soft selectors (e.g., attention) are differentiable but may introduce interference between states.
- *MSIS batch size*: Larger batches lower overlap variance but increase per‑iteration cost linearly with the number of states; the batch size must balance stability and speed.
- *Pfaffian size*: Direct O(N^3) evaluation limits applicability to large systems; truncation or approximate methods may be required.

**Failure signatures**  
- Overlap matrix becomes ill‑conditioned or off‑diagonal entries remain large → orthogonality not enforced.
- Energy estimates diverge or show high variance → MSIS samples insufficient or batch size too small.
- Model collapses to a single state (others have high energy) → backbone lacks capacity to differentiate states.
- Discrepancy between shared‑model results and separate‑model baselines on diverse states → architectural bottleneck.

**3 first experiments**  
1. **Baseline with/without MSIS** on a small molecule (e.g., H₂O) for 2–3 states; verify that MSIS reduces overlap variance and improves convergence stability.  
2. **Ablation of sharing**: train separate per‑state networks, a fully shared model without selectors, and the proposed hybrid on a state set with varying correlation; compare parameter counts, wall‑clock time, and final energies.  
3. **Scaling verification** on the carbon dimer: measure total training time and energy error versus number of states (2,4,6,8,10,12); fit the scaling exponent and confirm ≈ O(Ns^0.14).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The authors did not explicitly list any open questions; the focus remains on the presented methodology and results.

## Limitations
- **Generalization scope**: Cross‑molecule generalization is demonstrated on only 12 small‑to‑medium molecules; scalability to larger or more complex systems (e.g., transition metals, extended materials) is untested.
- **Architectural limits**: The shared backbone with state‑specific selectors may not be sufficiently expressive for highly dissimilar excited states or regimes with strong correlation; no failure‑mode analysis is provided.
- **Comparative baselines**: Advantages are shown against prior neural VMC excited‑state methods, but direct comparisons to established classical quantum chemistry approaches (EOM‑CCSD, CASPT2) on the same benchmark set are missing.

## Confidence
- **Near‑constant scaling O(Ns^0.14) vs. O(Ns^4)**: High (quantitatively supported on carbon dimer).
- **MSIS variance‑independent overlap estimation**: High (theoretically argued and empirically validated).
- **Chemical accuracy (<1.6 mHa) on most of 12 molecules**: Medium (lacks statistical reporting across seeds).
- **Generalized wave functions across compounds**: Low (assertion not rigorously defined; applicability beyond tested set remains speculative).

## Next Checks
1. **Test on larger, diverse systems**: Apply MSIS + Excited Pfaffians to a benchmark of ≥20 molecules including conjugated polymers, radicals, and transition‑metal complexes; report state‑averaged errors versus high‑level classical references (EOM‑CCSD, CASPT2).
2. **Ablation study of architectural components**: Isolate the contribution of the shared backbone vs. state‑specific selectors by training (a) separate models without sharing, (b) a fully shared model without selectors, and (c) the proposed hybrid, on a subset of states with varying correlation character.
3. **Statistical robustness**: Run ≥10 random initializations for MSIS training on a representative system (e.g., carbon dimer); quantify variance in final energies and overlap estimates to confirm stabilization claims.
