# SampoNLP: A Self-Referential Toolkit for Morphological Analysis of Subword Tokenizers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04469
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04469
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** Introduces SampoNLP, a corpus-free toolkit that creates high-purity morphological lexicons from noisy dictionaries and uses them to evaluate BPE tokenizers for Uralic languages, finding optimal vocab ranges of 80k–150k and revealing BPE's severe limitations, with Finnish's max IPS at only 0.31.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the absence of clean morpheme lexicons for intrinsic evaluation of subword tokenizers on morphologically rich Uralic languages. It presents SampoNLP, a toolkit that builds high-purity morpheme lists from noisy dictionary entries using an MDL-inspired, self-referential iterative scoring algorithm (IMDP). These lexicons enable systematic assessment of BPE tokenizers across vocabulary sizes (8k–256k) for Finnish, Hungarian, and Estonian. The authors propose the Integrated Performance Score (IPS) to balance morpheme coverage against over-splitting, identifying optimal ranges: 80k–128k for Hungarian and Estonian, and 80k–150k for Finnish. Results show BPE's severe shortcomings for highly agglutinative languages, exemplified by Finnish's plateau at IPS 0.31. The toolkit and resources are released publicly.

## Method Summary
SampoNLP's core is the Iterative Morpheme Discovery Process (IMDP), which transforms a noisy dictionary of word forms into a high-purity morpheme lexicon without a corpus. Starting with a whitelist of single-character morphemes, it enters an iterative loop: words are segmented using the current lexicon; all candidate substrings receive a score based on Morfessor's Boundary Entropy Penalty (BEP), which measures how consistently a substring appears at morpheme boundaries; low-scoring candidates are removed. The loop continues until convergence. The final lexicon is then applied to train and evaluate BPE tokenizers at various vocabulary sizes. For each tokenizer, the toolkit computes morpheme coverage (the fraction of morphemes present in the vocabulary) and over-splitting (the fraction of tokens that are not full morphemes), combining them into the IPS to identify the optimal vocabulary size.

## Key Results
- Optimal BPE vocabulary sizes: 80k–128k for Hungarian and Estonian; 80k–150k for Finnish.
- Finnish tokenizers achieve a maximum IPS of only 0.31, demonstrating standard BPE's severe limitations for highly agglutinative languages.
- The SampoNLP toolkit and generated morpheme lexicons are publicly available.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The self-referential iterative mechanism exploits the consistency of true morphemes across word forms. Each iteration refines the lexicon by segmenting words and scoring candidates via BEP, which penalizes substrings that appear in ambiguous boundary contexts. Low-scoring candidates, likely non-morphemes, are pruned. The MDL inspiration appears as a bias toward shorter, reusable units that efficiently compress words. The initial whitelist anchors atomic units. Over iterations, the lexicon converges to a stable set of high-purity morphemes without needing corpus frequencies, making it suitable for low-resource settings.

## Foundational Learning
- **Minimum Description Length (MDL):** A principle favoring models that compress data best. *Why needed:* The BEP score approximates MDL by rewarding morphemes that yield a shorter description of word forms. *Quick check:* How would you compute the description length of a word given a morpheme inventory?
- **Agglutinative morphology:** Words built by concatenating morphemes, each with one grammatical function. *Why needed:* Such languages produce many rare long words, challenging tokenizers that rely on frequency. *Quick check:* Break the Finnish word "kirjoissamme" ("in our books") into morphemes.
- **Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE):** Subword algorithm merging the most frequent adjacent symbol pairs. *Why needed:* BPE's frequency-driven merges often cross morpheme boundaries in agglutinative languages. *Quick check:* Why might the pair "ssa" be merged frequently in Finnish despite aligning with a single morpheme?
- **Morpheme:** Smallest meaning-bearing unit. *Why needed:* SampoNLP aims to extract atomic morphemes as a gold standard for tokenizer evaluation. *Quick check:* Are the forms "do" and "did" in English separate morphemes? Why or why not?
- **Integrated Performance Score (IPS):** Metric combining morpheme coverage (recall) and over-splitting (non-morpheme tokens). *Why needed:* A good tokenizer should cover morphemes without fragmenting them; IPS balances these competing goals. *Quick check:* If a tokenizer has 90% coverage but 40% over-splitting, is it desirable? Why?
- **Self-referential iterative scoring:** Using the current solution to refine itself over cycles. *Why needed:* Allows bootstrapping from a small seed lexicon and gradually improving purity without external labels. *Quick check:* What could cause the iterations to fail to converge?

## Architecture Onboarding
The SampoNLP pipeline transforms noisy dictionary lists into morphemes and evaluates tokenizers:

Raw Dictionary -> Preprocess (normalize) -> Initialize Whitelist (single chars) -> [Iteration Loop: Segment words with current lexicon -> Compute BEP for all candidates -> Remove below threshold -> Update lexicon] -> Converged Morpheme Lexicon -> (Evaluation) Train BPE (k=8k-256k) -> Compute coverage & over-splitting -> Calculate IPS -> Identify optimal k*

**Critical path:** The iterative loop's convergence determines lexicon quality; the evaluation stage requires training many tokenizers, which is computation-heavy.

**Design tradeoffs:** Corpus-free design suits low-resource languages but discards token frequencies that could improve purity. Self-referential refinement risks reinforcing initial biases. BEP is type-only, simple but may miss frequency patterns.

**Failure signatures:** Lexicon grows unbounded (threshold too low) or collapses (threshold too high). Evaluation shows high over-splitting (too many sub-morphemic tokens) or low coverage (missing morphemes). Flat IPS curves suggest the metric or language properties are not discriminative.

2. For Hungarian, compute IPS for BPE models at 32k, 64k, 128k; verify coverage rises with k while over-splitting also rises, peaking near 80k-128k.
3. Compare SampoNLP lexicon coverage against a small set of words hand-segmented by a linguist to sanity-check recall.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific linguistic properties of Finnish cause standard BPE to achieve such a low morphological performance ceiling (max IPS ~0.31) compared to Hungarian and Estonian?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The Results and Discussion sections explicitly state that Finnish's low IPS plateau quantitatively demonstrates BPE's severe limitations for highly agglutinative languages and attribute this to Finnish's "rich system of consonant gradation and stem alternations."
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the phenomenon but does not conduct a granular linguistic or algorithmic analysis to isolate which specific morphological processes (e.g., consonant gradation, vowel harmony, extensive compounding) are most disruptive to BPE's merge operations.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed error analysis comparing BPE's subword merges against gold-standard morphological segmentations for Finnish, Hungarian, and Estonian, quantifying the frequency and type of misalignments (e.g., splitting a single morpheme, merging across morpheme boundaries) attributable to specific morphophonological rules.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent do the qualitative differences in segmentation errors (beyond the coarse LMC/OSR metrics) impact downstream NLP task performance for Uralic languages?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors acknowledge that the IPS metric "abstracts away qualitative segmentation differences" as a "necessary compromise for scalability." This introduces an unstated assumption that such qualitative differences are negligible for the optimal vocabulary recommendation, which is not tested.
- Why unresolved: The evaluation is purely intrinsic and morphological; there is no extrinsic validation (e.g., on named entity recognition, machine translation, or parsing) to determine if tokenizers with similar IPS scores but different error profiles yield significantly different downstream results.
- What evidence would resolve it: An extrinsic evaluation study training small downstream models (e.g., BiLSTM-CRF for NER) with tokenizers at various vocabulary sizes and comparing performance, while analyzing correlation between specific segmentation error patterns (e.g., over-splitting of frequent functional morphemes vs. under-splitting of fused stems) and task metrics.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does incorporating token frequency information from a corpus improve the purity or linguistic validity of the morpheme lexicons produced by the corpus-free IMDP pipeline?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The core methodological innovation is the "corpus-free, type-only" nature of IMDP, which deliberately avoids using token frequencies, motivated by low-resource settings. This introduces an unstated assumption that frequency-based cues (used by methods like Morfessor) are either unnecessary or harmful for creating a high-purity atomic morpheme list.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not include an ablation study comparing IMDP's output to a version that incorporates frequency (e.g., weighting the BEP calculation by token frequency). Therefore, it is unknown whether the "high-purity" lexicons could be further refined or if frequency would introduce undesirable bias toward common but non-atomic forms.
- What evidence would resolve it: Re-running the IMDP pipeline with modified scoring that incorporates corpus frequency (e.g., multiplying BEP by log-frequency) and comparing the resulting lexicons against the original via a human evaluation or a downstream task where lexicon quality is critical (e.g., morphological reinfection).

### Open Question 4
- Question: How robust are the identified optimal vocabulary ranges (e.g., 80k–150k for Finnish) when tokenizers are trained on noisy, non-standard text (e.g., social media, user-generated content) as opposed to clean Wikipedia corpora?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The Discussion explicitly states a limitation: "Our use of clean, standardized corpora... does not reflect the noise of real-world data." This is presented as a clear avenue for future work, implying the current recommendations may not generalize.
- Why unresolved: All tokenizer training and evaluation used pre-processed Wikipedia dumps. The interaction between vocabulary size, morphological segmentation, and textual noise (typos, code-switching, informal morphology) is not investigated.
- What evidence would resolve it: Repeating the entire experimental pipeline (lexicon creation via SampoNLP, tokenizer training at various k, IPS calculation) using comparable but noisy corpora (e.g., sampled social media posts) for the same languages and comparing the resulting optimal k* ranges to those from the paper.

### Open Question 5
- Question: Is the "MDL-inspired Self-Referential Atomicity Scoring" principle and the SampoNLP pipeline effective for creating morpheme lexicons in other agglutinative language families beyond Uralic (e.g., Turkic, Bantu, or Dravidian)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper's scope is firmly on Uralic languages (Finnish, Hungarian, Estonian). The Conclusion frames the toolkit as a resource for "the Uralic language family" and states the need for "more morphologically-aware tokenization methods" more broadly, implying the method's generalization is an open question.
- Why unresolved: The pipeline's design, particularly its reliance on orthographic patterns and the whitelist of single-character morphemes (W), is tailored to the specific orthographic and morphological conventions of the studied Uralic languages. Its applicability to languages with different writing systems (e.g., abjads, syllabaries) or morphological paradigms is not evaluated.
- What evidence would resolve it: Applying the SampoNLP pipeline to raw dictionary lists for representative languages from other highly agglutinative families (e.g., Turkish, Swahili, Telugu) and conducting a manual evaluation or comparison against existing rule-based analyzers to assess the precision and recall of the extracted atomic morpheme lists.

## Limitations
- The toolkit's output purity depends on the quality of input noisy dictionaries; gaps or errors in these dictionaries limit achievable accuracy.
- Evaluation is confined to BPE and three Uralic languages; findings may not hold for other tokenization algorithms or language families.
- The claim of "high-purity" morpheme lexicons lacks external validation against a gold-standard morphological database or human annotation.
