# Valid Feature-Level Inference for Tabular Foundation Models via the Conditional Randomization Test

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06609
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06609
- **Reference count:** 10
- **Primary result:** Combines the Conditional Randomization Test (CRT) with TabPFN to produce finite-sample valid p-values for conditional feature relevance, achieving perfect power (1.00) in 8 of 11 synthetic benchmarks while controlling type-I error at or below the nominal 0.05 level in 6 benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a method for performing valid statistical hypothesis tests on individual feature relevance within complex, black-box machine learning models for tabular data. It integrates the Conditional Randomization Test (CRT)—a procedure that yields finite-sample valid p-values—with TabPFN, a pretrained probabilistic foundation model for tabular data. The approach uses TabPFN to model both the response distribution \(p(Y|X)\) and the conditional covariate distribution \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\). The expected log predictive density (ELPD) derived from \(p(Y|X)\) serves as the test statistic. By replacing the observed feature \(X_j\) with samples from its estimated conditional distribution and comparing the resulting ELPDs to the observed value, the method computes a p-value that is valid under the null hypothesis of conditional independence, without model retraining or parametric assumptions. Simulation studies across diverse synthetic datasets demonstrate strong empirical performance, though results indicate sensitivity to the accuracy of the estimated conditional distribution \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\) and to the complexity of the true data-generating process.

## Method Summary
The proposed method, TabPFN-CRT, addresses the problem of testing whether a feature \(X_j\) is conditionally relevant to the response \(Y\) given all other features \(X_{-j}\). It employs the Conditional Randomization Test (CRT) framework. The core steps are: (1) A single TabPFN model is fitted on the training data to estimate the full conditional distribution \(p(Y|X)\). (2) For each feature \(j\) under test, a separate TabPFN is fitted to estimate \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\). (3) Using this conditional model, \(B\) null datasets are generated by replacing the observed column \(X_j\) with samples drawn from \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\), while keeping all other data unchanged. (4) For each null dataset and for the original observed data, the test statistic—the expected log predictive density (ELPD) computed using the fixed \(p(Y|X)\) model from step (1)—is evaluated. (5) A p-value for feature \(j\) is calculated as the proportion of null statistics that are at least as extreme as the observed statistic, with a correction to ensure finite-sample validity. This procedure provides a p-value that is valid under weak conditions, primarily relying on the accurate estimation of \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\) and the exchangeability it induces under the null.

## Key Results
- In simulation studies across 11 synthetic datasets, the method achieved perfect power (1.00) in 8 datasets, including linear, nonlinear, and correlated settings.
- Type-I error was controlled at or below the nominal 0.05 level in 6 of the 11 benchmarks, with notable exceptions (e.g., 0.10 in the "Correlated Linear" dataset) highlighting sensitivity to covariate correlation.
- For irrelevant features, the distribution of p-values closely followed a Uniform(0,1) distribution on most benchmarks, indicating proper calibration of the test.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Finite-Sample Validity via Exchangeability
- **Claim:** The Conditional Randomization Test (CRT) yields finite-sample valid p-values by constructing an exchangeable null distribution.
- **Mechanism:** Under the null hypothesis \(H_0: Y \perp\!\!\!\perp X_j | X_{-j}\), the CRT replaces the observed feature values \(X_j\) with samples drawn from the conditional distribution \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\). This preserves all dependencies among covariates while destroying any direct association between \(X_j\) and \(Y\). The observed test statistic is then compared to statistics computed on these conditionally randomized datasets. Because the randomization respects the conditional distribution, the observed and null statistics are exchangeable under \(H_0\), enabling a valid p-value via a simple rank count.
- **Assumption:** The conditional distribution \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\) can be accurately sampled. Violation leads to invalid p-values.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract and Section 3 describe the CRT procedure and its finite-sample validity property. The validity is taken as established from the CRT literature.
- **Break condition:** Poor approximation of \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\) (e.g., in high-dimensional or highly nonlinear regimes) breaks exchangeability, leading to inflated type-I error, as seen in the "Correlated Linear" dataset (type-I error 0.10).

### Mechanism 2: TabPFN as a Unified Conditional Model
- **Claim:** TabPFN serves as a unified, pretrained probabilistic model for both \(p(Y|X)\) and \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\), enabling efficient and flexible inference without task-specific retraining.
- **Mechanism:** TabPFN is a transformer trained via in-context learning on a large distribution of synthetic tabular datasets. It performs Bayesian inference in a single forward pass, outputting calibrated posterior predictive distributions for regression and classification. For the CRT, one TabPFN models \(p(Y|X)\) to compute the ELPD, and separate TabPFNs model \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\) for generating null samples. Its pretraining on diverse synthetic data allows adaptation to various data-generating processes without refitting.
- **Assumption:** TabPFN's pretraining distribution sufficiently covers the true data distribution so its predictions are accurate for both response and covariates.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract and Section 3 state that TabPFN is "well suited for both components of the CRT" due to its ability to model both regression and classification without task-specific retraining.
- **Break condition:** When the true conditional distributions lie far outside TabPFN's pretraining manifold (e.g., extreme nonlinearities or novel data types), predictive accuracy degrades, compromising both the test statistic and the null distribution's validity.

### Mechanism 3: ELPD as a Powerful Test Statistic
- **Claim:** The Expected Log Predictive Density (ELPD) is an optimal and sensitive test statistic for detecting conditional relevance.
- **Mechanism:** The ELPD, \(T = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(y_i | x_i)\), is a proper scoring rule maximized in expectation by the true conditional distribution. Under the alternative, if \(X_j\) is conditionally relevant, the full model's ELPD (using all features) will be higher than when \(X_j\) is replaced by null draws that omit its signal. The CRT compares the observed ELPD to its null distribution; a sufficiently high observed value indicates \(X_j\)'s contribution. Likelihood-based statistics are theoretically optimal against point alternatives.
- **Assumption:** The ELPD computed using TabPFN's predictive distribution is a sensitive measure of whether \(X_j\) provides information about \(Y\) beyond \(X_{-j}\).
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3 motivates the ELPD choice by referencing theoretical optimality results from Katsevich and Ramdas (2022).
- **Break condition:** If TabPFN's \(p(Y|X)\) is misspecified and cannot capture the true functional form, the ELPD may not increase sufficiently under the alternative, reducing power. This is suggested by lower power in Friedman 2 (0.60) and Friedman 3 (0.40).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Conditional Independence**
  - **Why needed:** The null hypothesis \(H_0: Y \perp\!\!\!\perp X_j | X_{-j}\) states that given all other features, \(X_j\) provides no additional information about \(Y\). Understanding this is essential to interpret p-values correctly and distinguish conditional from marginal relevance.
  - **Quick check:** If \(X_j\) is highly correlated with another feature \(X_k\) that is truly relevant to \(Y\), what should the CRT p-value for \(X_j\) be under \(H_0\)? (Answer: It should be uniformly distributed, as \(X_j\) itself carries no additional direct information about \(Y\) beyond \(X_k\).)
- **Concept: Proper Scoring Rules**
  - **Why needed:** The ELPD is a proper scoring rule; its expected value is maximized when the predictive distribution equals the true conditional distribution. This property ensures that if \(X_j\) is relevant, the full model's ELPD will tend to be higher than when \(X_j\) is replaced by null draws.
  - **Quick check:** Why is maximizing expected log predictive density a principled way to measure predictive performance for a test statistic? (Answer: Because it is a strictly proper scoring rule, incentivizing honest and calibrated predictions, and is directly linked to the likelihood principle.)
- **Concept: Finite-Sample Validity vs. Asymptotic Guarantees**
  - **Why needed:** The CRT provides *finite-sample* validity because the exchangeability argument holds for any sample size, conditional on accurate \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\). This contrasts with many classical tests that rely on large-sample asymptotics, which can be unreliable in small or medium tabular datasets.
  - **Quick check:** What is the key difference between a test with finite-sample validity and one that only has asymptotic validity? (Answer: A finite-sample valid test guarantees type-I error control at the nominal level for any sample size under its assumptions, while an asymptotically valid test only guarantees control as the sample size tends to infinity.)
- **Concept: Exchangeability**
  - **Why needed:** The CRT's finite-sample validity hinges on the exchangeability between the observed test statistic and those computed under the null. This property allows the p-value to be interpreted as a rank probability without relying on asymptotic approximations.
  - **Quick check:** Under the null hypothesis, why does replacing \(X_j\) with samples from \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\) yield a set of statistics that are exchangeable with the observed statistic? (Answer: Because under \(H_0\), the joint distribution of \((Y, X)\) is invariant to such replacements, making the observed and null statistics identically distributed.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Data -> Train Split -> TabPFN (A) : estimates p(Y|X) -> fixed for all tests
  Train Split + Feature j -> TabPFN (B_j) : estimates p(X_j|X_{-j}) -> sampler for feature j
  Eval Data -> Apply TabPFN (A) -> compute ELPD(T_obs)
  Eval Data with X_j replaced by sampler(B_j) -> Apply TabPFN (A) -> compute ELPD(T_null^{(b)})
  Repeat for B null samples per feature -> compute p-value
- **Critical path:**
  1. Split data into train/evaluation sets.
  2. Fit TabPFN Model A on the training set to estimate \(p(Y|X)\).
  3. For each feature \(j\) of interest: fit a separate TabPFN Model B_j on the training set to estimate \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\).
  4. Compute the observed test statistic \(T_{\text{obs}}\) (ELPD) on the evaluation set using the fixed Model A.
  5. For feature \(j\), generate \(B\) null datasets by replacing column \(j\) with samples from Model B_j's conditional sampler given \(X_{-j}\).
  6. For each null dataset, compute the test statistic \(T^{(b)}\) using the fixed Model A.
  7. Compute the p-value for feature \(j\) as \(p = \frac{1 + \sum_{b=1}^B \mathbb{I}\{T^{(b)} \geq T_{\text{obs}}\}}{B+1}\).
  8. Repeat steps 3–7 for all features \(j\) and across Monte Carlo repetitions if needed.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Accuracy vs. Speed:** Using TabPFN avoids retraining per resample (fast inference) but requires fitting \(p+1\) models (one for \(Y|X\), \(p\) for \(X_j|X_{-j}\)). This is faster than retraining a neural net \(B\) times per feature, but \(O(p)\) model fits may still be costly for very high \(p\).
  - **Generality vs. Calibration:** TabPFN's pretraining gives flexibility across data types but may degrade on distributions far from its synthetic pretraining set. A task-specific generative model (e.g., a GAN for \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\)) might be more accurate but requires more tuning and training.
  - **Test Statistic Choice:** ELPD leverages TabPFN's probabilistic output and theoretical optimality. A simpler statistic (e.g., prediction accuracy) might be less sensitive.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Inflated type-I error** in correlated settings (e.g., "Correlated Linear" type-I error 0.10), indicating \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\) may be poorly approximated, breaking exchangeability.
  - **Low power** in complex nonlinear/interaction regimes (Friedman 2, Friedman 3), suggesting the ELPD statistic is insensitive or Model A/B is misspecified.
  - **Non-Uniform null p-value ECDF** (QQ plot deviations), signaling fundamental calibration failure, often due to poor conditional modeling.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Linear Sparse:** Baseline test with independent Gaussian features and linear signal. Expected: power=1.00, low type-I error. Validates basic functionality.
  2. **Correlated Features:** Tests robustness to covariate correlation (ρ≈0.7). Expected: power=1.00 but potentially elevated type-I error (observed 0.10). Highlights dependency on accurate conditional modeling.
  3. **Friedman 1:** Complex nonlinear, additive signal with interactions. Expected: power=1.00, low type-I error. Validates performance in strong nonlinear regimes where TabPFN should excel due to its pretraining on diverse synthetic functions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability:** How can the TabPFN-based CRT be scaled to handle very large datasets or high-dimensional feature spaces while maintaining finite-sample validity and computational efficiency? The current computational cost is \(O((n^2 + p^2)\cdot(B+1))\) model evaluations, which becomes prohibitive for large \(n\) or \(p\), and no scalable alternatives are provided.
2. **Causal Integration:** How can the CRT be integrated with causal inference frameworks to enable causal rather than purely associative claims about feature relevance? The CRT tests conditional independence, which does not establish causality; integrating with causal frameworks requires handling interventions, unmeasured confounding, and different data-generating assumptions.
3. **Diagnostics:** What practical diagnostics can be developed to detect when TabPFN's approximation of \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\) is insufficient, thereby warning of potential p-value miscalibration in real-world applications? In practice, the true conditional distribution is unknown, and approximation errors may occur silently.
4. **Complexity Sensitivity:** How does the complexity of \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\)—such as nonlinearity, feature interactions, or correlation strength—affect the finite-sample validity and power of the CRT, and what quantitative thresholds define "sufficient" approximation quality? The paper identifies patterns (e.g., elevated error in correlated settings, reduced power in nonlinear datasets) but lacks a systematic analysis linking specific complexities to performance metrics.

## Limitations
- The method's finite-sample validity critically depends on accurate estimation of the conditional covariate distribution \(p(X_j|X_{-j})\); empirical type-I error inflation (e.g., 0.10 in correlated linear settings) indicates sensitivity to misspecification.
- Power drops notably on complex nonlinear benchmarks (e.g., 0.40 on Friedman 3), suggesting limitations of the ELPD statistic or TabPFN's representational capacity under strong interactions.
- The computational cost scales linearly with the number of features \(p\) due to fitting a separate conditional model per feature, which may be prohibitive for very high-dimensional data.
- Generalizability beyond the reported synthetic benchmarks is unknown; real-world data may exhibit heavier tails, missing values, or distributions far from TabPFN's pretraining manifold.

2. **Real-data stress test:** Apply the method to 2–3 open tabular datasets with known ground-truth feature relevance (e.g., via designed interventions or expert curation). Check if p-values for irrelevant features remain calibrated and if relevant features are detected with plausible power.
3. **Correlation severity sweep:** Re-run the "Correlated Linear" benchmark across a range of correlation coefficients \(\rho \in [0.5, 0.9]\). Plot type-I error vs. \(\rho\) to identify the breaking point and determine if error inflation correlates with conditional model degradation (e.g., via KL divergence estimates).
