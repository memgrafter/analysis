# Continual Adaptation for Pacific Indigenous Speech Recognition

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06310
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06310
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** LoRA reduces overfitting for extremely low-resource languages (e.g., Lelepa at 2h) but full fine-tuning better preserves prior knowledge in sequential adaptation, revealing a plasticity‑stability trade‑off.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates adapting speech foundation models (Whisper‑Small) to three low‑resource Pacific Indigenous languages (Bislama, Nafsan, Lelepa) using a newly collected 32‑hour corpus. Comparing full fine‑tuning and LoRA under data scarcity and in sequential continual learning, the authors find that LoRA achieves lower word error rates on the most linguistically distant language (Lelepa) at very low data (2 hours) by preventing overfitting, while full fine‑tuning retains performance on previously learned languages better in sequential scenarios. Representational drift analysis shows that adaptation to distant languages causes significant changes in early encoder layers, indicating a fundamental trade‑off between plasticity and stability that current methods cannot resolve.

## Method Summary
The study uses a new parallel speech‑text corpus (23,843 samples, 32.13 hours) for Bislama, Nafsan, and Lelepa, split 80/10/10 per language. The base model is Whisper‑Small, pretrained on multilingual speech. For each target language, the tokenizer vocabulary is extended with observed characters, initializing new tokens from the average of pretrained embeddings. Two adaptation strategies are compared: full fine‑tuning (all parameters updated) and LoRA (low‑rank adapters injected into query and value projections of both encoder and decoder). Models are trained with AdamW (learning rate 1e‑4, batch size 16) using data augmentation (time shifts, in‑domain noise). Evaluation uses word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER). To analyze representational drift, layer‑wise hidden states from a fixed evaluation subset are extracted before and after adaptation; average cosine distances are computed and min‑max normalized per language. For continual learning, a sequential training regime (Nafsan then Lelepa) is used, with performance on all seen languages measured after each stage.

## Key Results
- At 2 hours of training data, LoRA yields a lower WER on Lelepa (75.66) compared to full fine‑tuning (84.10), demonstrating better generalization in extreme low‑resource settings.
- In sequential learning (Nafsan → Lelepa), full fine‑tuning better retains knowledge of the first language (average WER 64.7% on Nafsan) than LoRA (76.5%), indicating greater stability.
- Representational drift analysis shows that adaptation to the linguistically distant Lelepa induces significant changes in the early encoder layers, while adaptation to Bislama and Nafsan affects later layers, linking drift patterns to linguistic distance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** Adaptation to linguistically distant languages (like Lelepa) forces reorganization of early encoder representations because the acoustic‑phonetic patterns diverge from the pretraining distribution. The pretrained encoder’s low‑level filters are tuned to high‑resource languages; adjusting them for Lelepa causes high cosine‑distance drift in the first few layers. Evidence: abstract and section 4.2 note severe internal drift for distant languages; corpus context supports linguistic distance.

**Mechanism 2:** LoRA’s low‑rank updates act as a regularizer, preventing overfitting on tiny, divergent datasets. However, in sequential learning, the limited capacity of low‑rank subspaces leads to interference between tasks, causing catastrophic forgetting of previously learned languages. Evidence: LoRA outperforms full FT at 2h for Lelepa but suffers forgetting in Nafsan→Lelepa sequences (section 4.4, abstract).

**Mechanism 3:** Updating only the acoustic encoder (via encoder‑only LoRA) destroys general acoustic features, leading to worse catastrophic forgetting on high‑resource source languages (English) compared to full fine‑tuning. In contrast, decoder‑only updates preserve encoder knowledge and minimize forgetting. Evidence: Table 4 shows encoder‑only LoRA yields English WER 31.26% vs full FT 26.24%, while decoder‑only LoRA achieves 18.27%.

## Foundational Learning
- **Self‑Supervised Pretraining of Speech Foundation Models**  
  Pretraining on large unlabeled multilingual speech data (e.g., Whisper) learns general acoustic and cross‑lingual phonetic representations. *Why needed:* Adaptation builds on these representations; understanding what is pretrained clarifies what must change for new languages. *Quick check:* Does performance on a held‑out high‑resource language (e.g., English) represent a stable prior that should be preserved? What does degradation signify?

- **Catastrophic Forgetting in Neural Networks**  
  When a model is updated on a new task, it tends to overwrite parameters important for previous tasks, leading to performance drops. *Why needed:* The paper’s core problem is forgetting of previously acquired language knowledge during adaptation. *Quick check:* Why would updating all parameters cause more forgetting on English than updating only the decoder, even though the encoder processes raw audio?

- **Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning (PEFT)**  
  Methods like LoRA freeze most pretrained weights and inject trainable low‑rank matrices, reducing the number of updated parameters. *Why needed:* LoRA is a primary adaptation method; its mechanism explains its behavior in low‑resource and sequential settings. *Quick check:* If LoRA updates fewer parameters, why does it sometimes achieve lower WER than full FT on a new target language? What does this imply about the data regime?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**  
  Audio waveform (16kHz) -> Feature Extractor (log‑Mel spectrogram) -> Encoder (12 transformer layers) -> Decoder (12 transformer layers with cross‑attention) -> Text tokens (with extended vocabulary).
- **Critical path**  
  1) Extend tokenizer vocabulary for target language, initializing new tokens from average pretrained embeddings.  
  2) Load pretrained Whisper‑Small checkpoint.  
  3) Apply adaptation: either unfreeze all parameters (Full FT) or inject LoRA matrices into attention layers.  
  4) Train on target language corpus with language‑specific tokenization.  
  5) Evaluate WER/CER on test sets; for continual learning, also evaluate on previous language test sets and English.
- **Design tradeoffs**  
  Full fine‑tuning offers high plasticity but causes severe forgetting and representational drift. LoRA provides better stability for single‑language adaptation (especially extreme low‑resource) but catastrophically forgets in sequential settings. Component‑specific LoRA (encoder‑only) adapts acoustics but destroys source language performance; decoder‑only preserves source but fails to learn distant languages.
- **Failure signatures**  
  - High WER on a new distant language (e.g., Lelepa) after full FT on 2h data → overfitting.  
  - Sharp increase in English WER after adapting to any Pacific language → general representational collapse.  
  - Large jump in previously learned language WER (e.g., Nafsan) after sequential training on a new language → catastrophic forgetting (observed for LoRA).  
  - Early‑layer encoder drift magnitude (cosine distance) > later layers for a language → high linguistic distance.
- **First 3 experiments**  
  1. **Single‑language adaptation benchmark:** For each language (Bislama, Nafsan, Lelepa), train with Full FT and LoRA at varying data amounts (0.5h, 1h, 2h, 5h, 10h). Record test WER to compare adaptation efficiency.  
  2. **Representational drift profiling:** Extract layer‑wise hidden states from a fixed evaluation subset using the pretrained model and adapted models (Full FT and LoRA after 2h training). Compute average cosine distance per layer and min‑max normalize per language to produce drift curves. Expect Lelepa to show early encoder drift peak.  
  3. **Sequential learning stress test:** Train first on Nafsan, then sequentially on Lelepa. After each stage, evaluate WER on Nafsan, Lelepa, and English. Compare Full FT, LoRA, and other PEFT variants. Expect Full FT to retain Nafsan better but learn Lelepa worse; LoRA to show opposite pattern.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What adaptation strategies can effectively mitigate catastrophic forgetting while maintaining competitive target accuracy for linguistically distant, low‑resource Pacific Indigenous languages in sequential learning?
- What underlying mechanisms cause severe representational drift in early encoder layers when adapting to highly distant languages like Lelepa, and how can this drift be controlled to improve adaptation stability?
- Why does encoder‑only LoRA induce more catastrophic forgetting than full fine‑tuning, as indicated by higher English WER, and what encoder‑decoder update balances can optimize the plasticity‑stability trade‑off?
- Can established continual learning regularization techniques (e.g., Elastic Weight Consolidation, Synaptic Intelligence) be effectively integrated with speech foundation model fine‑tuning to prevent catastrophic forgetting in sequential language adaptation?

## Limitations
- Limited to Whisper‑Small architecture and only three Pacific languages; findings may not generalize to other models or language families.
- LoRA hyperparameters (rank, target modules, alpha) are not fully specified, hindering exact reproduction.
- Representational drift measurement lacks detail on activation pooling and normalization, leading to potential variability in replication.

## Confidence
- Empirical results (WER improvements, forgetting patterns): **High confidence**.
- Mechanistic explanations (drift causes, LoRA forgetting): **Medium confidence** (correlational, not causal).
- Generality to other models/languages: **Low confidence** (only one model, three languages tested).
