# Is Your Diffusion Sampler Actually Correct? A Sampler-Centric Evaluation of Discrete Diffusion Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19619
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19619
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Even with an exact oracle denoiser, few-step discrete diffusion samplers exhibit large transition-level mismatch; standard aggregate metrics like GenPPL and MAUVE fail to detect these errors.

## Executive Summary
Discrete diffusion language models (dLLMs) generate text by iteratively denoising from a masked or noisy state. Evaluating these models is difficult because errors can arise either from the learned denoiser’s inaccuracies or from the sampler’s update dynamics itself. This paper introduces an oracle framework that replaces the learned denoiser with an exact Hidden Markov Model posterior derived from a ground‑truth Markov chain, thereby isolating the effect of the sampler. The authors find that widely used few-step samplers (SEDD, MDLM, ReMDM, LLaDA) produce outputs with substantial divergence from the target transition kernel, and that common metrics such as negative log‑likelihood, generative perplexity, and MAUVE do not reliably reflect this mismatch.

## Method Summary
The authors first estimate a first‑order Markov chain transition matrix *P*′ from a held‑out corpus (OpenWebText), sparsifying it via top‑*K* per state and adding a small teleport mixture using the unigram distribution. For a noisy sequence with masked positions, they compute the exact posterior marginals *γ*<sub>*t*,*u*</sub> = *p*<sup>*⋆</sup>(*x*<sub>*u*</sub> | *z*<sub>*t*</sub>) via the forward‑backward algorithm on the resulting HMM. This “oracle denoiser” is substituted into existing discrete diffusion samplers (SEDD, MDLM, ReMDM, LLaDA) while preserving all other sampling logic (e.g., parallel unmasking, remasking schedules). The samplers are run for varying numbers of steps *S* (from 8 up to the sequence length 1024), generating 512 sequences per setting. Transition‑level correctness is measured by comparing the empirical bigram transition matrix of generated sequences to *P*′ using KL divergence, total variation, and entropy rate. Standard external metrics (GenPPL with GPT‑2 Large, MAUVE) are also computed.

## Key Results
- Few‑step samplers (even with exact oracle) show large transition‑level KL and TV; only when diffusion steps approach the sequence length do they converge to the target Markov kernel.
- Generative perplexity (GenPPL) decreases under intentional score sharpening while the true transition KL rises; MAUVE remains high across a wide range of KL values, indicating insensitivity.
- LLaDA achieves low sequence NLL but suffers from high transition KL and severe entropy collapse (repetitive outputs); ReMDM with confidence‑based remasking outperforms loop‑based schedules in matching the oracle kernel.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The oracle denoiser, derived from an exact HMM posterior, eliminates denoiser approximation error, isolating the effect of the sampler’s update dynamics.
- **Mechanism:** The ground‑truth data distribution is modeled as a discrete‑time Markov chain with transition kernel *P*′. Given a noisy sequence (masked positions), the forward‑backward algorithm computes the exact marginal posterior *γ*<sub>*t*,*u*</sub> for every position. This posterior is substituted for the learned denoiser in each sampler, while preserving all other sampling logic.
- **Core assumption:** The Markov chain prior captures true data dependencies; masking is applied independently per token; forward‑backward computation is exact.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.1–3.2 define the Markov prior and HMM posterior; related work on masked diffusion motivates the need for an oracle.
- **Break condition:** If real language has long‑range dependencies beyond first‑order Markov, the oracle prior under‑represents true structure; if forward masking is not independent, the posterior could differ from the neural denoiser’s training objective.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Transition‑level metrics (KL, TV, entropy rate) directly quantify the mismatch between the sampler’s empirical token‑to‑token transitions and the oracle transition kernel *P*′.
- **Mechanism:** Generated sequences are treated as paths of a discrete‑time Markov chain. The empirical transition matrix is estimated from bigram counts. State‑weighted KL and TV measure distributional correctness; entropy rate diagnoses over‑sharpening or excess randomness.
- **Core assumption:** The transition kernel characterizes sequential properties of interest; empirical estimate converges with enough samples; *P*′ is a valid approximation of the data distribution.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions transition‑level mismatch; Section 5 defines metrics; corpus on convergence rates for masked diffusion indirectly supports transition‑level analysis.
- **Break condition:** If the true data distribution has higher‑order Markov structure (e.g., dependencies on two previous tokens), pairwise transition metrics may not capture all errors.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Standard aggregate metrics (NLL, GenPPL, MAUVE) can be misleading: they may improve while transition‑level error worsens, because they reflect surface fluency rather than correct transition dynamics.
- **Mechanism:** Controlled perturbations (temperature scaling for SEDD, re‑masking schedules for ReMDM) are applied under an exact oracle denoiser. Decreasing sequence NLL/GenPPL coincides with increasing transition KL and reduced n‑gram diversity; MAUVE remains high despite substantial KL variations.
- **Core assumption:** External metrics computed with a pretrained LM or embedding divergence are aligned with the true distribution; shown to be false under the oracle.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Sections 7.2–7.4 provide temperature scaling, GenPPL sharpening, and MAUVE insensitivity results.
- **Break condition:** If the pretrained evaluator perfectly matches the oracle transition kernel, GenPPL and MAUVE would track KL; but in practice they do not.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Ground truth Markov chain (top‑*K* bigram *P*′ + teleport) → Oracle denoiser (HMM posterior via forward‑backward) → Sampler dynamics (SEDD concrete scores; MDLM/LLaDA parallel sampling; ReMDM confidence/loop remasking) → Evaluation metrics (transition KL/TV/entropy, GenPPL, MAUVE).

**Critical path:**  
1. Build Markov chain from held‑out data: estimate bigrams, sparsify via top‑*K* (preserving 99% mass; *K*=206 for OWT), add teleport ε=10⁻⁴ with unigram ν. Compute stationary distribution π₀ by power iteration.  
2. Implement log‑domain forward‑backward with sparse transitions to compute exact marginals γ<sub>*t*,*u*</sub> for any noisy sequence.  
3. Replace neural denoiser in each sampler with γ, preserving hyperparameters (step count *S*, temperature β, re‑masking caps, nucleus *p*).  
4. Generate *N*=512 sequences of length *T*=1024 per sampler and *S* ∈ {8,16,32,64,128,256,512,1024}.  
5. Compute: empirical bigram transitions → transition KL, TV, entropy rate; n‑gram diversity, duplication, support fraction; GenPPL with GPT‑2 Large; MAUVE vs. AR reference.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *K* (sparsity) vs. fidelity: smaller *K* reduces compute but discards low‑probability transitions.  
- ε (teleport) vs. zero‑prob transitions: small ε avoids underflow but slightly biases transitions.  
- Sequence length *T*: longer sequences stabilize transition estimates but increase memory (O(*TVK*) per step).  
- Number of samples *N*: more samples reduce variance; *N*=512 is reasonable.  
- Diffusion step count *S*: fewer steps are faster but incur larger sampler error.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High transition KL at low *S* (e.g., >0.5 for SEDD at 8 steps) indicates sampler error despite exact oracle.  
- LLaDA: NLL approaching AR while KL remains high; n‑gram diversity drops; outputs become repetitive.  
- GenPPL vs. KL anti‑correlation: lowering GenPPL (via temperature sharpening or re‑masking) coincides with rising KL.  
- MAUVE insensitivity: scores remain >0.9 across wide KL range (0.1–1.3).  
- Support fraction collapse: too few distinct bigrams observed, especially with nucleus sampling.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Oracle step‑wise evaluation:** Run all samplers (AR baseline, SEDD, MDLM, ReMDM‑conf, LLaDA) for *S* ∈ {8,16,32,64,128,256,512,1024}. Plot transition KL, NLL, entropy. Confirm all few‑step samplers show large KL and only converge when *S* → *T* (except LLaDA).  
2. **Temperature scaling in SEDD:** With oracle denoiser, sweep β ∈ {1,2,5,10,25} at low steps (e.g., *S*=32). Record transition KL, NLL, entropy, 3‑gram diversity, GenPPL, MAUVE. Demonstrate GenPPL decreases while KL and diversity drop.  
3. **ReMDM variants:** Compare ReMDM‑conf vs. ReMDM‑loop, with and without nucleus (*p*=0.9). For *S* ∈ {8,16,32,64,128,256,512,1024}, compute transition KL, NLL, entropy, support fraction, MAUVE. Show confidence‑based re‑masking yields lower KL, and MAUVE varies little despite large KL differences.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can we design discrete diffusion samplers that achieve distributional correctness with a small number of sampling steps? Current samplers require many steps to approximate the target Markov kernel while preserving parallel decoding.
- What evaluation metrics reliably capture transition‑level correctness for discrete diffusion models? Surface‑level metrics can be gamed and MAUVE is insensitive to transition deviations.
- Why does LLaDA exhibit severe entropy collapse and template repetition under an exact oracle denoiser, despite achieving low sequence NLL? The coupling between confidence‑based remasking and learned denoiser priors may break under oracle.
- What explains the superior performance of confidence‑based remasking (ReMDM‑conf) over loop‑based remasking (ReMDM‑loop) in matching the oracle transition kernel? The adaptive per‑position re‑masking likely interacts better with oracle confidence distributions and transition sparsity.

## Limitations
- Exact tokenizer (ByteBPE V=4096) and held‑out split size (100k) not fully specified; may affect bigram estimation.
- Noise schedules (σ(t), unmasking fractions) and LLaDA remasking details are assumed from code defaults; slight variations could alter convergence rates.
- The oracle prior uses first‑order Markov chain; long‑range dependencies in real text may be under‑represented.
- External metrics (GenPPL, MAUVE) rely on specific pretrained LMs (GPT‑2 Large) and binning; results might generalize differently with other models.

## Confidence
- **High:** Main isolation claim (oracle removes denoiser error, isolating sampler bias); few‑step sampler mismatch (high KL/TV at low *S*); LLaDA paradox (low NLL but high KL); ReMDM confidence superiority over loop.
- **Medium:** Aggregate metrics misleading (GenPPL/MAUVE not tracking KL) – depends on choice of external evaluator.

## Next Checks
1. **Higher‑order Markov prior:** Replace bigram *P*′ with trigram or a neural sequence model (e.g., GPT‑2) as oracle prior to test robustness of transition‑level conclusions.
2. **Cross‑dataset generalizability:** Repeat oracle evaluation on different corpora (e.g., Toronto Books Corpus, Wikipedia) to ensure findings are not OWT‑specific.
3. **Human evaluation alignment:** Collect human judgments on fluency/coherence for outputs with varying transition KL and correlate with transition metrics to verify they capture perceptual quality.
