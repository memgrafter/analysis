# From Steering to Pedalling: Do Autonomous Driving VLMs Generalize to Cyclist-Assistive Spatial Perception and Planning?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10771
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10771
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Generalist VLMs significantly outperform driving-specialized models on a new cyclist-assistive benchmark, with key weaknesses in spatial sign-lane association and temporal reasoning.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether vision-language models (VLMs) trained for autonomous driving can generalize to assistive perception and planning for cyclists. It introduces CyclingVQA, a benchmark with 2,009 multiple-choice questions derived from 695 egocentric cyclist images across eight tasks. Thirty-one VLMs are evaluated zero-shot. Results reveal that generalist models (e.g., Qwen3-VL-8B) consistently surpass driving-specialized ones (e.g., Dolphins, DriveMM). The best driving model achieves only 57.5% accuracy on spatial understanding versus 75.4% for the top generalist. Critical failure modes involve traffic rule misinterpretation and incorrect lane-sign associations. Notably, Chain-of-Thought prompting degrades instruct-model performance, and generation length does not predict accuracy. The study concludes that vehicle-centric training yields limited transfer to cyclist-centric scenarios.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct a zero-shot evaluation of VLMs on the CyclingVQA dataset, which comprises 695 egocentric cyclist images (∼3060×4080 pixels) annotated with traffic-sign bounding boxes, lane segmentations, and spatial cues. From these, 2,009 multiple-choice questions are generated across eight tasks: traffic sign grounding, spatial understanding, temporal ordering, lane recognition, referring expression detection, traffic sign recognition (with/without bounding boxes), and sign-action association. Some tasks include visual markers (bounding boxes, lane masks) overlaid on images. Models are prompted using task-specific templates (instruct, reasoning, or CoT) and their free-text responses are parsed by a separate Gemma2-9B model to extract a discrete choice. Accuracy per task and mean accuracy across tasks are reported, alongside analyses of CoT impact and generation verbosity.

## Key Results
- Generalist VLMs (Qwen3-VL-8B, Ovis2.5-9B) significantly outperform driving-specialized models across nearly all tasks.
- On spatial understanding, the best driving model scores 57.5% vs. 75.4% for the top generalist; lane-sign association and temporal ordering remain challenging (top accuracy ∼55%).
- Chain-of-Thought prompting degrades instruct-model performance by an average of 4.0 percentage points.
- Error analysis identifies four primary failure modes: traffic-rule misinterpretation, missed signs, lane-sign misassociation, and irrelevant sign attribution.
- There is no correlation between response length and accuracy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Driving-specialized VLMs underperform on cyclist-assistive tasks primarily due to training data domain bias, where models overfit to vehicle-centric visual patterns and traffic semantics.
**Mechanism:** Models trained on vehicle-mounted camera data (e.g., Waymo, nuScenes) internalize associations between road layouts, sign placements, and vehicle-specific rules. Cyclist egocentric views present a distribution shift—lower camera height, closer proximity to signs, bicycle-specific lanes/signs—that degrades perception accuracy.
**Core assumption:** Model performance is tightly coupled to the visual and semantic distribution of its training data; a narrow domain (vehicle-centric) limits transfer to related but distinct domains (cyclist-centric).
**Evidence anchors:**
- Abstract: "driving-specialized models underperform strong generalist VLMs, indicating limited transfer from vehicle-centric training to cyclist-assistive scenarios."
- Section 4.2: "driving-specialized models consistently lag behind strong generalist VLMs on CyclingVQA... specialist instruct models such as Dolphins (36.7%) and DriveMM (49.1%) perform substantially worse than generalist baselines."
- Related work context: Existing spatial QA benchmarks (e.g., NuScenes-SpatialQA) are vehicle-centric, highlighting a broader research gap.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** A fundamental limitation in fine-grained spatial association—specifically linking traffic signs to the correct navigable lane—is a primary failure mode for both specialist and generalist VLMs.
**Mechanism:** Tasks like Sign–Action Association and Lane Recognition require precise alignment between a sign's regulatory meaning, its physical location, and the cyclist's intended path. Models often mis-associate signs based on proximity to lane markers (e.g., sign pole location) rather than regulatory scope, revealing a lack of robust spatial reasoning about applicability zones.
**Core assumption:** VLMs treat traffic signs as generic objects without internalizing the deictic (frame-relative) nature of traffic rules—which lane a sign governs depends on the actor's position and direction.
**Evidence anchors:**
- Section 4.4: Failure mode "Lane–sign association errors... models may recognize all signs but fail to determine which applies to the cyclist’s trajectory."
- Tables 13 & 14: Qualitative examples showing models rely on physical sign pole location above a lane, not semantic rule.
- Abstract: "particularly in... associating signs with the correct navigational lanes."

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Instruction-tuned VLMs are more effective than explicit Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting for cyclist-assistive VQA, indicating current models lack robust internal deliberation for spatial-traffic reasoning.
**Mechanism:** CoT prompting, which encourages step-by-step reasoning, degrades performance on average (-4.0% for InternVL3.5-8B). This suggests that (a) the models' internal "fast" perception path is better calibrated than their generated reasoning trace, and (b) they may hallucinate or drift during open-ended reasoning, introducing errors not present in direct answer generation.
**Core assumption:** For narrow, well-defined VQA tasks (multiple-choice), the model's direct inference path is more reliable than a generated reasoning chain, which may introduce unfounded intermediate conclusions.
**Evidence anchors:**
- Section 4.3: "CoT prompting does not lead to consistent improvements and instead results in average accuracy drops... Overall, the predominantly negative deltas suggest that, for cycling-specific VQA, direct instruction-following is more reliable."
- Figure 4: Shows negative delta across all three instruct models tested.
- Section 4.5: "Generation verbosity does not necessarily translate to improved performance" – Cosmos-Reason1 (546 tokens) ranks 21st, while Qwen3-VL-8B (50 tokens) ranks 3rd.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Egocentric Perspective-Taking**
  - *Why needed here:* Cyclist-view images have fundamentally different geometry (lower height, closer objects, different field-of-view) than vehicle-mounted datasets. A model must adjust its spatial priors (e.g., size-distance relationships, lane widths) accordingly.
  - *Quick check question:* Given two identical traffic signs at different heights in an image, which is likely closer to a cyclist vs. a driver? (Answer: For cyclist, the lower sign is likely closer due to ground-proximity; vehicle view may see higher signs first).
- **Concept: Traffic Sign Regulatory Semantics**
  - *Why needed here:* Cyclist-specific rules (e.g., "mandatory cycle path" vs. "no cycling") use subtle visual distinctions (blue circle vs. red circle with line). Misinterpretation leads to unsafe action predictions (SAA task).
  - *Quick check question:* What is the action implication of a blue circle with a white bicycle vs. a red circle with a white bicycle and diagonal line? (Answer: Blue = mandatory/required lane; Red with line = prohibition).
- **Concept: Deictic Spatial Reasoning**
  - *Why needed here:* The "correct" lane for a cyclist depends on their current position and direction. A sign's meaning is not absolute but relative to the actor. This requires associating sign semantics with a specific lane label out of multiple options.
  - *Quick check question:* If a "bicycle-only" sign is physically located over Lane A, but the cyclist is currently in Lane B, which lane is mandated? (Answer: Typically the sign governs the lane it's positioned over, regardless of cyclist's current lane; but must also consider direction).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input (image + visual markers + text question) -> Vision Encoder (CLIP-ViT) -> Language Model Core (generative) -> Parser (Gemma2-9B) -> Output (choice + reasoning)
- **Critical path:** Image → Visual features → Textual question fusion → Token generation → Parser extraction → Accuracy calculation. **Failure points:** Vision encoder missing small signs; fusion module not aligning sign text with lane markers; LM misinterpreting sign semantics; parser failing on non-standard output.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Generalist vs. Specialist: Generalists have broader pretraining, aiding transfer; specialists have deeper driving tuning but suffer dataset narrowness.
  - Instruct vs. Reason: Instruct models are concise and accurate; reasoning models generate longer chains but are less reliable for this benchmark.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Traffic rule misinterpretation: Correct sign detection but wrong semantic label (e.g., "shared path" vs. "no cycling").
  - Sign detection failure: Missing a clearly annotated sign (low TSR accuracy).
  - Lane-sign misassociation: Correct sign and lanes identified but paired incorrectly.
  - Irrelevant sign attribution: Decision based on a peripheral sign while ignoring the governing one.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replication run: Evaluate Qwen3-VL-8B and Dolphins on full CyclingVQA to confirm generalist-specialist gap; track per-task accuracy.
  2. Ablation on visual markers: Run with vs. without bounding box/lane mask overlays to test if explicit visual supervision helps specialist models.
  3. Error mode quantification: Manually classify 100 random errors from top-5 models into the four failure modes to validate taxonomy and identify most frequent per model family.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can the CyclingVQA dataset be automatically scaled to larger sizes while preserving the quality of its fine-grained annotations (traffic signs, lane masks, spatial cues)?
- How can the geographical coverage of cyclist-centric benchmarks be expanded to capture diverse global cycling environments (e.g., rural roads, cities with informal traffic norms)?
- To what extent do variations in prompting (wording, format, inclusion of chain-of-thought) affect VLM performance on cyclist-assistive tasks, and what are the key factors driving sensitivity?
- Can Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models be effectively adapted for cyclist-assistive tasks, and how do they compare to VLMs in perception, reasoning, and action prediction for cycling scenarios?

## Limitations
- Dataset scope limited to 695 images from Munich, lacking diversity in weather, lighting, and global sign variations.
- Reliance on Gemma2-9B for response parsing introduces potential error; different parsers may alter rankings.
- Proprietary model results (GPT-5.1, Gemini-2.5-Flash) are not fully reproducible.
- Static multiple-choice format does not assess real-time planning or safety-critical outcomes.

## Confidence
- Driving-specialized VLMs underperform: **Medium** – Evidence shows a gap, but causality (data bias vs. architecture) is not isolated.
- Sign‑lane association is a key bottleneck: **High** – Low scores on SAA/LR and qualitative error analysis confirm this.
- CoT prompting degrades instruct‑model accuracy: **High** – Consistent negative Δ across multiple models supports reliability.
- Generalist superiority indicates data diversity matters: **Medium** – Supported by comparative results but without controlled data ablation.

## Next Checks
1. **Diversify data:** Evaluate on an expanded cyclist dataset with varied weather, lighting, and sign styles to test robustness.
2. **Marker ablation:** Compare model performance with vs. without visual overlays (bounding boxes, lane masks) to quantify reliance on explicit cues.
3. **Reasoning step audit:** Manually inspect intermediate CoT outputs of top instruct models to pinpoint error stages (e.g., sign detection, semantic parsing, lane assignment).
