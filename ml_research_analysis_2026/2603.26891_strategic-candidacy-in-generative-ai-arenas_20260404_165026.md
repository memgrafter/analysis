# Strategic Candidacy in Generative AI Arenas

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26891
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26891
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Cloning yields significant rank gains in status-quo Bradley-Terry arenas; You-Rank-We-Rank (YRWR) neutralizes this effect and improves ranking accuracy even with noisy producer rankings.

## Executive Summary
AI arena leaderboards that rank generative models based on pairwise user preferences are vulnerable to a strategic cloning attack: producers can submit multiple copies of their models to exploit finite-sample noise and artificially boost ranks. The authors propose the You-Rank-We-Rank (YRWR) mechanism, which requires producers to submit a ranking of their own models and corrects Bradley-Terry scores by setting each model’s score to the minimum among all models ranked higher by its producer. This simple correction makes the distribution of the top-ranked clone identical to that of a single model, thereby neutralizing cloning incentives. Theoretical analysis proves the status quo is not clone-robust, while YRWR is approximately clone-robust—each distinct model is an ε‑dominant strategy. An uncertainty‑aware variant (UA-YRWR) retains these properties even when producers misrank. Simulations calibrated to the Chatbot Arena platform confirm: cloning can improve rank by over seven positions under the status quo, but YRWR nearly eliminates such gains while consistently producing rankings much closer to the ground truth, reducing Kendall‑Tau distances by tens of swaps even with noisy producer rankings.

## Method Summary
The paper’s experimental evaluation simulates pairwise votes based on ground‑truth Bradley‑Terry scores derived from the Chatbot Arena platform. For each arena, a fixed number of comparisons per pair (s) is used to generate synthetic vote counts. The authors then compare the Status Quo (Bradley‑Terry MLE) and YRWR mechanisms under varying numbers of clones per model. Rankings are assessed against the ground truth using Kendall‑Tau distance and rank gain (difference between the top clone’s rank and the rank of the original model without clones). The procedure: (1) obtain ground‑truth model qualities and s per arena; (2) replicate selected models to create clones; (3) sample pairwise comparisons; (4) compute rankings under each mechanism; (5) aggregate metrics over many random draws. This setup quantifies resilience to cloning attacks and estimation accuracy.

## Key Results
- Cloning can improve rank by over seven positions under the status‑quo Bradley‑Terry mechanism.
- YRWR nearly eliminates such gains while producing rankings much closer to the ground truth.
- Kendall‑Tau distances are reduced by tens of swaps even when producer rankings are noisy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Status Quo Clone Exploitation
- **Claim:** The status‑quo (SQ) Bradley‑Terry MLE ranking is clone‑nonrobust; producers can improve expected rank by submitting multiple copies due to finite‑sample noise.
- **Mechanism:** Clones act as multiple independent draws from the noisy comparison process, increasing the chance that one clone obtains a high fitted BT score (“lottery ticket” effect). The negative impact of adding a new competitor is bounded.
- **Evidence:** Theorem 3.2 and simulations show >7 rank position gains in Chatbot Arena arenas.
- **Break condition:** Cloning provides no benefit if the producer’s best model has near‑certain or near‑zero win probability across all leaderboards, or if s is extremely large.

### You‑Rank‑We‑Rank (YRWR) Score Correction
- **Claim:** YRWR neutralizes clone benefits by making the top clone’s score distribution equal to that of a single model.
- **Mechanism:** For each model *j* by producer *i*, compute corrected score  
  `qR_j = min{ ˆR_k : k ∈ M_i and π_i(k) < π_i(j) }`.  
  This enforces that a model’s score cannot exceed any higher‑ranked model by the same producer.
- **Evidence:** Theorem 4.1 proves approximate clone‑robustness; Proposition 4.2 shows accuracy improvement under truthful π_i; simulations confirm near‑elimination of rank gains and lower Kendall‑Tau distances.
- **Break condition:** The benefit holds only if producer rankings are not too noisy; highly inaccurate π_i may degrade accuracy.

### Uncertainty‑Aware YRWR (UA‑YRWR)
- **Claim:** UA‑YRWR maintains approximate clone‑robustness and asymptotic accuracy without requiring truthful producer rankings.
- **Mechanism:** Compute corrected score `qR_j^{UA}` as: if the confidence interval of ˆR_j overlaps with that of any higher‑ranked model (by π_i), then `qR_j = min{ ˆR_k }` among overlapping higher‑ranked models; else keep `ˆR_j`.
- **Evidence:** Corollary 4.6 extends clone‑robustness; Proposition 4.7 shows consistency; simulations indicate robustness to misranking.
- **Break condition:** Relies on well‑calibrated simultaneous confidence intervals; miscalibration (e.g., too narrow) may revert to YRWR sensitivity, while overly wide intervals collapse to SQ.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bradley‑Terry Model**  
  *Why needed:* Underlies both SQ and YRWR; defines how pairwise votes relate to latent model qualities `R_j`.  
  *Quick check:* If `R_A = 1.0` and `R_B = 0.5`, the probability a random voter prefers A is `e¹/(e¹+e⁰·⁵) ≈ 0.73`.
- **Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) for BT**  
  *Why needed:* SQ uses BT‑MLE on vote counts to estimate `ˆR`; YRWR uses these `ˆR` as input for correction.  
  *Quick check:* Why is an identifiability constraint (e.g., `∑R̂_j = 0`) necessary? (Answer: BT likelihood is invariant under adding a constant to all `R_j`; the constraint fixes the scale.)
- **Isotonic Regression / Monotonicity Enforcement**  
  *Why needed:* YRWR enforces that within a producer’s model set, corrected scores `qR` respect the producer’s ranking `π_i`. This is a coordinate‑wise minimum operation, a form of isotonic projection.  
  *Quick check:* With `π_i: A > B > C` and `R̂ = [0.2, 0.5, 0.1]`, YRWR yields `qR_A = 0.2`, `qR_B = 0.2`, `qR_C = 0.1`.
- **Leaderboard Utility Model**  
  *Why needed:* Producers care about winning specific subsets (leaderboards) with weights `ν_i(L)`, not just overall rank; explains why clones may be submitted for niche leaderboards.  
  *Quick check:* If a producer assigns weight `ν_i(L_all)=0.1` to the “all models” leaderboard and `ν_i(L_open)=0.9` to “open‑weight”, and their best open‑weight model is cloneable, why might they still clone? (Answer: The high weight on the open‑weight leaderboard makes it valuable to secure a top rank there, even via clones.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input: models *M* (with producer IDs), pairwise vote counts `v_{j→j'}`, and producer rankings `π_i`.  
  Common step: compute Bradley‑Terry MLE → baseline scores `ˆR`.  
  Status Quo path: sort by `ˆR` → output ranking `σ`.  
  YRWR path: for each producer *i* and each model *j*, compute `qR_j = min{ ˆR_k : k ∈ M_i and π_i(k) < π_i(j) }`; sort by `qR` → `σ`.  
  UA‑YRWR path: for each *j*, if `ˆR_j`’s confidence interval overlaps any higher‑ranked model’s interval, apply the min‑score correction; else keep `ˆR_j`; sort → `σ`.
- **Critical path:** The YRWR mechanism’s clone‑robustness hinges on the within‑producer score correction step. It must iterate over each producer’s models in the order of `π_i`, propagating the minimum `ˆR` downstream. Any indexing or min‑computation error breaks the guarantee.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Accuracy vs. robustness:* YRWR improves accuracy when `π_i` is truthful but can degrade if `π_i` is noisy; UA‑YRWR trades some accuracy (via overlap threshold) for robustness against misranking.  
  - *Implementation complexity:* UA‑YRWR requires simultaneous confidence intervals for all models, which is computationally heavier than YRWR or SQ.  
  - *Metadata dependency:* Both YRWR and UA‑YRWR rely on accurate producer identification and correct `π_i`; flawed metadata undermines the mechanism.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *SQ:* In repeated simulations with fixed ground truth and s, a producer’s top clone rank is consistently higher than the rank without clones.  
  - *YRWR with noisy `π_i`:* When producer rankings are perturbed, the producer’s utility (e.g., rank of best model) decreases compared to truthful reporting, indicating sensitivity.  
  - *UA‑YRWR:* If confidence intervals are artificially narrowed, the mechanism behaves like YRWR and shows sensitivity to `π_i` noise; if overly widened, it collapses to SQ and becomes vulnerable to cloning.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. *Validate clone benefit under SQ:* Use Arena‑like ground truth and s; for a model that is *(ε,δ)*-competitive, vary number of clones and s; measure probability that top clone ranks above a threshold (e.g., top‑5). Expect increasing benefit with more clones and smaller s.  
  2. *Test YRWR against optimal cloning strategy:* Given a producer with *k* distinct models, search over all possible clone allocations (subject to total model limit) to find maximum utility; compare to utility from submitting each model once. Expect improvement < *O(1/s)*.  
  3. *Assess accuracy under producer misranking:* Perturb truthful `π_i` by swapping adjacent ranks with probability `p_noise`; compute Kendall‑Tau distance between output ranking and true `R` for SQ, YRWR, UA‑YRWR. Expect YRWR degrades with `p_noise`, UA‑YRWR resists until `p_noise` is high.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Online extension:** How can YRWR be extended to a setting where votes and model submissions arrive sequentially? (Section 5 notes that naive extension may allow adaptive clone attacks, e.g., submitting clones, observing performance, then inserting a new clone above all previous ones.)
- **Black‑box clone detection:** Can practical methods detect clones using only black‑box API access, and how might they be integrated with ranking mechanisms? (YRWR obviates detection, but black‑box detection could enable alternative or hybrid mechanisms.)
- **Non‑uniform vote allocation:** How does non‑uniform allocation of pairwise votes across model pairs affect clone‑robustness and ranking accuracy? (Theory assumes uniform *s*; non‑uniformity may change the variance of BT estimates and the “new competitor” effect.)
- **Utility over multiple top positions:** What ranking mechanisms are clone‑robust when producer utilities depend on multiple top positions (e.g., scores for the top *d* ranks) rather than only first place? (Current utility model assumes producers win only if ranked first; smoothing over top positions may alter cloning incentives.)

## Limitations
- Exact arena parameters (per‑pair vote count *s*, model roster) are not tabulated in the paper; faithful reproduction depends on the authors’ code or scraping.
- Theoretical guarantees assume a homogeneous Bradley‑Terry model with fixed *s* per pair; accuracy results for YRWR further require truthful producer rankings, and UA‑YRWR relies on well‑calibrated simultaneous confidence intervals.
- Core mechanisms are novel with limited prior evidence; real‑world deployment may face practical challenges (e.g., noisy producer rankings, non‑uniform voting, or adversarial metadata).

- **YRWR approximate clone‑robustness:** High  
- **YRWR accuracy gains (under truthful π_i):** Medium
