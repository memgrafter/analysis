# Is Mathematical Problem-Solving Expertise in Large Language Models Associated with Assessment Performance?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.25633
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25633
- **Reference Count**: 22
- **Primary Result**: Assessment accuracy is substantially higher on items the model solved correctly than incorrectly (34.1–45.9 percentage points, p<0.001), but assessment remains much harder than solving (solve–assess gaps up to 86.0 points, F1 8.9%–17.5%).

## Executive Summary
This paper explores whether an LLM’s ability to solve mathematical problems is linked to its ability to assess step-level correctness in provided solution traces. The authors evaluated GPT‑4 and GPT‑5 on the GSM8K and MATH subsets of PROCESSBENCH, having each model independently solve problems and then assess gold‑standard solution traces. Results reveal a strong within‑model association: models assess correctly far more often on problems they solved correctly. However, step‑level assessment is markedly more difficult than solving, as shown by large solve‑assess gaps and very low F1 scores, particularly on error‑present items. The findings suggest that while problem‑solving skill supports assessment, reliable error diagnosis requires additional capabilities such as precise error localization.

## Method Summary
The study used PROCESSBENCH, containing 800 math problems (400 from GSM8K, 400 from MATH). For each problem, the model performed two zero‑shot tasks: solving (generating step‑by‑step reasoning and a final answer) and assessing (given the problem and a benchmark solution trace, identifying the earliest erroneous step or returning -1 if none). Solving performance was measured by exact match to the gold answer; assessment performance used exact match to the gold step index, plus accuracy on error‑present and no‑error subsets and F1. The association between solving and assessment was examined by comparing assessment accuracy on items the model solved correctly versus incorrectly, using Fisher’s exact test and computing the solve‑assess gap (solving accuracy minus assessment F1). Each experiment was repeated three times to obtain mean results.

## Key Results
- **Strong within‑model association**: Assessment accuracy was 34.1–45.9 percentage points higher on self‑solved‑correct items than on self‑solved‑incorrect items (p<0.001) across both models and datasets.
- **Assessment is much harder**: Solve‑assess gaps reached 86.0 pp (GPT‑4 on GSM8K); overall F1 scores were only 8.9%–17.5%, driven by abysmal error‑present accuracy (4.7%–10.0%).
- **Asymmetry between solving and assessment**: Models sometimes assessed correctly despite solving incorrectly, and vice versa, indicating that assessment draws on partially distinct capabilities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Overlapping Reasoning Capabilities
Successfully solving a problem implies the model has constructed a valid reasoning chain, which provides a reference for evaluating another trace. This shared internal representation leads to higher assessment accuracy on items the model solved correctly. Evidence includes the consistent within‑model accuracy gap and the correlation observed in neighboring research on LLM reasoning errors. If future studies on diverse models fail to show this association, the mechanism would be weakened.

### Mechanism 2: Inherent Difficulty of Step‑Level Localization
Assessing requires monitoring a given reasoning sequence, comparing each step to an implicit correct model, and pinpointing the first deviation—a finer‑grained task than generating a correct answer. The substantial solve‑assess gaps and extremely low error‑present F1 quantify this added cognitive load. Should assessment F1 on error‑present items converge toward solving accuracy for capable models, the difficulty claim would be mitigated.

### Mechanism 3: Asymmetry of Skill Transfer
The relationship is not perfectly symmetric: correct solving does not guarantee correct assessment (models may mislocalize errors), and incorrect solving does not always preclude correct assessment (models may still recognize valid or invalid structures via independent heuristics). This asymmetry suggests that assessment relies on separable sub‑skills, such as step alignment and local consistency checking, that are not wholly transferred from generative problem‑solving. The asymmetry would be falsified if all four outcome categories showed perfect dependence (e.g., 100% of solve‑correct items also assess‑correct).

## Foundational Learning
- **Object‑level vs. Meta‑level Monitoring (Nelson & Narens framework)**  
  *Why needed*: Distinguishes problem‑solving (object‑level reasoning) from assessment (meta‑level monitoring of a reasoning trace), framing them as related but distinct capabilities.  
  *Quick check*: Which task is meta‑level monitoring? Assessment, because it evaluates the reasoning process itself rather than producing an answer.

- **Step‑Level Exact‑Match Metric**  
  *Why needed*: Provides a strict, discrete measure of diagnostic precision by requiring an exact match with the human‑labeled step index.  
  *Quick check*: Why would identifying the correct error type on the wrong step still be incorrect? Because the metric emphasizes precise localization, not just error detection.

- **F1 Score for Error‑Sensitive Assessment**  
  *Why needed*: Balances performance on error‑present and no‑error items; prevents inflation from high no‑error accuracy and reveals bottlenecks in error detection.  
  *Quick check*: What does a low F1 (e.g., 8.9%) hide that accuracy might not? It shows failure on one sub‑task—here, almost always failing to detect errors in error‑present solutions—despite good performance on the other.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Input (Problem Text, Optional: Solution Trace) → Single LLM (with system prompt: “You are an expert math tutor”) → Task Branch 1 (Solve): outputs (Reasoning Steps, Final Answer); Task Branch 2 (Assess): outputs (Earliest Error Step Index, Rationale) → Evaluation (compare to gold labels).

**Critical Path**:  
1. Load dataset item.  
2. Execute Solve task, store outcome (correct/incorrect).  
3. Independently execute Assess task on the same item with benchmark trace.  
4. Group assessment results by stored solve outcome.  
5. Compute metrics (χ²/Fisher, accuracy differences, solve‑assess gap, F1).

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- *Same model vs. separate models*: Using the same model isolates the capability link; separate models could disentangle whether a skill gap is inherent or task‑specific.  
- *Exact‑match vs. softer metrics*: Exact‑match is strict and reproducible but penalizes semantically correct but mislocalized critiques.  
- *Benchmark traces vs. authentic student work*: Curated traces ensure clean labels but may not reflect noisy, diverse student errors.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Very high no‑error accuracy (>90%) coupled with extremely low error‑present accuracy (<10%): model can confirm correctness but cannot localize errors.  
- Large within‑model gap between assessment accuracy on solved‑correct vs. solved‑incorrect items: confirms association but also shows assessment failure even when solving succeeds.  
- Cases where solving correctly leads to assessment incorrectly: indicates failure in step‑level monitoring/localization.

**First 3 Experiments**:  
1. **Cross‑model replication**: Repeat the protocol with a non‑OpenAI model (e.g., Claude 3.5 Sonnet) on PROCESSBENCH GSM8K to test if the within‑model association persists.  
2. **Failure mode taxonomy**: Systematically analyze “solve correct, assess incorrect” cases to quantify whether errors stem from mislocalization, wrong error‑type judgment, or hallucination.  
3. **Boundary test on no‑error items**: Run assessment only on items with fully correct benchmark traces; if accuracy approaches or exceeds solving accuracy, it isolates the sub‑skill of monitoring a valid process.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalizability to other models and datasets**: Does the within‑model association hold for a wider range of LLMs (e.g., open‑weight, smaller) and beyond GSM8K/MATH? The current findings are limited to GPT‑4/5 and curated subsets.  
2. **Assessment of authentic student work**: How does LLM assessment performance compare when evaluating real, noisy student solution traces versus the clean benchmark traces? The benchmark may not capture the variability of actual student misconceptions.  
3. **Alternative evaluation metrics**: Can the strict exact‑match criterion be complemented with graded or partial‑credit metrics that reward semantically correct localization even if the exact step index is off? The current metric may undercount practically useful feedback.  
4. **Confounding by item difficulty**: Is the observed association driven merely by shared item difficulty (harder items yield lower solve and assess rates), or does successful solving provide specific diagnostic insight for that item? The study design correlates tasks on the same items but cannot disentangle difficulty effects.  
5. **Specific capabilities underlying asymmetry**: What capabilities (e.g., step‑by‑step consistency checking, counterfactual reasoning) enable models to sometimes assess correctly despite solving incorrectly, and vice versa? The exact nature of these separable sub‑skills remains unclear.

## Limitations
- The study evaluated only two proprietary models (GPT‑4, GPT‑5), limiting generalizability to other architectures.
- Exact system prompts were unreported, introducing potential prompt‑sensitivity effects.
- PROCESSBENCH’s curated solution traces may not reflect the diversity and messiness of authentic student errors; the exact‑match metric may penalize partially correct diagnoses.
- Findings are confined to two mathematics datasets; applicability to other domains remains unknown.

## Confidence
| Claim | Confidence |
|-------|------------|
| Within‑model solve‑assess association | High |
| Assessment fundamentally harder than solving | High |
| Asymmetry between solving and assessment capabilities | Medium |

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑model replication**: Validate the association using a non‑OpenAI model (e.g., Claude 3.5 Sonnet) on PROCESSBENCH GSM8K.
2. **Failure mode taxonomy**: Quantify the dominant failure modes in “solve correct, assess incorrect” cases (mislocalization, error‑type misclassification, hallucination).
3. **Isolate meta‑monitoring**: Run assessment only on items with fully correct benchmark traces; check if accuracy matches or exceeds solving accuracy, confirming separability of monitoring from error localization.
