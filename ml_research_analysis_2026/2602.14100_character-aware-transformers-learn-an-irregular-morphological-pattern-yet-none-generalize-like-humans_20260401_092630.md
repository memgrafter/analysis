# Character-aware Transformers Learn an Irregular Morphological Pattern Yet None Generalize Like Humans

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14100
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14100
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Position-invariant transformers acquire the Spanish L-shaped morphome under data scarcity but fail to generalize it productively to novel stems, diverging from human wug-test behavior.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether encoder-decoder transformers can learn the Spanish L-shaped morphome—an irregular pattern where the first-person singular present indicative stem is identical to all present subjunctive stems—and generalize it productively. Five model variants were compared, differing in their treatment of morphosyntactic tags: sequential vs. position-invariant positional encoding, and atomic vs. decomposed tag representations. Position-invariant models successfully recovered the correct paradigm clustering even when L-shaped verbs were rare (10% of training data), whereas sequential models only partially captured the pattern. However, none of the models productively generalized the L-shaped rule to novel nonce verb stems. On nonce verbs, position-invariant models showed a mood-based generalization (high accuracy in subjunctive cells, near-zero in 1sg indicative) while humans preferentially generalized to 1sg indicative. Models achieved higher overall nonce accuracy than humans (43–49% vs. 33%) but with a qualitatively different pattern, revealing a gap between statistical pattern learning and human-like morphological abstraction.

## Method Summary
The study used Spanish present tense verb paradigms (12 cells) from the Unimorph schema, with real verbs split into L-shaped (299) and non-L-shaped (4,859). Training conditions varied the proportion of L-shaped verbs (10%, 50%, 90%). Models were character-level encoder-decoder transformers (4-layer, 4-head, d=256) trained to generate a target inflected form given two source (form, tag) pairs. The five architectures manipulated two factors: (1) positional encoding for tags—sequential (tags receive sequential positions) vs. position-invariant (tags fixed at index 0); (2) tag representation—atomic (single token) vs. decomposed (one-hot or feature-geometric vectors). Evaluation on held-out real verbs measured sequence and stem accuracy, and paradigm shape via k-means clustering of per-cell stem accuracies. Generalization was tested on 15 nonce verbs from a prior wug study, predicting three cells (1sg.ind, 2sg.sbjv, 3sg.sbjv) and comparing per-cell accuracy to human data.

## Key Results
- Position-invariant models robustly learned the L-shaped clustering under low-frequency conditions (10%L), while sequential models failed or showed weaker separation.
- No model productively generalized the L-shaped pattern to nonce verbs; position-invariant models showed high subjunctive accuracy but near-zero 1sg indicative accuracy, opposite to the human bias toward 1sg indicative.
- Models outperformed humans in overall nonce verb accuracy (43–49% vs. 33%) but diverged in the distribution of errors, highlighting a qualitative difference in generalization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Position-invariant positional encoding enables acquisition of scarce morphomic patterns
- **Claim:** Position-invariant positional encoding for morphosyntactic tags allows models to treat features as unordered bundles, which is critical for acquiring the L-shaped morphome when such patterns are scarce in training data.
- **Mechanism:** Tags are embedded with a fixed positional index (e.g., index 0) while characters retain sequential positional encoding. This prevents the model from conflating feature order with feature semantics, aligning with the Unimorph schema’s view of feature bundles as inherently unordered.
- **Core assumption:** Morphosyntactic features are semantically independent of their position in the input sequence; their meaning is defined by their identities, not their order.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Positional encoding proves decisive: position-invariant models recover the correct L-shaped paradigm clustering even when L-shaped verbs are scarce, whereas sequential models only partially capture the pattern. [section 3.3.2] The UniMorph schema treats morphosyntactic features as unordered bundles. [section 4.1.3] In the 10%L condition, all three position-invariant models correctly cluster 1sg.ind with all subjunctive cells, while sequential models miscluster or show weaker differentiation.
- **Break condition:** Under 10%L, sequential models fail to achieve correct clustering (e.g., misclustering 1pl.sbjv and 2pl.sbjv with non-L indicative forms).

### Mechanism 2: Models rely on distributional frequency, not abstract structural knowledge, for generalization
- **Claim:** Models learn correlations between surface forms and feature bundles from training data and extrapolate based on frequency-driven patterns (e.g., mood-based generalization) rather than applying the L-shaped rule to novel stems, unlike humans.
- **Mechanism:** When faced with nonce verbs, models generalize based on learned statistical tendencies (e.g., shared mood contexts) instead of abstracting a paradigm-structuring principle (1sg.ind = all subjunctive). This yields a mood-based split (high subjunctive, low 1sg.ind) versus the human preference for 1sg.ind.
- **Core assumption:** Human-like morphological abstraction requires extracting and applying a principle to completely novel stems, independent of training frequency.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] None of the models productively generalize the L-shaped pattern; position-invariant models generalize across subjunctive cells but fail on 1sg.ind, producing a mood-based generalization. [section 4.2.2] Class 2 models show high subjunctive accuracy (79–88%) but near-zero 1sg.ind accuracy (0–4%) at 10%L, opposite to human data. [section 5] No model reproduces the human pattern, highlighting the gap between statistical reproduction and morphological abstraction.
- **Break condition:** On nonce verbs, models exhibit either a mood-based split (high subjunctive, low 1sg.ind) or uniformly low accuracy, rather than the human-preferred 1sg.ind bias.

### Mechanism 3: Positional invariance acts as an inductive bias mitigating data sparsity
- **Claim:** Positional invariance provides an inductive bias that reduces the sample complexity of learning the L-shape, allowing robust acquisition even from limited examples.
- **Mechanism:** By removing positional constraints on feature tags, the model’s attention can more easily detect cross-mood stem identity without disentangling feature order from meaning.
- **Core assumption:** Architectural inductive biases can compensate for data sparsity in learning abstract morphological patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Positional encoding proves decisive: position-invariant models recover correct clustering even when L-shaped verbs are scarce. [section 4.1.3] Under 10%L, all position-invariant models achieve correct clustering while sequential models do not. [section 5] Position-invariant encoding acts as an inductive bias that compensates for data sparsity.
- **Break condition:** Sequential models fail to achieve correct L-shape clustering under low-frequency conditions (10%L).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input sequence (characters of two source forms + their tags + target tag) → Token embeddings (character embeddings + tag embeddings) → Positional encoding layer (sequential for characters, fixed index 0 for tags in Class 2) → Transformer encoder-decoder (4-layer, 4-head) → Character-wise output distribution → Generated target form.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Preprocess data: For each lemma, generate all (source1, tag1), (source2, tag2) → target cell triples; subsample 25% of paradigms.  
  2. Split lemmas into train/dev/test (70/10/20) maintaining L/NL ratio per frequency condition.  
  3. Build encoder-decoder transformer backbone; implement Class 1 (sequential PE for all tokens) or Class 2 (fixed index 0 for tags) with atomic or decomposed tag representations.  
  4. Train with Adam (lr=0.001), linear warmup 4k steps, batch 400, dropout 0.3, label smoothing 0.1, max 10k steps.  
  5. Evaluate on held-out real verbs: sequence accuracy, stem accuracy, k-means clustering of per-cell stem accuracies (k=2).  
  6. Evaluate on nonce verbs: for 15 verbs × 3 cells × 2 source orderings, compute per-cell stem accuracy and compare to human data.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Sequential vs. position-invariant PE: Sequential is simpler but conflates feature order with meaning, harming unordered bundle learning. Position-invariant better captures feature semantics but requires careful mixed-token handling.  
  - Atomic vs. decomposed tags: Atomic reduces input dimensionality but forces implicit feature structure learning; decomposed provides explicit structure but increases input size and may need more data.  
  - Frequency vs. generalization: All models are frequency-sensitive; position-invariant mitigates but does not eliminate this tradeoff.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Real verbs (10%L):** Low L-shaped stem accuracy (<60%) and incorrect clustering (1sg.ind not grouped with all subjunctive cells).  
  - **Nonce verbs:** Near-zero 1sg.ind accuracy with high subjunctive accuracy (mood-based split) signals non-human generalization; uniformly low accuracy suggests no pattern acquisition.  
  - **Training instability:** High variance across seeds in L-shaped accuracy under low-frequency conditions, especially for sequential models.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline comparison:** Train all five architectures on 10%L condition; evaluate sequence and stem accuracy on real test verbs. Expect position-invariant models to show higher L-shaped stem accuracy and lower variance.  
  2. **Paradigm shape analysis:** For 10%L, compute per-cell stem accuracy and perform k-means clustering (k=2). Check if 1sg.ind clusters with all subjunctive cells. Expect correct clustering only for position-invariant models.  
  3. **Nonce verb generalization:** Evaluate all models on the 15 nonce verbs (3 cells each). Compare per-cell accuracy to human data. Expect position-invariant models to show mood-based split, sequential models to show uniform accuracy, and both to diverge from human pattern (higher 1sg.ind).

## Limitations
- **Dataset accessibility:** Exact Spanish verb dataset and IPA transcription of nonce stems from Nevins et al. (2015) are unavailable; reconstruction introduces potential mismatches in verb frequency distributions and nonce alternation patterns.
- **Implementation specifics:** Critical details of architecture integration (e.g., exact input sequence format, handling of fixed positional index 0) are omitted, risking variation in model behavior.
- **Nonce protocol:** Exact inference procedure for nonce verbs (order and combination of source forms) is unclear, which could affect the apparent model-human divergence.
