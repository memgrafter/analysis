# A Hierarchical Sheaf Spectral Embedding Framework for Single-Cell RNA-seq Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26858
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26858
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** HSSE achieves average Macro-F1 0.958 and Macro-Recall 0.954 across 12 scRNA-seq datasets, outperforming MDG and classical methods.

## Executive Summary
Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data often reflect complex, multi-scale biological organization, yet standard dimensionality reduction techniques such as PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE rely on a single neighborhood scale. This limitation can obscure hierarchical relationships among cell types or states. We propose Hierarchical Sheaf Spectral Embedding (HSSE), a method that constructs local cellular sheaves across multiple neighborhood sizes and embedding scales. By computing persistent sheaf Laplacians and aggregating their spectral statistics, HSSE derives robust features for downstream tasks.

In extensive experiments on 12 scRNA-seq datasets, HSSE achieves an average Macro-F1 score of 0.958 and Macro-Recall of 0.954, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art (MDG, Macro-F1 0.947) and classical methods by 1-4%. Ablations confirm the importance of multi-scale aggregation and sheaf-based encoding. HSSE thus offers an effective framework for capturing hierarchical structure in single-cell data.

## Method Summary
HSSE processes a gene expression matrix (cells × genes) in several stages. First, it generates multiple low-dimensional embeddings using UMAP with varied neighborhood parameters (five scales). For each embedding, pairwise distances are computed. Then, for every cell and each neighborhood size k (five sizes from 5 to 80), a local Rips complex (up to dimension 2) is constructed from the k-nearest neighbors. On this complex, a 1‑dimensional cellular sheaf is defined; restriction maps along edges incorporate a Gaussian kernel based on inter‑cell distance and optionally a label modulation term. Next, the distance interval [0, r_max] is uniformly partitioned into L segments. For each interval [a, b], the (a, b)‑persistent sheaf Laplacian is computed and its eigenvalues are summarized by statistics (sum, mean, max, min, standard deviation). These spectral descriptors, aggregated across all scales, neighborhood sizes, and intervals, form a feature vector per cell. Finally, a gradient boosting classifier (GBDT) is trained on these features for cell type classification.

## Key Results
- HSSE attains an average Macro-F1 of 0.958 across 12 scRNA‑seq datasets, exceeding MDG (0.947) and classical methods.
- Macro-Recall averages 0.954; Macro-AUC reaches 0.992, demonstrating strong class‑balanced performance.
- Ablations reveal that incorporating multiple neighborhood sizes (1–10) and embedding scales (1–5) yields significant gains, with performance plateauing after a few scales.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Hierarchical biological structure in single-cell RNA-seq data is captured by aggregating neighborhoods at multiple scales from scale-dependent low-dimensional embeddings, overcoming the limitation of fixed-neighborhood methods.
- **Mechanism:** Generate a family of embeddings \(\Phi(s)\) (e.g., UMAP with varying neighborhood parameters) to obtain diverse geometric views. For each embedding, compute pairwise distances and construct cell-centered neighborhoods of various sizes \(k\). This yields a set of local complexes at multiple resolutions.
- **Core assumption:** Cellular heterogeneity manifests as hierarchical clusters or continuums that become visible when analyzing data at different neighbor-scales.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Single-cell RNA-seq data exhibit hierarchical structures across scales, but common embeddings (PCA, UMAP, t-SNE) use fixed neighborhoods, limiting multi-scale capture."
  - [section 1] "Nonlinear embedding methods such as UMAP and tSNE typically rely on a single neighborhood resolution, which may limit their ability to capture hierarchical biological structure."
  - [corpus] The neighboring corpus includes multiscale manifold learning works (e.g., "Multiscale Grassmann Manifolds...") but no direct evidence for this specific multi-scale sheaf combination.
- **Break condition:** If the chosen scale parameters \(\mathcal{S}\) do not span the relevant scales, or if the data lack significant hierarchical organization, the multi-scale aggregation may not improve over single-scale baselines.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Encoding relationships within each local neighborhood via a cellular sheaf with interpretable restriction maps yields spectral features that integrate geometry and optional label information, providing richer descriptors than purely geometric graph Laplacians.
- **Mechanism:** For each cell-centered simplicial complex (Rips complex from distances), assign a one-dimensional stalk to every simplex. Restriction maps on codimension‑1 faces multiply by a kernel \(k(d)=\exp(-d^2/\eta^2)\) and optionally a label modulation \(\exp(-\alpha m(u,v))\); higher‑codimension maps are defined by composition. The resulting sheaf Laplacian captures local connectivity and compatibility.
- **Core assumption:** Sheaf-theoretic local‑to‑global consistency, together with data‑driven restriction maps, encodes relational patterns that are predictive of cell type and robust to rescaling.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "For each local neighborhood, we construct a data‑driven cellular sheaf that encodes local relationships among cells."
  - [section 3.3] Details the cell‑centered sheaf construction, restriction maps, and Proposition 3.1 (sheaf consistency).
  - [section 3.4] Proposition 3.2 (scaling invariance) shows that spectral directions are unaffected by uniform rescaling of restriction maps.
- **Break condition:** If the kernel scale \(\eta\) is poorly estimated (e.g., due to noisy distances), or if the binary label scheme fails to capture relevant distinctions, the sheaf may not reflect true biological relationships. The method also depends on having sufficient simplices (edges/triangles) in the local complex.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Persistent sheaf Laplacian spectra computed over a distance‑based filtration provide multi‑scale summaries of local relational structure; their statistical aggregates (sum, mean, max, min, std) form discriminative features for classification.
- **Mechanism:** From the local Rips complex, define a filtration by increasing distance thresholds. For each interval \([a,b]\) in a set of uniformly sampled intervals, compute the \((a,b)\)-persistent sheaf Laplacian \(L_r(a,b)\) and its eigenvalues. Concatenate summary statistics of these eigenvalues across all intervals, scales, and neighborhood sizes to produce the final cell feature vector.
- **Core assumption:** The evolution of sheaf Laplacian eigenvalues as the filtration grows encodes how local connectivity changes with scale, and the chosen statistics retain the predictive signal.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.4] Defines persistent sheaf Laplacians and their spectral properties.
  - [section 3.2] Describes distance‑induced filtrations and interval sampling; Algorithm 1 step 17 computes PSL spectra.
  - [section 4.2] Shows that HSSE outperforms baselines; the ablation (Section 4.3) indicates that using multiple scales and neighborhood sizes improves performance, consistent with the multi‑scale spectral aggregation.
- **Break condition:** If the filtration intervals are too sparse or do not cover the range where meaningful topological changes occur, key spectral features may be missed. Truncating the simplicial complex dimension (e.g., max dim = 2) might discard higher‑order information that could be important for some datasets.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input → Scale‑dependent embeddings (UMAP) → Distance matrices → Local neighborhoods & Rips complexes → Sheaf construction (1‑D stalks, restriction maps) → Persistent sheaf Laplacian (filtration intervals) → Spectral statistics (sum, mean, max, min, std) → Feature aggregation → Classifier (GBDT).

- **Critical path:** The sequential flow is: *embeddings → distances → neighborhoods/sheaves → PSL → aggregation*. Parallelization is possible across cells, scales, and neighborhood sizes. The main computational bottleneck is constructing many small Rips complexes and computing sheaf Laplacians; memory usage grows with \(|\mathcal{S}| \times |\mathcal{K}|\) but each complex is bounded by \(k\) vertices.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Number of scales \(|\mathcal{S}|\)**: More scales capture broader multi‑scale information but linearly increase feature dimension and computation.  
  - **Number of neighborhood sizes \(|\mathcal{K}|\)**: Similarly, more sizes provide richer context at the cost of features and time.  
  - **Filtration intervals \(L\)**: Finer sampling yields more detailed spectral evolution but adds features; the paper uses uniformly spaced intervals.  
  - **Max simplex dimension**: Limiting to dim 2 (edges/triangles) reduces cost; higher dimensions may help but increase complexity.  
  - **Sheaf label scheme**: The binary center / neighbor labeling is simple; incorporating more biological priors could improve performance but requires additional labels.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Performance collapse** across datasets (e.g., below PCA) suggests poor parameter choices (wrong \(\eta\), \(\alpha\), or insufficient scales/sizes).  
  - **High variance** across random seeds indicates instability, possibly due to very small neighborhoods or noisy distances.  
  - **Memory errors** when \(k\) or dataset size is large; remedy by reducing \(|\mathcal{K}|\) or truncating complex dimension.  
  - **Overfitting** on small datasets if feature dimension explodes; may need feature selection or regularization.  
  - **Slow runtime** if all combinations are naively computed; leverage parallelization over cells and scales.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Benchmark classification:** Compare HSSE against MDG, PCA, UMAP, NMF, t‑SNE on 12 scRNA‑seq datasets using GBDT with 5‑fold CV. HSSE achieves average Macro‑F1 = 0.958 and Macro‑Recall = 0.954, outperforming MDG (0.947, 0.947) and classical methods.  
  2. **Ablation on \(|\mathcal{K}|\):** Fix \(\mathcal{S}\), vary number of neighborhood sizes from 1 to 10. Performance improves rapidly at small \(|\mathcal{K}|\) and plateaus, showing benefit of multi‑scale aggregation.  
  3. **Ablation on \(|\mathcal{S}|\):** Fix \(\mathcal{K}\), vary number of scales from 1 to 5. Using multiple scales consistently beats single‑scale; gains saturate after a few scales, indicating robustness to the exact scale count.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What alternative sheaf constructions (e.g., different restriction maps or stalk structures) can better capture complex biological relationships in single‑cell data beyond the current binary labeling and geometric kernels?
- **Basis:** [explicit] The conclusion states that future work will explore "alternative sheaf constructions."
- **Why unresolved:** The current sheaf uses a simplistic binary center/neighbor label and a Gaussian kernel. Biological contexts (e.g., cell type hierarchies, signaling pathways) likely require more nuanced, domain‑informed restriction maps to encode meaningful relationships.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic benchmarking of sheaf designs that incorporate biological priors (e.g., gene set similarity, pathway membership) across multiple datasets, with performance gains assessed against biological interpretability metrics.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can the computational complexity of HSSE be reduced to enable efficient application to very large single‑cell datasets (e.g., >100,000 cells) without sacrificing multiscale sensitivity?
- **Basis:** [explicit] The conclusion mentions exploring "more efficient implementations."
- **Why unresolved:** The framework constructs multiple local simplicial complexes per cell, which scales poorly. No theoretical complexity analysis or approximate algorithms (e.g., sparse graphs, downsampling) are provided, limiting applicability to modern large‑scale data.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of approximate or parallel algorithms with formal error bounds, validated by empirical scaling studies showing near‑linear time growth and preservation of classification accuracy on large datasets.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Under what specific dataset characteristics (e.g., number of cell types, manifold complexity, expression heterogeneity) do higher‑dimensional topological features (e.g., 1‑cycles) provide statistically significant improvements over 0‑dimensional features alone?
- **Basis:** [inferred] Ablation studies reveal 0‑D features often suffice, with 1‑D adding gains only on some datasets; the paper does not analyze why this variation occurs.
- **Why unresolved:** The observation that 0‑D features dominate is noted but not explained. The conditions favoring higher‑D information (e.g., datasets with strong loop‑like relationships in expression space) remain unidentified.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A correlation analysis between the performance delta from adding 1‑D features and quantitative metadata (e.g., intrinsic dimension, cluster separation metrics) across a broad benchmark, identifying predictive factors.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the selection of scales (S) and neighborhood sizes (K) in HSSE be made fully data‑adaptive instead of relying on fixed heuristics (e.g., power‑based sampling), and would such adaptation improve robustness across diverse data regimes?
- **Basis:** [inferred] The paper uses predefined scale pools and neighborhood sets with sensitivity analysis showing diminishing returns but no automated selection method; parameter choice likely depends on dataset‑specific properties.
- **Why unresolved:** Optimal parameters may vary with data size, noise, and biological structure. The current approach requires manual tuning or cross‑validation, which is impractical for large‑scale or automated pipelines.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An algorithm that infers optimal parameters from data properties (e.g., stability of spectral features across scales), validated by cross‑dataset consistency in performance without manual tuning.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How should the HSSE framework be adapted for spatial transcriptomics data, where spatial coordinates provide an explicit geometry, to effectively integrate spatial and expression‑derived relational structures?
- **Basis:** [explicit] The conclusion proposes applications to "spatial transcriptomics data."
- **Why unresolved:** The current construction uses expression‑based embeddings and unlabeled restriction maps. Spatial data introduces spatial adjacency and tissue architecture, which may require spatially informed sheaf maps (e.g., combining Euclidean distances with expression similarity).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A modified HSSE version that uses spatial coordinates to define neighborhoods and restriction maps, evaluated on spatial transcriptomics datasets against state‑of‑the‑art spatial methods, with assessment of both classification accuracy and spatial coherence of embeddings.

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameters (scale values, filtration interval count L, label modulation coefficient α) are not documented and must be inferred from code defaults.
- Ablations do not isolate the contribution of sheaf‑specific encoding from that of multi‑scale geometric aggregation alone.
- Computational scalability to very large datasets (e.g., >10k cells) is not discussed.
