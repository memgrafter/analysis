# Multi-Agent Influence Diagrams to Hybrid Threat Modeling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03526
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03526
- **Reference count:** 25
- **Primary result:** Market restrictions most often optimal due to strong denial and mitigation; intelligence sharing cost-effective for mitigation; punishment-based measures highly variable; deterrence probability and cost are top sensitivity factors.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the challenge of assessing counter-hybrid measures amid ambiguity, cross-domain threats, and uncertain adversary responses. It introduces a multi-agent influence diagram framework that merges probabilistic uncertainty (via causal influence diagrams) with strategic game theory (via multi-agent influence diagrams). Through 1000 semi-synthetic simulations of a cyber attack on critical infrastructure with five countermeasures, the analysis finds that market restrictions frequently emerge as optimal, while deterrence probability and cost are the most influential parameters. Subgame perfect equilibria reveal rare attacks and a preference for cost-efficient deterrents when adversaries act strategically.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a two-stage modeling approach. First, a Causal Influence Diagram (CID) models the defender precommitting to a countermeasure, with the adversary’s response captured by static probability distributions (deterrence success, mitigation). The defender’s expected payoff is maximized via an integer linear program. Second, a Multi-Agent Influence Diagram (MAID) extends this by treating the adversary’s action as a strategic decision node, enabling computation of subgame perfect equilibria through backward induction. Sensitivity analysis uses SHAP values across 1000 semi-synthetic scenarios to identify which parameters (cost, deterrence probability, mitigation probability) most drive optimal strategy selection.

## Key Results
- Market restrictions (`d4`) are most frequently optimal under both CID optimization and MAID equilibria due to high deterrence probability and moderate cost.
- Intelligence sharing (`d1`) is cost-effective for mitigation but less optimal for deterrence.
- Punishment-based measures (offensive cyber, open messaging) show highly variable performance depending on parameter draws.
- Sensitivity analysis identifies deterrence probability (`q_ij`) and countermeasure cost (`γ_i`) as the dominant factors influencing effectiveness across all measures.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The causal influence diagram (CID) optimizes counter-hybrid measures by explicitly modeling probabilistic uncertainties (deterrence, mitigation) and separating deterministic costs.
- **Mechanism:** The CID structures the problem with decision node `D` (countermeasure), chance node `C` (adversary action), and utility nodes. The defender maximizes expected total pay-off `DP = Θ - Γ` by intervening `do(D=d)`, where `Θ` (interaction pay-off) depends on `d` and `c`, and `Γ` (cost) depends only on `d`. This factorization allows computation via an integer linear program (ILP) that samples from elicited probability distributions for `P(c|d)` (deterrence) and `P(θ|d,c)` (mitigation).
- **Core assumption:** The adversary’s responsiveness to a pre‑committed countermeasure can be captured by static, ex‑ante probability distributions (`q_ij` and `w_ijh`), independent of strategic game dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "...unifying previously bifurcating hybrid threat modeling methods through a (multi‑agent) influence diagram framework. The model balances the costs of countermeasures, their ability to dissuade the adversary... and their potential to mitigate the impact..." [section 3.1] "In the first proposed modeling approach, the defender preemptively commits to a selected counter‑hybrid posture... The adversary’s response is driven by the estimated probability of successful dissuasion."
- **Break condition:** If the adversary’s decision is not independent of the defender’s chosen measure in a way that creates sequential rationality problems (e.g., non‑credible threats), the CID optimization becomes misaligned with strategic equilibrium outcomes.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Extending to a multi‑agent influence diagram (MAID) captures strategic interaction by converting the adversary’s hybrid conduct `C` from a chance node to a decision node, enabling the computation of subgame perfect equilibria (SPE) via backward induction.
- **Mechanism:** In the MAID, `C` is a decision node for agent `A`, while `D` remains a decision node for agent `B`. Each agent has distinct utility nodes (`CP` for `A`, `DP` for `B`). The game is solved by finding strategy profiles where, at every decision point (subgame), each agent’s choice maximizes their utility given the other’s strategy, eliminating non‑credible threats.
- **Core assumption:** Both agents are rational, have common knowledge of the game structure (including the cost and probability distributions), and seek to maximize their total pay‑off (`CP = -Θ - H`, `DP = Θ - Γ`).
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2] "In the context of the hybrid threat game, non‑credible threat equilibria emerge when the attacker threatens to conduct a hybrid operation despite it not being in their best interest... To address this, we adopt the concept of subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE)." [section 5] "These subgame perfect equilibria for the same 1000 experiments are displayed... The low occurrence of hybrid attacks indicates that the chosen counter‑hybrid measures focus on deterring the adversary rather than mitigating the consequences..."
- **Break condition:** If agents are not perfectly rational, have incomplete/private information about distributions (e.g., `q_ij`), or if the game has more than two stages with imperfect recall, SPE derived from this MAID may not reflect real‑world behavior.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Sensitivity analysis (via SHAP values) identifies that the effectiveness of any counter‑hybrid measure is most sensitive to the deterrence probability parameter (`q_ij`) and the measure’s own cost (`γ_i`), with mitigation probabilities (`w_ijh`) having secondary influence.
- **Mechanism:** For each of the 1000 semi‑synthetic scenarios, the model’s output (optimal `d_i` rank) is computed. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values are calculated to attribute changes in the output to each input feature (cost `γ_i`, deterrence prob `q_i`, mitigation probs `w_ih`). High |SHAP| for `q_i` and `γ_i` across measures indicates they are primary drivers of optimal strategy selection.
- **Core assumption:** The linear relationship between input features (as sampled from distributions) and the final ranking/output is sufficiently monotonic and additive for SHAP to provide valid global sensitivity rankings.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 5] "As can be observed from the figure [SHAP summary plot], the probability of successfully deterring the adversary `q_14`, as well as the costs of the measure `γ_4` are the most influential factors..." [Table 3] For all measures, `q_i` is the top feature; cost `γ_i` is top for `d2`, `d4`, `d5`, `d6`.
- **Break condition:** If the relationship between inputs and output is highly non‑linear or involves strong interactions not captured by additive SHAP values, or if the input distributions are misspecified, the sensitivity rankings may be misleading.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Bayesian Networks / Causal Influence Diagrams (CIDs)**
  - **Why needed:** The entire model structure relies on factorizing joint distributions over chance variables according to a directed acyclic graph. Understanding conditional probability tables and do‑calculus for intervention is essential to grasp how the ILP optimization works.
  - **Quick check:** In the CID (Figure 2), what is the difference between the arrow from `D` to `C` (representing `P(C|D)`) and the arrow from `(D,C)` to `Θ` (representing `P(Θ|D,C)`)? How does this structure enable the calculation of `E[DP|do(D=d)]`?
- **Concept: Game Theory (Subgame Perfect Equilibrium)**
  - **Why needed:** The MAID extension requires understanding how to convert a sequential move game into normal form, define subgames, and apply backward induction to find SPE. This distinguishes credible from non‑credible threats.
  - **Quick check:** Why might the Nash equilibrium of this two‑player game include non‑credible threats, and how does the SPE refinement (via backward induction) specifically eliminate them in the context of the `D` then `C` move order?
- **Concept: Sensitivity Analysis (SHAP for Global Importance)**
  - **Why needed:** The paper’s key policy implication is that deterrence probability and cost are the dominant factors. Understanding SHAP summary plots is necessary to interpret why some measures (like market restrictions) are often optimal.
  - **Quick check:** Looking at Table 3 and Figure 4, why is the mitigation probability feature (`w_ih`) never the #1 most important feature for any counter‑hybrid measure, even for `d1` (intelligence sharing) which is primarily a mitigation tool?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Elicitation Module -> CID Simulator -> MAID Equilibria Solver -> Sensitivity Engine -> Validation/Contextualization Layer
- **Critical path:** Elicitation -> Generate N parameter samples -> For each sample: Run CID optimization -> Run MAID SPE -> Store results -> Aggregate & run SHAP -> Interpret/Contextualize.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - Probabilistic vs. Strategic: CID optimization is computationally cheaper (ILP) but assumes static adversary response probabilities. MAID SPE is more realistic for strategic actors but requires solving a game for each parameter sample, increasing complexity.
  - Semi‑synthetic data: Enables exploration of "what‑if" scenarios where real data is scarce, but introduces elicitation bias and limits empirical validation.
  - Feature abstraction: Mitigation is modeled as categorical impact reduction, simplifying but potentially losing nuance of continuous damage spectra.
- **Failure signatures:** 
  - Model instability: wildly varying optimal `d_i` across similar parameter samples indicates excessive sensitivity to elicitation noise.
  - SPE dominance of `d6` (no measure): frequent attacks with defender inaction suggests countermeasures are insufficient or too costly.
  - SHAP contradiction: low importance of `q_ij` for punishment‑based measures would contradict deterrence theory, indicating modeling error.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline CID:** Use mean/median of all elicited distributions. Run ILP to find optimal `d_i` under probabilistic adversary response. Verify if `d4` (market restrictions) wins.
  2. **Strategic Shift:** Using same parameter sample as Exp 1, run MAID SPE solver. Check if optimal `d_i` changes (e.g., `d1` might win if cheaper and still deters).
  3. **Parameter Stress Test:** Fix `d4` and vary only its `q_41` (deterrence prob) from 5th to 95th percentile. Re‑run CID optimization. Determine at what `q_41` `d4` ceases to be optimal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Which specific characteristics (e.g., cost, deterrence probability, mitigation probability) of counter‑hybrid measures are most effective for defending against cyber attacks on critical infrastructure under deep uncertainty?
- **Question 2:** Under which conditions do specific counter‑hybrid measures form subgame perfect equilibria (SPE) against a strategically operating adversary, and how do these equilibria shift based on the adversary’s cost‑benefit calculus?
- **Question 3:** How can the elicitation of conditional probability tables (CPTs) for influence diagrams be systematically improved to reduce subjectivity and increase reliability in the hybrid threat domain?
- **Question 4:** How do cross‑domain hybrid threats (involving simultaneous cyber, information, economic, and kinetic elements) alter the effectiveness and equilibrium properties of counter‑hybrid measures compared to single‑domain (cyber‑only) scenarios?

## Limitations
- Semi‑synthetic data rely on expert‑elicited distributions, introducing potential bias and limited generalizability.
- Model reduces hybrid campaigns to a binary attack decision, omitting multi‑stage, cross‑domain dynamics.
- MAID assumes perfect rationality, common knowledge of distributions, and a fixed defender‑first move order—unlikely in real conflicts.
- No empirical validation against observed incidents; conclusions remain hypothetical.

## Confidence
- **CID optimization premise:** Medium (ILP formulation sound but static response probabilities may fail under strategic reasoning)
- **Strategic SPE outcomes:** Medium (game theory foundation strong, but assumptions of perfect rationality and information symmetry are strong)
- **Sensitivity claims (q_ij, γ_i dominate):** Medium (SHAP valid for additive approximations, but non‑linear interactions could distort rankings)
