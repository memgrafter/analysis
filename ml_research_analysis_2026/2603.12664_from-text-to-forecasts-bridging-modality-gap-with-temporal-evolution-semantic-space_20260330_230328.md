# From Text to Forecasts: Bridging Modality Gap with Temporal Evolution Semantic Space

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.12664
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12664
- **Reference count**: 38
- **Primary result**: Up to 29% reduction in forecasting error (MSE) compared to state-of-the-art unimodal and multimodal baselines.

## Executive Summary
Multimodal time‑series forecasting suffers from a modality gap: textual descriptions are implicit, qualitative, and often contain redundant tokens, making them difficult to fuse directly with numerical data. This paper introduces TESS (Text-to-Evolution Semantic Space), a method that bridges the gap by distilling text into a compact set of numerically grounded temporal primitives—mean shift, volatility, shape, and lag. A frozen large language model (LLM) extracts these primitives via structured prompting; a confidence‑aware gating mechanism then filters unreliable extractions and combines them into a semantic prefix. The prefix is concatenated with patch embeddings of the historical time series and fed to a transformer forecaster. Evaluated on four real‑world datasets, TESS achieves up to a 29% reduction in MSE against strong baselines, with ablations confirming the necessity of both the semantic space and gating. Controlled semi‑synthetic experiments further show that existing multimodal approaches fail because they over‑attend to redundant context and cannot decode qualitative semantics into quantitative signals. The work provides an interpretable and theoretically motivated bridge between language and time‑series forecasting.

## Method Summary
TESS processes a historical time series \(X_{\text{time}}\) and an aligned textual description \(X_{\text{text}}\). The text is passed through a frozen LLM (e.g., GPT‑5.2) with per‑primitive structured prompts. For each of the four temporal primitives, the LLM returns a categorical distribution over predefined categories (e.g., increase, decrease, no change for mean shift) and a confidence margin derived from the log‑probability difference between the top two candidates. A lightweight gating network takes the primitive’s embedding and the margin and outputs a weight in \([0,1]\). The weighted embeddings of the four primitives form a "gated semantic prefix". Meanwhile, the time series is normalized, patched, and projected into embeddings. These patch tokens are concatenated with the prefix tokens and processed by a transformer encoder. A final MLP head maps the transformer output back to the original scale to produce the forecast. Training optimizes a combined loss: MSE between forecast and target, plus binary cross‑entropy for the gate using ground‑truth primitives computed from the future series. All components except the LLM are trainable.

## Key Results
- **Significant error reduction**: TESS lowers MSE by up to 29% compared to state‑of‑the‑art unimodal and multimodal forecasting baselines on four real‑world datasets.
- **Ablation necessity**: Removing either the temporal evolution semantic space or the confidence‑aware gating leads to substantial performance drops, confirming their complementary roles.
- **Failure of direct fusion**: Semi‑synthetic experiments demonstrate that standard multimodal models cannot overcome the modality gap—they attend to irrelevant text tokens and fail to extract predictive semantics—while TESS succeeds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Intermediate Temporal Evolution Semantic Space
- **Claim**: Distilling text into a compact set of numerically grounded temporal primitives bridges the modality gap by providing an interpretable interface between qualitative descriptions and quantitative forecasting.
- **Mechanism**: The paper defines four primitives—mean shift, volatility, shape, lag—that are computable from numerical series. Text is processed by an LLM to predict these primitives, converting implicit semantics into explicit signals that a forecaster can consume.
- **Core assumption**: The primitives capture sufficient information about event‑driven non‑stationarity (semantic sufficiency), and LLMs can map text to them reliably.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “introducing an intermediate bottleneck that distills text into four interpretable, numerically grounded temporal primitives”
  - [section 4.1]: Formal definitions of primitives and their numerical verifiability via thresholds.
  - [corpus]: Neighboring papers focus on direct fusion or LLM‑as‑predictor paradigms; the structured semantic space approach is not directly paralleled, so corpus evidence is weak and indirect.
- **Break condition**: If the predefined primitives do not cover the non‑stationarity patterns in a given dataset, or if the LLM consistently fails to extract them from domain‑specific text.

### Mechanism 2: LLM‑Based Primitive Extraction via Structured Prompting
- **Claim**: A frozen LLM, guided by carefully crafted prompts, can reliably classify temporal evolution primitives from natural language without task‑specific fine‑tuning.
- **Mechanism**: For each primitive, a prompt containing definitions and examples is combined with the input text. The LLM’s log‑likelihood over the discrete candidate categories yields a categorical distribution; the top‑1 category is taken as the prediction, while the distribution provides a confidence signal.
- **Core assumption**: LLMs possess inherent knowledge of temporal dynamics and, when given clear instructions, can align free‑form text with the predefined primitive taxonomy.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “extracted from text by an LLM via structured prompting”
  - [section 4.2]: Prompt template design and the scoring procedure using log‑likelihoods.
  - [corpus]: Related works (e.g., EventTSF, BALM‑TSF) also use LLMs for time series but adopt different fusion strategies; support from the corpus is tangential.
- **Break condition**: LLM may misinterpret complex or contradictory narratives, especially when multiple events interact, or if the prompt does not adequately cover edge cases.

### Mechanism 3: Confidence‑Aware Gating
- **Claim**: A learnable gating mechanism that weights primitive embeddings based on LLM uncertainty filters extraction errors, improving robustness without sacrificing useful signals.
- **Mechanism**: For each primitive, the gate combines the semantic embedding (mapped from the predicted category) with the log‑prob margin between the top two candidates. A small neural network outputs a weight in [0,1], trained via binary cross‑entropy using ground‑truth primitives (computed from future data). During inference, gated primitives are used.
- **Core assumption**: The margin between top‑1 and top‑2 LLM probabilities is a reliable indicator of prediction correctness; a lightweight learned gate can calibrate this signal effectively.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “confidence‑aware gating mechanism filters unreliable extractions”
  - [section 4.2]: Gating network architecture, loss function, and inference weighting.
  - [section 5.3]: Ablation results show gating yields consistent (though modest) improvements, confirming its utility.
  - [corpus]: No neighboring paper implements a similar gating scheme; corpus evidence is weak.
- **Break condition**: If the LLM’s confidence (margin) is not correlated with actual correctness—e.g., overconfident on wrong predictions—the gate may fail to suppress errors. The gate might also overfit to the training distribution and not generalize to new text styles or domains.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Modality Gap
- **Why needed**: The paper’s core motivation is the mismatch between textual (implicit, qualitative) and numerical (explicit, quantitative) representations, which causes direct fusion to fail (e.g., attention on redundant tokens). Understanding this gap explains the need for an intermediate space.
- **Quick check**: How does the modality gap lead to attention distraction in multimodal forecasting models, according to the semi‑synthetic experiments?

### Concept: Information Bottleneck
- **Why needed**: TESS is theoretically justified as an information bottleneck that preserves predictive information while reducing irrelevant details. Theorem 4.1 provides a generalization improvement guarantee under certain assumptions.
- **Quick check**: What are the key assumptions (semantic sufficiency, sub‑Gaussian loss, i.i.d. samples) in Theorem 4.1, and how do they justify the use of a fixed primitive space?

### Concept: Structured Extraction with LLMs
- **Why needed**: The method relies on extracting discrete labels from an LLM via prompting, not fine‑tuning. This requires understanding the strengths and limitations of LLMs in following structured instructions.
- **Quick check**: Why is a frozen LLM used instead of training a dedicated classifier, and what are the trade‑offs in terms of flexibility and computational cost?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
`X_time, X_text -> LLM Extractor (structured prompting) -> Confidence Gate (learnable network) -> Gated Semantic Prefix (weighted embeddings) -> Time-Series Encoder (patching & projection) -> Transformer (with prefix) -> Forecast Head (MLP) -> hat{y}`

### Critical path
- **Training**:
  1. For each batch, forward pass through LLM (frozen) to get primitive predictions and margins.
  2. Compute gated primitives using current gate network.
  3. Encode time series, concatenate with gated primitives, run transformer, get forecast.
  4. Compute MSE loss between forecast and target, and gating BCE loss using ground‑truth primitives.
  5. Backpropagate total loss to update gate network and forecaster parameters (LLM remains frozen).
- **Inference**: Same as training but without gating loss and using learned gate.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Fixed primitives vs. learned**: Fixed primitives enable supervision and interpretability but may miss domain‑specific non‑stationarity patterns.
- **Frozen LLM**: Avoids catastrophic forgetting and reduces compute, but may not adapt to niche terminology; domain‑specific fine‑tuning could help but risks losing general reasoning.
- **Gating supervision**: Relies on ground‑truth primitives from future data, available only during training. Test‑time gate relies on learned calibration, which may degrade under distribution shift.
- **Backbone choice**: PatchTST is efficient for long series and works well with prefix tokens, but the framework could use other architectures (e.g., linear, MLP) at the cost of possibly lower performance.

### Failure signatures
- **Low primitive extraction accuracy** (<60% on validation): Indicates LLM or prompt issues; may need prompt redesign or a different LLM.
- **Gating weights near zero** for many samples: Gate may be too conservative, possibly due to class imbalance in gating supervision or poorly calibrated margins.
- **Degraded performance on specific primitive categories** (e.g., “peak” shape): Suggests primitive definitions are inadequate or LLM cannot recognize those patterns.
- **Training loss divergence or plateau**: Check learning rate, λ balance, or whether gating loss dominates; also verify gradient flow through transformer.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Primitive extraction sanity check**: Evaluate LLM extraction accuracy on a held‑out set using ground‑truth primitives. Expected: >70% accuracy for most primitives.
2. **Ablation study**: Remove TESS (direct text fusion) and remove gating individually to confirm contributions (replicating Table 2). Expect TESS removal to cause largest drop.
3. **Gating effectiveness visualization**: Plot distribution of gating weights for correctly vs. incorrectly extracted primitives (like Figure 8b). Expect clear separation; if not, gating may not be learning properly.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What specific linguistic or structural properties of textual descriptions prevent direct decoding into numerical forecasting signals, even after removing redundant tokens?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The semi‑synthetic experiments show that “Signal‑Only” text (with redundant tokens removed) still significantly underperforms numerical statistical features, indicating a “fundamental representational mismatch” where qualitative expressions (e.g., “significant rise”) resist translation into quantitative cues.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper identifies the symptom but does not analyze which textual attributes (e.g., ambiguity, implicit temporal references, lack of numerical grounding) causally impede decoding. Understanding this could inform better text augmentation or primitive design.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments varying text phrasing (e.g., adding explicit percentages vs. qualitative descriptors) or probing a frozen LLM’s ability to extract numerical values from event descriptions, correlated with downstream forecast error.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How robust is the confidence‑aware gating mechanism to distribution shifts in LLM extraction errors between training and deployment, given that it is supervised using ground‑truth primitives derived from the forecast window?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The gating network \(g_{t,k}\) is trained with labels \(y_{t,k} = \mathbb{I}[\hat{v}_{t,k} = v^*_{t,k}]\), where \(v^*_{t,k}\) is computed from the ground‑truth future series (Section 4.2). At inference, no ground‑truth is available, so the model assumes LLM error patterns on unseen text will match the training distribution.
- **Why unresolved**: Real‑world text may contain novel event types, ambiguous phrasing, or domain shifts that produce LLM errors not seen during training. The paper does not evaluate gating under such shifts or investigate whether the margin‑based confidence score (\(m_{t,k}\)) reliably indicates extraction correctness across diverse text.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Evaluate TESS on text from completely new event categories or domains without retraining the gating network, measuring degradation relative to in‑distribution text. Ablate the gating supervision source (e.g., train with synthetic LLM error models) and compare performance.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Are the four predefined temporal primitives (mean shift, volatility, shape, lag) sufficient to capture all relevant non‑stationary patterns for accurate forecasting, or could additional primitives (e.g., higher‑order statistics, cross‑variable interactions) yield further gains?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The primitives are designed based on “classical forecasting literature” and expert intuition (Section 4.1). The ablation study (Figure 8a) shows each contributes to performance, but only these four are evaluated. The paper does not question whether the primitive set is exhaustive or optimal.
- **Why unresolved**: Non‑stationarity may manifest in ways not covered (e.g., changes in autocorrelation, skewness, or multivariate dependencies). The choice of four primitives is arbitrary; a larger or different set might better capture complex event impacts but could introduce noise or computational cost.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic expansion of the primitive space (e.g., adding Kurtosis, Cross‑correlation shift) with regularization to prevent overfitting, evaluated on diverse datasets. Analyze residual forecast errors after TESS to identify missing patterns.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: How does TESS handle temporally misaligned or contextually irrelevant textual information (e.g., news about events occurring after the forecast horizon or unrelated to the target series)?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The problem formulation (Section 3.1) assumes text \(s_t\) is temporally aligned and relevant to the prediction window. The semi‑synthetic benchmark constructs text from future statistical features, ensuring perfect relevance. Real‑world news may mention past events, future uncertainties, or tangential topics not impacting the immediate forecast.
- **Why unresolved**: The structured prompting and gating mechanism assume the LLM can filter irrelevant content, but the paper does not test robustness to irrelevant or mistimed text. The confidence gate may suppress some errors, but could still be misled by plausible but irrelevant narratives.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Introduce controlled noise into the text (e.g., insert sentences about unrelated events or future dates beyond H) and measure forecast degradation compared to clean text. Compare TESS’s error rates to direct fusion baselines under such perturbations.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: What is the computational and latency overhead of using a frozen LLM for primitive extraction in TESS, and can this be reduced without sacrificing performance (e.g., via model distillation or alternative lightweight extractors)?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] TESS relies on a frozen LLM (GPT‑5.2 in semi‑synthetic experiments) for text‑to‑primitive classification via structured prompting (Section 4.2). The paper focuses on accuracy gains but does not report inference time, cost, or scalability. The LLM is a black‑box, making deployment resource‑intensive.
- **Why unresolved**: For real‑time or large‑scale forecasting (e.g., thousands of series), LLM query costs and latency may be prohibitive. The paper does not explore trade‑offs between extraction quality and efficiency, nor does it consider whether smaller, fine‑tuned models could replace the LLM.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Benchmark end‑to‑end latency and cost of TESS against baselines on a large dataset. Ablate the LLM component by replacing it with a smaller fine‑tuned classifier (trained on LLM labels) and measure performance‑efficiency trade‑offs.

## Limitations
- **Primitive coverage**: The four fixed primitives may miss complex or domain‑specific non‑stationarity patterns, limiting semantic sufficiency.
- **LLM extraction brittleness**: Structured prompting relies on the frozen LLM’s understanding; performance could degrade on out‑of‑distribution text styles, jargon, or contradictory narratives.
- **Gating supervision**: The gate uses ground‑truth primitives from future data during training; under distribution shift its learned calibration may fail.
