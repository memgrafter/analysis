# Steering LLMs for Culturally Localized Generation

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.23301
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23301
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** White-box steering via Culture Embeddings (CuE) reduces cultural default concentration by 84.7% and improves cultural faithfulness (48% win rate vs. explicit prompting's 24% loss).

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) exhibit a cultural bias toward data‑rich (Anglophone) cultures, especially when prompts are underspecified. Existing localization methods like prompting or alignment are black‑box and cannot distinguish whether poor cultural performance stems from missing knowledge or poor elicitation. This work introduces Culture Embeddings (CuE), a white‑box steering method that uses sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to identify interpretable, monosemantic features encoding cultural information. By aggregating these features into country‑specific prototypes via mutual information selection, CuE generates steering vectors that amplify culturally salient features during generation. Evaluations on Gemma‑2‑9B and LLaMA‑3.1‑8B show that steering significantly improves cultural faithfulness (48% win rate vs. explicit prompting’s 24% loss) and surfaces rarer, long‑tail cultural concepts (53% win rate vs. 17% loss). Additionally, steering reduces cultural default concentration by 84.7% and remains complementary to explicit prompting, demonstrating that relevant cultural knowledge is often latent but structurally organized and can be controllably elicited.

## Method Summary
The method comprises four steps:  
1. **Feature extraction:** For each cultural assertion from the CANDLE dataset, extract residual stream activations at all layers and apply pretrained sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to obtain a high‑dimensional feature activation vector; max‑pool over tokens to retain the strongest activation per feature.  
2. **Feature selection:** Compute mutual information (MI) between each feature’s binned activations and country labels; rank features and select those whose cumulative MI exceeds a threshold ρ=0.1.  
3. **Prototype construction:** For each country, average the CuE vectors (restricted to selected features) over its assertions to obtain a prototype p_c.  
4. **Steering:** For a target country c, compute the difference δ = p_c − mean(p_{c′≠c}), decode it back to the residual space per layer via the SAE decoder (v_l = W_{dec,l}^T · δ_l), and at inference add α · v_l to the residual stream at every layer. The steering strength α is tuned per country to maximize cultural faithfulness while maintaining fluency.

## Key Results
- Steering improves cultural faithfulness with a **48% win rate** compared to explicit prompting’s **24% loss** rate.
- It surfaces rarer, long‑tail cultural concepts with a **53% win rate** versus **17% loss**.
- Cultural default concentration (Anglophone bias) is reduced by **84.7%**.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: SAE‑Based Feature Extraction
- **Claim:** Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) decompose model activations into interpretable, monosemantic features that encode cultural information.
- **Mechanism:** For each input, extract residual stream activations, apply a pretrained SAE to obtain a high‑dimensional feature activation vector, and max‑pool across tokens to retain the strongest activation per feature.
- **Core assumption:** SAE features are sufficiently monosemantic to isolate distinct cultural concepts (e.g., cuisine, geography) from polysemantic neurons.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] “Leveraging sparse autoencoders, we identify interpretable features that encode culturally salient information.”  
  [section 2.2] Details max‑pooled activation extraction: `a_j(x) = max_t z_j(t)`.  
  [corpus] Neighbors focus on prompting/alignment; SAE approach is novel here, so corpus evidence for this specific mechanism is weak.
- **Break condition:** If SAE features remain polysemantic, steering may inadvertently affect unrelated behaviors or fail to capture clean cultural semantics. Mitigated by using established SAE checkpoints (Gemma Scope/Llama Scope).

### Mechanism 2: Mutual Information–Based Feature Selection and Prototype Construction
- **Claim:** Mutual information (MI) between feature activations and country labels selects culturally diagnostic features; averaging these across assertions yields country‑specific prototypes (CuE).
- **Mechanism:** Compute `I(A_j; C)` globally, rank features, and select those whose cumulative MI reaches threshold `ρ`. For each country `c`, prototype `p_CuE = mean(CuE(assertions_c))`.
- **Core assumption:** High‑MI features are predictive of cultural labels, and their averaged activation patterns form a stable representation of typical cultural knowledge.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] “aggregating them into Cultural Embeddings (CuE).”  
  [section 2.3] Defines MI computation and prototype averaging.  
  [corpus] No direct anchor; related work uses contrastive prompts, not MI‑based feature selection.
- **Break condition:** If dataset artifacts (e.g., country names in CANDLE) drive MI, selected features may capture shallow lexical cues rather than deep cultural semantics. Paper mitigates via data augmentation (Appendix A) and shows layer analysis (early layers get fewer features when augmented).

### Mechanism 3: Steering via Residual Stream Intervention
- **Claim:** A feature‑space steering vector, derived from the difference between target and non‑target CuE prototypes, can amplify culturally salient features during generation.
- **Mechanism:** Compute `δ = p_target − mean(p_others)`. Decode to residual direction: `v_CuE = W_dec^T · δ`. At inference, modify residual: `h′ = h + α · v_CuE`.
- **Core assumption:** Cultural knowledge is linearly represented in the residual stream and can be modulated by adding a direction that enhances target‑culture features while suppressing others.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] “CuE‑based white‑box steering interventions… amplify culturally salient features during generation.”  
  [section 2.5] Gives steering equation and `α` tuning.  
  [section 4] Shows win rates (e.g., 48% vs 24% for faithfulness) and bias concentration reduction (84.7%).
- **Break condition:** If the target culture’s knowledge is absent (not represented) or non‑linear, steering fails. Paper notes per‑culture variation: Egypt/Israel need explicit prompt anchoring (RQ5), implying steering vector alone may not activate the correct subspace without contextual cues.

## Foundational Learning

**Sparse Autoencoders (SAE)**
- **Why needed:** To transform dense, polysemantic LLM activations into a sparse dictionary of interpretable features.
- **Quick check:** Why are SAE features considered more interpretable than raw neuron activations?

**Mutual Information for Feature Selection**
- **Why needed:** To objectively identify which SAE features are statistically associated with cultural labels, avoiding manual curation.
- **Quick check:** Why use a cumulative MI threshold (`ρ`) instead of a fixed top‑`k` features?

**White‑Box Steering vs. Black‑Box Prompting**
- **Why needed:** Prompting adjusts surface behavior without controlling internal mechanisms; steering directly manipulates latent representations, offering explainable and composable control.
- **Quick check:** Why decode the steering vector from feature space back to the residual stream rather than adding it directly in the SAE space?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
CANDLE dataset → SAE activation extraction (max‑pool) → MI‑based feature selection → Country prototype averaging → Steering vector computation (δ, v_CuE) → Generation with residual intervention (add α·v_CuE)

**Critical path:**  
1. Extract residual activations from cultural assertions using the target model.  
2. Apply layer‑specific SAEs and max‑pool over tokens to obtain feature vectors.  
3. Compute mutual information between each feature and country labels; select features above cumulative MI threshold ρ.  
4. For each country, average the selected‑feature activations to build a CuE prototype.  
5. For a target country, compute δ = prototype(target) − mean(prototypes of other countries); decode to per‑layer residual directions via SAE decoder.  
6. During generation, add α·v_CuE to the residual stream at every layer.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **SAE width:** Wider SAEs capture finer concepts but increase memory/computation.  
- **Layer selection:** Steering applied to all layers (paper) vs. subset; affects where cultural signals are amplified.  
- **Steering strength (α):** Tuned per country; too low yields no effect, too high degrades fluency.  
- **Cumulative MI threshold (ρ):** Controls feature set size; paper uses 0.1 (top ~10%).  
- **α scaling:** Could normalize δ before decoding to manage magnitude.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Low fluency scores (<5.0):** α too high or steering affecting unrelated layers; reduce α or restrict to later layers.  
- **High bias concentration (still Anglophone):** Selected features may be lexical artifacts; inspect top MI features for superficial cues.  
- **Per‑culture failure (e.g., Egypt in Steer Implicit):** May require explicit prompt anchoring; switch to Steer Explicit.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Linear probe replication (RQ1):** Train a probe on residual activations to verify cultural signals are linearly decodable; expect macro‑F1 significantly above chance (4.5%).  
2. **Top MI feature inspection (RQ2):** Use Neuronpedia to label selected features; expect semantically coherent cultural clusters (e.g., East Asian cuisine, Mediterranean olive oil).  
3. **Implicit bias measurement (RQ3):** Generate responses without cultural cues and compute bias concentration index; expect ~60% default to US/UK. After applying Steer Implicit, expect >80% reduction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why does steering only improve faithfulness for certain cultures (e.g., Egypt, Israel) when combined with explicit prompting, whereas others show gains with steering alone?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper states in RQ5 that for Egypt and Israel, *Steer Implicit* loses to explicit prompting, but *Steer Explicit* wins. They note "Why this happens remains unclear, but it would be worth exploring in future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The pattern is identified but not explained. Possible reasons include noisier CuE vectors for these cultures, greater reliance on surface lexical cues, or the need for explicit cues to disambiguate representations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyzing the similarity between CuE vectors for these "anchor‑dependent" cultures and their nearest neighbors; probing whether their cultural features are less linearly separable; testing if adding explicit cues changes which SAE layers are most influential during steering.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do cultural features in LLMs emerge and change throughout training, and what does this reveal about the acquisition of cultural knowledge?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion suggests exploring "how cultural features emerge during training" as future work.
- **Why unresolved:** The study uses pretrained models and SAEs, providing only a static snapshot. The temporal dynamics—when cultural features appear, how they layer, and how they stabilize—are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Training models from scratch with regular checkpoints, extracting SAE features at each stage, and tracking the formation, layer distribution, and coherence of culture‑specific features over time, correlated with data mixture changes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can alternative feature selection methods (beyond mutual information with a cumulative threshold) yield more robust or comprehensive Cultural Embeddings (CuE)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method relies on mutual information (MI) between SAE feature activations and country labels, with a cumulative threshold ρ. Appendix A shows that dataset artifacts (e.g., country names in CANDLE) can bias early‑layer feature selection, suggesting MI may capture superficial cues.
- **Why unresolved:** MI maximizes discrimination but may overlook features that are culturally salient yet not top‑discriminative, or include spurious correlations. The choice of cumulative threshold lacks theoretical grounding and may affect coverage.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparing CuE built with other selection criteria (e.g., feature importance from linear probes, causal impact via patching, sparsity‑promoting methods) and evaluating downstream steering effectiveness, feature interpretability, and stability across datasets.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What factors determine the variability in steering effectiveness across cultures, and can we predict which cultures will benefit most from CuE‑based interventions?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] RQ5 shows high‑benefit cultures (Thailand, India) have low baseline cultural scores, while already‑represented cultures (US, UK) show modest gains. The paper does not analyze why baseline scores vary or what predicts steering success.
- **Why unresolved:** Low baseline scores could indicate missing knowledge, poor elicitation, or both. Without decomposing these factors, it’s unclear if steering is eliciting latent knowledge or amplifying noise. This limits generalizability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Correlating per‑culture steering gains with properties such as training data frequency for that culture, baseline model performance on cultural benchmarks, sparsity or coherence of CuE vectors, and the degree of feature overlap with neighboring cultures.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How does CuE generalize to finer‑grained, non‑country cultural definitions (e.g., regions, ethnic groups, linguistic communities), and can it disentangle intra‑cultural diversity?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The limitations state that using country as a proxy flattens intra‑country diversity, but the method is "generalizable and flexible by design for any label set." However, experiments are conducted only with country labels.
- **Why unresolved:** Cultural groups within countries may have overlapping or conflicting representations. It is unknown if CuE can produce distinct, culturally meaningful embeddings for subnational groups without granular, high‑quality data, or if steering would produce stereotyped or conflicting outputs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Applying CuE to datasets with subnational labels (e.g., states, dialects, ethnicities) and evaluating whether: (a) resulting embeddings are interpretable and distinct, (b) steering toward one subgroup does not inadvertently activate features of another, and (c) outputs are validated by members of those cultural groups.

## Limitations
- **Judge Models:** Unknown which LLMs served as judges; our choice impacts faithfulness/rarity scores and win rates.
- **Dataset & Augmentation:** Our GPT‑generated cultural assertions will differ from the paper’s (GPT‑4.1) and may lack their post‑verification, potentially contaminating CuE prototypes.
- **SAE Monosemanticity:** Confidence in feature interpretability depends on external SAE checkpoints (Gemma/Llama Scope); polysemantic features could leak unrelated signals into steering.
- **MI Discretization:** Binning strategy for MI calculation is unspecified; arbitrary choices may select spurious lexical correlations over deep cultural semantics.
- **Scalability:** Results demonstrated for 22 countries; efficacy for low‑resource cultures with less training data in CANDLE remains unclear.

## Confidence
- **Steering Efficacy (High):** Strong empirical support from win rates (48% vs 24%) and 84.7% bias reduction across two models.
- **SAE Feature Extraction (Medium):** Relies on external SAEs; paper shows layer analysis but direct evidence for cultural monosemanticity is limited to qualitative inspection.
- **MI‑Based Feature Selection (Low):** Cumulative threshold ρ=0.1 is heuristic; risk of capturing dataset artifacts (e.g., country name mentions) rather than conceptual knowledge.

## Next Checks
1. **Lexical Artifact Check:** Inspect top 100 MI‑selected features via Neuronpedia; quantify prevalence of surface cues (country/region names, demonstratives) vs. conceptual markers (cuisine, festivals). If >30% are lexical, re‑run with stricter data augmentation (Appendix A) and compare feature set stability.
2. **Layer Ablation Study:** Replicate steering using only early (1–12), middle (13–24), or late (25–42) layers. Expect late layers to dominate cultural steering; significant drop in efficacy with early layers confirms cultural knowledge emerges in later representations.
3. **Judge Agreement Audit:** Use three diverse judges (e.g., GPT‑4o, Claude‑3.5‑Sonnet, Llama‑3.1‑70B) on a fixed 200‑sample subset. Compute Cohen’s κ for pairwise faithfulness scores; κ < 0.6 indicates high judge variance, necessitating median scoring or a more aligned judge model.
