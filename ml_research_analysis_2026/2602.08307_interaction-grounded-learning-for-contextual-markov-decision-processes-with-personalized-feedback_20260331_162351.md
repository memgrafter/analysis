# Interaction-Grounded Learning for Contextual Markov Decision Processes with Personalized Feedback
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08307
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08307
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Extends single-step interaction-grounded learning to multi-step contextual MDPs with personalized indirect feedback, achieving a sublinear regret of $\tilde{O}(T^{3/4})$ and near-optimal empirical performance on synthetic and real-world data.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses interaction-grounded learning (IGL) for contextual Markov decision processes (MDPs) where the agent receives only indirect, personalized feedback at the end of each episode. It extends prior single-step IGL to sequential decision-making by constructing a Lipschitz reward estimator that decodes latent rewards from observed feedback. The core approach first identifies terminal states that are reliably reachable, then learns an inverse kinematic posterior distribution over actions for each such state under a uniform policy, and finally defines a smooth surrogate that distinguishes heterogeneous from homogeneous states. This estimator feeds into an inverse-gap-weighting algorithm with a regression oracle to optimize policies, yielding a sublinear regret guarantee and strong empirical results.

## Method Summary
The algorithm operates in two stages. First, for every terminal state a homing policy is learned (via EULER) to maximize visitation frequency; states with insufficient reachability are discarded. Second, for each remaining reachable state, interaction data is collected where actions are chosen uniformly, and the posterior distribution $h_s(x,y)$ is learned via empirical risk minimization in a function class $\mathcal{H}_s$. A Lipschitz continuous reward surrogate $J(v,a)$, built from these posteriors, converts latent feedback into proxy rewards. In the online phase, the surrogate rewards are used with an occupancy-measure-based policy learner that incorporates an online regression oracle (AlgSq) and a smoothed transition model, leading to policies that converge to near-optimal with sublinear regret.

## Key Results
- Theoretical guarantee: $\tilde{O}(T^{3/4} S^{1/2} K^{1/4} H^{1/4} L^{1/2} + \sqrt{T S K H \operatorname{Reg}_{\text{Sq}}})$ regret under standard assumptions.
- Empirical success: On a synthetic episodic MDP and a real-world user booking dataset, the algorithm effectively learns personalized objectives and approaches optimal cumulative reward.
- Novelty: First IGL method for multi-step contextual MDPs with personalized indirect feedback, bridging the gap between single-step IGL and full episodic RL.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Reachable State Filtering
- **Claim:** Filters out terminal states that are intrinsically hard to reach, focusing exploration on those that can be visited often enough for reliable reward decoding.
- **Mechanism:** For each terminal state $s$, a homing policy $\hat{\pi}_s$ (via EULER) is trained to maximize visitation. Empirical visitation $\hat{p}_s$ is computed; states with $\hat{p}_s < 4\varepsilon$ are excluded. High-probability bounds (Lemmas 3.1, 3.2) guarantee that excluded states have true reachability $p^*_s \le 6\varepsilon$, thus limiting regret loss.
- **Core assumption:** The MDP transition structure supports nontrivial reachability for important states, and homing policy optimization succeeds up to $\varepsilon$.
- **Break condition:** If a high-value state has low maximum reachability and $\hat{p}_s$ underestimates, it may be dropped, leading to linear regret on those instances; the bound assumes total contribution $O(T S \varepsilon)$.

### Mechanism 2: Inverse Kinematic Posterior Learning
- **Claim:** Learning the posterior distribution of actions under a uniform policy, conditioned on context, state, and feedback, yields a representation that distinguishes heterogeneous from homogeneous states and encodes the latent reward.
- **Mechanism:** For each reachable state $s \in \hat{\mathcal{S}}_{H,\varepsilon}$, data tuples $(x,s,a,y)$ are collected using $\hat{\pi}_s$ (which samples actions uniformly). The posterior $h^*_s(x,y)$ is approximated via ERM in class $\mathcal{H}_s$; the vector $\hat{h} = (\hat{h}_s)_s$ is used as input to the reward estimator.
- **Core assumption:** Realizability ($h^*_s \in \mathcal{H}_s$) and conditional independence (Assumption 2.1); sufficient uniform samples because only reachable states are retained.
- **Break condition:** Misspecification of $\mathcal{H}_s$ or low sample size can cause $\hat{h}_s$ to deviate from uniform even for homogeneous states, resulting in misclassification and over-estimation.

### Mechanism 3: Lipschitz Reward Surrogate
- **Claim:** A Lipschitz‑continuous surrogate $J(v,a)$ constructed from the posterior estimate produces proxy rewards robust to estimation errors and works for both heterogeneous and homogeneous states.
- **Mechanism:** Given $v \in \Delta[K]$, compute $\Delta(v) = \|v - \frac{1}{K}\mathbf{1}\|_\infty$. If $\Delta(v) \le \kappa/2$, output constant $c$ (homogeneous). If $\Delta(v) \ge \kappa$, apply a ramp function $G(v_a, \theta/M - \xi, \xi)$ (heterogeneous). For $\Delta(v) \in (\kappa/2,\kappa)$ linearly interpolate. Lipschitz constant $L = 4/\kappa + 1/\xi$ bounds misspecification effects.
- **Core assumption:** Separation ratio $\sigma = \theta(K-M)/M > 1$ ensures true posteriors for heterogeneous states deviate by at least $\kappa$; constant $c$ for homogeneous states is known.
- **Break condition:** Too small $\kappa$ yields large $L$ and sensitivity to posterior noise; incorrect $c$ introduces unrecoverable bias for homogeneous states.

## Foundational Learning
- **Contextual Markov Decision Processes:** Provides the framework where contexts are drawn per episode and transitions are context‑independent. It defines the problem structure and is essential for analyzing the algorithm.  
  *Quick check:* How does a contextual MDP differ from a standard MDP? What role does the context play in defining the task while keeping dynamics fixed?
- **Inverse Kinematics (Posterior Decoding):** Encodes the latent reward via the conditional distribution of actions given context, state, and feedback. This decoding is the key to turning indirect feedback into a usable reward signal.  
  *Quick check:* Why does the posterior deviate from uniform exactly when the terminal state is heterogeneous? Which quantities determine the magnitude of deviation?
- **Lipschitz Continuity and Smoothing:** The natural threshold‑based decoder is discontinuous and fragile to small errors; a Lipschitz surrogate bounds such errors and enables stable learning guarantees.  
  *Quick check:* In the piecewise definition of $J(v,a)$, why is the interpolation region $(\kappa/2, \kappa)$ necessary? What would happen if we used the discontinuous indicator directly?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Reachable State Identifier -> Inverse Kinematic Learner -> Lipschitz Reward Estimator -> Policy Learner

### Critical path
1. Offline pre‑processing: Learn homing policies for all terminal states (Algorithm 2) → filter reachable states $\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{H,\varepsilon}$ using empirical visitation.
2. Offline data collection: For each $s \in \hat{\mathcal{S}}_{H,\varepsilon}$, run $\hat{\pi}_s$ to gather $N_0$ tuples $(x,s,a,y)$ → train posterior models $\hat{h}_s$ (Algorithm 3).
3. Online phase: For each episode, predict reward $\hat{r}_t$ via $J(\hat{h}(x_t,y_t,s_{t,H}), a_{t,H})$, maintain an online regression oracle $\text{AlgSq}$ for reward prediction, compute policy from an occupancy‑measure optimization with a smoothed transition model, execute policy, observe feedback, and update models (Algorithm 4).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Exploration budget:** Smaller $\varepsilon$ expands $\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{H,\varepsilon}$ (better coverage) but increases pre‑processing and data collection cost; larger $\varepsilon$ reduces cost but may miss valuable states.
- **Surrogate smoothness:** Parameters $\kappa$ (fixed by problem constants) and $\xi$ (smoothing width) control the Lipschitz constant $L$. Larger $\xi$ reduces sensitivity to noise but introduces bias in $J$ for heterogeneous states.
- **Function class complexity:** Larger $\mathcal{F},\Phi,\mathcal{H}$ improve approximation but raise the squared‑loss regret $\text{Reg}_{\text{Sq}}$ and estimation error; the bound trades off with $\sqrt{\text{Reg}_{\text{Sq}}}$.

### Failure signatures
- **FM1:** Homing policies fail to reach target states (low empirical $\hat{p}_s$).  
- **FM2:** Inverse kinematics model $\hat{h}_s$ overfits (high validation loss) or remains near uniform for heterogeneous states.  
- **FM3:** Lipschitz estimator mis‑specified (wrong $\kappa,\xi$, or $c$).  
- **FM4:** Online policy does not improve (flat cumulative reward).  
- **FM5:** Transition estimates $\hat{P}_t$ inaccurate due to low counts $N(s,a)$.

### First 3 experiments
1. Reproduce synthetic MDP results (Section 5.1): Run Algorithm 1 with $H=3$, binary context. Verify average cumulative reward converges to near‑optimal (0.729) and decoded rewards track ground‑truth.
2. Ablate the reachable‑state threshold $\varepsilon$: Test $\varepsilon \in \{0.01,0.05,0.1\}$ and compare regret; expect U‑shaped curve due to exploration cost vs. coverage.
3. Test robustness to feedback noise: Corrupt feedback $y$ by flipping with probability $\rho \in \{0.0,0.1,0.2\}$; measure performance degradation; the Lipschitz estimator should tolerate small noise.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Tighter regret:** Can the bound be improved from $\tilde{O}(T^{3/4})$ to match the single‑step $\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})$ rate? The current gap stems from the overhead of learning homing policies for all terminal states.
- **Minimax lower bound:** What is the minimax regret for IGL in contextual MDPs with personalized feedback? The interplay of latent rewards, indirect feedback, and multi‑step decisions makes this non‑trivial.
- **Context‑dependent dynamics:** Does the framework extend to MDPs where transition probabilities depend on the context? The reachability analysis relies on context‑independent transitions.
- **Intermediate feedback:** Can the approach handle feedback observed after several steps (not just at the terminal state)? The current inverse kinematic posterior assumes a single terminal feedback and conditional independence.

## Limitations
- Unknown problem‑specific constants $M$, $\theta$, $c$ are required; in practice these may be unavailable and need to be estimated.
- Hyperparameters $\varepsilon$, $N_0$, $\gamma$ are provided only in asymptotic form; poor choices can lead to excessive exploration or unstable reward estimates.
- Computational overhead: Pre‑processing $O(S)$ homing policies and solving an occupancy‑measure convex program each episode may be prohibitive for large state spaces.
