# Sequence Repetition Enhances Token Embeddings and Improves Sequence Labeling with Decoder-only Language Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17585  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17585  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Sequence repetition (SR) enables decoder-only LMs to achieve state-of-the-art sequence labeling by inducing bidirectional context; Mistral-7B (r=4) reaches 83.79 avg micro F1, 7.7 points above the strongest encoder.

## Executive Summary
Decoder-only language models are inherently limited for sequence labeling because their causal mask permits only left-to-right attention, preventing tokens from accessing right-side context. This paper introduces sequence repetition (SR), a minimally invasive technique that repeats the input sequence *k* times before processing. The repeated sequence creates block-structured attention matrices where later repetitions can attend to all tokens in earlier repetitions, effectively providing bidirectional context while preserving the native causal mask. Fine-tuning with SR on four sequence labeling datasets (CoNLL03, ACE05, Rest14, NLU++) shows that SR-enhanced decoders produce superior token embeddings, outperforming encoder-only baselines (e.g., RoBERTa, ModernBERT) and unmasked decoders. The best model (Mistral-7B with *r*=4) achieves an average micro F1 of 83.79, a 7.7‑point gain over the strongest encoder. Additionally, extracting embeddings from intermediate layers (early exiting) maintains near‑peak accuracy while delivering up to 3.96× inference speedup.

## Method Summary
The SR technique concatenates the input sequence *k* times, yielding a length of *k·n* tokens. The decoder processes this repeated sequence under its standard causal mask. The resulting block attention matrix has dense blocks for all token pairs where the source repetition index *j* is less than the target index *i*, enabling tokens in the final repetition to attend bidirectionally to the entire sequence. During fine‑tuning, the model (e.g., Mistral‑7B) is adapted with QLoRA (4‑bit quantized adapters) to perform token‑level classification. At inference, token embeddings are extracted from the last repetition (positions *(k‑1)·n*+1 … *k·n*) and fed to a linear classification head. Early exiting is supported: the forward pass can stop at an intermediate layer *L*, using those embeddings to reduce compute.

## Key Results
- SR‑enhanced decoders generate token embeddings that surpass encoder‑only baselines and unmasked decoders across four SL datasets.  
- Mistral‑7B with *r*=4 achieves an average micro F1 of 83.79, 7.7 points above the strongest encoder (RoBERTa).  
- Increasing the repetition count consistently improves performance, contradicting earlier reports of degradation; gains saturate around *r*=4 for large models.  
- Early exiting (e.g., at layer 19 or 24) preserves near‑peak accuracy while providing up to 3.96× inference speedup.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: SR creates bidirectional attention blocks within the causal mask.**  
Repeating the input *k* times forms a block‑structured attention matrix. For any block where the target repetition index *i* exceeds the source index *j* (*i>j*), the entire block becomes dense because causal masking across repetitions allows tokens in later repetitions to attend to all tokens in earlier ones. As *k* grows, the proportion of such bidirectional blocks approaches 1, giving tokens in the final repetition near‑full bidirectionality. Fine‑tuning adjusts the model to exploit these dense blocks, enriching token representations. (Evidence: [abstract], Section 2; corroborated by neighboring work arXiv:2402.15449.) *Break condition:* Model capacity may limit benefit beyond a certain repetition; overfitting risk on small datasets (e.g., ACE05 with *r*=8).

**Mechanism 2: SR yields superior token embeddings via enriched context.**  
The dense blocks provide each token with right‑side information absent in standard causal decoding. Fine‑tuning re‑purposes the decoder to integrate this enriched context, effectively boosting per‑token processing capacity. The resulting embeddings better capture global sequence cues, as measured by sequence labeling performance. (Evidence: [abstract], Table 1 and Figure 2; compared to encoder‑only baselines and unmasked decoders.) *Break condition:* Suboptimal hyperparameters for higher *r* may cause instability; smaller models (≤2B) often fail to benefit from *r*>2.

**Mechanism 3: Early exiting retains accuracy while cutting inference cost.**  
Intermediate transformer layers already encode contextual information. Fine‑tuning up to an early exit layer *L* adapts these representations for the downstream task. Because SR supplies bidirectional context even in shallower layers, exiting early incurs minimal accuracy loss while skipping the quadratic attention cost of deeper layers. (Evidence: [abstract], Section 4.4–4.5; neighboring work on layer‑wise representations.) *Break condition:* Exiting too early (e.g., *L*≤9) degrades accuracy, especially with low *r*; speedup gains diminish as *r* grows because the repeated sequence dominates compute.

## Foundational Learning
- **Causal Masking in Decoder‑Only Transformers**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s core problem is the causal mask’s restriction to left‑to‑right attention, which limits token‑level tasks. Understanding its structure is essential to grasp why SR is necessary.  
  *Quick check:* "What does the causal mask matrix look like, and how does it shape the attention pattern for a given token?"

- **Sequence Labeling Requires Bidirectional Context**  
  *Why needed:* Sequence labeling (e.g., NER) assigns a label to each token based on the entire sequence; bidirectional context is known to improve performance, explaining why encoder‑only models dominate this setting.  
  *Quick check:* "Why would a model with only left‑to‑right context struggle with named entity recognition compared to a bidirectional model?"

- **Early Exiting for Efficient Inference**  
  *Why needed:* SR introduces computational overhead; early exiting offsets this by using intermediate layers. Knowing how and why earlier layers can be repurposed is key to the efficiency claim.  
  *Quick check:* "What is early exiting, and under what conditions might it preserve accuracy while reducing compute?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input Tokenizer → Repetition Module (repeat *k* times) → Decoder Backbone (with causal mask) → Exit Controller (select layer *L*) → Classification Head → Token Labels.  
  *(Training utilities: QLoRA adapters, cross‑entropy loss, AdamW optimizer.)*

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Tokenize input → sequence of length *n*.  
  2. Repeat sequence *k* times → new length *k·n*.  
  3. Forward pass through decoder up to chosen exit layer *L* (or all *N* layers).  
  4. Extract token embeddings for the last repetition (positions *(k‑1)·n*+1 … *k·n*).  
  5. Apply classification head → predict IOB2 tags.  
  6. Compute loss, back‑propagate through fine‑tuned layers (QLoRA adapters), update parameters.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Repetition count* (*r*): ↑ *r* → more bidirectional context → better accuracy but linear increase in sequence length → quadratic attention cost; benefits saturate around *r*=4 for large models.  
  - *Early exit layer*: Shallow *L* → faster inference but risk of under‑trained reps; optimal *L* tends to increase with *r*.  
  - *Adaptation method*: SR preserves architecture vs. unmasking (modifies attention mask); SR is less invasive but adds repetition overhead.  
  - *QLoRA*: 4‑bit quantization reduces memory but may limit fine‑tuning expressiveness; authors used *nf4* format.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Accuracy collapse for *r*>2 on small models (<2B params) indicates capacity limit.  
  - Large confidence intervals on ACE05 with high *r* suggest overfitting or dataset shift.  
  - Early exit at *L*≤ 9 yields > 2 F1 point drop, especially when *r* is low.  
  - If *r*=0, performance matches masked decoder, confirming SR’s necessity.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline establishment:** Fine‑tune each decoder with *r*=0 (no repetition) and evaluate on all datasets; confirm the limitation of causal mask.  
  2. **SR scaling sweep:** Test *r* ∈ {1,2,4,8} for all models; identify optimal *r* and observe performance saturation or degradation patterns.  
  3. **Early‑exit trade‑off:** For the best *r* from Exp 2, fine‑tune up to exit layers *L* ∈ {9,14,19,24}; measure accuracy and inference speed to determine the optimal operating point.

## Limitations
- Evaluation restricted to four English datasets and large models (>2B parameters); generalizability to low‑resource languages and smaller architectures is unclear.  
- Fine‑tuning uses 4‑bit QLoRA quantization, which may constrain representation quality; full‑precision results could differ.  
- Early exiting speedup analysis does not account for hardware‑specific factors (e.g., memory bandwidth); real‑world gains may vary.

## Confidence
- SR induces bidirectional attention blocks (theoretical guarantee): **High**.  
- SR‑enhanced embeddings outperform encoders/unmasked decoders (empirical across datasets): **High**.  
- Performance scales monotonically with repetition count: **Medium** (capacity‑limited, potential saturation).  
- Early exiting preserves accuracy with speedup: **Medium** (validated on specific models/layers).
