# Characterizing Human Semantic Navigation in Concept Production as Trajectories in Embedding Space

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05971
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05971
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Transformer-based trajectory metrics from concept production reliably distinguish clinical groups and semantic categories across four datasets.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a framework to quantify how humans navigate semantic space during concept production tasks by modeling the sequence of generated words as trajectories in transformer embedding space. By using cumulative embeddings—where each step’s vector is conditioned on all prior words—the authors capture the history-dependent nature of semantic search. Five geometric/dynamical metrics (distance-to-next, velocity, acceleration, entropy, distance-to-centroid) are extracted from these trajectories to characterize navigation strategy. Applied to neurodegenerative patients, swear fluency, and multilingual datasets, the metrics consistently discriminated clinical groups and semantic categories, with robustness across multiple embedding models.

## Method Summary
Participants produce ordered lists of words (e.g., animals, properties, swear words) for given concepts. For each trial, cumulative transformer embeddings are generated by encoding the concatenated sequence of produced items up to each step. Five trajectory metrics are computed: mean cosine distance to next item, entropy of binarized step distances, velocity and acceleration magnitudes, and mean cosine distance to the centroid of unique items. Statistical analysis uses GLMMs with participant and concept as random effects, and models are compared across different transformer embeddings (OpenAI, Google, Qwen, fastText) to assess robustness.

## Key Results
- Trajectory metrics reliably distinguished patient groups (PD, bvFTD) from controls and between semantic categories (e.g., animals vs. swear words).
- Patients showed higher velocity, acceleration, and entropy but lower centroid distance compared to controls; swear words induced higher entropy and tighter clustering.
- Cumulative embeddings improved discrimination for longer sequences; cross-model analyses showed highly correlated metric patterns, indicating robustness.
- FastText (non-cumulative) performed comparably for short sequences, suggesting flexibility in model choice.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Cumulative embeddings create context-sensitive trajectory points that capture the history-dependent nature of semantic search.
- **Mechanism:** Each embedding at step *t* is computed from all preceding words (1:*t*), aggregating semantic context built during retrieval.
- **Core assumption:** Semantic retrieval is dynamic, with successive items influenced by the sequence of previous retrievals (working memory dependence).
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] defines cumulative construction and states it “works best for longer trajectories”; [section 2.1] explicitly defines *x_t* as summarizing items 1:*t*; [corpus] neighbors do not specify cumulative construction.
- **Break condition:** If retrieval is memory-independent (e.g., random sampling), cumulative aggregation adds no signal and non-cumulative metrics would perform equally or better, especially for short sequences.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Geometric and kinematic metrics extracted from these trajectories quantify distinct dimensions of navigation strategy.
- **Mechanism:** Velocity/acceleration capture local directional changes; entropy and centroid distance characterize global variability and spatial focus.
- **Core assumption:** Semantic retrieval can be modeled as movement through a continuous geometric space where vector differences meaningfully correspond to cognitive shifts.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] lists the five metrics and notes they “capture both scalar and directional aspects”; [section 2.2] provides mathematical definitions; [section 3] shows consistent group differences; [corpus] neighbors use analogous geometric concepts but not identical metrics.
- **Break condition:** If embedding spaces are anisotropic or non-Euclidean, physics-inspired metrics would fail to correlate with cognitive states.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The observed metric patterns are robust to the choice of transformer embedding model.
- **Mechanism:** Different embedding models produce highly correlated metric scores across datasets, suggesting metrics reflect stable properties of human semantic navigation rather than model artifacts.
- **Core assumption:** Diverse language models learn sufficiently similar semantic geometries that core relational structures are preserved.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] reports “highly correlated metric patterns” across models; [section 3.5] presents Figure 6 with high cross-model correlations; [section 4] discusses cross-model robustness; [corpus] neighbor on “shared global and local geometry” supports convergent representation spaces.
- **Break condition:** If future models with radically different training objectives produce divergent metric signatures, robustness claim would be limited to current monolingual/text-only transformers.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Transformer Text Embeddings**
  - Why needed here: The pipeline depends on mapping words to continuous vectors where geometric relationships reflect semantic similarity. Understanding context-sensitive embeddings is crucial for cumulative aggregation.
  - Quick check question: *Why might a bidirectional encoder (e.g., Gecko) and a causal decoder (e.g., Qwen) produce similar trajectory metric patterns despite different attention mechanisms?*

- **Concept: Cumulative vs. Static Representation**
  - Why needed here: The key innovation is using history-dependent embeddings. Engineers must understand why *x_t = f(word_1, ..., word_t)* is necessary for modeling memory-dependent retrieval versus *x_t = f(word_t)*.
  - Quick check question: *For a 5-word sequence, how does the embedding for the 5th word differ conceptually between cumulative and non-cumulative approaches?*

- **Concept: Trajectory Metrics as Physics Analogs**
  - Why needed here: Metrics (velocity, acceleration, entropy, centroid distance) are defined by analogy to physical motion. Engineers must translate cognitive phenomena into their mathematical definitions to implement and interpret them correctly.
  - Quick check question: *What would a high average acceleration score indicate about a participant’s semantic search pattern, in plain cognitive terms?*

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Data ingestion → Embedding choice & cumulative toggle → Metric calculation → Statistical modeling
- **Critical path:** Data ingestion → embedding choice & cumulative toggle → metric calculation → statistical modeling. The cumulative toggle is an early branching decision with downstream consequences for all metrics.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Cumulative vs. Non-cumulative:** Cumulative captures context but increases computational cost; non-cumulative is cheaper but loses history. Paper finds cumulative better for N>~10, non-cumulative for very short sequences.
  - **Euclidean vs. Non-Euclidean:** Metrics assume Euclidean vector space; future work may need Riemannian metrics if embedding manifolds are curved.
  - **Model selection:** Tradeoff between speed/cost (fastText, small transformer) and representational richness (large proprietary models). Paper finds patterns consistent across models, allowing cheaper options.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Anisotropy artifacts:** If embedding space has uneven variance, centroid distance and velocity magnitudes become unreliable. Check: covariance matrix should be near-diagonal after ZCA.
  - **Short sequence instability:** For N<4, entropy and acceleration cannot be computed reliably. Avoid using these metrics for very short trials.
  - **Model-specific centroid shift:** Distance-to-centroid shows low inter-model correlation. If using this metric for cross-study comparison, ensure same embedding model is used throughout.
  - **API latency/rate limits:** Cumulative approach makes many sequential calls; implement batching or use local model to avoid throttling.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Sanity check:** For a single trial (e.g., “dog, cat, fish...”), compute cumulative embeddings manually for first 3 steps. Verify that *x_3* is not equal to the embedding of “dog cat fish” but should be numerically close to it.
  2. **Cumulative effect replication:** On a small subset, compute both cumulative and non-cumulative metrics. Confirm pattern: for longer sequences (N>10), cumulative metrics show larger group differences (higher Cohen’s *d*) as per Table 2.
  3. **Metric independence check:** Compute all five metrics for 100 random trials. Calculate correlation matrix. Expect: `distance_to_next` highly correlated with `velocity_mag`; `acceleration` moderately correlated with both; `entropy` independent of magnitude but correlated with variability of `distance_to_next`; `centroid_distance` potentially uncorrelated with others.

## Limitations
- The assumption that transformer embeddings faithfully represent human semantic similarity for geometric interpretation remains unverified.
- Cumulative aggregation has not been benchmarked against alternatives like recurrent neural networks or memory-augmented models.
- Findings are limited to fluency tasks and cross-sectional data, so generalizability to spontaneous speech or other semantic tasks is unknown.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence:** Reproducibility of group differences across models and datasets; unambiguous mathematical definitions of metrics.
- **Medium Confidence:** Interpretability of metrics as cognitive constructs; supported by clinical correlations but lacking direct behavioral validation.
- **Low Confidence:** Claim that cumulative embeddings are universally superior; paper shows mixed results but comprehensive comparison is absent.

## Next Checks
1. **Behavior-Metric Alignment:** Correlate trajectory metrics with independent behavioral measures (e.g., response time variability, switch counts) in the same trials to validate whether velocity/acceleration truly reflects search volatility.
2. **Ablation on Cumulative Construction:** For a subset of trials, compare cumulative embeddings against a sliding-window context model (e.g., last 3 words) to test whether the full history is necessary or if a fixed window suffices.
3. **Cross-Domain Generalization:** Apply the pipeline to a non-fluency dataset (e.g., picture naming or story recall) to assess whether the same metric patterns hold for different semantic retrieval contexts.
