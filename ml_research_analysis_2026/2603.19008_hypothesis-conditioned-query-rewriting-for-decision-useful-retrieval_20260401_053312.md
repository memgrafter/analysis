# Hypothesis-Conditioned Query Rewriting for Decision-Useful Retrieval

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19008
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19008
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: HCQR improves accuracy by 5.9 points on MedQA and 3.6 points on MMLU-Med over Simple RAG.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses a key limitation in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for decision-oriented tasks: retrieval guided by a single initial query often returns topically relevant but non-discriminative evidence, failing to support final answer selection. It introduces Hypothesis-Conditioned Query Rewriting (HCQR), a training-free, pre-retrieval method that first formulates a lightweight working hypothesis from the question and candidate answers, then rewrites this into three targeted queries to retrieve evidence that supports the hypothesis, distinguishes it from competing options, and verifies salient clues in the question. On medical QA benchmarks (MedQA and MMLU-Med), HCQR consistently outperforms single‑query RAG and stronger post‑retrieval baselines, improving average accuracy by 5.9 and 3.6 points respectively. An analysis using an LLM‑based judge shows HCQR substantially increases the Decision‑Useful Rate of retrieved contexts while reducing non‑useful retrieval, confirming that its gains stem from better alignment of first‑pass retrieval with the downstream decision.

## Method Summary
HCQR is a three‑stage pipeline. First, a Hypothesis Formulator uses an LLM to produce a provisional best guess and identify discriminating features and evidence needs from the question and multiple‑choice options. This hypothesis is not exposed to the final generator. Second, a Query Rewriter converts the hypothesis into three distinct queries: one seeking supporting evidence for the hypothesis, one seeking criteria that distinguish it from alternatives, and one verifying key clues in the question stem. Third, each query retrieves the top 5 documents using a dense retriever (MedCPT); the results are merged and deduplicated to form a final context set under a budget of 15 documents. This context, along with the original question and options, is fed to a final generator (LLM) to produce the answer. The method is training‑free, relies only on frozen LLM prompts, and is agnostic to the retriever and generator used.

## Key Results
- HCQR achieves average accuracy gains of +5.9 points on MedQA and +3.6 points on MMLU‑Med over Simple RAG across four model configurations (3B to 30B parameters).
- It consistently outperforms stronger post‑retrieval baselines like Rerank‑RAG and MAIN‑RAG.
- Context‑utility analysis (via LLM judge) shows HCQR significantly increases the Decision‑Useful Rate (ENTAILED+USEFUL) and reduces NOTUSEFUL retrieval, indicating better alignment with decision‑making needs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Hypothesis‑guided retrieval alignment
- **Claim:** HCQR improves retrieval quality by aligning first‑pass queries with the downstream decision boundary rather than broad topical relevance.
- **Mechanism:** The system forms a lightweight working hypothesis to identify discriminating features and expected evidence. The query rewriter then generates three targeted queries: support, distinction, and key features verification. This shifts retrieval from generic background gathering to focused evidence collection bearing directly on answer selection.
- **Core assumption:** A provisional answer hypothesis can predict what evidence would be decision‑useful; the final generator still reasons from retrieved context, so hypothesis quality is not critical.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and section 3.2 describe the hypothesis formulation and query roles. The lack of neighboring work with this exact design pattern (corpus) supports novelty.
- **Break condition:** If the hypothesis is systematically biased or misses the true decision boundary, rewritten queries may retrieve irrelevant evidence, degrading performance.

### Mechanism 2: Distraction reduction via pre‑filtered retrieval intent
- **Claim:** HCQR reduces “distracting context” by pre‑filtering retrieval intent, ensuring the final context set is more likely to contain decision‑useful passages.
- **Mechanism:** Three distinct, evidence‑seeking queries diversify retrieval intent in a structured way, increasing probability of hitting comparative or clue‑based evidence. The fused results statistically shift context composition toward ENTAILED/USEFUL and away from NOTUSEFUL.
- **Core assumption:** Decision‑useful evidence has a different linguistic/semantic signature (comparative language, diagnostic criteria, causal explanations) than background; targeted queries elicit this more reliably.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.2 shows HCQR increases ENTAILED and USEFUL rates while reducing NOTUSEFUL; Table 6 confirms complementary roles of each query type.
- **Break condition:** If the corpus lacks documents with explicit comparative criteria or clue‑verification facts, targeted queries may return equally non‑discriminative results.

### Mechanism 3: Training‑free pre‑retrieval design for broad robustness
- **Claim:** HCQR's training‑free, pre‑retrieval design allows combination with any retriever/generator, and gains are robust across model scales because it improves input to the generator.
- **Mechanism:** Hypothesis formulation and query rewriting use the same LLM as generator but are separable modules; rewritten queries are plain text usable with any retriever. The generator receives only original question and context, isolating improvement to evidence selection. This explains consistent gains across Llama and Qwen models.
- **Core assumption:** Final generator performance is bounded by context quality under a fixed budget; improving context yields proportional gains regardless of generator strength.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.5 notes same prompt for generator so differences attributable to retrieval; Table 1 shows gains across all model sizes; Table 7 indicates option‑exposure control yields only modest gains, highlighting hypothesis‑to‑query pipeline benefit.
- **Break condition:** If the generator is extremely weak at utilizing retrieved context, improved context selection may not translate to accuracy gains.

## Foundational Learning
- **Decision‑Useful Retrieval:** Shifts focus from “topical relevance” to “decision usefulness”—evidence must help distinguish between answer choices. Needed to interpret DUR and context labels. Quick check: For a multiple‑choice question about treating a newborn's foot deformity, a decision‑useful passage would compare treatment options or link specific clues to differentials, not just describe the deformity.
- **Pre‑Retrieval vs. Post‑Retrieval Optimization:** HCQR is pre‑retrieval (query planning); baselines include post‑retrieval (reranking, filtering). Post‑retrieval methods cannot recover discriminative evidence absent from the initial candidate pool. Quick check: What's the limitation of reranking if the first query returns only general disease descriptions?
- **Working Hypothesis as a Steering Signal (Not Final Answer):** The hypothesis formulator outputs a provisional guess and evidence needs, used to craft queries but not passed to the generator. This prevents answer anchoring and keeps retrieval‑focused. Quick check: Why might feeding the hypothesis directly to the generator be risky, and how does HCQR avoid it?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Hypothesis Formulator (H) → Query Rewriter (Q) → Retriever (R) and Fuser (Fuse) → Final Generator (G). H takes (question, options) and outputs hypothesis state h; Q takes (question, h) and outputs three queries; for each query, R retrieves top k=5 documents; Fuse merges unique documents under budget 15; G takes (question, options, context) and outputs answer.
- **Critical path:** Input (x, A) → H(x,A) → Q(x,h) → [R(q₁), R(q₂), R(q₃)] → Fuse() → C → G(x,A,C) → Output ˆy. The hypothesis h is internal; only x, A, C reach the generator.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Hypothesis quality vs. generator flexibility: Poor hypothesis may misdirect q₁/q₂, but q₃ (key features) hedges by targeting stem clues independent of guess.
  - Query count vs. budget: 3 queries (5 docs each) risk under‑filling budget (max 15) due to overlap, but provides higher intent diversity compared to 1 query with 15 docs.
  - Training‑free vs. learnable: HCQR uses frozen LLM prompts for simplicity and deployability; a learnable variant might optimize queries but requires training data.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. Hypothesis collapse: H outputs generic/incorrect guess and vague features → q₁/q₂ misaligned, q₃ may still work → DUR and accuracy drop.
  2. Retriever mismatch: Retriever insensitive to nuanced query differences → all queries return similar topical docs → no DUR gain over simple rewriting.
  3. Generator ignores context: Even with high‑DUR context, generator relies on parametric knowledge → high ENTAILED rate but low accuracy on ENTAILED subset (unlikely given results).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Ablate query roles: Run HCQR minus q₁, minus q₂, minus q₃ separately; expect all removals hurt performance (validates complementary roles).
  2. Option‑exposure control: Compare Rewriting+options (add options to multi‑query prompt) vs. HCQR−options (HCQR without options in H); expect HCQR−options > Rewriting+options, showing structured hypothesis state matters, not just seeing options.
  3. Retriever sensitivity test: Replace MedCPT with BM25; if HCQR gains diminish, gains are retriever‑dependent.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Hypothesis sensitivity:** How does the accuracy of the initial working hypothesis affect downstream decision‑useful retrieval and final answer accuracy? The paper notes dependence on hypothesis quality but does not quantify tolerance to errors. Resolution requires systematic perturbation experiments (e.g., forced incorrect guesses, missing features).
2. **Cross‑domain generalizability:** To what extent do HCQR's gains transfer beyond medical multiple‑choice QA to other domains (e.g., open‑domain QA, decision tasks without explicit options) and to models with stronger parametric knowledge? The evaluation is limited to medical MCQA, so applicability elsewhere is open. Resolution requires replication on diverse benchmarks and model scales.
3. **Judge reliability:** Can the LLM‑based judge for context‑utility analysis introduce systematic bias, and how do its labels correlate with human annotations? The judge (GPT‑4.1) is used without human validation, so label reliability is uncertain. Resolution requires human evaluation studies comparing judge labels to human judgments.
4. **Post‑retrieval method deficiencies:** Why do post‑retrieval selection methods (Rerank‑RAG, MAIN‑RAG) fail to match HCQR despite inspecting larger candidate pools? The paper suggests first‑pass alignment is key but does not isolate which aspects of HCQR's query rewriting are irreplaceable. Resolution requires error analysis comparing candidate pools and categorizing missed evidence.
5. **Query design optimality:** Under a fixed context budget, is the three‑query design optimal? Could fewer/more queries or alternative query‑intent structures achieve similar or better results with different fusion strategies? The paper uses three fixed roles and simple union fusion; the design space is unexplored. Resolution requires systematic evaluation of query count, roles, and fusion methods.

## Limitations
- Corpus details (sources, chunking) are not fully disclosed, hindering exact reproduction.
- Context‑utility labels rely solely on a single GPT‑4.1 instance without human validation.
- Evaluation is confined to medical multiple‑choice QA; cross‑domain robustness is unknown.

## Confidence
- HCQR increases Decision‑Useful Rate (DUR): High (directly measured and consistently improved).
- HCQR yields accuracy gains across model configurations: Medium (robust within medical domain but external validity untested).
- All three query roles are necessary: Medium (ablations show performance drops, but optimal weighting unexplored).

## Next Checks
1. **Retriever ablation:** Replace MedCPT with BM25 to test if gains are dependent on dense retrieval semantics.
2. **Cross‑domain replication:** Evaluate HCQR on non‑medical multi‑choice QA (e.g., MMLU general) to assess generalizability.
3. **Hypothesis oracle:** Implement an oracle formulator that always provides the true best guess; compare accuracy/DUR to standard HCQR to quantify sensitivity.
