# Intrinsic Lorentz Neural Network

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23981  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23981  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Achieves state-of-the-art among hyperbolic models and outperforms Euclidean ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 (95.36%) and CIFAR-100 (78.41%), with strong gains on genomic benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the inconsistency in existing hyperbolic neural networks that mix Euclidean operations or rely on extrinsic parameterizations. It introduces the Intrinsic Lorentz Neural Network (ILNN), a fully intrinsic architecture where all computations remain inside the Lorentz model of hyperbolic space. Key innovations include: (1) a point-to-hyperplane fully connected layer (PLFC) that replaces affine logits with closed-form Lorentzian distances to learned hyperplanes; (2) GyroLBN, a batch normalization that couples gyro-centering and gyro-scaling for stable and efficient training; and (3) a log-radius-aligned concatenation for convolutional feature fusion, along with Lorentz dropout. Experiments on CIFAR-10/100 and genomic datasets (TEB, GUE) demonstrate consistent improvements over Euclidean and prior hyperbolic baselines, validating the advantage of strict intrinsic design.

## Method Summary
ILNN replaces every Euclidean component in a neural network with an intrinsic Lorentz counterpart. The core layer, PLFC, parameterizes hyperplanes in the tangent space at the origin and computes logits as signed Lorentz distances from input features to these hyperplanes, thereby preserving geometric margins. For normalization, GyroLBN uses the closed-form Lorentzian centroid for centering and a gyro-scaling based on Fréchet variance, unifying the speed of Lorentz batch norm with the distributional properties of gyro-based norm. Convolutional blocks employ a log-radius-aligned concatenation (via a digamma-based scaling) to maintain the expected spatial radius when fusing feature maps, avoiding geometric drift. Biases are added via gyroaddition, and dropout operates by masking and re-projecting onto the hyperboloid. All modules respect the hyperboloid constraint \( \langle x, x \rangle_L = 1/K, x_0 > 0\). The network is trained with Riemannian optimizers (SGD or Adam) on a global curvature \(K=-1\). The design yields a fully intrinsic hyperbolic network that is both theoretically consistent and empirically effective.

## Key Results
- On CIFAR-10, ILNN attains 95.36% accuracy, surpassing Euclidean ResNet-18 by 0.22%.
- On CIFAR-100, ILNN reaches 78.41%, a 0.69% improvement over the Euclidean baseline.
- On genomic benchmarks, ILNN achieves 83.9 Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) on GUE core promoter detection (vs. 79.5 for HCNN-S) and 70.26 on TEB processed pseudogenes (vs. 68.30).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ILNN’s performance gains stem from three key innovations that preserve hyperbolic geometry throughout the network.

**Point-to-Hyperplane Fully Connected (PLFC).** Traditional hyperbolic classifiers (e.g., Lorentz Fully Connected) replace the Euclidean affine map with a tangent-space linear layer followed by exponential map, but this distorts decision margins (Theorem 2). PLFC directly uses closed-form Lorentz distances from features to learned hyperplanes, defined by parameters \((z_k, a_k)\) in the tangent space. The logit for class \(k\) is computed via Eq. (6) and the final output \(y\) is reconstructed exactly from these distances (Eq. 8b) and re-projected onto the manifold (Eq. 8c). This construction preserves the hyperbolic margin between classes (Theorem 2), aligning the decision function with the data’s intrinsic curvature. Degeneracy is avoided by the parallel transport parameterization.

**GyroLBN.** Standard Lorentz batch norm (LBN) uses the closed-form centroid for centering but ignores dispersion; GyroBN employs an iterative Fréchet mean, which is accurate but slow. GyroLBN bridges the gap: it centers using the exact Lorentzian centroid, then scales via \(\gamma \sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon}\) using the Fréchet variance, and finally adds a bias \(\beta\) by left gyroaddition (Eq. 10). All steps are intrinsic and avoid tangent-space approximations. This coupling yields faster convergence and higher accuracy than both LBN and GyroBN, as shown in Table 4. Instability may occur if the centroid denominator is near zero (e.g., antipodal data).

**Log‑Radius‑Aligned Concatenation.** Naively concatenating spatial Lorentz feature blocks increases the expected spatial radius, distorting subsequent layers. ILNN scales each block \(i\) by \(s(n,d_i) = \exp\bigl(\psi(n/2) - \psi(d_i/2)\bigr)\) (based on the digamma function \(\psi\)) so that the expected log‑radius matches that of the concatenated dimension \(n\). After scaling, the time coordinate is recomputed to satisfy the hyperboloid constraint. This preserves the geometric distribution across blocks, stabilizing training. The approximation may falter for extreme dimensions.

## Foundational Learning
*Concept: Hyperbolic Geometry (Lorentz Model)*  
- **Why needed:** All ILNN operations are defined on the hyperboloid \( \mathcal{L}_K^n = \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1} : \langle x, x \rangle_L = 1/K,\; x_0 > 0 \}\) with Minkowski inner product. Understanding the model, geodesic distance, and the constraint on the time coordinate is essential.  
- **Quick check:** Given \(x \in \mathcal{L}_K^n\), what constraint does its time coordinate satisfy, and what is \(\langle x, x \rangle_L\)?

*Concept: Gyrovector Spaces*  
- **Why needed:** Gyroaddition (\(\oplus\)) and gyroscaling (\(\odot\)) replace Euclidean vector addition and scaling, enabling intrinsic operations such as gyro‑bias and normalization. Gyrogroup algebra corrects for non‑associativity induced by curvature.  
- **Quick check:** What gyro‑operation corresponds to subtracting the batch mean in GyroLBN, and why is left gyroaddition used?

*Concept: Manifold Batch Normalization*  
- **Why needed:** Euclidean batch normalization uses arithmetic mean and variance; hyperbolic networks require Fréchet mean (Lorentz centroid) and dispersion. GyroLBN is a specific instantiation that couples both.  
- **Quick check:** In the flat‑space limit \(K \to 0\), what does GyroLBN’s update (Eq. 10) reduce to?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input → CNN Stem (Lorentz convolutions with log‑radius concatenation) → PLFC Head (point‑to‑hyperplane fully connected layer, optional gyro‑bias) → internal modules: GyroLBN after convolution blocks, Lorentz activations (spatial part only), Lorentz dropout → Output (Lorentz distances as logits)

**Critical path**  
1. Ensure all features lie on \(\mathcal{L}_K^n\) (hyperboloid constraint). Every module (conv, PLFC, GyroLBN) preserves this.  
2. Replace the Euclidean final fully connected layer with PLFC. This is the core change; it converts feature representations into curvature‑aware decision margins.  
3. Replace Euclidean or partially intrinsic batch normalization with GyroLBN for stable training.  
4. Integrate log‑radius concatenation in CNN blocks to maintain scale invariance across feature map concatenations.  
5. Tune curvature \(K\) (often fixed \(-1\)) and use manifold‑aware optimizers (RiemannianSGD/Adam).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **PLFC vs. LFC:** PLFC retains curvature‑awareness and margin preservation but requires per‑neuron distance computation; LFC is simpler but contracts margins. ILNN chooses correctness over speed.  
- **GyroLBN vs. LBN vs. GyroBN:** GyroLBN uses closed‑form centroid (fast) and gyro‑scaling (distribution preserving). It improves accuracy and reduces wall‑clock time compared to GyroBN (314 s → 169 s on CIFAR‑10) but may be less robust than iterative Fréchet mean for heterogeneous batches.  
- **Log‑radius concat vs. naive:** The digamma scaling is parameter‑free and preserves the hyperboloid constraint; naive concatenation biases radii, potentially requiring careful initialization.

**Failure signatures**  
- **Geometry violation:** Features exiting PLFC/GyroLBN with \(\langle x, x \rangle_L \neq 1/K\) or \(x_0 \leq 0\) → check re‑projection after dropout and time‑coordinate recomputation.  
- **Exploding gradients:** Particularly in PLFC distance computation if \(a_k\) or \(z_k\) grow large → use weight decay and Riemannian gradient clipping.  
- **Degenerate hyperplanes:** Denominator in Eq. 5 near zero → regularize \((a_k, z_k)\).  
- **Batch norm collapse:** For genomics tasks, running statistics must be disabled; otherwise MCC drops.  
- **Poor speed/accuracy tradeoff:** Using GyroBN with many Fréchet iterations increases fit time 2× with minimal gain → default to 2–5 iterations.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Swap the final FC layer of a baseline (HCNN or Euclidean ResNet) with PLFC. Compare test accuracy against the LFC baseline, expecting margin improvements especially on hierarchical splits.  
2. In a PLFC‑only model, replace LBN with GyroLBN. Monitor training loss and wall‑clock time, expecting faster convergence and higher final accuracy.  
3. Modify HCNN’s Lorentz convolution to use the log‑radius concatenation operator. Evaluate on tasks with large receptive fields (e.g., genomic sequences of length 1000), expecting reduced feature collapse and more consistent performance across kernel sizes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Why do PLFC layers yield superior classification accuracy compared to LFC layers?** The paper ablates and shows consistent gains but does not isolate whether the advantage stems from distance‑to‑hyperplane decision boundaries, margin preservation, or other geometric factors. Understanding this would clarify whether benefits generalize to other architectures and datasets with different hierarchical structures.  
- **Does the intrinsic design of ILNN scale efficiently to very deep networks and large‑batch training regimes?** All experiments use ResNet‑18 and moderate batch sizes (≤256). Manifold operations (exponential, logarithmic, centroid) may become a bottleneck at larger scales, and the reported efficiency gains might not hold.  
- **Is a fixed global curvature \(K = -1\) optimal for all layers and tasks?** The paper fixes \(K = -1\) throughout, while related work uses per‑layer or learnable curvatures. The impact of curvature adaptivity on representational capacity and training stability within an intrinsic framework remains unknown.  
- **Does the fully intrinsic design provide consistent benefits across all domains that employ hyperbolic representations (e.g., NLP, graphs)?** Evaluation covers vision and genomics but not standard NLP or a wide range of graph benchmarks. The transferability of ILNN’s gains across modalities is an open question.

## Limitations
- **Replication hurdles:** The paper omits exact command‑line flags and default parameters (e.g., weight decay, number of workers), and genomic data preprocessing details are not fully described, making faithful reproduction difficult without inspecting the released code.  
- **Lack of independent validation:** The work has not yet been widely cited (reference count 40, neighbor citation count 0), so its claims remain unverified by external groups and may not be compared against the latest Euclidean or hyperbolic baselines.  
- **Theoretical scope gaps:** The margin preservation guarantee (Theorem 2) applies only to the PLFC decision function, not to the entire end‑to‑end network where preceding layers’ approximations could still introduce geometric distortions. The “fully intrinsic” assumption depends on perfect implementation of every module.

## Confidence
- **High:** Core technical claims (PLFC definition, GyroLBN mechanism, log‑radius concatenation) are explicitly derived, ablated, and supported by experiments within the paper.  
- **Medium:** The assertion of a “fully intrinsic” design is contingent on correct and stable implementation of all components; external validation is required.  
- **Low:** The generality of the findings beyond the evaluated datasets (CIFAR, TEB, GUE) and the long‑term stability of training with strict intrinsic operations across diverse tasks remain uncertain.
