# CLiGNet: Clinical Label-Interaction Graph Network for Medical Specialty Classification from Clinical Transcriptions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22752
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22752
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Corrects a critical data leakage issue in the MTSamples benchmark, revealing that medical specialty classification is substantially harder than previously reported; CLiGNet achieves a macro-F1 of 0.279, with the label graph providing the largest single gain (+0.066 macro-F1).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a fundamental flaw in the widely used MTSamples benchmark for medical specialty classification, showing that prior results were inflated by severe data leakage from applying SMOTE oversampling before train-test splitting. The authors establish a corrected, leakage-free evaluation protocol and introduce CLiGNet, a novel architecture that integrates a Bio_ClinicalBERT text encoder with a two-layer Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) operating on a specialty label graph. The label graph encodes semantic and ontological relationships between specialties, significantly improving performance. While CLiGNet achieves state-of-the-art macro-F1 (0.279) on the corrected benchmark, the work highlights the inherent difficulty of the 40-class task and demonstrates a key trade-off: post-hoc probability calibration greatly reduces Expected Calibration Error (to 0.007) but at the cost of ranking performance.

## Method Summary
CLiGNet processes variable-length clinical transcriptions by first chunking them into 512-token windows (max 4 chunks) and encoding each with Bio_ClinicalBERT (first 6 layers frozen), mean-pooling the [CLS] vectors to obtain a document embedding. Separately, a static label graph is constructed for the 40 medical specialties: node features are mean BERT embeddings from ~30 training documents per specialty, and edges are created if the cosine similarity between node features plus a 0.20 ICD-10 chapter bonus exceeds a 0.30 threshold. This graph is processed by a two-layer GCN with ReLU and dropout 0.3 to produce enriched label embeddings. For classification, a per-label attention gate dynamically fuses the projected document embedding with each enriched label embedding. The model is trained with focal binary cross-entropy loss (γ=2.0) and inverse-frequency class weighting to handle the extreme 181:1 imbalance, without any oversampling. An optional calibrated variant (B8) applies per-label Platt scaling and threshold optimization on the validation set.

## Key Results
- Correcting data leakage in MTSamples by splitting before any oversampling reveals the true difficulty of 40-class specialty classification, drastically lowering previously reported accuracy.
- CLiGNet (B6) achieves the highest macro-F1 of 0.279 across seven baselines; the label graph GCN ablation (A1) causes a −0.066 macro-F1 drop, confirming it provides the single largest performance gain.
- Adding per-label Platt scaling (B8) yields well-calibrated probabilities (ECE=0.007) but reduces macro-F1 to 0.240, illustrating a clear calibration-versus-discrimination trade-off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Correcting data leakage by performing train-test splitting before any oversampling (e.g., SMOTE) yields a realistic, leakage-free benchmark that reveals the true difficulty of the 40-class medical specialty classification task.
- **Mechanism:** Applying SMOTE or any oversampling to the entire dataset before splitting creates synthetic minority-class samples that contain information from the future test set. This contaminates the training data with test-set statistical properties, inflating performance metrics. The correction involves a strictly stratified split *first*, then applying techniques like focal loss *only* on the training set to handle imbalance without altering the data distribution.
- **Core assumption:** Model evaluation must simulate real-world deployment where test data is unseen and unaffected by training pipeline manipulations. Data leakage violates this assumption.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "prior work on the widely used MTSamples benchmark suffers from severe data leakage caused by applying SMOTE oversampling before train test splitting... the true task difficulty is substantially higher than previously reported." [section 3.2] Details the leakage risk and establishes the corrected protocol: "Our benchmark applies stratified splitting *before* any data augmentation."

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Explicitly modeling the semantic and ontological relationships between medical specialties via a label graph processed by a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) provides a significant performance gain by allowing label representations to inform each other during classification.
- **Mechanism:** A graph is constructed where nodes are specialty labels. Node features are embeddings from a few training documents per specialty. Edges are created based on a hybrid criterion: cosine similarity of label embeddings + a bonus if specialties share an ICD-10 chapter. Two GCN layers propagate and transform information across this graph, enriching each label's representation with its neighbors' context. During classification, a per-label attention gate dynamically fuses the document representation with this enriched label representation.
- **Core assumption:** Medical specialties are not independent; they have inherent relationships (e.g., "Surgery" and "Orthopedic" share procedural contexts). Leveraging this structure as an inductive bias improves learning, especially in data-scarce regimes.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "GCN label graph provides the single largest component gain (increase of 0.066 macro-F1)." [section 4.2] Details graph construction: "Edges are established using a hybrid criterion combining semantic similarity and medical ontology... an edge is added if: *s_ij + b_ij* ≥ τ." [section 6.3] Ablation results: "A1: No GCN... ΔMa-F1 = -0.066".

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Using focal binary cross-entropy loss with inverse-frequency class weighting effectively handles the extreme class imbalance (181:1 ratio) without resorting to data-altering techniques like SMOTE, by focusing training on hard, misclassified examples, particularly from minority classes.
- **Mechanism:** Standard binary cross-entropy is weighted by inverse class frequency (α_k) to give more importance to rare classes. The focal loss component (1 - p_t)^γ further down-weights the loss contributed by well-classified examples (high p_t), forcing the model to focus on the hard-to-classify instances, which are often from minority specialties.
- **Core assumption:** In a long-tailed distribution, the majority of the loss comes from easy majority-class examples, which can drown out signals from minority classes. Modulating the loss based on example difficulty mitigates this.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "trained with focal binary cross-entropy loss to handle extreme class imbalance (181:1 ratio)." [section 4.5] Defines the loss: "L_focal = - (1/K) Σ α_k (1 - p_t,k)^γ log(p_t,k)". [section 6.3] Ablation "A2: No focal loss... ΔMa-F1 = -0.013", showing a consistent, if smaller, gain.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Data Leakage in ML Pipelines**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's core contribution is correcting a benchmark. Understanding how pre-splitting oversampling causes leakage is fundamental to interpreting *why* prior results were invalid and *why* the new benchmark is more difficult.
  - *Quick check question:* If you apply SMOTE to a dataset and then split it into train/test, why does the test set performance become optimistically biased?
- **Concept: Graph Neural Networks for Structured Label Spaces**
  - *Why needed here:* CLiGNet's primary innovation is the GCN label graph. One must understand how graph convolution propagates information between nodes to grasp how label relationships are modeled.
  - *Quick check question:* In a GCN layer, what does the normalized adjacency matrix (Equation 3) mathematically achieve in terms of aggregating neighbor information?
- **Concept: Focal Loss for Imbalanced Classification**
  - *Why needed here:* The dataset has a 181:1 imbalance. Standard loss functions would be dominated by majority classes. Focal loss is a key technique to address this without synthetic data.
  - *Quick check question:* What happens to the loss contribution of an example as its predicted probability *p_t* for the true class increases, given γ > 0 in the focal loss formula?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Raw clinical transcription -> Sliding window chunking (512 tokens, stride 128) -> Bio_ClinicalBERT encoding (frozen first 6 layers) -> Mean-pooled [CLS] vectors -> Document embedding (h_doc). Parallel: Training documents per specialty -> Mean BERT embeddings -> Label graph nodes (H⁽⁰⁾) -> GCN (2 layers, ReLU, dropout 0.3) -> Enriched label embeddings (H⁽²⁾). Fusion: h_doc projected to 256D -> Per-label attention gate (σ) using [h_proj || h_k⁽²⁾] -> Weighted sum z_k -> Linear head + sigmoid -> 40 independent predictions.
- **Critical path:** Text encoding (document representation) and label graph processing (static side-input) run in parallel, then fuse per-label; the label graph is built once from training data and does not update during training.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Calibration (Platt scaling + threshold search) improves probability reliability (low ECE) but sacrifices macro-F1; sliding windows handle long documents but truncate at 4 chunks, losing context for >1000-word notes; static label graph is simple but may not capture dynamic, task-specific relationships.
- **Failure signatures:** Systematic confusions between clinically similar specialties (e.g., General Medicine vs. Consult); rare classes (<20 samples) often achieve F1=0.0; accuracy drops for short documents (<200 words); Platt scaling reduces macro-F1 as it prioritizes calibration over ranking-optimized thresholds.
- **First 3 experiments:** 1) Reproduce leakage: Train a baseline with flawed (SMOTE-before-split) vs. corrected protocol; expect major performance drop. 2) GCN ablation: Compare full CLiGNet (B6) to no-GCN (A1); expect ~0.066 macro-F1 loss. 3) Calibration trade-off: Compare B6 (0.5 threshold) to B8 (Platt + threshold search); expect ECE drop and macro-F1 reduction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How does CLiGNet generalize to larger clinical datasets with more diverse and numerous medical specialties?** The study is limited to MTSamples (∼5k docs, 40 classes); validation on larger corpora like MIMIC-III is needed.
- **Can a dynamic label graph that evolves during training improve CLiGNet's performance compared to the static graph construction?** The current static graph may miss task-specific, evolving label dependencies; a learnable adjacency mechanism is a proposed future direction.
- **How do large language models (LLMs) compare to CLiGNet in few-shot clinical specialty classification settings?** The rapid emergence of LLMs suggests few-shot/zero-shot approaches may be competitive, but no comparison is provided.

## Limitations
- Missing explicit specialty-to-ICD-10 chapter mapping, which is critical for exact label graph reproduction.
- Ambiguity in the calibration procedure order (whether Platt scaling is applied before or after per-label threshold search).
- Evaluation restricted to MTSamples, limiting confidence in generalizability to larger, more diverse clinical corpora.

## Confidence
- **Data leakage correction:** High – The methodological flaw is clearly identified, and the corrected benchmark yields dramatically lower, more plausible performance.
- **GCN label graph benefit:** Medium – The ablation shows a large gain (+0.066 macro-F1), but no statistical testing or hyperparameter sensitivity analysis is provided.
- **Focal loss for imbalance:** Medium – The gain is modest (+0.013 macro-F1) and primarily benefits rare classes; effect may not transfer to other datasets.

## Next Checks
1. Rebuild the label graph using an external ICD-10 chapter mapping (e.g., from CMS) and compare CLiGNet's performance to the original; a stable gain would validate robustness.
2. Perform paired statistical tests (e.g., McNemar's) between CLiGNet (B6) and key ablations (A1, A2) on the same test set to confirm significance of improvements.
3. Assess the stability of the calibration trade-off by training B8 across multiple random validation splits, reporting variance in ECE and macro-F1.
