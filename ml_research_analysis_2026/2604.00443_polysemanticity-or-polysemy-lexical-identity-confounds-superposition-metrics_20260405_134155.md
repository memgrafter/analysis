# Polysemanticity or Polysemy? Lexical Identity Confounds Superposition Metrics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00443  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00443  
- **Reference count:** 33  
- **Primary result:** Sharing a word form (e.g., “bank” for financial vs. river senses) produces more neuron activation overlap than sharing a meaning with different words (e.g., “bank” vs. “institution”), revealing a lexical confound in polysemanticity metrics.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the standard interpretation of polysemanticity metrics in language models by demonstrating that much of the measured neuron overlap attributed to “superposition” (compression of unrelated concepts) is actually driven by lexical identity—neurons that fire for a specific word token regardless of its intended sense. Using a 2×2 factorial decomposition that crosses same/different word with same/different meaning, the authors show that same-word/different-sense pairs (PS) consistently exhibit higher activation overlap than different-word/same-meaning pairs (SYN) across nine models (110M–70B parameters). This lexical confound occupies a compact subspace (≤1% of dimensions) but harms downstream tasks: filtering it improves word sense disambiguation by 5–6 percentage points and makes knowledge edits more selective. The findings indicate that a substantial portion of measured polysemanticity reflects superficial lexical processing rather than meaningful concept compression, and this bias is correctable.

## Method Summary
The core method is a 2×2 factorial decomposition applied to MLP intermediate activations of language models. From a sense-annotated corpus (SemCor), the authors construct sentence pairs for four conditions: same word/same sense (SL), same word/different sense (PS), different word/same meaning (SYN), and different word/different meaning (CL). Overlap metrics (cosine similarity, Jaccard) are computed pairwise within each condition and averaged per word. The lexical contribution ratio \( R_{\text{lex}} = (\text{mean}_{\text{PS}} - \text{mean}_{\text{SYN}}) / (\text{mean}_{\text{SL}} - \text{mean}_{\text{CL}}) \) quantifies the confound. Neuron-level analysis classifies units as sense-selective or sense-blind via the Sense Selectivity Index (SSI). The lexical identity subspace (LIS) is identified by PCA on difference vectors between a word’s sense means and its synonyms’ means. Downstream validation uses word sense disambiguation and knowledge editing (ROME) with ablations of identified neuron groups.

## Key Results
- PS > SYN across all models and layers, confirming lexical identity drives more overlap than shared meaning.
- The confound occupies ≤1% of activation dimensions; removing 20–50 LIS dimensions reduces the PS–SYN gap by 37–51% and can reverse it.
- Filtering sense-blind neurons improves word sense disambiguation accuracy by 5–6 percentage points and increases editing selectivity (p=0.002).
- In sparse autoencoders, 18–36% of features are sense-blind, blending multiple senses of the same word.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Lexical identity inflates overlap
Substantial neuron overlap labeled as polysemanticity is caused by neurons that respond to a word’s surface form (e.g., “bank”) irrespective of its sense. These “sense-blind” neurons create high overlap between contexts where the same word appears with different meanings (PS condition), which standard metrics misinterpret as superposition. The 2×2 decomposition isolates this effect because PS consistently exceeds SYN across models. If PS ≤ SYN in any model/layer, the confound hypothesis fails.

### Mechanism 2: Compact lexical subspace
Form-related information resides in a low-dimensional subspace within the high-dimensional activation space. PCA on (word sense – synonym) difference vectors yields the Lexical Identity Subspace (LIS). Projecting out just 20–50 LIS dimensions (≤1% of space) monotonically reduces the PS–SYN gap by 37–51%; at 50 dimensions the gap reverses. If projection did not reduce the gap or required many dimensions, the compact-subspace claim would be undermined.

### Mechanism 3: Functional harm via sense-blind neurons
Sense-blind neurons cause collateral damage in interventions (ablation, editing) because they activate indiscriminately across senses. Ablating sense-selective neurons yields high specificity (6.6× more on GPT-2) while sense-blind ablation has minimal sense-specific effect. Removing sense-blind neurons improves downstream WSD and editing selectivity, proving a causal distinction. If ablating sense-blind neurons improved specificity similarly to sense-selective ones, the functional harm claim would be invalid.

## Foundational Learning

- **Polysemanticity (as compression artifact)**: The idea that a single neuron or feature activates for multiple unrelated concepts due to capacity limits in the model. Why needed: The paper questions whether measured polysemanticity truly reflects compression or is confounded by lexical identity. Quick check: If a neuron fires for both “river bank” and “savings bank,” is it compressing unrelated concepts or just processing the token “bank”?

- **Lexical identity confound**: The inflation of neuron overlap metrics by neurons sensitive to the surface word form rather than semantic sense. Why needed: This is the paper’s core construct that explains why polysemanticity scores may be misleading. Quick check: How would you design an experiment to separate the effect of shared word form from shared meaning in activation overlap?

- **2×2 factorial decomposition**: A methodological design crossing two factors—same/different word and same/different meaning—to disentangle lexical and semantic contributions to overlap. Why needed: Enables quantification of the lexical confound via the Rlex ratio. Quick check: Which two conditions (PS and SYN) are critical for comparing lexical vs. semantic drivers, and why?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input (sense-annotated sentences) -> Data preparation (filter words, build condition pairs) -> Activation extraction (MLP intermediate layers) -> Decomposition run (compute condition overlaps) -> Quantify confound (calculate Rlex) -> Neuron classification (SSI, form detectors) -> Validation (probes, ablation) -> Corrective analysis (LIS, adjusted score) -> Downstream test (WSD, editing) -> Outputs (Rlex values, neuron groups, LIS dimensions, performance deltas).
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Prepare dataset: filter polysemous words from SemCor, find WordNet synonyms for SYN, cap pairs at 200.  
  2. Extract MLP activations (last subword of target) across layers for all sentences.  
  3. Compute pairwise overlap metrics per condition, average per word, then across words.  
  4. Calculate \( R_{\text{lex}} \), confirm PS > SYN holds.  
  5. Classify neurons via SSI and independent form detectors (consistency × specificity).  
  6. Probe neuron groups for word-form vs. sense information; perform mean-ablation causal tests.  
  7. Compute LIS via PCA on (word – synonym) mean differences; test projection dose-response.  
  8. Evaluate WSD classifier and ROME editing with/without sense-blind neuron filtering.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Corpus choice: SemCor provides gold senses but is dated; CoarseWSD-20 validates on modern text but covers only nouns.  
  - SSI threshold: SSI > 2 gives high-precision selective neurons but low recall; lower thresholds risk contamination.  
  - Synonym requirement: SYN needs synonyms with ≥3 SemCor sentences, biasing toward frequent words and reducing coverage (~62%).  
  - Metric choice: Cosine similarity is primary; Jaccard and magnitude divergence complement.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Decomposition invalid: \( M_{\text{SL}} \leq M_{\text{PS}} \) or \( M_{\text{PS}} \leq M_{\text{SYN}} \) consistently across layers.  
  - No LIS effect: Projecting top-*k* LIS components does not monotonically reduce PS–SYN gap.  
  - Functional equivalence: Sense-blind ablation specificity matches sense-selective ablation.  
  - SAE artifact: Much larger SAE dictionaries (e.g., 4×) drastically reduce collision ratios, implying confound is SAE-specific.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate 2×2 decomposition on GPT-2 small for 10 polysemous words; plot condition means per layer; verify PS > SYN.  
  2. Compute \( R_{\text{lex}} \) and compare to token-embedding baseline (\( R_{\text{emb-lex}} \)); confirm MLP \( R_{\text{lex}} > R_{\text{emb-lex}} \) in autoregressive models.  
  3. Run single-word causal ablation (e.g., “bank”): classify neurons via SSI into selective vs. blind; ablate each group; compute sense-specific perplexity change and specificity; expect high specificity for selective, low for blind.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How can we remove the lexical identity confound from polysemanticity measurements without using sense labels?** Current \( R_{\text{lex}} \) adjustment requires sense annotations; an unsupervised correction would generalize broadly. Evidence: An algorithm that identifies the lexical identity subspace (e.g., via distribution of form detectors across many words) and shows unsupervised projection reduces the PS–SYN gap to near zero across models.
- **Do sense-blind neurons contribute to any model capabilities beyond representing word form?** They have little causal effect on sense disambiguation but might support other functions like language modeling robustness. Evidence: Ablating sense-blind neurons across diverse tasks (perplexity, QA, recall) and measuring performance changes, especially on inputs involving polysemous words.
- **Can SAE training be regularized to prevent the emergence of sense-blind features?** Current SAEs retain 18–36% sense-blind features. Evidence: Train SAEs with a “lexical-identity penalty” that discourages features from activating on multiple senses of the same word (using WordNet or synonymous contexts) and evaluate collision rates and downstream task fidelity.

## Limitations
- Exact word list and sentence pairs for the decomposition are not disclosed; independent reconstruction may yield different subsets and affect \( R_{\text{lex}} \) magnitude.
- SYN condition requires WordNet synonyms with ≥3 SemCor sentences, biasing toward high-frequency words and underrepresenting rare senses.
- LIS identification relies on PCA; alternative factorization methods (ICA, NMF) might produce different subspaces, questioning the ≤1% generality.
- Analysis restricted to MLP intermediate activations; attention and residual streams may exhibit different dynamics.

## Confidence
- Lexical confound exists and inflates polysemanticity metrics: **High** (cross-model consistency across 9 architectures, robust to metrics, PS > SYN always holds).
- LIS is compact and separable: **Medium** (dose-response shows monotonic gap reduction, but subspace depends on PCA and validated on only two models).
- Removing confound improves downstream performance: **Medium** (significant gains on WSD and editing, but evaluation limited to two tasks; broader transfer untested).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the 2×2 decomposition using an alternative sense inventory (e.g., OntoNotes 5.0) to rule out corpus-specific biases.
2. Replace PCA with a different subspace identification method (e.g., Independent Component Analysis) and verify that removing those dimensions reduces the PS–SYN gap to a similar extent.
3. Test the utility of filtering sense-blind neurons across a diverse suite of tasks (e.g., HellaSwag, RTE, SST-2) to assess generalizability beyond WSD and knowledge editing.
