# RECLAIM: Cyclic Causal Discovery Amid Measurement Noise

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20585
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20585
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** RECLAIM enables causal discovery in cyclic latent graphs with noisy indirect measurements, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines on synthetic data and recovering biologically relevant cycles on the Sachs dataset.

## Executive Summary
RECLAIM tackles causal discovery under two challenging conditions: cyclic causal structures and indirect (noisy) measurements. Existing methods typically assume acyclicity and direct observation. RECLAIM employs an expectation‑maximization (EM) framework combined with residual normalizing flows to maximize the likelihood of observed measurements, handling both Gaussian additive noise and linear measurement systems. The method provides theoretical consistency guarantees and demonstrates strong empirical performance. On synthetic benchmarks, it surpasses baselines (NODAGS, DCDI, Anchored‑CI) in AUPRC and SHD, notably under high noise and large graph sizes, and shows robustness to the number of cycles and nonlinearity. On the real protein signaling dataset (Sachs), RECLAIM achieves an SHD of 19, matching the best baseline and recovering a biologically plausible feedback cycle (Raf ↔ Mek) that acyclic methods cannot.

## Method Summary
RECLAIM formulates a latent variable causal discovery problem where the true variables follow a cyclic structural causal model (SCM) and are observed only through noisy measurements. An EM algorithm alternates between inferring the latent variables (E‑step) and updating model parameters (M‑step). The E‑step uses Sampling Importance Resampling (SIR) with a Gaussian proposal to approximate the posterior over latent states. The M‑step maximizes a surrogate Q‑function that lower‑bounds the observed‑data log‑likelihood. Causal mechanisms are parameterized as contractive neural networks (spectrally normalized to have Lipschitz constant < 1) to ensure invertibility of the forward map (I − f) and enable tractable computation of log‑determinants via a power‑series expansion with Hutchinson trace estimation. Measurement noise parameters are identifiable under an intervention coverage condition: each latent node must be intervened upon at least once. For linear measurement systems, isolating projections are used to solve for noise variances. A sparsity‑inducing L1 penalty on the adjacency mask encourages sparse graphs.

## Key Results
- RECLAIM consistently outperforms NODAGS, DCDI, and Anchored‑CI on synthetic data across varying noise levels, graph sizes, and numbers of cycles.
- On the Sachs dataset, it achieves an SHD of 19 and recovers a biologically relevant feedback cycle (Raf ↔ Mek) that acyclic baselines miss.
- The method is robust to increased nonlinearity and the presence of multiple cycles.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: EM Likelihood Maximization**  
RECLAIM optimizes the observed‑data likelihood via EM. The E‑step approximates the posterior over latent X using SIR with a Gaussian proposal. The M‑step updates parameters (neural weights, graph mask, noise variances) by maximizing a surrogate Q‑function that lower‑bounds the log‑likelihood, guaranteeing non‑decreasing observed‑data likelihood (Wu, 1983). Core assumptions: the EM bound holds and the model class contains the ground truth. Evidence: derivation in Section 3.2; related EM methods for cyclic graphs. Break conditions: poor E‑step proposal or insufficient samples bias the Q‑function; insufficient model expressiveness leads to suboptimal convergence.

**Mechanism 2: Contractive Networks for Invertibility**  
Each causal mechanism is a contractive neural network with spectrally normalized weights (Lipschitz constant < 1). This ensures (I − f) is a diffeomorphism, guaranteeing a unique stationary distribution for the cyclic SCM (Banach fixed point). The log‑determinant of the Jacobian is computed via a convergent power series:  
`log|det J(I − f)(X)| = -∑_{m=1}^∞ (1/m) Tr( (J_f(X))^m )`  
Combined with Hutchinson trace estimation and Russian roulette truncation, it yields an unbiased estimator. Evidence: Section 3.4, Appendix A; related work on invertible residual networks. Break condition: if spectral normalization fails (Lipschitz ≥ 1), (I − f) may become non‑invertible, causing NaN or divergence.

**Mechanism 3: Measurement Noise Identification**  
Under Condition 2 (every latent node is intervened upon at least once), noise variances become identifiable. For Gaussian additive noise (p = d), variance decomposition gives σ²ᵢ = Var(Yᵢ) − Var(Xᵢ). For linear measurements (p ≥ d), the method samples projection vectors *t* such that they isolate each latent variable (A_{−i}ᵀt = 0, a_iᵀt ≠ 0) and solves a least‑squares problem for σ² (eq. 16). Theorem 4 shows that for generic full‑rank A, a set of projections with full rank exists. Evidence: Section 3.3, Proposition 3, Theorem 4, Algorithm D.1. Break conditions: missing interventions violate Condition 2; a degenerate measurement matrix A yields rank‑deficient projections and unstable noise estimates.

- **Expectation‑Maximization (EM) for latent variable models.**  
  *Why needed:* The observed‑data likelihood marginalizes out latent X; EM provides an iterative scheme to optimize parameters by alternating between inferring X and updating the model. RECLAIM uses SIR due to nonlinear mechanisms.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map (plain text):**  
  Interventional targets & measurements → Measurement estimator → ( noise variances ϕ )  
  Latent model (contractive NN + Gumbel‑mask) → Likelihood module (log‑density via power‑series)  
  Sampler (E‑step: SIR) → Surrogate Q‑function → Optimizer (M‑step: Adam) → Updated parameters  
  (Iterate EM loop until convergence → threshold mask → final graph)
- **Critical path:**  
  Intervention data → noise variance estimation → EM loop (E: SIR → compute importance weights → Q; M: Adam update of NN weights & mask) → repeat → threshold adjacency mask.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - SIR sample size *S*: larger *S* reduces Q‑variance but increases compute.  
  - Power‑series truncation depth: more terms reduce bias in log‑determinant; Russian roulette allows stochastic truncation with minimal bias.  
  - Gumbel‑Softmask: enables differentiable edge probabilities but introduces small bias; temperature annealing required.  
  - Measurement redundancy (linear case): higher *p* improves noise estimation but enlarges least‑squares problem.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Noise estimation failure: ELBO plateaus high; check Condition 2 coverage.  
  - Non‑invertible forward map: NaNs in log‑determinant; inspect spectral norms (≥ 1 indicates violation).  
  - Poor posterior: SIR weights highly concentrated → effective sample size low; indicates proposal mismatch.  
  - Graph sparsity issues: final graph density far from expected; tune λ.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Synthetic GAN noise (vary σ_min): RECLAIM degrades gracefully in AUPRC with increasing noise, widening gap over baselines.  
  2. Linear measurement system (vary *p*): RECLAIM’s AUPRR rises sharply when *p* > *d*, while baselines remain poor.  
  3. Real Sachs dataset: RECLAIM recovers a biologically plausible feedback cycle (Raf ↔ Mek) that acyclic methods miss.

## Limitations
- Real‑world applicability is hindered by unspecified Sachs data preprocessing (missing values, intervention‑to‑latent mapping) and reliance on strong identifiability conditions (e.g., Condition 2).  
- Performance is sensitive to implementation choices: EM convergence depends on SIR sample size, power‑series truncation, spectral norm enforcement, and mask thresholding; hyperparameters are not fully detailed.  
- Theoretical guarantees assume exact contractivity and complete intervention coverage; violations in practice may lead to failure or biased estimates.
