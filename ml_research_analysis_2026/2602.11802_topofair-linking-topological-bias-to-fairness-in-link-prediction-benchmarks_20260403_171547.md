# TopoFair: Linking Topological Bias to Fairness in Link Prediction Benchmarks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11802
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11802
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A comprehensive bias taxonomy and controlled graph generator reveal that topological measures (especially assortativity) strongly predict fairness disparities in link prediction, but current fairness-aware methods remain sensitive to non-assortativity biases.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the critical gap in understanding how diverse topological biases in social graphs—beyond simple homophily—affect fairness in link prediction (LP). It introduces TopoFair, a benchmarking framework comprising a fine-grained taxonomy of structural bias measures (node-level and graph-level) and an extended Barabási–Albert graph generator. This generator allows independent control over four key biases: homophily, sensitive attribute imbalance, community structure, and degree heterogeneity. Evaluating over 1,000 synthetic graphs per use case (Opinion, Friendship, Collab) and multiple LP models (classical and fairness-aware), the study finds that structural bias measures explain a large portion of fairness outcome variance (R² > 0.8), with assortativity alone accounting for over 75% in some cases. However, fairness-aware methods prove sensitive to non-assortativity biases (e.g., heterogeneity, information unfairness) even when homophily is fixed, highlighting a lack of robustness and the need for fairness evaluations that consider broader topological contexts.

## Method Summary
The core method is a two-part framework. First, an extended Barabási–Albert (BA) graph generator is parameterized to independently vary four structural biases: sensitive attribute imbalance (α), homophily intensity (β), community structure (via anchor nodes), and degree heterogeneity (via Gamma-distributed edge counts). Second, a bias taxonomy defines node-level and graph-level measures (assortativity, heterogeneity, information unfairness, power-law exponent, etc.). For each generated graph, LP models are trained on an 80% train edge split, produce rankings of candidate links, and are evaluated on test edges using accuracy (Hit@10, AUC) and fairness metrics (Statistical Parity, Equal Opportunity). The bias measures for each graph are then regressed (via Random Forest) against the fairness outcomes across thousands of graphs to identify which topological properties most strongly predict unfairness. Control experiments partition graphs by fixed assortativity to isolate the impact of other biases.

## Key Results
- Structural bias measures predict fairness disparities with high explanatory power (R² consistently > 0.8) across LP models and use cases.
- Assortativity is the dominant predictor, alone explaining over 75% of variance in Statistical Parity for Opinion and Collaboration graphs.
- Fairness-aware LP methods (Fairwalk, Debayes, CrossWalk, FairAdj, FLIP) remain sensitive to non-assortativity biases (heterogeneity, information unfairness, power exponent) even when homophily (assortativity) is held constant, revealing limited robustness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Controlled Graph Generation via Extended BA
The extended BA generator decouples four bias dimensions by modularly altering the preferential attachment rule. Sensitive attribute assignment is controlled by α; homophily intensity β biases attachment probability toward same-attribute nodes; anchor nodes create community structure; and a Gamma distribution governs the number of edges added per step (m′), controlling degree heterogeneity. This allows systematic isolation of each bias's effect on LP fairness.
- **Break condition:** Non-linear interactions between parameters (e.g., α inadvertently altering degree distributions beyond Gamma's variance) would confound causal attribution by making biases inseparable.

### Mechanism 2: Bias Taxonomy as Predictors of Fairness
By quantifying disparities in a unified set of topological measures (from node-level neighbor attribute diversity to graph-level assortativity and information flow unfairness) and regressing them against fairness metrics, the framework empirically demonstrates that unfairness is not monolithic but driven by specific, measurable structural properties. The consistently high R² (>0.8) validates the taxonomy's comprehensiveness for the studied LP tasks.
- **Break condition:** Omission of critical topological dimensions (e.g., higher-order motifs, temporal dynamics) would cause R² to drop significantly across diverse graph sets, indicating the taxonomy's incompleteness.

### Mechanism 3: Isolating Non-Assortativity Sensitivity
Leveraging the strong predictive power of assortativity (RQ1), the analysis partitions generated graphs into high/low assortativity groups. Within each group, graphs that differ significantly in other biases (heterogeneity, information unfairness, power exponent) are compared. Significant fairness metric differences between these subsets for the same LP model prove that methods are independently affected by non-assortativity factors.
- **Break condition:** If assortativity is perfectly collinear with other biases across the generated parameter space (e.g., varying β always changes heterogeneity in lockstep), the partitioning would fail to create cleanly separated subsets, invalidating the sensitivity claim.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Graph Topological Metrics (e.g., Assortativity, Heterogeneity)**
  - *Why needed:* These are the independent variables in the bias taxonomy. Assortativity measures same-attribute edge prevalence; heterogeneity measures neighbor attribute diversity per node.
  - *Quick check:* For a node whose neighbors all share its sensitive attribute, its heterogeneity score is 0 (no diversity).
- **Concept: Link Prediction as a Ranking Task with Dyadic Fairness**
  - *Why needed:* LP models produce ranked candidate edges. Fairness metrics (SP, EO) are computed on these ranked lists to compare treatment across sensitive groups.
  - *Quick check:* Equal Opportunity in LP measures whether true positive edges are ranked equally well for nodes in both sensitive groups.
- **Concept: Controlled Graph Generation via Preferential Attachment**
  - *Why needed:* The extended BA generator's parameters (α, β, anchor, γ) map directly to intended structural biases, enabling causal experiments.
  - *Quick check:* Increasing the homophily parameter β directly increases the probability of attaching to nodes with the same sensitive attribute, raising graph assortativity.
- **Concept: Information Unfairness**
  - *Why needed:* Captures disparities in how easily information (or random walks) reaches nodes of different groups, a bias beyond simple edge counts.
  - *Quick check:* High information unfairness indicates one group's nodes are systematically harder to reach from the rest of the graph via random walks.
- **Concept: Power-law Exponent in Degree Distributions**
  - *Why needed:* Degree heterogeneity (variance in node degrees) is a key topological bias; the power-law exponent characterizes this.
  - *Quick check:* A lower power-law exponent indicates a heavier tail and greater degree heterogeneity (more high-degree hubs).

## Architecture Onboarding
1. Graph Generator (takes n, α, β, m, anchor, γ) -> G, S
2. Bias Metric Calculator (takes G, S) -> bias vector ω(G)
3. LP Training Pipeline (takes G_train) -> ranking function f
4. Evaluation Module (takes f, G_test, S) -> fairness & accuracy scores
5. Analysis Engine (correlates ω(G) with scores across corpus)

- **Critical path:** Generator (1) creates graphs -> Bias Calculator (2) and LP Pipeline (3) run in parallel per graph -> Evaluation (4) aggregates results -> Analysis (5) performs regression and subset tests.
- **Design tradeoffs:** The generator prioritizes interpretability and independent bias control over perfect fidelity to any single real graph. This enables causal attribution but means synthetic graphs are approximations. The analysis uses Random Forest for robustness to non-linearities but sacrifices linear coefficient interpretability.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Generator: Bias measures (e.g., assortativity) do not vary monotonically with intended parameter (β).
  - Analysis: R² scores are low (<0.5) for most model/use-case combos, indicating missed bias dimensions.
  - Evaluation: All fairness-aware methods show no sensitivity to non-assortativity biases in subset analysis (all % changes near zero).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Parameter Sanity Check:** For Opinion use-case, sweep β and plot resulting assortativity. Verify monotonic increase and range overlaps real Polblogs value (Table 5).
  2. **Bias Prediction Replication:** For Collab use-case, generate ≥100 graphs, compute bias measures, train Random Forest to predict SP/EO for N2V and CrossWalk. Verify R² > 0.8 and assortativity as top feature (Figure 2).
  3. **Isolated Bias Test:** For Friendship, generate two subsets at fixed β: (a) high assortativity & low heterogeneity, (b) high assortativity & high heterogeneity. Train CrossWalk on both, compare SP@10 distributions via Mann-Whitney U test; expect p < 0.05.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can structural bias measures and the TopoFair graph generation framework be extended to handle multivalued and multivariate sensitive attributes?
- **Open Question 2:** How can fairness‑aware link prediction methods be made robust to non‑assortativity structural biases (e.g., degree heterogeneity, neighborhood heterogeneity, information flow disparities) when homophily is controlled?
- **Open Question 3:** To what extent do the findings about topological bias and fairness in link prediction generalize to other graph learning tasks (e.g., node classification, graph classification, recommendation)?
- **Open Question 4:** How faithfully does the extended Barabási–Albert generator in TopoFair capture the diversity of real‑world network topologies relevant to fairness, and what modifications might be needed to improve realism while preserving bias controllability?

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter ambiguity:** Exact settings for all LP models (e.g., Node2Vec p/q, SVD rank, NMF initialization) are unreported, possibly affecting result replication.
- **Evaluation details missing:** Unspecified edge split strategy and negative candidate sampling for ranking impact metric calculations.
- **Generator realism gap:** For the Friendship use-case, generated graphs still fail to match real-world high mean degree despite adjustments; the anchor configuration for Collab/Opinion is also incomplete.

## Confidence
- **Extended BA generator bias control:** *Medium* – Relies on parameters not interacting non-linearly; break if collinearity prevents clean subset partitioning.
- **Taxonomy predictive power (R² > 0.8):** *High* – Robust across models and use cases; assortativity dominance is consistent.
- **Fairness methods’ sensitivity to non-assortativity biases:** *Medium* – Depends on generator decoupling holding in subset analysis; sensitivity could be confounded by residual collinearity.
