# AgentLeak: A Full-Stack Benchmark for Privacy Leakage in Multi-Agent LLM Systems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11510
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11510
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Multi-agent systems reduce final-output leakage but increase total exposure to 68.9% due to unprotected internal channels (C2, C5), and output-only audits miss 41.7% of violations.

## Executive Summary
AgentLeak is the first full‑stack benchmark for privacy leakage in multi‑agent LLM systems, measuring data exposure across all seven communication channels—including internal inter‑agent messages and shared memory. Evaluating 4,979 traces from five production LLMs in coordinator‑worker topologies reveals that while multi‑agent configurations lower final‑output leakage (C1), total system exposure rises to 68.9% because internal channels (C2, C5) lack privacy controls. Consequently, output‑only audits fail to detect 41.7% of violations, and no existing defenses protect these internal pathways.

The benchmark uses 1,000 scenarios spanning four domains (healthcare, finance, legal, corporate) and a three‑tier detection pipeline (regex, structured PII extraction, LLM‑as‑judge) to quantify leakage. It establishes a new standard for evaluating privacy in multi‑agent deployments and highlights the urgent need for architectural safeguards on internal communication.

## Method Summary
AgentLeak comprises 1,000 structured scenarios (JSON) that specify a task, a sensitive data vault, an allowed set of permissible fields, tools, and topology (single‑agent or multi‑agent). The benchmark runs across four frameworks (LangChain, CrewAI, AutoGPT, MetaGPT) and five LLMs (GPT‑4o, GPT‑4o‑mini, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.3 70B, Mistral Large), capturing all seven privacy channels via framework adapters that emit normalized JSONL traces.

A three‑tier detection pipeline identifies leakage: Tier 1 matches explicit canaries via regex; Tier 2 extracts structured PII (SSN, credit cards, ICD‑10); Tier 3 uses an LLM judge (Qwen‑2.5‑7B) with a semantic similarity threshold τ=0.72 to catch paraphrases. Metrics include Channel Leakage Rate (CLR), Exact Leakage Rate (ELR), Attack Success Rate (ASR), and Task Success Rate (TSR). The full evaluation produced 4,979 traces.

For reproduction, a minimal configuration can limit scenarios to 10, use CrewAI, and enable inspection of inter‑agent messages and memory. However, full replication requires API keys for five models and may cost $400–600.

## Key Results
- Multi‑agent setups reduced final‑output leakage (C1) to 27.2% from 43.2% in single‑agent, but total system exposure rose to 68.9% due to internal channels.
- Inter‑agent messages (C2) leaked at 68.8%, 2.5× higher than C1; the pattern C2 ≥ C1 held for all five models and all four domains.
- Output‑only audits miss 41.7% of violations; existing defenses (output sanitizers, guardrails) reduce C1 but have 0% effect on C2/C5.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Multi‑agent architectures introduce internal communication channels (C2: inter‑agent messages, C5: shared memory) that lack privacy controls, causing a substantial increase in total system exposure despite lower final‑output leakage.
- **Mechanism**: Task delegation and shared memory transmit full context—including sensitive data from the vault—without sanitization. Frameworks do not filter these internal pathways by default, whereas external outputs (C1) often receive safety‑aligned filtering.
- **Core assumption**: Current multi‑agent frameworks (LangChain, CrewAI, AutoGPT, MetaGPT) provide no built‑in mechanisms to restrict or monitor data propagation through inter‑agent messages or memory.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "multi‑agent configurations reduce final output leakage (C1: 27.2% vs 43.2% in single‑agent) but introduce unmonitored internal channels that raise total system exposure to 68.9%"
  - [section] Section III.B defines the seven channels, distinguishing external (C1, C3, C4, C6, C7) from internal (C2, C5). Section II.A reviews frameworks and notes the absence of default message sanitization or memory access controls.
  - [corpus] Neighboring work (e.g., MAGPIE [4], Information‑Theoretic Privacy Control [1]) also identifies inter‑agent communication as a privacy risk, but AgentLeak is the first to quantify leakage across all channels at scale.
- **Break condition**: If framework developers implement built‑in privacy guards for C2 and C5 (e.g., field‑level access control, message redaction), internal leakage rates could approach external rates. The consistent C2 ≥ C1 pattern across all tested models suggests this is not currently happening.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Output‑only auditing—focusing exclusively on final user‑facing outputs (C1)—fails to detect 41.7% of privacy violations because it ignores leaks through internal channels.
- **Mechanism**: Many execution traces produce a safe C1 while sensitive data escapes via C2 or C5. These “hidden leakage” cases (H1) would pass any audit that inspects only C1, creating a false sense of compliance.
- **Core assumption**: Organizations and existing benchmarks predominantly rely on final‑output inspection for privacy validation.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "output‑only audits fail to detect 41.7% of violations"
  - [section] Section VI.E, Finding 3: "41.7% of violations occur in internal channels while the final output passes checks" (Table 10). The H1 rate is above 40% in three domains (Healthcare 62.4%, Finance 44.9%, Legal 34.9%).
  - [corpus] Prior benchmarks (AgentDojo [4], PrivacyLens [5], TOP‑Bench [16]) all audit only final outputs or model responses, confirming the output‑only paradigm. The corpus shows no full‑stack alternative before AgentLeak.
- **Break condition**: If auditing practices expand to include internal channel monitoring (e.g., mandatory logging and scanning of inter‑agent messages and memory states), the H1 miss rate could drop. Current tools, however, do not support this.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The pattern C2 ≥ C1 holds universally across all five evaluated LLMs and all four domains, confirming inter‑agent messages as the primary vulnerability.
- **Mechanism**: Safety‑aligned models apply stronger privacy filters to user‑facing outputs (C1) due to training and RLHF, but treat internal coordination messages (C2) as ordinary conversation, leading to over‑sharing. Shared memory (C5) also exhibits elevated leakage.
- **Core assumption**: Model safety training focuses on external boundaries (user inputs/outputs) and does not extend to internal agent‑to‑agent communication.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Across all five models and four domains, the pattern C2 ≥ C1 holds consistently"
  - [section] Table 13 and Figure 9 show per‑model rates: e.g., GPT‑4o C2=76.8% vs C1=17.2%, Claude‑3.5‑Sonnet C2=28.1% vs C1=3.3%. Section II.C notes that all surveyed defenses target external channels only.
  - [corpus] The neighbor "1‑2‑3 Check" [2] proposes a multi‑agent design to improve privacy, implicitly acknowledging that standard multi‑agent setups leak. Other neighbors (e.g., GAMA [6]) also target internal privacy.
- **Break condition**: If models are fine‑tuned or prompted to distinguish internal vs. external channels—or if frameworks enforce differential privacy on C2—C2 rates could fall below C1. The current data shows no such mitigation in practice.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Data Minimization (GDPR Art. 25, HIPAA “minimum necessary”)  
  **Why needed**: The benchmark operationalizes this legal principle into an empirical test: only fields in the allowed set may appear in any channel. The vault‑to‑allowed median ratio of 9.7:1 forces agents to select a tiny subset from a large sensitive pool.  
  **Quick check**: What is the exact condition that defines a leakage event in AgentLeak’s formalism? (Answer: ∃ vᵢ ∈ (V \ A) and ∃ Cⱼ ∈ C such that sim(vᵢ, content(Cⱼ)) > τ.)

- **Concept**: Contextual Integrity Theory (Nissenbaum 2004)  
  **Why needed**: It grounds the privacy definition: information flows are appropriate if they conform to contextual norms. The *allowed set* encodes the norm for each task context (e.g., scheduling requires time slots but not diagnoses).  
  **Quick check**: How does the paper differentiate between internal exposure, effective leakage, and real harm? (Answer: Internal exposure = processing within agent; effective leakage = unauthorized transmission across trust boundary; real harm = actual exploitation by an unauthorized entity.)

- **Concept**: Multi‑Agent Leakage Channels (C1–C7)  
  **Why needed**: The seven‑channel taxonomy is the lens through which the entire evaluation is conducted. Understanding the split between external (C1, C3, C4, C6, C7) and internal (C2, C5) is essential to interpret the results and the “audit gap.”  
  **Quick check**: Which channels are classified as internal? (Answer: C2 inter‑agent messages and C5 shared memory.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Benchmark Scenarios -> Framework Adapters -> Defense Plug‑ins -> Three‑Tier Detection Pipeline -> Metrics Engine

### Critical Path
1. Load scenario → instantiate agents/tools according to topology.
2. Execute task while adapters capture all channel events (C1–C7).
3. Write raw traces to immutable JSONL store.
4. For each trace segment, run Tier 1 → Tier 2 → Tier 3 (stop at first detection).
5. Aggregate per‑trace leakage flags per channel.
6. Compute metrics and generate report (tables, Pareto fronts).

### Design Tradeoffs
- Coverage vs. cost: Full instrumentation of all seven channels across 1,000 scenarios is computationally heavy; the large‑scale evaluation focused on C1, C2, C5 and sampled C3, C6.
- Detection precision: Tier 3 (LLM judge) introduces a 7.4% FNR but is necessary to catch semantic paraphrases (82% of leaks). Tier 1–2 provide high‑precision anchors.
- Privacy vs. utility: Strict internal redaction (protecting C2/C5) reduces leakage dramatically but lowers Task Success Rate by ~4.7 points (Section VII, Defense Interceptor Experiment).

### Failure Signatures
- C2 leakage rate ≫ C1 across all models (e.g., GPT‑4o 76.8% vs 17.2%) and domains.
- Output‑only defenses (e.g., output sanitizers) reduce C1 by up to 98% but show 0% reduction on C2/C5 (Table 8, Figure 6).
- H1 rate (output‑safe but internal‑leaked) at 41.7%—nearly half of violations invisible to output audits.
- In the dataset, every C5 leak co‑occurs with a C2 or C1 leak (Table 9), suggesting memory leakage often requires prior channel compromise.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate core finding**: Run 50 scenarios (10 per domain) on GPT‑4o and Claude‑3.5 in both single‑agent and 2‑agent coordinator‑worker modes. Verify: (a) C1 lower in multi‑agent, (b) C2 leakage > C1, (c) total exposure (C1 ∨ C2 ∨ C5) higher in multi‑agent.
2. **Defense evaluation**: Apply three configurations (vanilla, privacy prompt, output sanitizer) to 100 multi‑agent traces per model. Measure CLR for C1 and C2/C5 separately. Expect: output sanitizer nearly eliminates C1 but leaves C2/C5 untouched.
3. **Interceptor prototype**: Build a lightweight filter that removes any vault field not in the allowed set from inter‑agent messages (C2) and memory writes (C5). Deploy on 120 traces (30 per domain, balanced A0/A1/A2). Measure Δ internal leakage and Δ TSR. Expect large leakage drop (from ~30% to ~2%) and a TSR decrease of ~4–5 points.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How do different multi‑agent topologies (e.g., peer‑to‑peer, hierarchical with >2 agents) affect privacy leakage rates across internal channels like inter‑agent messages (C2) and shared memory (C5)?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] From Section VII.D: "We used the coordinator‑worker topology with two agents. Real‑world deployments with hierarchical, peer‑to‑peer, or larger topologies may exhibit different leakage profiles."
- **Why unresolved**: The study only evaluated coordinator‑worker topologies; alternative communication patterns could alter data flow risks, but this remains unmeasured.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Run AgentLeak across varied topologies (e.g., 3+ agents, peer networks) using the same scenarios and models, then compare C2/C5 leakage rates and total exposure.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Does the high rate of semantic leakage (82% via Tier 3) persist in multilingual scenarios, and how do language and cultural norms impact the detection pipeline's accuracy?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] From Section VII.D: "We tested only English‑language scenarios. Multilingual deployments could show different leakage profiles: model performance varies by language, NER coverage for non‑English PII formats has gaps, and cultural expectations around what counts as 'sensitive' differ."
- **Why unresolved**: The LLM judge threshold was calibrated on English data; non‑English PII formats and cultural privacy norms may change leakage patterns and false positive/negative rates.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Extend AgentLeak to include non‑English scenarios, recalibrate detection thresholds per language, and measure cross‑language leakage rates and judge performance.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can framework‑level selective disclosure mechanisms (e.g., field‑level access controls on inter‑agent messages) reduce C2 leakage below 31.5% without causing >5‑point drops in task success rate (TSR)?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] From Section VII.E: "Privacy‑Aware Coordination Protocols. Selective disclosure at the framework level... open questions around formally verifiable data‑flow policies and dynamic workflow management." And from Section VII: prototype interceptor reduced leakage to 2.4% but TSR fell 4.7 points.
- **Why unresolved**: The proof‑of‑concept interceptor showed utility loss; it is unknown if more granular, policy‑driven disclosure can better balance privacy and utility.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implement and evaluate selective disclosure protocols (e.g., based on allowed sets) in major frameworks using AgentLeak, measuring C2 leakage, TSR, and policy enforcement robustness.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Is the observed lower internal channel leakage in safety‑aligned models (e.g., Claude‑3.5‑Sonnet) causally linked to alignment techniques, or does it correlate with other factors like training data or model size?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] From Section VIII: "Safety‑aligned models achieve lower leakage... yet no model eliminates it: the vulnerability is architectural, not model‑specific. (The correlation with safety alignment is suggestive but not causal: we cannot isolate it from other architectural differences...)" 
- **Why unresolved**: The paper shows model variance but cannot disentangle alignment from confounders; causality remains speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Conduct ablation studies comparing aligned vs. non‑aligned variants of the same base model, controlling for size and data, or analyze internal representations to identify alignment effects on coordination behavior.

## Limitations
- High API cost ($400–600) for full evaluation and lack of guidance on a minimal, statistically valid subset.
- Synthetic scenarios may not fully represent real‑world multi‑agent complexities or adversarial conditions.
- Benchmark limited to four frameworks and coordinator‑worker topologies; other architectures remain untested.

## Confidence
- Internal channels are the primary vulnerability (C2 ≥ C1 pattern): High
- Output‑only audits miss 41.7% of violations: Medium
- Multi‑agent systems reduce final‑output leakage but increase total exposure: High
- No existing defenses effectively protect internal channels: Medium
- AgentLeak is the first full‑stack benchmark: High

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the study with at least two additional multi‑agent frameworks (e.g., Microsoft AutoGen, OpenAI Swarm) to assess generalizability of internal channel leakage.
2. Perform a human audit on 100 traces flagged as H1 (output‑safe but internally leaked) to validate the LLM‑judge’s accuracy and classification reliability.
3. Implement a full interceptor that sanitizes inter‑agent messages (C2) and shared memory (C5) across all models and domains; measure the trade‑off between leakage reduction and task success rate compared to the prototype.
