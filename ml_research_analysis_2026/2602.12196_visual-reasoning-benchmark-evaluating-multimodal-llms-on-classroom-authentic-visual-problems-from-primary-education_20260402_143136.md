# Visual Reasoning Benchmark: Evaluating Multimodal LLMs on Classroom-Authentic Visual Problems from Primary Education

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12196
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12196
- **Reference count:** 7
- **Primary result:** Top MLLMs achieve 76-78% accuracy on VRB, but most models near chance (25%) with a pronounced "spatial ceiling" where performance on dynamic operations (folding, rotation, reflection) drops 5-9 percentage points relative to static skills like counting.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the Visual Reasoning Benchmark (VRB), a dataset of 701 authentic, minimal-text multiple-choice questions from primary school exams in Zambia and India. The benchmark is designed to evaluate multimodal large language models (MLLMs) on real-world visual reasoning without relying on textual cues. Results show a significant performance gap: proprietary models like Gemini-3.0 reach 76-78% accuracy, while many models perform near random chance (25%). A core finding is a "spatial ceiling"—models handle static skills (counting, scaling) relatively well but fail on dynamic spatial transformations (folding, reflection, rotation). Performance also degrades by ~8 percentage points on images with moderate photocopy noise, indicating poor robustness. The work highlights fundamental limitations in current MLLMs' ability to perceive and reason over spatial structures in authentic, cluttered educational imagery.

## Method Summary
The VRB dataset comprises 701 unedited JPEG images sourced from national primary-level exams. Each question is a multiple-choice problem with minimal embedded text. A verified human consensus answer key was established, and every item was annotated with one of six task categories (e.g., Matching, Pattern Completion) and ten spatial skill tags (e.g., Counting, Rotation, Folding). Models are evaluated in a zero-shot, single-turn setting: they receive one image and a standardized prompt, then must output the correct letter choice (A-D). Primary analysis measures per-item accuracy, with secondary breakdowns by task, skill, and image error severity (categorizing noise from photocopying artifacts like faded lines or misalignment). The evaluation uses bootstrap confidence intervals and an average marginal effects regression to isolate the impact of individual skills while controlling for model and task.

## Key Results
- Top proprietary models (Gemini-3.0) achieve 76-78% accuracy, while most models, especially open-weight ones, cluster near the random baseline of 25%.
- A "spatial ceiling" exists: static skills (Counting: +1.4pp, Scaling: +5pp) have positive marginal effects on accuracy, while dynamic operations cause large drops (Folding: -9pp, Reflection: -5.8pp, Rotation: -5pp).
- Accuracy declines by approximately 8 percentage points on images with "Medium" error severity (moderate noise), with the steepest decline observed for the highest-performing models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Perceptual Bottleneck in Spatial Representations
- **Claim:** Current MLLMs exhibit a "perceptual bottleneck" where they can detect discrete visual objects but fail to maintain structural integrity through spatial transformations.
- **Mechanism:** The vision encoder likely produces a representation optimized for object classification or region proposal rather than preserving affine-invariant geometric properties. This leads to breakdowns when reasoning requires mentally rotating, reflecting, or folding an object, as the internal representation cannot reliably track the object's identity across transformations.
- **Core assumption:** Visual features are aggregated into a global or bag-of-objects representation that loses precise relational and topological information necessary for dynamic spatial operations.
- **Evidence anchors:** The "jagged frontier" where static skill performance is higher but dynamic operations cause major drops; marginal effects analysis showing Folding (-9pp), Reflection (-5.8pp), and Rotation (-5pp) as largest negative predictors; weak direct evidence but tangential support from benchmarks like TangramPuzzle and GSR-BENCH.
- **Break condition:** Introduction of a differentiable, geometry-preserving module (e.g., neural renderer, spatial transformer network) trained on large-scale transformation datasets should narrow the static-dynamic skill gap.

### Mechanism 2: Overreliance on Heuristics and Textual Shortcuts
- **Claim:** MLLMs rely excessively on residual textual cues and patterned visual heuristics rather than constructing a faithful spatial model, leading to brittle performance on authentic, minimal-text images with noise.
- **Mechanism:** During web-scale training, models learn strong statistical correlations between image regions and language tokens. In VRB's minimal-text setting, these shortcuts are unavailable or misleading, causing the reasoning chain to decouple from the true visual ground truth. This brittleness manifests as performance collapses with moderate image noise.
- **Core assumption:** The next-token prediction objective favors exploiting easy, correlated signals over building robust, grounded spatial representations.
- **Evidence anchors:** Models can leverage linguistic cues effectively but struggle with spatial structures; accuracy declines by 8pp on images with moderate noise, steepest for top models; the paper states "without a solid grasp of what it is seeing, the model’s reasoning becomes decoupled from the visual ground truth."
- **Break condition:** Training regimes that force representation alignment (e.g., contrastive objectives between visual reasoning steps and transformations) or process-aware metrics that penalize invalid reasoning steps regardless of final answer.

### Mechanism 3: Mismatch Between Compositional Tasks and Single-Pass Architecture
- **Claim:** The "jagged frontier" stems from a mismatch between the compositional, multi-step nature of many visual reasoning tasks and the predominantly feedforward, single-pass generation architecture of current MLLMs.
- **Mechanism:** Tasks like "Match (Process)" require sequentially applying transformation rules and maintaining working memory of intermediate states. The autoregressive decoder may lack an explicit, iterative reasoning loop or persistent world model, causing errors to accumulate or the model to "forget" earlier visual constraints.
- **Core assumption:** Standard transformer-based MLLMs are optimized for simultaneous perception and shallow reasoning, not deep, multi-hop spatial planning.
- **Evidence anchors:** "Match (Process)" is the worst-performing task for most top models, with near 35pp gaps between best and worst tasks for some open-source models; the paper mentions "cognitive load of rule induction" and navigating spatial transformations; neighboring benchmarks (MARBLE, ORIGAMISPACE) emphasize multi-step spatial planning as a distinct unsolved challenge.
- **Break condition:** Dedicated, differentiable spatial reasoning module with iterative refinement (e.g., recurrent controller, program synthesis interpreter) that can propose, execute, and verify intermediate visual states.

- **Concept:** Spatial Reasoning Skill Taxonomy (Static vs. Dynamic Operations)
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's core finding is the "spatial ceiling" distinction. This taxonomy is essential to interpret the skill marginal effects (Section 4.2.2) and understand why skills like rotation are harder than counting.
  - **Quick check:** Why might "counting" (a static skill) be easier for an MLLM than "folding" (a dynamic skill), based on how vision encoders typically operate?
- **Concept:** Benchmark Design for Authenticity & Minimal Text
  - **Why needed here:** VRB's methodological contribution is its use of unedited, minimal-text images from LMIC exams. This design exposes limitations (jagged frontier, noise vulnerability) and differentiates VRB from curated multimodal math benchmarks.
  - **Quick check:** How does using unedited, photocopied images with "moderate" noise change evaluation compared to a clean, curated dataset?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input (Minimal-text image + prompt) -> Vision Encoder (e.g., SigLIP, CLIP-ViT) -> Cross-Modal Adapter/Fusion -> LLM Backbone (e.g., Gemini, GPT, Llama) -> Output head (token prediction) -> Extracted Answer (A-D)

**Critical path:** The integrity of spatial information must survive the Vision Encoder → Fusion → LLM processing chain. The breakage likely occurs at the Fusion step (lossy projection) or within the LLM's inability to perform explicit spatial transformations on the fused representation.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Static vs. Dynamic Features:** High-resolution patch tokens may help with static counting but increase computational cost without improving mental rotation. Tradeoff between token count and richness of encoded spatial relations.
- **Noise Robustness:** Training on synthetic distortions could improve robustness but might degrade performance on clean academic images. Optimal curriculum for LMIC-authentic noise is unknown.
- **Open vs. Proprietary:** On-device open models face severe parameter/accuracy tradeoffs. Achieving frontier accuracy may require trillion-parameter models unsuitable for local hosting.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Skill-specific collapse:** >25pp accuracy drop on `Folding`/`Reflection`/`Rotation` items relative to `Counting`.
- **Noise sensitivity:** >5pp drop from "No Error" to "Medium" error severity images.
- **Task volatility:** Intra-model accuracy range >20pp across the 6 task categories.
- **Jagged frontier pattern:** High overall score masks catastrophic failure on specific skill-task combinations (e.g., "Match (Process)" + `Rotation`).

**First 3 experiments:**
1.  **Skill Ablation:** Evaluate a SOTA model (e.g., Gemini-3.0 Flash) on a held-out VRB subset where only one skill tag is present. Measure accuracy per skill to confirm the marginal effects profile.
2.  **Noise Robustness Test:** Take a subset of "No Error" images, synthetically apply the "Minor" and "Medium" error types defined in Appendix B (e.g., Gaussian blur for faded lines, slight rotation for misalignment). Re-evaluate and quantify accuracy decay.
3.  **Process-Aware Intervention:** Use a model with "thinking" mode (e.g., Claude Opus 4 Thinking). Analyze generated reasoning chains for `Folding` questions. Count frequency of valid geometric inference steps vs. guesswork or incorrect state descriptions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1.  **Why the spatial ceiling for dynamic operations?** Why do MLLMs struggle specifically with folding, reflection, and rotation while static skills remain intact? The paper identifies the pattern but not the root cause: is it vision encoder limitations, training data absence, lack of geometric inductive bias, or decoder inability to simulate?
2.  **True reasoning vs. pattern matching?** To what extent are correct answers driven by faithful spatial reasoning versus superficial heuristics? VRB scores only final accuracy, not reasoning process, making it unclear if models execute mental transformations or exploit spurious correlations.
3.  **Noise sensitivity of top models?** Why do highest-performing models show greater accuracy degradation on noisy images? The paper speculates reliance on "precise pixel matching" but provides no direct evidence on whether their representations are brittle.
4.  **Interactive evaluation gap?** Would a multi-turn version of VRB (hints, dialogue) reveal different failure modes? Single-turn benchmarks may not predict a model's ability to track misconceptions or generate scaffolds in conversational tutoring.
5.  **Open vs. proprietary performance gap?** What drives the persistent gap (e.g., Gemini 78% vs. most open models <60%)? Is it training data scale/quality, architecture, reasoning-specific fine-tuning, or inference-time scaling like chain-of-thought?

## Limitations
- **Data Access:** The 701 exam images are private, blocking independent replication and broader testing.
- **Prompt Exactness:** The precise standardized prompt wording is unreported; deviations in reproduction could affect model performance.
- **Causal Attribution:** While marginal effects isolate skill impacts, they cannot definitively prove architectural flaws versus training-data biases.
