# Slack More, Predict Better: Proximal Relaxation for Probabilistic Latent Variable Model-based Soft Sensors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.11473
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11473
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: KProxNPLVM significantly outperforms eight state-of-the-art baselines on three industrial soft‑sensing datasets, achieving higher R² and lower RMSE/MAE/MAPE (p < 0.05).

## Executive Summary
Industrial soft sensors rely on probabilistic latent variable models to predict quality variables from process measurements. Traditional approaches use amortized variational inference (VI), which approximates the posterior with a neural network but introduces an amortization gap that degrades accuracy. This paper introduces KProxNPLVM, a method that replaces the KL‑based VI objective with a Wasserstein‑distance proximal operator. The resulting KProx algorithm implements a velocity‑field particle dynamics in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) to directly approximate the true posterior without amortization. Theoretical convergence guarantees are provided. Extensive experiments on three real industrial datasets demonstrate substantial improvements over eight SOTA baselines, with statistically significant gains in R², RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. Ablation studies confirm that both the KProx latent inference and the Wasserstein‑based training are essential components.

## Method Summary
KProxNPLVM employs a two‑stage alternating training loop. In the **decoder training** phase, for each training sample `(x, y)`, the KProx algorithm (Algorithm 1) is applied: starting from ℓ particles drawn from the prior, it performs T gradient steps using a velocity field that combines the decoder log‑likelihood gradient, the prior log‑density gradient, and a repulsive term derived from an RKHS approximation of the log‑density of the current particle set. The final particles approximate the posterior `p(z|y)`. The decoder parameters θ are then updated by ascending the log‑likelihood of `y` given these latent particles. In the **encoder training** phase, a dataset of `(x, latent particles)` pairs is compiled. The encoder network `q_φ(z|x)` is trained to match the distribution of these particles by minimizing a Sinkhorn‑regularized 2‑Wasserstein distance (Algorithm 2). This decoupled procedure avoids the amortization error while still learning an efficient encoder for online inference.

## Key Results
- KProxNPLVM achieves higher R² and lower RMSE/MAE/MAPE compared to eight state‑of‑the‑art baselines on all three industrial datasets.
- The improvements are statistically significant (p < 0.05) across Debutanizer Column (DBC), Carbon‑Dioxide Absorber (CAC), and Catalysis Shift Conversion (CSC) datasets.
- Ablation studies verify that removing either the KProx latent inference or the Wasserstein‑based encoder loss degrades performance, confirming the necessity of both components.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The key insight is to treat the intractable posterior inference as an infinite‑dimensional optimization problem and relax it via a proximal operator based on the Wasserstein distance. This yields a particle‑based dynamics that directly refines an approximation of the posterior without relying on a parametric encoder. The RKHS formulation provides a tractable way to compute the necessary velocity field using kernel gradients, while the alternating training allows a fast encoder to be learned for deployment. By sidestepping amortization, the method eliminates the approximation error inherent in standard VI, leading to more accurate latent variable estimates and ultimately better predictive performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Variational Inference (VI)**: Approximates Bayesian posterior with a simpler family by maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO). *Why needed*: Exact inference is intractable for complex models. *Quick check*: Derive the ELBO and explain the role of the KL divergence term.
- **Amortized Variational Inference**: Uses a neural network to map data x directly to variational parameters. *Why needed*: Scales to large datasets by sharing statistics across examples. *Quick check*: Recognize that amortization introduces a gap between the variational optimum per data point and the shared encoder.
- **Wasserstein Distance**: Optimal transport metric that accounts for geometry of the underlying space. *Why needed*: Provides a smoother, more informative discrepancy than KL, especially for multimodal distributions. *Quick check*: Compare Wasserstein‑2 to KL in terms of sensitivity to support mismatches.
- **Proximal Gradient Methods**: Optimize non‑smooth objectives by alternating between a gradient step on the smooth part and a proximal step on the non‑smooth part. *Why needed*: Guarantees convergence for objectives like `f + λg`. *Quick check*: Given `min f(x) + λ||x||₁`, write the proximal operator (soft‑thresholding).
- **Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)**: A function space induced by a positive‑definite kernel, enabling simple linear operations in a high‑dimensional feature space. *Why needed*: Allows representing the velocity field `∇log Q_t(z)` via kernel mean embeddings. *Quick check*: State the reproducing property `⟨f, k(·,x)⟩ = f(x)`.
- **Sinkhorn Algorithm**: Computes an entropically regularized optimal transport plan efficiently via iterative scaling. *Why needed*: Provides a differentiable approximation of the Wasserstein distance for training the encoder. *Quick check*: Describe how adding `ϵ·H(plan)` to the transport objective affects sparsity and gradient computation.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Data `(x, y)` → KProx inference (Algorithm 1) → latent particle set `Z = {z_i}`; `Z` serves two purposes: (i) Decoder `p_θ(y|z)` log‑likelihood gradient → update `θ`; (ii) Encoder `q_φ(z|x)` samples compared to `Z` via Sinkhorn loss → update `φ`. At test time: `x → q_φ(z|x) → z → p_θ(y|z) → ŷ`.

**Critical path**:  
1. For each batch, run KProx to obtain `Z` for every `(x, y)`.  
2. Accumulate decoder gradients using `Z`.  
3. Collect pairs `(x, Z)` for encoder training.  
4. Minimize Sinkhorn loss between `q_φ(z|x)` samples and `Z` to update `φ`.  
5. Repeat until convergence.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Fixed KProx iterations `T` vs. adaptive stopping; larger `T` improves posterior approximation but increases cost.  
- RBF kernel bandwidth `σ` in KProx: too large blurs the repulsion, too small causes fragmentation.  
- Particle count `ℓ`: more particles better approximate the posterior but raise computational and memory demands.  
- Encoder architecture: a unimodal Gaussian may fail for multimodal posteriors; a mixture or normalizing flow could help but complicates training.  
- Sinkhorn regularization `ϵ`: controls trade‑off between transport accuracy and numerical stability.  
- Sequential vs. joint training: decoupled updates are stable but may accumulate errors; joint end‑to‑end training could improve coherence but requires differentiable KProx.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Particle norms exploding during KProx indicates step size `ε` too large or kernel gradient miscalculation.  
- Encoder mode collapse: all `q_φ(z|x)` outputs collapse to a single mode despite multimodal `Z`.  
- Sinkhorn loss stagnates or oscillates: `ϵ` poorly chosen or encoder lacks capacity.  
- Decoder overfitting: KProx particles become degenerate (all near a single point), often due to overly aggressive decoder updates.

**First three experiments**:  
1. **Synthetic validation**: Implement KProx on a known 2‑D mixture posterior. Tune `σ` and `ε` to minimize KL divergence to the true posterior.  
2. **Baseline comparison**: Train full KProxNPLVM on the Debutanizer dataset using default hyperparameters (`T=200`, `ε=0.1`, `ℓ=10`). Compare test R² against a standard VAE (amortized VI) baseline.  
3. **Ablation – Wasserstein loss**: Replace the Sinkhorn loss with a simple KL divergence between `q_φ` and `Z` (using a Gaussian approximation). Measure impact on performance to confirm necessity of the Wasserstein proximal operator.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Neural velocity field** – *Can a neural network parameterization of `∇log Q_t(z)` improve expressiveness in high‑dimensional latent spaces?*  
   *Basis*: Future work mentions that the current RKHS approximation may be limited. *Why unresolved*: The RKHS approach might fail to capture complex, high‑dimensional posteriors. *Resolving evidence*: Implement a neural network to approximate the velocity field, test on synthetic high‑dimensional multimodal posteriors, and compare KL divergence and convergence speed.

2. **Alternative discrepancy metrics** – *How do other metrics (e.g., KL divergence, Hellinger distance) perform as proximal operators compared to Wasserstein?*  
   *Basis*: Explicitly suggested in the limitations. *Why unresolved*: Theoretical derivation and convergence guarantees are specialized to Wasserstein; other metrics require different updates whose properties are unknown. *Resolving evidence*: Derive the proximal gradient update for a chosen alternative metric, implement the algorithm, and conduct a comparative study on benchmark soft‑sensor datasets (accuracy, stability, convergence).

3. **Scalability** – *How can the O(N²) computational complexity from kernel evaluations be reduced for large‑scale applications?*  
   *Basis*: Supplementary complexity analysis reveals O(N²) bottleneck. *Why unresolved*: Current implementation may be infeasible for millions of data points or high particle counts. *Resolving evidence*: Incorporate kernel approximation methods (Nyström, random Fourier features) or stochastic particle subsampling; then measure runtime, memory usage, and prediction quality on large datasets.

4. **Joint training** – *Does end‑to‑end joint training of encoder and decoder (by making KProx differentiable) improve overall performance and convergence?*  
   *Basis*: The training alternates between decoder and encoder updates, which may misalign modules. *Why unresolved*: The decoupled strategy might lead to sub‑optimal parameters compared to a unified objective. *Resolving evidence*: Design a differentiable KProx (e.g., via implicit differentiation), train the full model jointly, and compare against the sequential baseline in terms of final prediction error and training stability.

## Limitations
- **Critical hyperparameters unreported**: The RBF kernel bandwidth `σ` in KProx, the Sinkhorn entropy regularization `ϵ`, and the exact encoder architecture are not specified, making faithful reproduction sensitive to arbitrary choices.
- **Convergence criteria vague**: KProx uses a fixed iteration count `T=200` without justification; the theoretical condition `ε = 1/√T` differs from the empirical `ε=0.1`, raising concerns about whether the assumptions of the convergence proof are satisfied in practice.

## Confidence
- **Accuracy gains**: Medium – Strong experimental results on three industrial datasets, but sensitivity to unreported hyperparameters (`σ`, `ϵ`, encoder design) may limit generalizability.
- **Convergence**: Medium – Theoretical convergence proof exists, but empirical settings (fixed `T`, step size `ε`) might not meet the required conditions, and early‑stopping details are missing.
- **Ablations**: Medium – Ablation studies support the contribution of both KProx and Wasserstein components, but configurations of the ablated baselines are not fully described, leaving some uncertainty.

## Next Checks
1. **σ sensitivity analysis**: Run KProx on a synthetic multimodal posterior task; vary `σ` and measure approximation error (e.g., KL divergence) to identify a robust default value.
2. **Encoder & Sinkhorn ϵ ablation**: Systematically vary encoder capacity (layers/units) and Sinkhorn regularization `ϵ` on validation sets; quantify impact on test R² to determine critical ranges.
3. **Convergence diagnostics**: Track particle distribution changes (e.g., maximum displacement, effective sample size) during KProx for the three industrial datasets; assess whether `T=200` is sufficient or if an adaptive stopping rule (based on particle stability) is needed.
