# SymbolSight: Minimizing Inter-Symbol Interference for Reading with Prosthetic Vision

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17326
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17326
- Reference count: 29
- Primary result: Heterogeneous symbol sets reduce predicted confusion by a median factor of 22× compared to native alphabets.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles inter-symbol interference in sequential reading with retinal prostheses, where afterimages of previous symbols cause recognition errors. The proposed framework, SymbolSight, optimizes symbol-to-letter mappings by combining simulated prosthetic vision (capturing spatial and temporal distortions) with a neural network proxy observer that estimates pairwise symbol confusability. Language-specific bigram statistics then weight these confusion values to minimize expected reading errors. Across simulations for Arabic, Bulgarian, and English, optimized heterogeneous symbol pools (drawing from multiple alphabets and synthetic patterns) cut predicted confusion by a median factor of 22 relative to native alphabets. The results suggest that non‑standard, computationally designed symbol sets can significantly improve reading under the tight bandwidth constraints of prosthetic vision.

## Method Summary
SymbolSight constructs a pool of 146 glyphs from five visual families (Latin, Braille, Arabic, Cyrillic, DCT basis). Simulated prosthetic vision (SPV) applies spatial distortions via `pulse2percept` and temporal ghosting via MixUp superposition to generate training images. A MobileNetV3 CNN, fine‑tuned on these distorted symbols, acts as a proxy observer: its validation confusion matrix **F** ranks symbol pair discriminability under overlap. Bigram probabilities **C** are extracted from language‑specific Wikipedia corpora. The optimization minimizes the cost Σ_{i≠j} C_{i,j} * F_{π(i),π(j)} where π maps letters to symbols; the Hungarian algorithm finds the optimal assignment. Performance is evaluated against native alphabets and randomized baselines using the scaled cost metric.

## Key Results
- Optimized heterogeneous symbol sets reduce predicted confusion by a median factor of 22× compared to native alphabets across Arabic, Bulgarian, and English.
- The assignment optimization consistently outperforms randomized selections, confirming the efficacy of the bigram‑weighted cost formulation.
- Cross‑family visual diversity—especially inclusion of DCT basis patterns—drives most of the improvement, as such symbols are rarely confused with traditional letters.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Symbol confusability under prosthetic distortions can be computationally estimated using a neural network proxy observer on simulated data.
- **Mechanism**: A convolutional neural network (MobileNetV3) is fine‑tuned on images of symbols rendered through a spatiotemporal distortion model (pulse2percept + MixUp augmentation). The network's classification errors across a held‑out validation set are aggregated into a pairwise confusion matrix **F**, which ranks symbol pairs by estimated discriminability under overlap.
- **Core assumption**: A CNN trained on prosthetic‑simulated symbols provides a consistent, high‑capacity discriminator whose confusion patterns reflect the *structural distinguishability* of the signal itself, serving as an upper‑bound proxy for human performance under the same distortion constraints.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [abstract] "Using simulated prosthetic vision (SPV) and a neural proxy observer, we estimate pairwise symbol confusability..."
  - [section II.B] "We do not use the network to predict absolute human error rates. Instead, the resulting confusion matrix **F** is used to rank the relative separability of symbol pairs under overlap... if a high‑capacity feature extractor cannot reliably distinguish two superimposed symbols under simulated distortion, the signal itself likely lacks sufficient information..."
- **Break condition**: If the proxy observer's confusion matrix shows no systematic structure (e.g., uniformly random errors), the simulation or model capacity may be inadequate, undermining the ranking assumption.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Temporal interference in sequential reading can be mitigated by optimizing symbol assignments to avoid visual collisions for frequently adjacent letters.
- **Mechanism**: The framework minimizes a cost function: **Cost = Σ_{i≠j} C_{i,j} * F_{π(i),π(j)}**, where **C** is a language‑specific bigram probability matrix (P(next letter | current letter)). The Hungarian algorithm assigns each letter to a distinct symbol such that high‑probability letter transitions are mapped to symbol pairs with low predicted confusion.
- **Core assumption**: Weighting confusion by bigram frequencies directly reduces expected reading errors caused by inter‑symbol interference, as the dominant error mode is misidentifying a symbol due to the afterimage of its predecessor.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [abstract] "...minimizing expected confusion cost using language‑specific bigram statistics."
  - [section II.D] "This cost increases when frequently co‑occurring letters are assigned to visually confusable symbols. Minimizing Eq. 2 reduces expected reading errors due to temporal interference."
- **Break condition**: If human reading errors under prosthetic vision do not correlate with high‑bigram‑frequency symbol collisions, the bigram‑weighted cost would not predict real‑world performance gains.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Heterogeneous symbol pools (mixing alphabets and synthetic patterns) enable larger performance gains than native alphabets because they provide geometrically distinct shapes that resist distortion‑induced collisions.
- **Mechanism**: The search space includes symbols from multiple visual families (Latin, Braille, Arabic, Cyrillic, DCT basis). The optimization selects a subset where symbols have minimal pairwise confusion under distortion *and* are assigned to letters to minimize weighted bigram costs. The cross‑family visual distinctiveness reduces the chance that any two letters, especially frequent bigrams, will be mapped to confusable symbols.
- **Core assumption**: Standard alphabets are suboptimal because their glyphs share design principles (e.g., stroke‑based typography) that increase within‑alphabet confusability under low‑resolution, persistent phosphene percepts; a diverse pool contains families that are perceptually orthogonal in the distorted feature space.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [abstract] "the resulting heterogeneous symbol sets reduced predicted confusion by a median factor of 22 compared to native alphabets."
  - [section III.A] "cross‑family confusion occurs predictably between Latin and Cyrillic symbols... DCT symbols are rarely confused with letter‑based symbols from other sets, as their periodic grating structure is visually distinct."
  - [section III.B] "Diversity alone improves legibility... randomized assignments from the mixed pool consistently outperform the native baseline."
- **Break condition**: If human learners cannot acquire or discriminate heterogeneous symbol sets at reasonable speeds, the theoretical confusion reduction would not translate to usable reading performance.

## Foundational Learning
### Simulated Prosthetic Vision (SPV)
- **Why needed here**: The entire optimization depends on a computational model that predicts how a retinal prosthesis’s spatial (phosphene spread, axonal streaks) and temporal (persistence, MixUp superposition) distortions transform ideal symbols into percepts. Without a validated SPV model, the confusion matrix **F** cannot be generated.
- **Quick check question**: How does changing the β parameter in the MixUp augmentation (currently β=2.0) alter the estimated pairwise confusion matrix, and what does that imply about the assumed strength of temporal ghosting?

### Proxy Observer Validation
- **Why needed here**: The neural network is not a black box but a *consistent* metric. The paper argues its errors are informative about signal ambiguity. Understanding the validation (separate training/validation sets, held‑out classes) and the cited prior work [24] showing CNN‑human correspondence across tasks is key to trusting the cost function.
- **Quick check question**: What specific evidence from reference [24] (Skaza et al., 2025) would you need to confirm that "task‑optimized deep networks operating on simulated prosthetic vision exhibit strong correspondence with human performance" for symbol recognition?

### Combinatorial Assignment Optimization
- **Why needed here**: The search space (mapping *L* letters to *S* symbols, *S*>>*L*) is factorial. The Hungarian algorithm efficiently finds the π that minimizes the linear sum assignment problem defined by the cost matrix **C ⊗ F** (element‑wise product, summed over assignments). Grasping this allows one to see the optimization as a graph matching problem, not a heuristic search.
- **Quick check question**: If you wanted to add a constraint that certain symbol families must be excluded (e.g., no Braille for aesthetic reasons), how would you modify the input to the Hungarian algorithm?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Symbol Generator → SPV Pipeline → Proxy Observer Trainer → Bigram Statistic Loader → Cost Constructor & Solver → Evaluator

**Critical path**: The proxy observer training is the most computationally expensive sequential step, occurring after generating the distorted symbol dataset and before extracting the confusion matrix for optimization.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Proxy Observer Fidelity vs. Speed: MobileNetV3 was chosen for efficiency but may sacrifice some biological plausibility; a larger model might yield different confusion patterns.
- Temporal Model Simplicity: Linear MixUp superposition ignores nonlinear decay dynamics; it is a feature‑space interference proxy, not a full time‑series simulation.
- Symbol Pool Diversity: The pool's composition (which families included) directly bounds the potential gains; excluding a family like DCT might reduce best‑case improvement.

**Failure signatures**:
- Degenerate Confusion Matrix: If **F** is nearly uniform (no discriminable pairs), the optimization cannot improve over random; indicates SPV model or symbol rendering is too destructive.
- Native Baseline Outperformance: If native alphabet cost is lower than all heterogeneous sets (including optimized), it suggests the distortion model is too mild or that typographic cohesion provides a countervailing benefit not captured.
- Optimization Instability: Large variance in "best randomized" seeds (Table I std) suggests the cost landscape is noisy; Hungarian solution may be sensitive to small fluctuations in **F** or **C**.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **SPV Distortion Sanity Check**: Render a single symbol (e.g., 'A') at increasing spatial distortion levels (ρ=100,300,500 µm). Visualize outputs to confirm expected blurring/streaking. Verify MixUp of two distinct symbols (e.g., 'A'+'B') creates a blended percept.
2. **Proxy Observer Baseline Training**: Train the CNN on *undistorted* symbols only. Expect near‑perfect accuracy and a confusion matrix with a strong diagonal. This establishes that observed confusion in distorted conditions is due to the SPV pipeline.
3. **Bigram Cost Sensitivity Test**: For a fixed confusion matrix **F**, manually assign two letters with high bigram probability (e.g., 'T' and 'H' in English) to two highly confusable symbols. Compute cost. Then swap one with a highly discriminable symbol. Confirm cost drops significantly. This validates the cost function's responsiveness to the bigram weighting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does SymbolSight's optimized symbol set improve actual human reading speed and accuracy compared to native alphabets in prosthetic vision?
2. How well do CNN‑derived confusion probabilities correlate with human symbol confusability under prosthetic vision conditions?
3. Does the linear MixUp model of temporal interference accurately represent phosphene persistence, and would a more realistic temporal model change the optimal symbol assignments?
4. How do device‑specific and user‑specific phosphene maps affect the SymbolSight optimization, and can personalization yield further improvements over the generic approach?

## Limitations
- Symbol pool specifications (exact glyphs, fonts, DCT basis) are unreported, limiting reproducibility and possibly affecting the reported confusion patterns.
- The proxy observer uses a CNN; while it provides a consistent confusability ranking, its correspondence to human perception under prosthetic vision is not validated.
- The evaluation is based solely on simulation; no human‑subject data confirm that optimized symbol sets yield real reading improvements.

## Confidence
- Proxy observer confusability ranking: Medium
- Bigram‑weighted cost formulation: Medium
- Heterogeneous symbol set advantage: High (within simulation)

## Next Checks
1. Human reading study: Conduct a psychophysics experiment where participants read sequences using optimized vs. native symbol sets under a validated prosthetic vision simulator; measure accuracy and speed.
2. Proxy‑human correlation test: Collect human discrimination data for a stratified sample of symbol pairs under identical SPV conditions; compute correlation with the CNN’s confusion matrix to validate the proxy’s ranking fidelity.
3. Bigram model robustness: Re‑run the optimization using trigram probabilities or a simple neural language model; assess whether the same symbol sets maintain low cost, thereby testing sensitivity to the bigram approximation.
