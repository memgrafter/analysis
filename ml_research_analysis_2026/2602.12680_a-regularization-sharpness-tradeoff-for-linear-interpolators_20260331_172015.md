# A Regularization-Sharpness Tradeoff for Linear Interpolators

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.12680  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12680  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** The Interpolating Information Criterion (IIC) decomposes into a regularization term (penalty magnitude of the interpolator) and a sharpness term (geometric sensitivity of the interpolation manifold). For smooth ℓp (p≥2) penalties, sharpness increases with model size while regularization decreases; for ℓ1, both decrease. IIC shows very high Spearman correlation (up to 1.0) with test MSE, providing a robust model selection tool in overparameterized linear regression.

## Executive Summary
The paper revisits model selection in overparameterized regimes where classical bias‑variance tradeoffs break down. It introduces the Interpolating Information Criterion (IIC) for linear interpolators with ℓp penalties, derived via a Bayesian duality that reduces the overparameterized model to a lower‑dimensional dual. The IIC cleanly separates into a regularization term (capturing alignment between the penalty and the interpolator) and a sharpness term (capturing geometric sensitivity on the interpolation manifold). Theoretical analysis shows that for smooth penalties (p≥2) sharpness increases with model size while regularization decreases; for ℓ1 both terms decrease, offering a sharper reduction in IIC. Empirical studies on real datasets with random Fourier features confirm these patterns and demonstrate that IIC correlates strongly with test MSE (Spearman up to 1.0), providing a robust criterion for selecting interpolating models.

## Method Summary
The method computes IIC for linear models that achieve zero training error under an ℓp penalty. Given a data matrix X (full row rank) and labels y, the unique minimum‑norm interpolant θ* is found as the solution to min∥θ∥_p^p subject to Xθ=y. Using Bayesian duality, the marginal likelihood of the high‑dimensional model is transformed into a dual integral over the null space of X. For smooth p≥2, Laplace’s method applied to this integral yields a closed‑form expression: IIC = log∥θ*∥_p^p + (1/n)log det(XXᵀ) + additional curvature terms. For ℓ1, the integral is evaluated exactly via geometric volume arguments, giving IIC = log∥θ*∥_1 + sharpness term that includes a factor V₀ capturing the geometry of the ℓ1 unit ball. The IIC thus decomposes into a regularization part (depending on the penalty) and a sharpness part (depending on the data geometry, e.g., log det(XXᵀ) and for p=1 a term –(2/n)log V₀). The authors then study how these components vary with the model dimension d, and validate the decomposition empirically by constructing features (random Fourier features or polynomial expansions), computing θ*, IIC, and test MSE across a range of d and penalties.

## Key Results
- IIC = regularization term + sharpness term; regularization reflects the penalty magnitude of the interpolator, sharpness captures sensitivity of the interpolation manifold.
- For ℓp (p≥2): regularization decreases with d, sharpness increases with d.
- For ℓ1: both regularization and sharpness decrease with d, leading to a net IIC reduction.
- IIC shows very high Spearman correlation (up to 1.0) with test MSE across datasets, effectively distinguishing interpolators with good vs. poor generalization.
- Polynomial features do not consistently exhibit the same tradeoff patterns, indicating the role of feature geometry.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### 1. Bayesian Duality Evades Singularity
The marginal likelihood of an overparameterized interpolating model can be exactly rewritten as a lower‑dimensional dual integral, bypassing the singular Fisher information that plagues Laplace approximations. As the likelihood temperature γ→0⁺, the posterior concentrates on the interpolation manifold. Lemma B.1 (Bayesian duality) shows that integrating over θ∈ℝᵈ with prior π(θ) is equivalent to integrating over z∈ℝⁿ with dual prior π*(z), where the change of variables accounts for the Jacobian of F(θ)=Xθ. This reduces the problem to an exactly identified model. Core assumption: X has full row rank so that XX⊤ is positive definite and a unique minimum‑norm interpolator exists. Break condition: rank(X)<n or prior with infinite normalizing constant invalidates duality.

### 2. Laplace Approximation on Duality Output Yields Decomposition
In the τ→0⁺ limit (prior temperature), the dual integral can be approximated via Laplace’s method, producing an IIC of the form A·log R(θ*) + B, separating regularization from geometric sharpness. The dual integrand exp(–∥θ*+Qw∥ₚᵖ/τ) has a unique global minimum at w=0. Theorem A.1 gives the asymptotic: the integral is dominated by a Gaussian with variance determined by the Hessian ∇²h(w). Taking –log and scaling yields the decomposition. For p≥2, the Hessian is positive definite automatically; for p=1 a separate volume argument replaces Laplace. Break condition: for p>2, d ≤ n·p/(p–2) is required to avoid the cold posterior effect; for ℓ1, non‑differentiability requires exact capacity calculations.

### 3. Sparsity Reverses the Sharpness Trend
The ℓ¹ regularizer changes the sign of the sharpness term’s dependence on d: both regularization and sharpness decrease with increasing d, whereas for smooth ℓᵖ (p≥2) sharpness increases. For smooth p, sharpness contains (1/n)log det(XX⊤) plus (p–2)/n Σ log|θ*_j|; both typically grow with d. For ℓ¹, sharpness contains –(2/n)log V₀, where V₀ is the volume of a subset of the ℓ1 unit ball. Lemma C.7 shows V₀ grows exponentially in d–n, so –(2/n)log V₀ becomes a large negative that increases (i.e., sharpness decreases) with d. Core assumption: for the simplified V₀ formula, |S|=n (full sparsity); more generally, the polyhedral ℓ¹ ball accommodates more null‑space directions as d grows. Break condition: if |S| ≪ n, V₀ may not grow fast enough; also ℓ¹ advantage relies on exact interpolation.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
(X, y) -> compute minimum‑norm interpolant θ* under ℓp penalty -> build orthonormal basis Q for ker(X) -> form dual integral -> apply Laplace approximation (p≥2) or volume calculation (p=1) -> obtain IIC (regularization + sharpness) -> output

### Critical Path
1. Verify X has full row rank (n ≤ d) and that the regularized interpolation problem yields a unique θ* (e.g., ℓ1 requires spark condition).  
2. Compute Q, an orthonormal basis for the null space of X (dimension d–n).  
3. For p≥2: compute Hessian of h(w)=∥θ*+Qw∥ₚᵖ at w=0; ensure positive definiteness.  
4. For p=1: identify support S of θ*, construct matrix Ψ and vector ψ=Ψᵀ sign(θ*_S); check |ψ_k|<1 for finiteness; compute volume V₀.  
5. Determine optimal prior temperature τ* by minimizing free energy.  
6. Plug into IIC formula from Theorem 4.4 (ℓ2), 4.5 (p≥2) or 4.6 (ℓ1).  
7. To study tradeoff, repeat steps 1–6 for increasing d.

### Design Tradeoffs
- ℓ² vs ℓ¹: ℓ² simplest algebra (closed‑form) but sharpness increases with d; ℓ¹ introduces combinatorial support dependence but can achieve decreasing sharpness.  
- Smooth p≥2 vs sparse p=1: Smooth p allows uniform Laplace but imposes d ≤ n·p/(p–2); ℓ1 circumvents this bound but requires support‑size assumptions.  
- Feature map choice: Random Fourier features (RFF) typically yield benign overfitting (IIC ↓ with d); polynomial features often do not (Figure 3). Choose features to reflect realistic kernel regimes.

### Failure Signatures
- Cold posterior for p>2 and large d: Laplace minimum not at θ*; IIC formula invalid.  
- ℓ1 weak sharpness decrease: If |S| ≪ n or features not sufficiently overparameterized, V₀ grows slowly; sharpness term may not drop.  
- Rank‑deficient X: det(XX⊤)=0 → dual prior infinite; IIC undefined.  
- Polynomial features: IIC may plateau or increase with d (Figure 3), indicating no beneficial overparameterization.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Decomposition visualization (RFF)**: On a real dataset (e.g., FLIR), construct RFF features for various d. For each p∈{1,2,3}, compute IIC, its regularization term, and sharpness term. Plot all vs d. Verify: for p=1, sharpness decreases; for p≥2, sharpness increases.  
2. **Correlation with test error**: Across several datasets (CIFAR‑10, MNIST, etc.), vary d. Compute test MSE and IIC for each (d,p). Report Spearman ρ between IIC and test MSE. Expect ρ ≈ 1, confirming IIC is a proxy for generalization.  
3. **Polynomial contrast**: Repeat Experiment 1 but using polynomial feature expansion. Observe that decomposition does not show clear decreasing IIC trend; sharpness may not decrease for ℓ¹. This highlights that the tradeoff depends on feature geometry.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Can the IIC framework be extended to overparameterized non‑linear models, such as deep neural networks, to provide a practical model selection tool?  
*Basis in paper*: The Limitations section states: "The IIC framework can hold for more general models, and while an extension of our findings (with the ℓ1 regularizer in particular) to that level of generality would be very challenging, it would be substantially more relevant to the deep learning context."  
*Why unresolved*: The current theory is restricted to linear models with ℓp penalties. Non‑linear models involve non‑convex loss landscapes and complex parameter interactions, making the Bayesian duality and Laplace approximation techniques inapplicable without significant generalization.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Developing a theoretical foundation for IIC in neural networks using approximations like the neural tangent kernel or mean‑field theory, and conducting experiments that validate whether the regularization‑sharpness tradeoff extends to deep learning settings with interpolating solutions.

### Open Question 2
For ℓp penalties with p > 2, how can the IIC be applied when the model dimension d exceeds the bound d ≤ np/(p−2)?  
*Basis in paper*: The Limitations section notes: "for p≥2, there is an upper bound for the IIC to ensure the existence of an optimal temperature τ. Consequently, the IIC framework is not effective for studying interpolators with large d when p >2; a new strategy is required here." Theorem 4.5 formally states the condition d ≤ np/(p−2).  
*Why unresolved*: The derivation relies on a Laplace approximation that assumes a well‑defined minimum point in the dual integral. When d is too large, the prior mass concentration invalidates this assumption, so the standard IIC expression may not hold.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Proposing alternative asymptotic methods or reformulating IIC for regimes beyond the current bound, and verifying via simulations that such extensions accurately predict generalization performance in highly overparameterized linear models with p > 2.

### Open Question 3
How does non‑uniqueness of the regularized interpolator affect the IIC decomposition and its practical utility?  
*Basis in paper*: Assumption 4.3 assumes a unique solution θ* to the regularized interpolation problem. The theory builds on this uniqueness for the Laplace approximation around a single point. In cases like ℓ1 with certain designs, multiple interpolators can exist, which is not covered.  
*Why unresolved*: With multiple minimizers, the integral over the interpolating manifold may not localize around a single point, altering the IIC’s form and interpretation. The current derivation does not account for this multiplicity.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Extending the analysis to settings with multiple solutions (e.g., by averaging over the solution set or using geometric measure theory), deriving modified IIC formulas, and testing empirically whether these adjusted criteria still correlate with test error in such scenarios.

## Limitations
- Requires full‑rank X and a unique minimum‑norm interpolator; violations (e.g., rank deficiency, ℓ1 without spark) break the IIC formulas.  
- Laplace decomposition for p≥2 may fail (“cold posterior”) when d > n·p/(p−2); empirical evidence beyond this bound is lacking.  
- ℓ1 sharpness reduction proof assumes |S|=n; in practice sparsity level < n may weaken the effect.  
- Paper omitted RFF bandwidth σ; polynomial features did not consistently reproduce the tradeoff, questioning generality.  
- Test‑MSE correlation claims rely on limited datasets; may not generalize.

## Confidence
- Bayesian duality: **High** (rigorous proof).  
- Laplace decomposition: **High** under regularity; **Low** if cold posterior occurs (d beyond bound).  
- Sparsity sharpness reversal: **Medium** (proven under unrealistic |S|=n; empirical support limited).  
- IIC predicts test MSE: **Medium** (strong but limited empirical evidence).
