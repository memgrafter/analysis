# GradAlign: Gradient-Aligned Data Selection for LLM Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.21492
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21492
- Reference count: 16
- Primary result: GradAlign consistently outperforms baseline data selection methods in challenging RL settings with noisy rewards, distribution imbalance, and low-utility data, achieving up to 9.7 percentage points improvement (68.2% vs. 58.5% on AMC23-test).

## Executive Summary
GradAlign tackles the problem of selecting high-quality training problems for LLM reinforcement learning from large, noisy corpora. Traditional accuracy-based heuristics break down under non‑stationary policy updates and unreliable rewards. The proposed solution computes a target gradient direction from a small, trusted validation set and ranks candidate problems by the cosine similarity of their on‑policy gradients to this direction, creating an adaptive curriculum. Evaluated across three challenging regimes—unreliable rewards, distribution imbalance, and low‑utility data—GradAlign consistently surpasses random selection, accuracy greedy, and in‑training gradient alignment baselines. Notable results include 68.2% on AMC23‑test under noisy rewards (vs. 58.5% for random) and a 45.7% average on WebInstruct tasks (vs. 44.4% random), with more stable training curves and only modest extra computation.

## Method Summary
GradAlign operates in iterative rounds. At the start of each round, a small validation set is re‑sampled to compute a target gradient \(G_v\) by averaging per‑problem GRPO policy gradients. Each candidate training problem from a large pool is then scored by the cosine similarity between its own on‑policy gradient (estimated from a small number of rollouts) and \(G_v\). The top‑scoring problems form the training subset for that round. The policy is updated via GRPO on this subset. This process repeats, periodically refreshing \(G_v\) to remain aligned with the evolving policy. The method is specifically designed for scenarios where reward signals are noisy, data distributions are imbalanced, or a substantial portion of the pool is low‑utility, conditions under which static selection fails.

## Key Results
- Under 50% reward corruption (noisy rewards), GradAlign achieves 68.2% on AMC23‑test, a 9.7‑point improvement over random selection and accuracy greedy.
- On WebInstruct unverified tasks, GradAlign yields a test‑set average of 45.7% versus 44.4% for random, demonstrating consistent gains across distribution‑imbalanced and low‑utility regimes.
- Training curves are more stable and final performance higher than baselines, with only a modest computational overhead from periodic validation gradient resampling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Selecting training problems whose policy gradients align with a validation set gradient direction improves downstream performance in non‑stationary RL.
- **Mechanism:** A small, trusted validation set provides a stable "target direction." For each candidate problem, its on‑policy gradient is computed and scored via cosine similarity to the aggregated validation gradient. Problems with high alignment are prioritized, as their updates are more likely to improve validation performance under a first‑order approximation.
- **Core assumption:** The direction of the policy gradient on the validation set is a reliable proxy for the gradient of expected validation accuracy (Theorem 4.1, 4.2). This assumes advantage estimation is directionally unbiased and that small steps generalize.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...prioritize training problems whose policy gradients align with validation gradients, yielding an adaptive curriculum."
  - [section 4.2] Theorem 4.1 and 4.2 formalize the directional alignment between the GRPO gradient and expected accuracy gradient.
  - [corpus] Evidence from neighboring papers is weak/absent; this mechanism is novel to GradAlign.
- **Break condition:** If the validation set is not representative of the target downstream task, the target gradient direction becomes misleading, leading to poor curriculum and performance degradation.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Periodic on‑policy resampling of the validation set gradients is critical to maintain alignment signals as the policy evolves.
- **Mechanism:** At each selection round, fresh rollouts are generated from the *current* policy for all validation problems to recompute the target gradient. This adapts the "target direction" to the policy's current state, mitigating bias from stale rollouts whose advantages were computed under a different policy.
- **Core assumption:** Stale rollouts provide increasingly biased gradient estimates as the policy diverges from the sampling policy. Re‑sampling corrects this bias faster than importance sampling corrections (Section 4.4).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.4] "Because the policy evolves during RL training... we resample all validation problems and recompute the average validation gradient at the beginning of each data‑selection round."
  - [section 4.4] Contrast with Zhu et al. (2025) using offline rollouts; resampling avoids high‑variance importance sampling.
  - [corpus] No direct support; this is an implementation design choice addressed in the paper.
- **Break condition:** If the validation set is too large, resampling overhead becomes prohibitive, forcing a trade‑off with stale gradients.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Cosine similarity, as an alignment score, better discriminates high‑quality problems from noisy/misleading ones than accuracy‑based heuristics, especially under reward noise and distribution shift.
- **Mechanism:** Accuracy is a scalar, noisy signal that fails to capture gradient direction. Two problems can have similar accuracy but opposing gradients. Cosine similarity operates in high‑dimensional gradient space, where misaligned (orthogonal) updates from corrupted or irrelevant data are naturally down‑weighted, even if their accuracy is moderate.
- **Core assumption:** Gradient direction contains information about *how* a problem affects learning that scalar accuracy does not. In high dimensions, random or misleading gradients tend to be near‑orthogonal to a coherent target direction derived from a clean validation set.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "accuracy‑based heuristics fail... GradAlign consistently outperforms... underscoring the importance of directional gradient signals."
  - [section 7] Ablation: "cosine similarity provides clearer separation between clean and corrupted candidates."
  - [corpus] Neighbors focus on reward selection or training‑free inference, not gradient alignment for RL data selection.
- **Break condition:** If the target gradient direction itself is noisy (e.g., validation set is too small or unrepresentative), cosine similarity ranking becomes unstable and may not correlate with utility.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Policy Gradient Theorem**
  - *Why needed here:* GradAlign's core is estimating and comparing policy gradients (∇θ ˜L_GRPO). Understanding that this gradient estimates the direction to increase expected return (or accuracy) is fundamental. Theorem 4.1 explicitly relies on it.
  - *Quick check question:* Under what conditions is the expected policy gradient equal to the gradient of expected reward? (Answer: On‑policy sampling, with an unbiased advantage estimator.)

- **Concept: Non‑Stationarity in RL**
  - *Why needed here:* The entire motivation for GradAlign is that RL data selection is non‑stationary. The data distribution (rollouts from π_θ) and the optimal policy change as training progresses. Static selection heuristics fail here.
  - *Quick check question:* Why is a data selection method designed for SFT (fixed trajectories) likely ineffective for on‑policy RL?

- **Concept: Cosine Similarity in High‑Dimensional Spaces**
  - *Why needed here:* GradAlign uses cosine similarity to compare gradient vectors. In high dimensions, random vectors are nearly orthogonal. This property allows separating coherent (aligned) update directions from random noise.
  - *Quick check question:* If two vectors are random and high‑dimensional, what is the typical value of their cosine similarity? (Answer: Approximately 0.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Resampling Controller -> Validation Gradient Estimator -> Candidate Scorer -> Curriculum Selector -> RL Updater -> (back to Resampling Controller for next round)

**Critical path:**  
1. Initialize policy π_θ.  
2. Round r starts: Resample P_v -> compute G_v.  
3. For each candidate p_r,i in pool P_r: estimate g_r,i -> compute score = cos(g_r,i, G_v).  
4. Select S_r (top‑scoring candidates).  
5. Update π_θ via GRPO on S_r.  
6. Loop to step 2.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *k_v vs. k_r sample count:* Larger k_v gives a more precise, stable G_v (critical). Smaller k_r reduces candidate scoring overhead (acceptable for ranking). Trade‑off: precision vs. cost of scoring huge pool.  
- *Selection ratio q:* Smaller q increases stringency but risks losing diversity; larger q includes more noise. Paper uses 5%‑25%.  
- *Resampling frequency:* Resampling every round is clean but costly; could be done less often, risking stale target gradients.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Degradation under noisy validation: If P_v contains misleading examples, G_v points away from true improvement; monitor validation accuracy trend for drops after a round.  
- Stagnation with poor initialization: Very weak initial policies yield chaotic gradients on P_v; consider a warm‑up phase with simpler data first.  
- Computational explosion: Scoring millions of candidates with rollouts is expensive; mitigate by pre‑filtering (e.g., pass rate) before gradient scoring.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate Scenario 1 (Noisy Rewards): Confirm that GradAlign selects fewer 50% corrupted samples and maintains higher accuracy on AMC23‑test vs. AccGreedy.  
2. Ablation: Resampling Impact: Disable resampling (use stale G_v from round 1). Expect faster performance decay, especially later rounds, confirming Mechanism 2.  
3. Sanity Check: k_v Sensitivity: Vary k_v (8, 16, 32, 64). Performance should improve up to a plateau; very low k_v (e.g., 1) should cause instability.

## Limitations
- Strong dependence on a small, clean validation set; if unrepresentative or corrupted, the target gradient becomes misleading.
- Implementation specifics (GRPO gradient estimation, judge prompting) rely on code, hindering exact reproduction.
- Computational cost of on‑policy gradient scoring for millions of candidates each round may be prohibitive without pre‑filtering.

## Confidence
- Performance superiority (vs. random/AccGreedy/LearnAlign): **High** — backed by experiments across three regimes with clear metrics.
- Core mechanism (validation gradient as proxy for expected accuracy): **Medium** — supported by theory and ablations, but assumptions may degrade under severe non‑stationarity.
- Periodic resampling necessity: **Medium** — plausible argument, but quantitative ablation (e.g., varying frequency) is not fully explored.

## Next Checks
- Boundary test for validation corruption: Run GradAlign with varying corruption rates (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%) in the validation set and measure performance decay.
- Resampling frequency ablation: Compare fixed‑interval resampling (every 1, 5, 10 rounds) against always‑fresh validation gradients; track validation accuracy volatility and final test performance.
- Gradient scoring robustness: Replace cosine similarity with alternative metrics (dot product, angular distance) under different noise levels; also test different k_v and k_r values to establish minimal reliable sample counts for stable ranking.
