# Vision Transformers that Never Stop Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07787
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07787
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** ARROW improves average accuracy across tasks (AAT) on CIFAR‑100 by up to ~7% relative to strong baselines, reaching 64.21% for 10 tasks.

## Executive Summary
Vision Transformers (ViTs) suffer from catastrophic plasticity loss when trained sequentially on multiple tasks, with degradation worsening in deeper layers and especially in feed‑forward networks. ARROW is a novel optimizer that preserves plasticity by adaptively reshaping gradient directions using an online low‑rank curvature estimate. It rescales updates to amplify directions with small curvature, counteracting the concentration of gradients onto a limited set of dominant directions. Experiments on CIFAR‑100 and ImageNet‑R show ARROW consistently outperforms existing mitigation strategies like TRAC, CBP, and NaP, establishing a new state‑of‑the‑art for continual learning with ViTs.

## Method Summary
ARROW replaces the standard optimizer with a curvature‑aware update rule. It maintains a window of the last *W* gradients per parameter and computes a covariance matrix \(C_t = \frac{1}{W}\sum_{i=t-W+1}^{t} g_i g_i^\top\). The parameter update is then \(\Delta \theta = -\eta_t (\alpha I + \beta C_t)^{-1} g_t\), where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are damping coefficients. The inverse is applied efficiently using the Woodbury identity, keeping computational overhead low. ARROW is applied selectively to the attention modules in the deeper blocks (e.g., the last one to six blocks) where plasticity loss is most acute. Hyperparameters \(\alpha \approx 10^{-3}\), \(\beta \approx 0.9\), and window size \(W=20\) work well, often combined with an RMS‑like warm‑up to stabilize early training.

## Key Results
- ARROW achieves 64.21% Average Accuracy across Tasks (AAT) on CIFAR‑100 with 10 tasks, compared to 57.21% for the vanilla baseline and 63.58% for the strong TRAC method.
- An ablation shows that applying ARROW only to the deepest attention block yields 63.96% AAT, confirming its targeted benefits for deeper modules.
- Gains hold across multiple task set sizes (10, 20, 25 on CIFAR‑100; 20, 40, 50 on ImageNet‑R) and consistently reduce the effective rank collapse and weight magnitude growth observed in baseline ViTs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that plasticity loss in continual learning is a geometric phenomenon: gradients become aligned with a small set of dominant directions from earlier tasks, drastically reducing the effective update subspace. This directional concentration is particularly severe in feed‑forward networks (FFNs) within ViTs, where saturation and neuron dormancy cause representational collapse, while attention modules degrade more slowly. ARROW estimates the local curvature via a windowed gradient covariance and rescales updates by the inverse of \((\alpha I + \beta C_t)\). Eigenvectors with small eigenvalues (low curvature, under‑explored directions) are amplified, expanding the effective subspace and restoring the model’s ability to adapt to new tasks. The selective application to deep attention blocks addresses the depth‑amplified pattern of degradation.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input → Patch Embedding → [ViT Block (LayerNorm → MHSA → Residual → LayerNorm → FFN → Residual)] × 12 → CLS token → Classification head.  
  Critical modules for plasticity: **FFN layers** (especially FC1/FC2 in deeper blocks) and **Value (V) projections** in MHSA.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Diagnose plasticity: track effective rank and Fraction of Active/Dead Units (FAU/FDU) across blocks, focusing on the last 6 blocks; expect FFN degradation > MHSA.  
  2. Apply ARROW to the attention modules in the deepest blocks (e.g., last 1–6 blocks) rather than shallower ones.  
  3. Tune hyperparameters: window size \(W=20\), \(\alpha\) in \([10^{-3}, 5\times10^{-2}]\), \(\beta=0.9\), use RMS‑like warm‑up as in Table 1.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  • *Efficacy vs. cost:* ARROW adds \(O(d \times W)\) memory and \(O(W^3)\) computation per update (via Woodbury), comparable to TRAC in runtime.  
  • *Scope:* Applying only to deep blocks improves efficiency but may miss early degradation; full application is safer but may over‑correct stable layers.  
  • *Hyperparameter sensitivity:* Incorrect \(\alpha\) (too large → no amplification; too small → noise) or \(W\) (too small → noisy curvature; too large → slow adaptation) breaks ARROW.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  • *Immediate:* Training instability (loss spikes) if \(\beta > 1.3\) or \(W < 5\).  
  • *Progressive:* AAT still declining after many tasks indicates insufficient amplification (check effective rank of deep blocks; likely \(\alpha\) too high or \(\beta\) too low).  
  • *Unexpected:* FFN FDU continues rising despite ARROW on attention → FFN collapse requires separate interventions (e.g., neuron reset).

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline diagnosis:** Train vanilla ViT on CIFAR‑100 (10 tasks, 5 classes/task). Track effective rank and FAU/FDU per block; deep FFN rank should drop and FDU rise.  
  2. **Baseline comparison:** Replicate Table 3: compare ARROW vs. TRAC, NaP, CBP on CIFAR‑100 (10 tasks). Verify ARROW surpasses TRAC (e.g., task 10 AAT: 64.21% vs. 63.58%).  
  3. **Ablation validation:** Test ARROW variants: (i) \(\beta=0\) (first‑order only), (ii) apply to all blocks vs. last block only. Confirm last‑block application yields best AAT (expected ~63.96%).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** How can the sensitivity of \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), and \(W\) be mitigated to enable robust performance across diverse settings without extensive tuning? The paper admits ARROW introduces extra hyperparameters and requires careful tuning.
- **Evaluation metrics:** What alternative metrics can more objectively capture improvements in plasticity, especially when comparing models with different base performances? AAT conflates final accuracy with plasticity, so a more isolated measure is needed.
- **Root cause of degradation:** What underlying mechanism drives the observed depth‑amplified degradation and disproportionate collapse in FFNs during continual learning? The paper provides diagnostics but not a causal explanation.
- **Generality:** Does ARROW’s geometry‑aware gradient reshaping generalize beyond ViTs to other architectures (CNNs, MLPs, language transformers) and continual learning paradigms (class‑incremental, online)? The study is limited to ViTs in task‑incremental settings.

## Limitations
- Exact training schedule details (learning rate decay within tasks, whether ARROW multiplies or replaces base LR) are unspecified, risking replication gaps.
- Precise ARROW application scope (all projection matrices vs. only specific ones like V) remains ambiguous.
- Gradient buffer warm‑up procedure is unclear (first *W* steps use standard updates vs. a running estimate like RMSprop).
- Corpus validation is weak (average FMR 0.473, zero citations); mechanisms rely primarily on internal evidence.
- Evaluation is confined to task‑incremental CL on two datasets; generalization to other CL paradigms is unknown.

## Confidence
- **ARROW’s empirical efficacy on CIFAR‑100/ImageNet‑R:** High
- **FFNs as the primary bottleneck for plasticity loss:** Medium
- **Gradient direction concentration as the geometric cause:** High
- **Broad applicability of ARROW to other architectures:** Low
