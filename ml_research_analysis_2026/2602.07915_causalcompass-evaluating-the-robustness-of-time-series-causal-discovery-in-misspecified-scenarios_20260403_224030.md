# CausalCompass: Evaluating the Robustness of Time-Series Causal Discovery in Misspecified Scenarios

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.07915
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07915
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Introduces CausalCompass, a benchmark with eight misspecified scenarios evaluated on eleven TSCD algorithms; deep learning-based methods consistently achieve superior overall performance, especially in AUPRC, indicating lower spurious edge rates.

## Executive Summary
CausalCompass addresses the lack of robustness evaluation for time-series causal discovery (TSCD) methods under violated modeling assumptions. The authors generate synthetic multivariate time series from linear VAR and nonlinear Lorenz-96 models, then apply eight types of misspecifications (e.g., latent confounders, measurement error, nonstationarity). They test eleven TSCD algorithms, ranging from classical (VAR, LGC, PCMCI) to deep learning approaches (cMLP, cLSTM, CUTS, CUTS+), and evaluate performance using AUROC and AUPRC. Key findings reveal that no single method dominates all scenarios, but deep learning-based methods exhibit consistently superior robustness, particularly in AUPRC (i.e., fewer spurious edges). Additionally, NTS-NOTEARS shows extreme sensitivity to data standardization, performing poorly without it but excelling after preprocessing. The benchmark, code, and datasets are publicly released to enable reproducible research and future development.

## Method Summary
The benchmark constructs two families of generative models: linear vector autoregressive (VAR) processes and nonlinear Lorenz-96 systems. For each base model, the authors introduce controlled violations to simulate eight real-world misspecifications, including latent confounders, measurement error, missing data, mixed data types, nonstationarity (trend, seasonality, abrupt changes), and min–max normalization issues. Each of the eleven TSCD algorithms is wrapped into a common interface; hyperparameter search is performed over predefined ranges (e.g., lag order, regularization strength, learning rate) using validation performance. The evaluation computes off-diagonal AUROC and AUPRC against the ground truth adjacency matrix, averaging over multiple random seeds. The paper also reports optimal hyperparameters per scenario and conducts sensitivity analyses. All code and generated datasets are available at the provided GitHub repository.

## Key Results
- No single TSCD method achieves optimal performance across all eight misspecified settings; trade-offs exist between methods.
- Deep learning-based approaches (cMLP, cLSTM, CUTS, CUTS+) consistently rank highest in overall robustness, particularly in AUPRC, indicating lower false positive rates.
- NTS-NOTEARS exhibits strong dependence on data standardization: it performs poorly on raw or min–max normalized data but excels after Z-score standardization, contrasting with its behavior in i.i.d. causal discovery.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Granger Causality
Granger causality tests whether past values of one time series improve the prediction of another. The paper implements this through VAR/LGC for linear relationships and cMLP/cLSTM for nonlinear ones. Core assumptions include stationarity and absence of omitted confounders. Evidence: The abstract describes GC-based methods; Appendices A.1 and A.2 detail VAR/LGC; the corpus shows extensions like CUTS applying similar principles to irregular data. Break conditions: latent confounders, strong unmodeled nonlinearities, or nonstationarity degrade performance (Section 4.1).

### Score-Based Continuous Optimization (NOTEARs Family)
This family reformulates causal discovery as continuous optimization of a differentiable score (e.g., least squares) subject to a smooth acyclicity constraint. DYNOTEARS extends the approach to time series by adding lagged terms; NTS-NOTEARS replaces linear layers with 1D CNNs for nonlinearities. Assumptions: the true graph is a DAG, functional forms are correctly modeled, and noise is i.i.d. Evidence: The abstract mentions score-based methods like DYNOTEARS; Appendices A.5 and A.6 describe the optimization framework. Break conditions: model misspecification, cycles, or poor conditioning cause failure; the benchmark shows these methods struggle with mixed data and min–max normalization (Section 4.1).

### Topology-Based State-Space Reconstruction (TSCI)
Based on Takens’ embedding theorem, TSCI reconstructs state spaces via delay embedding and infers causality through cross-predictability (e.g., Convergent Cross Mapping). It models dynamics locally with tangent spaces or neural ODEs. Assumptions: deterministic system, sufficient embedding dimension, low noise. Evidence: The abstract cites topology-based methods like CCM and TSCI; Appendix A.7 details TSCI’s approach. Break conditions: stochasticity, high measurement noise, or insufficient embedding degrade performance; the benchmark shows TSCI is vulnerable to measurement error and nonstationarity (Section 4.1).

## Foundational Learning
- **Time-series stationarity**  
  Why needed: Most TSCD methods assume stationarity (joint distribution invariant over time). Nonstationarity (e.g., changing variance, trends) is a key violation evaluated in CausalCompass and severely impacts performance (e.g., Section 4.1 shows drops under trend/seasonality).  
  Quick check: If a time series has a linear trend, why would a stationary VAR model fail to recover true causal edges?

- **Causal sufficiency and latent confounding**  
  Why needed: The assumption that all common causes are observed is central to many TSCD methods. CausalCompass includes latent confounder scenarios (Section 3.2) to test robustness; methods that ignore latent variables often produce spurious edges (Section 4.1).  
  Quick check: How does an unobserved confounder create a false Granger causal relationship between two observed variables?

- **Model misspecification and robustness**  
  Why needed: The paper argues that real data rarely meet all assumptions; robustness to violations is critical for practical adoption. CausalCompass systematically evaluates this by introducing controlled misspecifications (eight scenarios).  
  Quick check: What is the difference between a method that is robust to measurement error and one that is not? Give an example from the results (e.g., deep learning vs classical).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Data generation module -> Method-wrapper layer -> Hyperparameter search engine -> Evaluation engine -> Result aggregation

**Critical Path**  
1. Clone repo and install dependencies (Python 3.8+, numpy, scipy, tigramite, causalnex, pytorch, etc.).  
2. Choose configuration: e.g., `--model linear --nodes 10 --T 1000 --scenario confounders --zeta 0.5`.  
3. Generate data: `python generate_data.py ...` producing `data.npz` with time series and ground truth adjacency.  
4. For each method, run hyperparameter search or a fixed setting: `python run_method.py --method var --data data.npz --hyperparams '{"tau_max":3, "threshold":0.01}'`.  
5. Collect predictions and evaluate: `python evaluate.py --pred pred.npz --gt data.npz` returns AUROC/AUPRC.  
6. Repeat for multiple seeds (e.g., 5) and average.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Realism vs control: Synthetic data allows exact ground truth and isolated violation effects, but may not capture complex real-world dynamics (e.g., heavy tails, nonlinear dependencies beyond Lorenz-96).  
- Breadth vs depth: The benchmark covers many methods and scenarios but does not exhaust all hyperparameter settings; users may need to extend ranges for their specific needs.  
- Reproducibility vs computational cost: Running all methods on all scenarios with full hyperparameter search is extremely expensive (110,000+ runs); the paper uses optimal per-dataset hyperparameters and also reports average-performance selections to trade off realism and feasibility.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Data generation errors: Parameters outside intended ranges (e.g., ζ > 1 for confounding) or mismatched dimensions cause invalid graphs or crashes.  
- Method wrapper crashes: Some methods have strict input requirements (e.g., PCMCI expects pandas DataFrame; CUTS expects irregular time indexing). Check logs for type mismatches or missing packages.  
- NaN metrics: If a method predicts no edges (all zeros), AUPRC becomes undefined; check that prediction matrices have at least one positive entry.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. Vanilla baseline: Run all methods on the vanilla linear 10-node, T=1000 dataset. Expect classical linear methods (VAR, LGC, DYNOTEARS) to achieve near-perfect AUROC/AUPRC, while noise-based (VARLiNGAM) and topology-based (TSCI) may underperform.  
2. Standardization sensitivity: Run NTS-NOTEARS on the same vanilla data with and without Z-score standardization. Should perform poorly without but excel after standardization.  
3. Deep learning robustness under confounding: Run cLSTM and CUTS+ on the confounding scenario (ζ=0.5) for linear data. Deep learning methods should maintain relatively high AUPRC while others drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Standardization effect on NTS-NOTEARS**: Why does Z-score standardization dramatically improve the performance of continuous optimization-based TSCD methods like NTS-NOTEARS in time-series settings, given that standardization typically harms such methods in i.i.d. causal discovery? The paper documents this empirically but lacks theoretical explanation.
2. **Robustness of deep learning TSCD**: Why do deep learning-based methods (cMLP, cLSTM, CUTS, CUTS+) consistently exhibit superior robustness across diverse violation scenarios? The paper speculates about function approximation capacity but provides no in-depth analysis of architectural features or training dynamics.
3. **Extension to event sequences**: How can CausalCompass be extended to evaluate causal discovery from event sequences under misspecified scenarios? Event sequences have irregular timing and discrete events, requiring new data generators and evaluation metrics.
4. **Coexisting violations**: How do simultaneous assumption violations (e.g., latent confounders, measurement error, nonstationarity) affect TSCD method performance? The paper evaluates each violation in isolation; interactions remain unknown.
5. **Hyperparameter sensitivity of deep methods**: What causes the high hyperparameter sensitivity of deep learning-based TSCD methods in linear settings, and how can their robustness to hyperparameter choices be improved? The paper notes higher standard deviations in linear regimes but does not investigate causes or remedies.

## Limitations
- **Synthetic data only**: The benchmark uses linear VAR and Lorenz-96 models, which may not capture real-world complexities such as heavy tails, nonstationarity, or high-dimensional confounders.
- **Limited hyperparameter search**: The search ranges are predefined and may not encompass optimal configurations for all methods, potentially biasing performance comparisons.
- **Computational and ecological validity**: Exhaustive evaluation is prohibitively expensive; the absence of real-data benchmarks leaves the generalizability of findings to practical applications uncertain.

## Confidence
- Deep learning methods achieve superior robustness across violations: **Medium**
- NTS-NOTEARS performance is highly sensitive to standardization: **High**
- No single TSCD method dominates all misspecified settings: **High**
