# Convex Dominance in Deep Learning I: A Scaling Law of Loss and Learning Rate

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07145
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07145
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: The paper establishes a scaling law for loss and optimal learning rate by leveraging empirical weak convexity, achieving R²≥0.95 and extrapolating up to 80× training horizon and 70× model size across ResNet, ViT, GPT2, MoE, and VLM with SGD, AdamW, and Muon-NSGD; the approach fails for test loss under severe overfitting.

## Executive Summary
This work investigates loss dynamics in deep neural networks by exploiting the empirical observation that the optimization landscape quickly becomes weakly convex after a short warm-up period. Building on convex SGD theory, the authors extend classical convergence bounds to a broad class of learning rate schedules and optimizers, showing that schedules satisfying a simple qualification condition (e.g., linear decay, cosine decay, and WSD) achieve an O(1/√T) convergence rate when the peak learning rate is scaled as 1/√T. Moreover, they derive an abstract loss bound and fit its parameters using small-scale experiments. This yields a data-driven scaling law that jointly predicts the final loss and the optimal learning rate across widely varying training horizons and model sizes. Extensive experiments on ResNet, ViT, GPT2, Mixture-of-Experts, and vision-language models—using SGD, AdamW, and Muon-NSGD—confirm the law with R²≥0.95 and demonstrate accurate extrapolation up to 80× longer training and 70× larger models. The method also informs parameter-efficient fine-tuning, though prediction of test loss breaks down under severe overfitting.

## Method Summary
The core is an abstract loss bound (Eq. 3.1) parameterized by L∞, D, G, derived from convex SGD theory but applied to the last iterate. For a schedule that qualifies under Condition 2.5—which requires the integral of η_t over time to be O(√T) and the integral of η_t² to be O(1)—the bound simplifies to EL(w_T) ≈ L∞ + (Q(η_ref))/√T where η_peak = η_ref/√T. The authors first verify Condition 2.5 analytically for linear, cosine, and WSD schedules. They then run small-scale training runs across a grid of η_ref and total steps T, recording validation loss. Using non-negative linear regression on the collected (η_t, loss) data, they estimate L∞ and Q(η_ref). The value of η_ref minimizing Q(η_ref) is taken as the optimal scaling factor. To predict loss for a larger model or longer horizon, they transfer the optimal η_ref and the fitted L∞, then compute the expected loss via the 1/√T law. This procedure is repeated for multiple architectures and optimizers, with hundreds of runs to ensure robustness.

## Key Results
- The fitted loss bound achieves R² ≥ 0.95 on held-out data for all tested architectures and optimizers (ResNet, ViT, GPT2, MoE, VLM; SGD, AdamW, Muon-NSGD).
- The scaling law accurately extrapolates up to 80× longer training horizons and 70× larger model sizes, enabling compute–optimal learning rate selection without expensive hyperparameter sweeps.
- The approach also predicts optimal learning rates for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, but fails to predict test loss when severe overfitting occurs (validation loss diverges from training loss).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
- **Mechanism 1: Empirical weak convexity emerges quickly.** After a few warm-up epochs, the Hessian spectrum shifts to become predominantly positive, and the optimization trajectory satisfies a star-convexity or iterate-wise convexity property. This allows the use of convex SGD analysis to bound the loss. Evidence: Multiple works (Zhou et al., 2019; Yao et al., 2020) observe Hessian positivity, and the paper's own experiments show that the abstract bound (3.1) fits well only after the initial transient, confirming the convex-like regime.
- **Mechanism 2: Qualified schedules yield O(1/√T) convergence.** For schedules that satisfy Condition 2.5—intuitively, the cumulative learning rate grows as O(√T) and its squared sum as O(1)—the abstract bound reduces to a 1/√T decay in the final loss when η_peak is proportional to 1/√T. Linear decay, cosine decay, and WSD meet this condition; constant or inverse-square-root schedules do not. This explains the optimal scaling of the peak learning rate with horizon.
- **Mechanism 3: Data-driven fitting produces a universal scaling law.** By fitting the abstract bound on a modest set of small runs, the authors estimate L∞ and Q(η_ref). Since the 1/√T term dominates and Q(η_ref) is nearly independent of model size and horizon under qualified schedules, the same coefficients can be transferred to much larger scales. The high R² and successful extrapolation confirm that the underlying convex-like behavior persists across architectures and optimizers, and that the optimal η_ref is largely size-agnostic.

## Architecture Onboarding
The system consists of four main components that are chained: a training runner launches experiments across various models (ResNet, ViT, GPT2, MoE, VLM), optimizers (SGD, AdamW, Muon-NSGD), and learning rate schedules; an observation logger records validation loss at each iteration together with the true peak learning rate and schedule parameters; a fitting engine constructs a design matrix from the η_t sequence, then applies non-negative linear regression to estimate either (L∞, D, G) or (L∞, Q(η_ref)); finally, a scaling predictor uses the fitted coefficients to compute the optimal peak learning rate as η_peak = η_ref^*/√T and to predict loss for new (model size, training horizon) pairs. The flow is: training runner → observation logger → fitting engine → scaling predictor.

Critical path:
1. Identify qualified schedules analytically by verifying Condition 2.5 (e.g., linear, cosine, WSD). → 2. Run a small-scale grid of experiments covering a range of η_ref and total steps T. → 3. Validate that the abstract bound (Eq. 3.1) fits the observed losses with R² ≥ 0.95. → 4. Fit the scaling law (Generalization 4) to obtain L∞ and Q(η_ref). → 5. Transfer the optimal η_ref to larger models and longer horizons, and use the 1/√T expression to predict loss.

Design tradeoffs:
- Averaged vs last iterate: The paper uses the last iterate bound, which provides a tighter, stepwise prediction but is slightly less theoretically clean than the averaged iterate bound.
- Continuous approximation: Condition 2.5 is derived in the continuous-time limit; this simplifies analysis but introduces minor errors for very small step counts or discrete schedules.
- Non-negative regression: Enforcing non-negativity on the estimated coefficients ensures physical plausibility (e.g., loss cannot be negative) but might underfit if the true bound contains negative terms; in practice the authors still achieve high R².

Failure signatures:
- Low R² (<0.9) when fitting Eq. 3.1 suggests the convexity assumption is violated—typical during very early training or on landscapes with large negative curvature.
- A mismatch between predicted and actual optimal η_ref when transferring across tasks indicates that the D or G coefficients are not invariant to model size or dataset.
- The bound fails to predict test loss once severe overfitting occurs, as evidenced by validation loss diverging from training loss after many epochs.

First 3 experiments:
1. Train ResNet18 on ImageNet using SGD with four schedules (linear, cosine, cyclic, WSD) and verify that the abstract bound (3.1) fits well across all qualified schedules but fails for non-qualified ones (see Figure 2).
2. Train GPT2 (124M) on OpenWebText with AdamW and Muon-NSGD under the same four schedules, confirming that qualified schedules achieve the promised O(1/√T) convergence rate while others do not (see Figures 4–5).
3. Scale GPT2 from 0.1B to 1B to 7B parameters using Muon-NSGD, transferring the optimal η_ref obtained from the 0.1B model. The predicted loss matches actual results across a 80× range of training steps and a 70× range of model size (see Figure 10).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions; its discussion of limitations and future work is captured in the "Limitations" and "Next Checks" sections.

## Limitations
- Empirical weak convexity after warm‑up is not universally guaranteed; it may fail for highly non‑convex or multimodal objectives (e.g., GANs).
- The qualification condition (Condition 2.5) relies on a continuous‑time approximation, which can deviate for discrete schedules or small horizons, especially with adaptive optimizers like AdamW.
- The scaling law has only been validated up to 80× longer training and 70× larger models within the families tested (ResNet, ViT, GPT2, MoE, VLM); its extrapolation to other architectures or extreme scales remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- Weak convexity emergence: Medium – The phenomenon is supported by empirical observations on multiple architectures, but it is not proven theoretically and may fail for highly non-convex objectives.
- O(1/√T) convergence for qualified schedules: High – The analysis is rigorous under the continuous-time approximation and Condition 2.5, and experimental results confirm the predicted rate.
- Scaling law accuracy (R² ≥ 0.95) across horizons and sizes: High – Extensive experiments across diverse models, optimizers, and scaling factors yield consistently high R² and accurate extrapolation up to 80× longer training and 70× larger models.
- Transferability to parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning: Medium – The paper shows promising results, but the evaluation is limited to a few scenarios; broader validation is required.
- Failure of test loss prediction under overfitting: High – The paper clearly demonstrates that the scaling law breaks down when severe overfitting occurs (validation loss diverges from training loss).

## Next Checks
- Test the method on a broader set of architectures, including diffusion models, graph neural networks, and reinforcement learning agents, to assess the generality of the convexity assumption.
- Perform a discrete‑step analysis of Condition 2.5 with AdamW, varying warm‑up length and schedule shape, to quantify deviations from the continuous approximation.
- Investigate whether explicit regularization (e.g., dropout, weight decay) and early stopping can preserve the scaling law’s validity for test loss under overfitting regimes.
