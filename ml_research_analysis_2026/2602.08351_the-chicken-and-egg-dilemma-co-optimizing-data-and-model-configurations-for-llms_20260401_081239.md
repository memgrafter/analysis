# The Chicken and Egg Dilemma: Co-optimizing Data and Model Configurations for LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08351
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08351
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: JoBS achieves 86.4% on GSM8K versus 76.7% (DARTS+DoReMi) under a 50k step budget, significantly outperforming baselines in joint optimization of data and model configurations.

## Executive Summary
JoBS tackles the chicken‑and‑egg problem of jointly optimizing language model training data mixtures and model fine‑tuning configurations (e.g., LoRA). Existing methods optimize these axes separately, leading to suboptimal results. JoBS introduces an amortized Bayesian optimization framework: a small set of full‑training runs is used to train a neural performance predictor; subsequent configuration evaluations are performed cheaply via this predictor within a Bayesian optimizer. Theoretically, the approach derives an optimal budget split between predictor training and BO iterations to minimize average regret. Across six language tasks, JoBS consistently outperforms multi‑fidelity BO and naive combinations of data and model optimizers, achieving higher accuracy with lower computational cost.

## Method Summary
JoBS allocates a fixed total training budget (e.g., 50k steps). It first performs a small number of full‑training runs on candidate data‑model configurations to gather ground‑truth performance data. These data train a neural network that predicts performance for arbitrary configurations. The predictor acts as a fast surrogate for the true training objective. A Bayesian optimizer (using a Gaussian process) then iteratively proposes new configurations, evaluates them via the predictor, and updates its model. This loop continues until the budget is exhausted, with the best predicted configuration ultimately validated. The method includes a theoretical analysis that determines how many full runs should be invested in predictor training versus BO iterations to minimize expected regret.

## Key Results
- JoBS attains 86.4% on GSM8K, surpassing the 76.7% achieved by DARTS+DoReMi under the same 50k step budget.
- It converges faster and is more computationally efficient than multi‑fidelity BO and naive combinations of data/model optimizers.
- The theoretically derived optimal budget split (≈25–35 full runs for predictor training in their setup) matches empirical performance gains.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is amortization: full‑training LLM evaluations are prohibitively expensive, but a performance predictor trained on a modest set of such runs can provide cheap approximate evaluations for a large number of configurations. By using these predictions as inputs to Bayesian optimization, JoBS effectively explores the joint data‑model space with far fewer actual training runs. The predictor captures complex interactions between data mixture and model hyperparameters, while the Bayesian optimizer balances exploration and exploitation based on the predictor’s uncertainty. Theoretically, the budget allocation problem is cast as minimizing expected regret; the authors derive a closed‑form optimal split that depends on the predictor’s learning rate, the BO horizon, and the information gain of the GP. This combination of learned surrogate and principled BO yields both sample efficiency and strong empirical performance.

## Foundational Learning
**Bayesian Optimization (BO)**  
*Why needed*: BO efficiently optimizes black‑box functions with expensive evaluations by modeling them with a probabilistic surrogate and selecting points via an acquisition function. In JoBS it guides the search over data‑model configurations.  
*Quick check*: Compare BO to random search on a synthetic function (e.g., Branin) and measure the reduction in function evaluations to reach a given optimum.

**Gaussian Process (GP) Surrogate**  
*Why needed*: GPs provide a flexible, uncertainty‑aware model of the objective, essential for the acquisition step in BO. JoBS uses a GP over the predictor’s outputs to quantify epistemic uncertainty.  
*Quick check*: Fit a GP to a small dataset, then predict on held‑out points; verify that predictive variance is higher far from training points.

**Neural Performance Predictor**  
*Why needed*: Full LLM training is too slow for BO; the predictor approximates the training outcome at a fraction of the cost. It encodes the mapping from configuration (data proportions, LoRA settings) to validation performance.  
*Quick check*: Train the predictor on initial runs, then compute Spearman correlation between its predictions and actual held‑out full‑training results.

**Amortized Optimization**  
*Why needed*: The cost of training the predictor is spread over many cheap BO iterations, drastically reducing the total number of full trainings needed. This amortization is key to JoBS’s efficiency.  
*Quick check*: Plot total full‑training steps versus the number of BO iterations for different predictor training set sizes; observe that beyond a certain point, additional BO iterations cost negligible full steps.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Initial full‑training runs → Train neural predictor → Initialize GP with predictor predictions → BO loop: (GP suggests configuration → predictor evaluates → GP updates) → Termination → Final selected configuration → full validation

**Critical Path**  
1. Collect initial full‑training data (fixed small set).  
2. Train the neural predictor on these data.  
3. Feed predictor outputs for a broad set of configurations into the GP to bootstrap its prior.  
4. Run BO: at each iteration, the GP proposes a new configuration, the predictor returns an estimated performance, and the GP updates its model.  
5. After exhausting the budget, the configuration with the best predicted performance is finally validated with an actual full training (optional).

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Predictor training set size*: More initial runs improve predictor accuracy but consume budget that could be used for BO exploration. The theoretical budget split aims to balance this.  
- *Predictor capacity*: A larger neural net may capture complex patterns but risks overfitting on the limited initial data and increases training time.  
- *Fidelity of initial runs*: JoBS uses full training for initial data; using a shorter, cheaper “partial” run could increase the number of initial points but might degrade predictor quality.  
- *GP kernel choice*: Determines smoothness assumptions; a mismatch can lead to poor uncertainty estimates and suboptimal acquisition.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Low correlation between predictor and true performance on validation configurations, indicating the surrogate is unreliable.  
- GP acquisition stagnates (e.g., repeatedly suggesting similar points), suggesting overfitting or insufficient exploration.  
- Total regret significantly exceeds the theoretical bound, hinting at a violation of assumptions (e.g., heavy‑tailed noise, discontinuities).  
- The final selected configuration performs no better than random search, pointing to a broken amortization loop.

**First Three Experiments**  
1. *Ablation on initial run count*: Vary the number of full‑training runs used to train the predictor (e.g., 5, 10, 15, 20) while keeping total budget constant; measure final task performance and compare to the predicted optimal N.  
2. *Predictor model comparison*: Replace the neural predictor with a simpler model (e.g., random forest or linear regression) and assess BO’s ability to find high‑performing configurations, quantifying the impact on regret.  
3. *Surrogate robustness test*: Introduce controlled noise into the predictor outputs (e.g., Gaussian noise with varying variance) and evaluate how sensitive JoBS is to surrogate inaccuracies, relating findings to the theoretical assumptions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
- Question: Can existing scaling law formulas be directly incorporated into Gaussian process priors to improve optimization efficiency in JoBS?  
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state in the conclusion: "Can we incorporate existing scaling law formulas into GP priors directly to improve optimization efficiency?"  
- Why unresolved: The paper uses a neural network predictor separate from the GP, but scaling laws provide interpretable formulas that might better inform the GP prior structure if properly integrated.  
- What evidence would resolve it: An ablation comparing JoBS with a modified version where scaling law constraints are encoded in the GP kernel or prior mean function, showing whether this reduces the number of required full‑training runs or improves final performance.

**Open Question 2**  
- Question: Can a single performance predictor trained on one set of tasks be reused across substantially different downstream tasks without retraining?  
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors ask: "Can we reuse the same performance predictor across different tasks to improve amortization efficiency?"  
- Why unresolved: The paper's predictor is trained specifically for a target task's configuration space; reusing it across tasks could drastically reduce computational cost if the performance landscapes transfer.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments where a predictor trained on one task's data/model configurations is applied to optimize a completely different task (e.g., training on GSM8K math data and reusing for code generation), measuring degradation in optimization performance.

**Open Question 3**  
- Question: Does the theoretically derived optimal budget allocation between predictor training and BO iterations (N≈25–35 in their setup) generalize to other model scales, architectures, or optimization budgets?  
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Theorem 5.1 derives an optimal N that depends on constants (k, B, γ_T) that may vary across tasks; the paper only validates this range on Llama‑3‑8B with C=50k.  
- Why unresolved: The theoretical bound contains problem‑specific terms (maximum information gain γ_T, prediction error constant k) whose scaling with model size, task complexity, or budget is not established.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments varying model size (e.g., from 1B to 70B), budget C, and task type to see if the optimal N consistently falls within the predicted range or requires per‑task tuning.

**Open Question 4**  
- Question: How robust is JoBS when the Gaussian process assumptions (e.g., sub‑Gaussian observation noise, smooth RKHS‑bounded objective) are violated in realistic LLM fine‑tuning landscapes?  
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The theoretical analysis (Theorem 5.1) relies on Assumption 1 (prediction error is R‑sub‑Gaussian with R=k/√N) and bounded RKHS norm; these may not hold if performance landscapes contain discontinuities or heavy‑tailed noise.  
- Why unresolved: The empirical results show JoBS works despite prediction errors, but the theory's conditions are idealized; understanding failure modes or relaxing assumptions is necessary for broader trust.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Simulations with controlled corruptions (e.g., non‑Gaussian noise, non‑synthetic objective functions) to test whether the regret bound holds or if performance degrades gracefully; development of a more robust theoretical framework.

**Open Question 5**  
- Question: Can JoBS's amortization approach be extended to joint optimization involving more than two fidelity levels (e.g., training steps beyond just B_small and B) or other continuous fidelity dimensions like data quantity?  
- Basis in paper: [inferred] JoBS uses a binary fidelity (short vs. full training) and treats B_small as a fixed fraction; the paper contrasts with multi‑fidelity BO but does not explore more granular fidelity schedules or continuous fidelities.  
- Why unresolved: LLM training often employs multiple intermediate checkpoints; leveraging more fidelities could further amortize cost but requires redesigning the predictor and budget allocation theory.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Designing a multi‑fidelity predictor that ingests performance at several step milestones and deriving a corresponding optimal budget split across fidelities, then empirically showing reduced regret compared to the binary‑fidelity JoBS.

## Limitations
- The required size of the initial full‑training set for reliable predictor training is not quantified, leaving a hidden dependency on budget scale and task difficulty.
- Evaluation is limited to six language tasks; comparisons against newer, more advanced joint optimization or hyperparameter optimization methods for LLMs are absent.
- Neighbor corpus analysis reveals topical mismatch with returned papers (e.g., chicken egg detection) and zero citations, indicating the work is either highly specialized or not yet integrated, which limits external validation.

## Confidence
- Core methodological claim (amortizing cost via a learned predictor for BO): **High**
- Empirical superiority on the reported six tasks: **Medium**
- Claims about broad applicability, optimal budget split theory in practical scenarios, and superiority over all alternative approaches: **Low**
