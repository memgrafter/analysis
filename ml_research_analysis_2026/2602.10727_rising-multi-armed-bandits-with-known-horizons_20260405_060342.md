# Rising Multi-Armed Bandits with Known Horizons

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10727
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10727
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: CURE-UCB achieves lower cumulative regret and better average rank than state-of-the-art rising and non-stationary bandit algorithms in finite-horizon settings.

## Executive Summary
Rising Multi-Armed Bandits (RMAB) model scenarios where pulling an arm increases its future reward, making the finite horizon T a critical factor for optimal decision-making. Existing methods often ignore horizon dependence, leading to suboptimal regret. This paper introduces CURE-UCB, a horizon-aware algorithm that, for each arm, estimates the average expected reward it would yield if played for the remaining horizon by combining a sliding-window recent average, a projected future gain based on a rising function model, and an exploration bonus. Theoretically, for concave rising functions in a Linear-Then-Flat (LTF) environment, CURE-UCB achieves a regret upper bound of O(T q Υµ(T/K, q) + …), strictly dominating the horizon-agnostic R-ed-UCB. Empirical evaluations across LTF, concave, and online model selection benchmarks confirm that CURE-UCB consistently attains lower cumulative regret and improved average rank, underscoring the necessity of horizon awareness in finite-horizon RMAB.

## Method Summary
CURE-UCB operates by maintaining, for each arm, a sliding window of recently observed rewards to estimate the current reward level. It then projects the expected future reward trajectory using an assumed rising function (e.g., concave) up to the remaining horizon, computing the average reward the arm would generate if played exclusively thereafter. This projected average is combined with a standard Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) exploration bonus to form an index. Arms are selected greedily according to this index. The sliding window ensures responsiveness to non-stationarity, while the projection explicitly accounts for the finite horizon, enabling the algorithm to balance immediate versus long-term gains more effectively than horizon-agnostic baselines.

## Key Results
- Theoretical regret bound: For concave rising functions in LTF environments, CURE-UCB achieves O(T q Υµ(T/K, q) + …) and strictly improves upon horizon-agnostic R-ed-UCB.
- Empirical performance: Across LTF, concave, and online model selection benchmarks, CURE-UCB yields lower cumulative regret and better average rank compared to state-of-the-art rising and non-stationary bandit algorithms.
- Horizon awareness: The results demonstrate that incorporating horizon information into index computation is essential for optimal performance in finite-horizon RMAB.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The algorithm's success stems from its explicit integration of the finite horizon into the decision rule. By estimating what each arm could yield over the remaining steps, CURE-UCB avoids myopic choices that maximize immediate reward at the expense of long-term potential. The sliding window provides a robust estimate of the current state of each arm, while the exploration bonus prevents premature convergence to suboptimal arms. The projection component leverages the structure of rising functions (concavity in particular) to extrapolate future gains reliably when enough data is available. This combination allows the algorithm to dynamically allocate pulls to arms that balance current performance with future improvement, leading to superior regret guarantees.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Data Stream -> Sliding Window Buffer -> Current Reward Estimation -> Future Projection Module -> Exploration Bonus Calculator -> Index Aggregator -> Arm Selection

Critical path: The algorithm processes each pull sequentially: updates sliding window with latest reward, recomputes current average for each arm, projects the average reward if that arm were played exclusively for the remaining horizon, adds an exploration bonus (typically logarithmic in time divided by visit count), and selects the arm with the highest index. The cycle then repeats with the chosen arm’s observed reward.

Design tradeoffs:  
- Sliding window size: smaller windows react faster to changes but increase variance; larger windows reduce variance but may lag behind true current state.  
- Exploration bonus scaling: a larger constant encourages exploration but may sacrifice short-term reward; smaller constants risk insufficient exploration.  
- Projection model fidelity: assuming concave or LTF forms simplifies projection but could be misspecified; more complex models increase computational cost.

Failure signatures:  
- High regret on non-concave or discontinuous rising functions, indicating model mismatch.  
- Sensitivity to window size: poor performance if window is not tuned to the rate of reward growth.  
- Over-exploration leading to low cumulative reward in regimes where the horizon is short and exploitation is more valuable.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis is restricted to concave rising functions and Linear-Then-Flat (LTF) environments; applicability to general rising functions remains open.
- Empirical evaluation relies solely on synthetic benchmarks; real-world performance and robustness to model misspecification are not validated.
- Hyperparameter choices (sliding window length, exploration bonus coefficient) are not discussed, leaving their tuning and impact on performance uncertain.
