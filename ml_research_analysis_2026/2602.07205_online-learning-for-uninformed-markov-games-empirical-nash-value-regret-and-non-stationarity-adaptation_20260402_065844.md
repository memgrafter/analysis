# Online Learning for Uninformed Markov Games: Empirical Nash-Value Regret and Non-Stationarity Adaptation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07205
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07205
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces empirical Nash-value regret (ENR) and a parameter-free meta-algorithm achieving ENR ≤ O(min{√K + (CK)^{1/3}, √(LK)}), adapting to opponent non-stationarity and interpolating between O(√K) for stationary opponents and O(K^{2/3}) for worst-case.

## Executive Summary
The paper studies online learning in two-player uninformed Markov games, where the opponent's actions are unobserved. Prior work showed that external regret cannot be achieved without exponential dependence on horizon H, and instead proposed Nash-value regret with O(K^{2/3}) bound that does not adapt to opponent non-stationarity. The authors introduce empirical Nash-value regret (ENR), a stronger measure that reduces to external regret when the opponent is fixed. They analyze epoch-based V-learning under ENR, achieving regret eO(ηC + √(K/η)) with η as epoch incremental factor and C measuring cumulative total variation between opponent policies and a fixed minimax mixture. Building on this, they design a parameter-free meta-algorithm that adaptively restarts epoch V-learning, proving with high probability ENR ≤ O(min{√K + (CK)^{1/3}, √(LK)}) where L is the number of opponent policy switches. This bound smoothly interpolates between O(√K) for stationary opponents (C=0) and O(K^{2/3}) worst-case, while also adapting to switches. The result also resolves an open question by showing V-learning can achieve O(√K) regret in a stationary environment.

## Method Summary
The authors combine epoch-based V-learning with a meta-learning framework. The base algorithm partitions visits to each state-step pair into geometrically growing epochs, using an adversarial bandit subroutine to update policies and optimistic value estimates with bonuses. The analysis under ENR decomposes per-step differences and leverages optimism to bound regret by ηC + √(K/η). For parameter-free operation, a meta-algorithm runs multiple sub-blocks with different epoch factors η: the first fixed set of sub-blocks uses η=1/H to test stationarity, while a final sub-block uses a doubling guess on C. By monitoring an upper bound Φ on ENR within each sub-block, the algorithm restarts if Φ exceeds a threshold, enabling adaptation to both non-stationarity (C) and switches (L). This yields the overall interpolation bound without prior knowledge of C or L.

## Key Results
- ENR is strictly stronger than Nash-value regret and reduces to external regret when the opponent is stationary.
- Epoch V-learning with known η achieves ENR eO(ηC + √(K/η)); with optimal η, this gives O(√K) when C=0 and O(K^{2/3}) in worst-case.
- Parameter-free meta-algorithm achieves ENR eO(min{√K + (CK)^{1/3}, √(LK)}), adapting to unknown C and L, and confirming V-learning can attain O(√K) in stationary uninformed setting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Empirical Nash-value regret (ENR) is a strictly stronger regret notion than Nash-value regret and naturally reduces to standard external regret when the opponent follows a fixed policy.
- **Mechanism:** ENR redefines the Nash value by restricting the min-player (opponent) to the empirical set of policies they have actually played over the K episodes. This restriction makes ENR ≥ Nash-value regret (since the min-player has fewer strategies) and aligns with external regret when the empirical set is a singleton (i.e., the opponent was stationary).
- **Core assumption:** The opponent’s actions and policies are unobserved; the learner only observes state transitions and rewards.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "ENR not only is strictly stronger than the Nash-value regret (Nr) considered in prior work, but also reduces to the standard external regret when the opponent follows a fixed policy."  
  [section] Definition 2 in Section 2 defines ENR and Fact 1 formalizes ENR ≥ Nr and Enr = Reg when opponent fixed.  
  [corpus] Weak: No direct corpus validation; concept is novel to this paper.
- **Break condition:** If the opponent’s policies are highly diverse but there exists a single fixed policy that would yield low external regret, ENR may still be large because it forces comparison to a dynamic empirical best-response, preventing the interpolation to external regret.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Epoch-based V-learning, when analyzed under ENR, achieves regret eO(ηC + √(K/η)), which interpolates between O(√K) (when C=0) and O(K^{2/3}) (worst-case), by tuning η based on the non-stationarity measure C.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm partitions visits to each (step, state) pair into geometrically growing epochs. Within each epoch, it uses an adversarial bandit subroutine to update a policy, while maintaining an optimistic estimate of the value function that incorporates a bonus. The regret analysis decomposes per-step differences δ^_k_h and leverages optimism (V*_h ≤ V^k_h) plus a key bound on the cumulative gap between optimistic estimates and empirical Nash values, which scales with C (the total variation between opponent policies and a fixed minimax mixture). The parameter η controls epoch growth, balancing the C-dependent term and the √(K/η) term.
- **Core assumption:** The opponent is oblivious (policies do not depend on learner’s actions); η ∈ [|S|/K, 1/H]; known C for optimal η selection.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "establishing an eO(ηC + √(K/η)) regret bound, where η is the epoch incremental factor and C is a certain non-stationarity measure."  
  [section] Theorem 3 states the bound with η ∈ [|S|/K, 1/H]; Corollary 4 shows O(√K) when C=0.  
  [corpus] Weak: No corroborating corpus results; analysis is novel.
- **Break condition:** If C is large but η is not set to O(K^{-1/3}), the bound degrades to O(K^{2/3}) or worse. The algorithm requires knowledge of C to achieve the interpolation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A parameter-free meta-algorithm that adaptively restarts epoch V-learning achieves ENR eO(min{√K + (CK)^{1/3}, √(LK)}), automatically adapting to both the variance measure C and the number of policy switches L.
- **Mechanism:** The meta-algorithm runs multiple “sub-blocks” within each “block.” For the first 2^b sub-blocks of block b, it sets η = 1/H to test stationarity—if the opponent does not switch, these sub-blocks do not terminate early (since Φ stays below threshold). The final sub-block uses η = 2^{-2b}/H (a doubling guess on C). When any sub-block’s estimated regret Φ exceeds a threshold proportional to ηC + √(K/η), that sub-block terminates and the algorithm restarts with a smaller η (larger guessed C). Switch detection occurs because fixed-η sub-blocks will terminate when a policy switch causes Φ to spike. The overall regret combines contributions from all sub-blocks, yielding the min{...} bound.
- **Core assumption:** Opponent is oblivious; L ≤ K; K large enough (K ≥ 16H^2|S|^{3/2} for adaptive case); √(LK) term dominates when many switches, else (CK)^{1/3} term.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "propose a parameter-free algorithm that achieves an eO(min{√K + (CK)^{1/3}, √LK}) regret bound."  
  [section] Algorithm 2 and Theorem 7 (oblivious) and Theorem 28 (adaptive).  
  [corpus] Weak: No competing algorithms in corpus directly verify this interpolation.
- **Break condition:** If the opponent switches very frequently (L ≈ K) but C is small, the √(LK) term may be loose compared to what an algorithm aware of L could achieve. Also, the meta-algorithm may incur overhead from restarts, leading to suboptimal constants.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Markov Games (Zero-Sum, Two-Player)**
  - **Why needed here:** Models strategic interaction where both learner and opponent choose actions influencing state transitions and rewards. The uninformed setting (opponent actions unobserved) is central to the paper’s challenge.
  - **Quick check:** What structural property of Markov games makes learning without opponent observations difficult? (Answer: The learner cannot disentangle opponent influence from environment transitions.)
- **Concept: Regret Metrics (External vs. Nash-value vs. Empirical Nash-value)**
  - **Why needed here:** Defines learning objectives. External regret is impossible without exponential H-dependence in uninformed setting; Nash-value regret is weaker but non-adaptive; ENR bridges the two.
  - **Quick check:** How does ENR reduce to external regret? (Answer: When opponent policy is fixed, the empirical set is a singleton, so ENR = Reg.)
- **Concept: Total Variation Distance**
  - **Why needed here:** Measures non-stationarity C. TV(ν^k_h(s), ν*_h(s)) captures how far each opponent policy is from a fixed minimax mixture, aggregating over states and time.
  - **Quick check:** Why TV instead of KL? (Answer: TV directly bounds the difference in expected value functions under different policies, facilitating regret decomposition.)
- **Concept: V-learning and Optimism**
  - **Why needed here:** Model-free algorithm using optimistic value estimates and adversarial bandit subroutines; forms the base for epoch-based analysis. Epoch structure enables adaptive η.
  - **Quick check:** What role does the “bonus” β_n play in V-learning? (Answer: Ensures optimism, i.e., V^k_h(s) ≥ V*_h(s) w.h.p., enabling the analysis to upper bound ENR by the learner’s value estimates.)
- **Concept: Adaptive Restarting (Doubling Trick)**
  - **Why needed here:** Allows parameter-free adaptation to unknown C and detection of policy switches L. Without it, one must know C to set η optimally.
  - **Quick check:** Why do the first 2^b sub-blocks use η=1/H? (Answer: To keep the ηC term negligible if opponent is stationary, so Φ stays below threshold and the sub-block won’t prematurely restart.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** ENR Calculator -> Epoch V-learning Core -> Meta-scheduler (with Non-stationarity estimator)
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Initialize: Set global K, δ, choose η schedule.  
  2. For each block b and sub-block ℓ:  
     a. Start new epoch V-learning instance with input (K, δ/(2K^6), η(b)_ℓ).  
     b. On each episode k in sub-block: run V-learning step, collect r^k_h, update V^k, compute Φ = Σ_k (V^k_1(s_k^1) - Σ_h r^k_h) + √(ι|T|).  
     c. If Φ exceeds D(b)_ℓ = Θ( η(b)_ℓ H^3 eC(b)_ℓ + H√(ι|S||T| log(K)/η(b)_ℓ) (with eC(b)_ℓ estimated or set to 0/1?), terminate sub-block.  
  3. After all sub-blocks, total ENR is sum over all completed sub-blocks’ actual rewards vs. empirical Nash values.
  **Note:** The algorithm as presented is conceptual; practical implementation requires estimating eC(b)_ℓ or using the threshold as a function of η only (as in the proof they use η(b)_ℓ H^3 eC(b)_ℓ but actually eC(b)_ℓ is unknown; in meta-algorithm they set thresholds based on η only and assume termination implies sufficient non-stationarity). The design in Algorithm 2 sets D based on η and |T| only, and the analysis shows that if opponent is stationary (C=0), then Φ will be ≤ D for η=1/H; if not, eventually some sub-block will exceed D.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Tuning η: Fixed-η epoch V-learning needs C to achieve interpolation; meta-algorithm avoids tuning but introduces overhead from multiple restarts and sub-block management.  
  - Switch detection: Using fixed-η sub-blocks (η=1/H) ensures O(√K) if no switches, but if switches occur, these sub-blocks may terminate early, incurring regret proportional to their lengths. The meta-algorithm balances by also running a doublingη sub-block that eventually captures the (CK)^{1/3} term.  
  - Computational cost: Meta-algorithm runs O(log K) instances of V-learning (one per sub-block) sequentially; this could be parallelized but increases memory.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  1. **C underestimated:** If C is larger than the guess used in the final sub-block’s η, the (CK)^{1/3} term may be larger than √K, but the meta-algorithm still adapts by restarting with smaller η? Actually the meta-algorithm attempts to guess C via doubling; if C is huge, it may take many restarts to reach right η, causing cumulative overhead.  
  2. **High-frequency switches:** If opponent switches very quickly (L ≈ K), the √(LK) bound may be loose, and the algorithm may not detect switches fast enough because fixed-η blocks need enough visits to build confidence; early switches might not trigger termination, leading to higher-than-√(LK) regret.  
  3. **Incorrect switch detection:** If the opponent is stationary but the algorithm sees a spike in Φ due to variance (false alarm), it may restart unnecessarily, increasing regret.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline validation on stationary MDP:** Implement single-agent MDP by setting opponent to single action; compare epoch V-learning (η=1/H) against UCB-H or other algorithms; verify O(√K) external regret and that ENR matches Reg.  
  2. **Controlled non-stationarity (known C):** Create opponent policies that vary with known TV distance C (e.g., mix between two policies with probability p_t). Test fixed-η V-learning with η tuned optimally vs. meta-algorithm; confirm interpolation: for small C, both near √K; for large C, both near K^{2/3}.  
  3. **Policy switch detection:** Construct opponent that switches policies at known intervals (L given). Run meta-algorithm and measure whether first 2^b sub-blocks terminate exactly at switches; check total ENR against √(LK). Compare against a variant that ignores switch detection (using only doubling η) to see benefit of L-adaptation.

## Limitations
- Oblivious opponent assumption: analysis requires opponent policies independent of learner’s actions; adaptive opponents may break guarantees.
- Bandit subroutine specificity: guarantees rely on a particular FTRL-based bandit; simpler alternatives may not achieve required regret.
- Practical constants: Hidden factors in confidence widths and thresholds may be large, making high-probability bounds loose in practice.
