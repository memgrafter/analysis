# Muon in Associative Memory Learning: Training Dynamics and Scaling Laws

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05725
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05725
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Muon optimizer equalizes learning across hierarchical frequency components, provides exponential speedup over gradient descent in noiseless regime, and under label noise with power-law spectrum achieves O(1/T^2) scaling versus GD's Ω(1/T^{1−1/β}), with empirical confirmation on synthetic long-tail classification and LLaMA pre‑training.

## Executive Summary
The paper theoretically investigates the Muon optimizer within a linear associative memory model featuring a hierarchical frequency spectrum. Standard gradient descent exhibits imbalanced learning rates across frequency components, bottlenecked by low-frequency items. Muon’s matrix‑sign update counters this imbalance, equalizing progress and achieving exponential convergence when data is noiseless. Under label noise with a power‑law decay of group frequencies, the authors derive scaling laws showing Muon’s excess risk decays as O(1/T^2), while gradient descent is limited to Ω(1/T^{1−1/β}). Muon is interpreted as an implicit matrix preconditioner arising from adaptive task alignment and the block‑symmetric structure of the gradient. These theoretical predictions are supported by experiments on synthetic long‑tail classification, imbalanced MNIST, and LLaMA pre‑training, demonstrating Muon’s superior data efficiency and faster convergence.

## Method Summary
The study centers on a linear softmax associative memory: query embeddings E ∈ ℝ^{d×K} and answer embeddings Ẽ ∈ ℝ^{d×K} (orthogonal, equinorm) are multiplied by a weight matrix W ∈ ℝ^{K×K} to produce logits Ẽ^T W E, trained with cross‑entropy. Data frequencies follow a hierarchical partition into M groups of equal size C=K/M, with group probabilities r₁ > r₂ > … > r_M. Gradient descent (GD) updates W using the raw gradient, which scales with group frequency p_j, causing slower learning for tail groups. Muon replaces the gradient with its matrix sign msgn(∇L), computed via SVD, effectively inverting the curvature along task‑aligned directions. Theoretical analysis yields: (i) GD’s per‑group loss decays as L_j ∝ 1/(p_j t), creating imbalance; (ii) in the noiseless case, Muon’s loss decays exponentially L ∝ e^{-t} ; (iii) with i.i.d. label noise and a power‑law spectrum (r_i ∝ i^{-β}), Muon’s excess risk follows O(1/T^2) (three‑phase dynamics), while GD’s lower bound is Ω(1/T^{1−1/β}). The paper validates these findings with (a) synthetic linear simulations confirming equalized group learning, (b) imbalanced MNIST classification using a 2‑layer MLP, and (c) LLaMA pre‑training showing steeper scaling for Muon.

## Key Results
- Muon’s matrix‑sign update equalizes learning across frequency components, eliminating GD’s low‑frequency bottleneck.
- In noiseless settings, Muon converges exponentially while GD converges only polynomially (Θ(1/t)).
- Under label noise with a power‑law spectrum, Muon’s scaling exponent is 2 versus GD’s 1−1/β, offering a provable speedup.
- Muon acts as an implicit matrix preconditioner that aligns with the task‑representation basis, unlike coordinate‑wise SignGD which fails without oracle access.
- Experiments on synthetic data, imbalanced MNIST, and LLaMA pre‑training demonstrate Muon’s superior data efficiency and faster convergence.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Muon’s matrix‑sign operator equalizes effective learning rates across frequency components by converting the gradient into a near‑identity matrix in the task‑representation basis, counteracting the frequency‑dependent scaling inherent in the raw gradient. In the linear softmax associative memory model, the gradient ∇L(W) scales with query frequency p_j, causing GD to progress slowly on low‑frequency items. The matrix sign msgn(∇L), computed via SVD, approximates I_K in the rotated space (xW = rE^T W E), making the update ΔxW ≈ η I_K for all directions. This isotropization relies on the hierarchical frequency spectrum (Assumption 3.2) and gradient alignment with the task representation. Evidence includes Lemma 6.1 showing msgn(G_t) ≈ I_K, and related work on spectral optimizers. The mechanism fails if the frequency spectrum is flat or if gradient noise corrupts the sign.

### Mechanism 2
Muon implicitly preconditions the update by aligning with the task‑representation basis, exploiting the block‑symmetric gradient structure that GD and coordinate‑wise SignGD ignore. The gradient ∇L(W) decomposes into a block‑diagonal plus low‑rank all‑ones structure (Equation 3). Its SVD yields singular vectors that match the task basis when group frequencies within groups are uniform. The matrix sign then inverts the effective curvature along task directions (Lemma 6.1). In contrast, SignGD applies element‑wise signs in the original parameter space, which cannot adapt to this latent geometry without oracle access to the basis E, Ẽ. The mechanism requires the block‑diagonal structure; if intra‑group frequencies are non‑uniform or the gradient is extremely ill‑conditioned, the near‑identity guarantee breaks.

### Mechanism 3
In the noiseless regime, Muon achieves an exponential convergence rate O(e^{-t}) while GD converges polynomially (Θ(1/t)), yielding an exponential speedup. Under label noise with power‑law spectra (r_i ∝ i^{-β}), the dynamics split into descent (exponential) and oscillation (constant) phases; the trade‑off leads to a learning‑rate‑dependent scaling law O(1/T^2) for Muon versus a heavy‑tail‑limited lower bound Ω(1/T^{1−1/β}) for GD. The exponential speedup requires noiseless separable data; noise introduces an oscillation floor O(η²). If β is close to 1, the GD exponent 1−1/β becomes small and the relative gain diminishes. Extreme learning rates can destabilize GD or cause Muon to oscillate prematurely.

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component Map
Input: Query and answer embedding matrices E, Ẽ (orthogonal, equinorm) → Model: Linear weight matrix W → Output: Softmax scores Ẽ^T W E → Loss: Cross‑entropy (with optional label noise α) → Optimizer core: Compute gradient ∇L(W) → SVD → msgn(∇L) → Parameter update: W ← W – η msgn(∇L). Data distribution: K items partitioned into M groups of equal size C, with decreasing group frequencies.

#### Critical Path
1. Forward pass computes logits Ẽ^T W E.
2. Cross‑entropy loss L is computed.
3. Gradient ∇L(W) is obtained.
4. SVD of ∇L yields singular vectors and singular values; msgn is formed by taking sign of singular values.
5. Parameter update: W ← W – η msgn(∇L). The SVD step is the computational bottleneck; practical implementations approximate it.

#### Design Tradeoffs
- **Muon vs. GD/Adam:** Muon trades higher per‑step compute (SVD) for faster convergence and better data efficiency.
- **Learning rate:** Too large η triggers premature oscillation (noiseless: stability requires η p(1) ≤ 1; noisy: excess risk ζ η²); too small slows descent. Theory suggests η ∝ (log K)/T for scaling laws.
- **Precision vs. speed:** Exact SVD guarantees theoretical isotropization; approximations (e.g., randomized methods) reduce memory/compute but may weaken guarantees (Lemma 6.1 relies on exactness).

#### Failure Signatures
- Divergence or sustained oscillation: Check if η violates stability (e.g., η p₁ > 1 for GD) or if η² term dominates noise floor.
- Persistent imbalance: Compute msgn(∇L) – I_K norm; if not o_K(1), the assumptions (e.g., M² ≪ K) may be violated or SVD implementation faulty.
- TRA‑SignGD underperformance: Requires oracle task basis; if E, Ẽ are estimated from noisy data or are non‑orthogonal, the advantage disappears.

#### First 3 Experiments
1. Synthetic linear simulation (K=100, M=C=10, hierarchical frequencies, α=0.1) comparing GD, Muon, TRA‑SignGD, and SignGD over T=50 steps; metrics: loss L(t) and imbalance Δ_t.
2. Imbalanced MNIST classification with 2‑layer MLP, group retention rates (many‑shot: 100%, medium: 50%, few: 25%), evaluating per‑group accuracy and convergence speed.
3. LLaMA pre‑training (~100M parameters) across token budgets 1B–6B, sweeping learning rates for Muon and Adam, fitting scaling law L(D) = L* + a D^{-γ}.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Does Muon’s speedup hold with non‑orthogonal or non‑uniform embeddings? The analysis assumes orthogonal equinorm embeddings (Assumption 3.1); real embeddings violate this, so the robustness of the theoretical guarantees is unknown.

### Open Question 2
How does momentum, disabled in the core theoretical analysis, affect training dynamics and scaling laws? Momentum may alter the block‑diagonal Hessian structure and oscillation phases.

### Open Question 3
Can Muon’s advantages be extended to deep nonlinear models like Transformers? The study uses a linear proxy; nonlinearities and multiple layers may break the gradient structure that enables isotropization.

### Open Question 4
How sensitive are the scaling laws to the hierarchical group frequency assumption? The derivation relies on a power‑law spectrum; real data may follow different long‑tail distributions, potentially changing the exponent 2.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis relies on simplified linear model with orthogonal embeddings and uniform intra‑group frequencies; real neural networks do not satisfy these, so the guarantees may not hold exactly.
- Scaling laws are asymptotic, requiring conditions like M² ≪ K and η ∝ (log K)/T; typical training setups may not meet these, leading to different exponents.
- LLaMA pre‑training results lack full experimental details (architecture, hyperparameters), hindering reproducibility and leaving open alternative explanations.
