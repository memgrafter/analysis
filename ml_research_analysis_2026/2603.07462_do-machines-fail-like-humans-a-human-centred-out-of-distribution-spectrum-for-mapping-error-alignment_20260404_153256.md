# Do Machines Fail Like Humans? A Human-Centred Out-of-Distribution Spectrum for Mapping Error Alignment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07462
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07462
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The paper redefines out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions as a spectrum of human perceptual difficulty quantified by Glass's Δ effect size, identifies four calibrated regimes (reference, near-OOD, far-OOD, extreme-OOD), and shows that model-human error alignment varies significantly across these regimes, with vision-language models (VLMs) being most consistent and architecture rankings (CNNs vs. ViTs) swapping between near- and far-OOD.

## Executive Summary
Current methods for comparing AI and human errors under out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions rely on model-centric definitions or arbitrary distortions, failing to provide a common scale for cross-distortion analysis. This paper introduces a human-centred framework that defines OOD based on quantified human perceptual difficulty. Human accuracy on distorted stimuli is logit-transformed and standardized via Glass's Δ effect size relative to an undistorted reference, creating a continuous OOD spectrum. A Gaussian Mixture Model partitions this spectrum into four distinct regimes of perceptual challenge. Applying this to object recognition models (CNNs, ViTs, VLMs), the study finds that model-human alignment, measured via normalized Error Consistency and Misclassification Agreement, is regime-dependent. VLMs maintain the most consistent alignment across near- and far-OOD conditions, while CNNs outperform ViTs in near-OOD but are surpassed by ViTs in far-OOD, revealing architecture-specific inductive biases that manifest differently along the human perceptual difficulty spectrum.

## Method Summary
The method constructs a human-centred OOD spectrum and uses it to compare model-human error alignment. Human accuracy data from the `modelvshuman` dataset for various distortion conditions is collected. Each condition's accuracy is logit-transformed to approximate normality, and its deviation from an undistorted reference is quantified using Glass's Δ effect size (mean difference divided by reference standard deviation), yielding a continuous OOD score. A Gaussian Mixture Model fitted to all OOD scores (including reference) selects a four-component solution, defining regimes: reference (undistorted), near-OOD, far-OOD, and extreme-OOD (chance-level). Model predictions are generated for all images. Alignment is measured using Error Consistency (EC) and Misclassification Agreement (MA) between each model and the average human response per condition. These raw scores are normalized by the human-human baseline (pairwise agreement among the four human participants per condition) to produce a normalized alignment ratio ρ. Models are ranked by their mean ρ across representative conditions within each regime, and family-level clustering is tested via permutation on Euclidean distances of full alignment vectors.

## Key Results
- Vision-language models (VLMs) exhibit the most consistent model-human alignment across near- and far-OOD regimes.
- In near-OOD conditions, CNNs align better with humans than Vision Transformers (ViTs); in far-OOD conditions, ViTs surpass CNNs.
- Perceptual difficulty (as quantified by the OOD spectrum) structures error patterns more strongly than the type of distortion applied.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Human-Perceptual-Difficulty-Based OOD Scoring
- **Claim:** OOD is redefined not by model training data deviation but by human perceptual difficulty, enabling principled comparison across diverse distortions.
- **Mechanism:** Human accuracy per condition is logit-transformed to achieve approximate normality. For each distorted condition, an OOD score is computed as Glass's Δ: the difference between its mean logit-accuracy and the undistorted reference mean, divided by the reference standard deviation. This effect size places all conditions on a common, dimensionless scale relative to a single reference distribution.
- **Core assumption:** Human perceptual difficulty is the appropriate invariant scale for comparing distortion impacts; the logit transform yields normal accuracy distributions suitable for parametric effect size calculation.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states the framework "redefines the degree of OOD as a spectrum of human perceptual difficulty... quantified by Glass’s ∆ effect size." Section 2.1.2 explains the operationalization using Glass's Δ to ensure comparability. The paper reports successful normality after logit transformation (Appendix A).

### Mechanism 2: Data-Driven Regime Identification via Gaussian Mixture Modeling
- **Claim:** The continuous OOD scores naturally cluster into four qualitatively distinct regimes (reference, near-OOD, far-OOD, extreme-OOD) corresponding to different levels of perceptual challenge.
- **Mechanism:** A Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) is fitted to the distribution of all OOD scores (including the reference). Model selection criteria (BIC, AICc) consistently select a four-component solution. Each component is interpreted as a regime, ordered by mean OOD score, with the extreme-OOD component corresponding to chance-level human performance.
- **Core assumption:** The distribution of human behavioral deviation across synthetic distortions reflects underlying qualitative shifts in information processing; the statistically optimal number of components reveals these regimes.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract mentions identifying "four distinct regimes of perceptual challenge." Section 2.1.3 details the GMM fitting and component selection. Section 2.2.1 shows that human-human alignment metrics vary systematically across the four regimes,支持 their behavioral meaningfulness.

### Mechanism 3: Regime-Dependent Alignment Assessment with Normalized Metrics
- **Claim:** Model-human error alignment is not static but varies with perceptual difficulty regime, revealing architecture-specific biases.
- **Mechanism:** For each model and condition, Error Consistency (EC) and Misclassification Agreement (MA) are computed against the average human response. These raw scores are normalized by the human-human baseline (average pairwise EC/MA among the four human participants) for that exact condition, yielding a ratio ρ. The mean ρ across representative conditions within a regime determines the model's regime-specific alignment. Rankings are derived from these means, and family clustering is tested on full alignment vectors.
- **Core assumption:** Normalizing by the condition-specific human-human baseline accounts for inherent variability in human consensus, providing a meaningful ceiling-relative measure. The human-human baseline serves as the empirical upper bound for that perceptual challenge.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract's key findings on VLM consistency and the CNN/ViT swap are based on this regime-specific normalized analysis. Section 2.3.4 presents these results. Section 2.2.1 demonstrates that human-human alignment itself varies by regime, justifying the normalization.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Human-Centered OOD Definition
- **Why needed here:** The entire framework replaces model-centric OOD definitions (based on training data statistics) with a definition rooted in human perceptual difficulty. This is the foundational shift that enables objective cross-distortion comparison of error patterns.
- **Quick check question:** Why is defining OOD based on model training data insufficient for comparing human and machine error patterns?

### Concept: Psychometric Effect Sizing (Glass's Δ)
- **Why needed here:** To place different distortion conditions on a common scale, the paper uses an effect size (Glass's Δ) that standardizes the mean difference in logit-accuracy relative to a single, shared reference distribution. This allows "apples-to-apples" comparison of perceptual difficulty across all conditions.
- **Quick check question:** What is the key difference between using a raw accuracy difference and using Glass's Δ effect size for comparing distortion conditions?

### Concept: Alignment Normalization by Human-Human Baseline
- **Why needed here:** Raw model-human alignment scores (EC, MA) lack context. Normalizing by the human-human alignment for the same stimulus set accounts for the fact that some distortions produce more consensus among humans than others, setting a dynamic, condition-specific ceiling.
- **Quick check question:** Why should we not interpret a model-human EC value of 0.4 as inherently "good" or "bad" in isolation?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Data Ingestion -> OOD Spectrum Construction Pipeline -> Alignment Calculation Module -> Regime-Level Aggregation & Ranking -> Family-Level Analysis

### Critical path
The pipeline is linear but regime assignment (Step 2) is the critical gating step. All downstream analysis (Steps 3-5) depends on correctly labeling each distortion condition as near-OOD or far-OOD. An error here fundamentally changes all alignment comparisons and rankings. The second critical dependency is the accurate computation of the human-human baseline for each condition for normalization.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Logit transformation vs. alternative transforms:** Chosen for normalizing bounded accuracy data. Alternatives (e.g., arcsine) exist but the paper reports success with logit.
- **GMM component selection:** Relies on BIC/AICc. A different criterion could yield a different number of regimes (e.g., 3 or 5), changing analysis granularity.
- **Representative condition selection:** For regime-level ranking, one condition per distortion type is selected from near/far-OOD. This simplifies analysis but introduces sample selection bias; using all conditions within a regime would be more robust but complicated by intra-type correlation.
- **Alignment metric choice:** EC and MA capture different aspects; averaging them into ρ_mean is a design choice that could be weighted or analyzed separately.
- **Normalization strategy:** Dividing by human-human alignment assumes it is a stable "ceiling." If human-human alignment is very low (e.g., in far-OOD), normalization can disproportionately inflate model scores.

### Failure signatures
1. **Non-normal logit-accuracies:** If Shapiro-Wilk tests on logit-transformed human accuracies reject normality for many conditions (p<0.05), the use of Glass's Δ (parametric) is questionable. *Check:* Appendix Table S4 should show most p>0.05.
2. **Unstable regime boundaries:** If GMM component means overlap substantially or condition assignments are inconsistent across random seeds, the four regimes are not robust. *Check:* Visual inspection of Figure 2D and t-SNE (Figure 3B) should show clear separation by regime color.
3. **Missing human-human baseline:** If a condition has too few human participants or their agreement is at chance, the normalized ratio ρ becomes unstable (division by near-zero). *Check:* Human-human EC/MA values in Figure 3A should be >0 for all non-extreme conditions.
4. **Regime contamination:** Including extreme-OOD (chance performance) conditions in ranking analysis produces meaningless alignment ratios. *Check:* Ensure all selected "representative" conditions have human accuracy well above chance (per Table S3).

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate OOD Spectrum Construction:** Using the provided human accuracy data, apply the logit transform, compute Glass's Δ for every distortion condition, fit a GMM, and validate that BIC/AICc selects 4 components. Manually inspect regime assignments against Figure 2C/D.
2. **Validate Regime Separation via Alignment:** For each assigned condition, plot human-human EC and MA. Confirm a clear step-wise decline from reference -> near -> far -> extreme, establishing that regimes are behaviorally meaningful.
3. **Test Family Clustering Hypothesis:** For the near-OOD regime, take each model's vector of EC and MA values across all distortion types. Compute pairwise Euclidean distances between all models. Run the permutation test for H2 (super-family vs. cross-super-family distances). Verify the reported effect size and p-value pattern.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How does denser sampling of distortion severity parameters affect the calibration and boundaries of the far-OOD transitional regime?** The current regime definitions may be influenced by sparse sampling; denser data could reveal finer-grained structure or redefine regime boundaries. *Evidence needed:* A new human study with more severity levels per distortion type, recomputing the spectrum and checking boundary stability.
2. **Does the framework remain valid when applied to natural distribution shifts (e.g., cross-dataset or real-world variations) rather than synthetic corruptions?** The current spectrum relies on controlled synthetic distortions; natural shifts may not align with the same perceptual difficulty structure. *Evidence needed:* Collect human accuracy on natural OOD benchmarks, compute OOD scores relative to a standard reference, and check if similar regime groupings emerge.
3. **To what extent do observed alignment differences across regimes reflect inherent architectural biases versus variations in training data, objectives, and supervision?** Models compared differ in training (e.g., CLIP vs. supervised ImageNet); disentangling architecture from training is necessary for causal understanding. *Evidence needed:* Train multiple architectures with identical protocols and compare alignment profiles; or use architecture-identical variants with different training.
4. **At what threshold of perceptual difficulty (OOD score) do VLMs lose their semantic advantage, causing error patterns to converge toward vision-only models?** VLMs align better than CNNs in near-OOD and comparably to ViTs in far-OOD, but the transition point is unknown. *Evidence needed:* Analyze alignment ratios as a continuous function of OOD score for VLMs vs. vision-only backbones; identify the OOD score where curves intersect or diverge significantly.
5. **Would increasing the number of human participants per condition substantially change estimated human-human baselines and thus normalized model-human rankings?** The study used 4 participants per condition; human variability might be underestimated, affecting the denominator in alignment ratios. *Evidence needed:* Replicate human data collection with larger groups (e.g., 20-30 per condition), recompute baselines, and assess sensitivity of normalized rankings.

## Limitations
- **Regime stability:** GMM boundaries may be sensitive to human data noise (small participant counts per condition) and initialization randomness.
- **Human scaling assumption:** The logit-Δ approach assumes normality and that human accuracy differences define OOD; this psychometric scaling lacks cross-validation.
- **Model fidelity:** Exact pretrained checkpoints and preprocessing details are not fully specified; using standard weights may affect reported rankings and alignment patterns.

## Confidence
- **Four-regime spectrum:** Medium (statistical support strong via GMM selection criteria, but psychometric validity unproven).
- **ρ alignment metric (normalized EC/MA):** Medium (methodology sound but relies on small human samples; human-human baseline variability is a concern).
- **Regime-dependent rankings (VLM consistency, CNN↔ViT swap):** Medium (observed in study but may not generalize to other models/distortions).
- **Difficulty-over-distortion primacy:** Low (evidence is indirect from this specific distortion set; needs broader tests with semantic corruptions).

## Next Checks
1. **Robustness check:** Recompute the OOD spectrum using non-parametric effect sizes (e.g., rank-based) and alternative clustering methods (e.g., hierarchical); confirm that the four-regime structure and condition assignments remain stable.
2. **Human scaling check:** Increase the number of human participants per distortion condition (to ≥10), recompute human-human baselines, and evaluate whether the normalized alignment rankings (ρ) for models change significantly.
3. **Breadth check:** Test additional architectures (e.g., ConvNeXt, Swin Transformer, Flamingo) and additional distortion families (e.g., fog, adversarial patches) to verify whether the observed pattern of regime-dependent alignment and the CNN/ViT swap generalizes.
