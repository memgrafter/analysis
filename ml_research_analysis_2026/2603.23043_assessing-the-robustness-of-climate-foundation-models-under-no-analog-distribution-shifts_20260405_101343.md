# Assessing the Robustness of Climate Foundation Models under No-Analog Distribution Shifts
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23043
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23043
- **Reference count:** 5
- **Primary result:** Climate foundation models show an accuracy-stability trade-off under no-analog shifts; ClimaX achieves lowest absolute error but highest relative sensitivity, with precipitation errors increasing up to 8.44% under extreme forcing.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates climate emulators on out-of-distribution (OOD) future states, addressing data contamination from training on simulations that include future scenarios. By restricting models to historical-only training (1850-2014), the authors benchmark robustness via temporal extrapolation (2015-2023) and cross-scenario forcing shifts (SSP1-2.6 vs. SSP5-8.5). Results reveal a trade-off: the ClimaX foundation model attains the lowest absolute errors but exhibits the highest relative sensitivity, with precipitation errors rising significantly under extreme forcing. Simpler CNN-based architectures demonstrate greater relative stability. Temperature projections remain robust, but precipitation degrades under forcing shifts, highlighting limitations of high-capacity models when constrained to historical dynamics.

## Method Summary
The study uses the ClimateSet dataset with monthly forcing agents (CO₂, CH₄, BC, SO₂) and outputs (surface air temperature, precipitation). Models are trained exclusively on 1850–2014 data and evaluated on two OOD benchmarks: (1) temporal extrapolation to 2015–2023 (historical forcing continuation), and (2) cross-scenario shifts between SSP1‑2.6 and SSP5‑8.5 (2015–2100). The primary metric is latitude‑longitude weighted RMSE (LL‑RMSE), with sensitivity measured as percent change relative to an in-distribution baseline. Three models are compared: a U‑Net, a ConvLSTM, and the ClimaX foundation model (fine‑tuned from CMIP6 pre‑trained weights). Strict temporal splits prevent future data leakage, but uncertainties remain regarding ClimaX’s pre‑training composition and exact preprocessing steps.

## Key Results
- **Accuracy‑stability trade‑off:** ClimaX achieves the lowest absolute LL‑RMSE but the highest percent increase under OOD shifts, particularly for precipitation.
- **Precipitation degrades significantly** under cross‑scenario forcing shifts (up to 8.44% error increase under SSP5‑8.5), while temperature remains stable or improves.
- **Simpler CNN‑based architectures** (U‑Net, ConvLSTM) exhibit lower relative sensitivity despite higher baseline errors, suggesting architectural inductive biases influence robustness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The observed robustness differences stem from two intertwined factors. First, **data contamination**: foundation models like ClimaX are often pre‑trained on CMIP6 data that may include future scenarios, potentially embedding patterns from out‑of‑distribution periods that do not generalize to true no‑analog states. The study’s historical‑only fine‑tuning mitigates but cannot fully eliminate this if pre‑training already exposed the model to future dynamics. Second, **architectural sensitivity**: transformer‑based global attention mechanisms in ClimaX are highly responsive to changes in forcing magnitudes, whereas CNN‑based models with local spatial priors impose smoother spatial variations that buffer against abrupt distribution shifts. This explains why ClimaX excels in‑distribution but suffers larger relative errors when forcing trajectories deviate from historical norms, especially for precipitation—a variable with nonlinear, stochastic physics that amplifies sensitivity to forcing changes.

## Foundational Learning
- **No‑analog distribution shifts**: Future climate states with no historical precedent; needed to evaluate true OOD generalization.  
  *Quick check:* Verify training data excludes any years beyond 2014.
- **Temporal extrapolation**: Testing on future years while holding forcing scenarios constant; isolates model’s ability to extend trends.  
  *Quick check:* Hold out 2015–2023 and ensure no temporal leakage.
- **Cross‑scenario forcing shifts**: Evaluating on different SSP pathways (e.g., SSP5‑8.5 vs SSP1‑2.6); tests sensitivity to external forcing magnitude.  
  *Quick check:* Train on one SSP, test on another, but here both are OOD.
- **Climate foundation models**: Large pre‑trained models (e.g., ClimaX) adapted to climate tasks; needed for transfer learning but risk contamination.  
  *Quick check:* Audit pre‑training data composition for future years.
- **Data contamination**: Inclusion of future data in training/pre‑training that artificially inflates OOD performance; critical to assess real robustness.  
  *Quick check:* Enforce strict temporal splits and audit all data sources.
- **Accuracy‑stability trade‑off**: Tendency for high‑capacity models to achieve lower in‑distribution error but higher OOD sensitivity; key design consideration.  
  *Quick check:* Compare % change metrics across models on same OOD tests.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Climate Forcings (CO₂, CH₄, etc.) -> Model Core (U‑Net: ConvEncoder -> Bottleneck -> ConvDecoder; ConvLSTM: ConvLSTM layers -> FC output; ClimaX: Patch Embedding -> Transformer Blocks -> Prediction Head) -> Outputs (TAS, PR)

**Critical path**  
Input forcings are spatially mapped, processed through architectural layers to predict temperature and precipitation fields. For ClimaX, patch embedding splits the globe into tokens, transformer layers model global interactions, and a task‑specific head produces gridded outputs.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Capacity vs robustness*: ClimaX’s large parameter count and global attention yield high ID accuracy but amplify sensitivity to forcing shifts. CNNs’ local convolutions provide spatial smoothing that enhances stability.
- *Pre‑training vs fine‑tuning*: ClimaX’s CMIP6 pre‑training may embed future patterns, while CNNs trained from scratch avoid contamination but learn less general features.
- *Global vs local context*: Transformers capture long‑range dependencies but may overfit to spurious correlations; CNNs prioritize local coherence, which may under‑capture teleconnections.

**Failure signatures**  
Under SSP5‑8.5, precipitation LL‑RMSE % change for ClimaX exceeds 8%; temperature errors remain flat. U‑Net/ConvLSTM show smaller % changes but higher absolute errors. Disparity between temperature and precipitation robustness.

**First experiments**  
1. Replicate baseline LL‑RMSE on ID (1850–2014) test set to match paper’s reported values (~0.8–1.1).  
2. Evaluate all models on 2015–2023 temporal hold‑out; compute % change vs ID baseline.  
3. Evaluate on SSP5‑8.5 (2015–2100) after training on SSP1‑2.6 or historical‑only; measure precipitation degradation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can data contamination from pre‑training on future scenarios be systematically mitigated to enable true zero‑shot OOD evaluation of climate foundation models?
2. Why do precipitation projections degrade significantly more than temperature under cross‑scenario forcing shifts, and what physical constraints are current models failing to capture?
3. What architectural properties cause transformer‑based foundation models (e.g., ClimaX) to exhibit higher relative sensitivity to forcing shifts compared to CNN‑based models, despite higher in‑distribution accuracy?
4. Can scenario‑aware training techniques (e.g., domain generalization, multi‑scenario fine‑tuning) effectively improve OOD robustness under no‑analog conditions without sacrificing in‑distribution accuracy?

## Limitations
- ClimaX pre‑training data composition (which CMIP6 simulations) is unspecified, risking undetected data leakage that could mask OOD degradation.
- ClimateSet preprocessing details (native grid resolution, normalization, area‑weighting implementation) are insufficiently documented, possibly leading to metric miscalculation.
- Hyperparameter selection (validation set, early stopping) is not described, introducing uncertainty about whether reported trade‑off is training‑protocol dependent.

## Confidence
- Existence of accuracy‑stability trade‑off (ClimaX lowest absolute error but highest % sensitivity): **Medium** (depends on uncontaminated pre‑training and correct LL‑RMSE weighting).
- Temperature stability vs. precipitation degradation: **Medium‑High** (consistent across scenarios, but precipitation sensitivity may be metric‑dependent).
- Simpler CNN architectures more stable: **Medium** (larger absolute errors could artificially lower relative changes; statistical testing absent).

## Next Checks

2. **Reproduce LL‑RMSE computation**: Match the paper’s baseline values (e.g., U‑Net PR LL‑RMSE ~1.0) by implementing correct grid‑cell area weighting using ClimateSet’s native latitude‑longitude grid.
