# A Consensus-Driven Multi-LLM Pipeline for Missing-Person Investigations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08954  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08954  
- **Reference count:** 33  
- **Primary result:** The consensus-driven multi-LLM pipeline produces conservative, parseable, schema-aligned outputs, mitigating individual model failures and enabling stable, auditable artifacts for downstream geospatial analysis within the critical 72-hour window.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the challenge of processing incomplete, unstructured case narratives in missing-child investigations under severe time constraints. It introduces a multi-LLM pipeline where task-specialized models (summarizer, extractor, weak labeler) generate candidate outputs. These are normalized and reconciled by a centralized consensus engine that resolves disagreements and enforces a fixed schema, thereby suppressing individual model failures such as overconfidence, speculation, and malformed JSON. The orchestration layer manages deadlines and fallbacks, ensuring timely delivery. QLoRA-based fine-tuning further strengthens candidate quality. The system’s reliability emerges from its consensus and validation architecture, making it suitable for producing stable, auditable inputs to downstream geospatial analysis tools used in search planning.

## Method Summary
The pipeline orchestrates case narratives through three parallel processing stages: summarization, entity extraction, and weak labeling. Each stage employs two fine-tuned small language models (Qwen2.5-3B and Llama-3.2-3B) served via a backend abstraction. The consensus engine normalizes each model’s output (type coercion, mapping to predefined vocabularies), computes field-level agreement, and invokes a referee LLM to adjudicate disputes or repair malformed JSON. A time-aware orchestrator allocates deadlines per task, falling back to the best-validated candidate if time expires. QLoRA fine-tuning on curated datasets steers the specialist models toward constrained, schema-compliant behaviors. Final canonical artifacts are persisted for investigators and downstream geospatial algorithms.

## Key Results
- The consensus mechanism systematically yields conservative, parseable, schema-aligned outputs, suppressing unsupported fields and speculation.  
- Multi-model validation mitigates individual LLM failures like overconfident labels, hallucinations, and malformed JSON.  
- Auditable trace artifacts enable stable downstream geospatial analysis (e.g., hotspot detection), making reliability a property of the system rather than any single model.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Consensus-Driven Normalization & Invariant Enforcement
The consensus engine first normalizes each candidate output (parsing JSON, coercing types, mapping to closed label sets). It then computes field-level agreement scores. Disagreements or structural violations trigger a referee LLM to adjudicate or repair within strict bounds, emitting traceable artifacts. This enforces structural correctness, grounds outputs in observed evidence, and suppresses unsupported fields. The core assumption is that individual LLM outputs are fallible; aggregating and cross-checking them under explicit constraints yields stable, auditable results. Evidence includes the abstract, Section 3.1 detailing normalization and repair, and neighboring works showing thematic alignment. If normalization fails after repair or the referee cannot produce a bounded output, the pipeline falls back to the best-validated candidate, potentially sacrificing completeness.

### Mechanism 2: QLoRA Fine-Tuning for Candidate Quality Lift
QLoRA-based parameter-efficient fine-tuning (<1% of parameters on quantized base models) is applied to the specialist models using curated datasets. This improves the baseline quality of candidate outputs, reducing malformed responses, adjudication ambiguity, and increasing inter-model agreement. The assumption is that small, targeted adaptations can instill role-specific, constrained behaviors without losing general language competence. Evidence appears in the abstract and Section 5 (training dynamics like monotonic loss reduction). However, overfitting to synthetic/curated data could cause distribution shift, degrading candidate quality on real narratives and increasing consensus interventions.

### Mechanism 3: Time-Aware Orchestration & Conservative Fallbacks
The orchestrator assigns deadline budgets per task and case, monitors remaining time, and can short-circuit expensive operations (e.g., referee calls) if time is insufficient. It defaults to the best-validated candidate available, avoiding timeouts. Parallel execution and caching optimize throughput. This prioritizes timely, conservative outputs within the critical 72‑hour window, accepting that a deterministic result delivered promptly is more valuable than a delayed or uncertain one. Evidence is implicit in the abstract and explicit in Section 3.3; domain context from time‑critical search literature supports the need. Under extreme load, the system might prematurely fallback to suboptimal candidates or face cache invalidation, risking timeliness.

## Foundational Learning

**Schema-First Processing & Validation**  
Why needed: The entire pipeline and downstream geospatial analysis rely on structured, type-correct, complete artifacts; unstructured outputs would break clustering and zone generation. Quick check: If an extractor returns free-text for “last_seen_location”, the normalization module parses and coerces it into the schema or triggers repair, logging the intervention.

**Weak Supervision & Noisy Signals**  
Why needed: LLMs are treated as noisy labelers; the consensus layer aggregates overlapping signals into a cleaner supervision signal, akin to Snorkel labeling functions. Quick check: When two models agree on a value but a third conflicts, the agreement score determines consensus; if agreement is low, a referee adjudicates; otherwise the supported value prevails.

**Geospatial Reasoning Boundaries for LLMs**  
Why needed: LLMs are unreliable for complex spatial computation; the system confines them to extraction/summarization/weak labeling, delegating spatial tasks (e.g., probabilistic search surfaces) to dedicated algorithms. Quick check: The consensus engine never asks an LLM to compute a search surface; that is handled by separate modules like Zone QA.

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:
Entry Module -> Orchestrator -> Backend Abstraction -> Specialist Models (Summarizer, Extractor, Weak Labeler) -> Consensus Engine -> Zone QA -> Persistence/Output

Critical path:
1. Entry module loads the case narrative and passes it to the Orchestrator.  
2. Orchestrator dispatches to the Summarizer VM; Backend sends prompts to both Qwen and Llama summarizers in parallel.  
3. Candidate summaries return and go to the Consensus Engine for normalization, agreement scoring, and potential referee adjudication.  
4. Steps 2–3 repeat for Extraction and Weak‑Labeling stages.  
5. After all three stages, Zone QA consumes case data to assess and reweight search zones.  
6. Final canonical artifacts are persisted.

Design tradeoffs:
- Reliability vs Cost: Multi-model consensus increases compute/latency but is chosen for operational stability.  
- Generality vs Control: General‑purpose LLMs risk subtle errors; mitigated by strict schema enforcement and consensus.  
- Automation vs Auditability: System emphasizes traceable logs for human‑in‑the‑loop review, accepting some overhead.

Failure signatures:
- High referee invocation rate indicates poor candidate quality or mismatched prompts.  
- Persistent JSON parse errors despite repair attempts suggest task complexity exceeds model capability.  
- Frequent timeouts and fallbacks in orchestrator logs reveal deadline enforcement failures.  
- Low inter‑model agreement (near zero) for key entities signals ambiguous narratives or weak model specialization.

First 3 experiments:
1. Unit Test Normalization Pipeline: Inject malformed JSON into extraction consensus; verify deterministic parsing/repair yields schema‑conformant output and correct trace logs.  
2. Deadline Pressure Test: Set artificially tight deadlines (e.g., 1 second per task); confirm graceful fallback to best pre‑repair candidate without hangs, with proper logging.  
3. Candidate Quality Stress Test: Process ambiguous narratives; measure overconfident single‑model labels, consensus tendency toward evidence‑anchored values, and reduction in referee calls after QLoRA fine‑tuning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can computational overhead be reduced without compromising stability, e.g., through adaptive model selection or dynamic consensus thresholds?  
2. How can the system be evaluated with real‑world investigative data given scarcity of ground truth and privacy constraints?  
3. Can LLMs be trusted for complex spatial reasoning, or must their role remain strictly limited to non‑spatial extraction and summarization?  
4. What are optimal consensus parameters (agreement thresholds, normalization rules) across different task types and narrative qualities?  
5. Does the consensus‑driven, schema‑first design generalize to other safety‑critical domains such as medical incident reports or disaster response logs?

## Limitations
- Exact prompt templates, schema definitions (field names/types/label sets), and curated fine‑tuning datasets are unspecified, hindering exact reproduction.  
- Evaluation relies on synthetic data; no public benchmark or comparative study validates real‑world performance or generalizability.  
- QLoRA fine‑tuning on curated data may overfit, potentially degrading candidate quality on the diverse, noisy narratives encountered in actual investigations.

## Confidence
- Consensus‑driven normalization & invariant enforcement: Medium‑High  
- QLoRA fine‑tuning for candidate quality lift: Medium  
- Time‑aware orchestration & conservative fallbacks: Medium
