# CRAFT: Grounded Multi-Agent Coordination Under Partial Information

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.25268  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25268  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Smaller open-weight models (e.g., Mistral-7B, Qwen-7B) achieve higher task progress (up to 0.63) than most frontier models (best 0.68) despite lower individual communication quality; correction spirals emerge as a key failure mode in frontier models.

## Executive Summary
CRAFT introduces a benchmark for multi-agent coordination under partial information: three directors with complementary 2D views guide a builder to construct a 3D structure. The builder selects moves from an oracle-verified candidate set, isolating communication failures from execution errors. Evaluating 7 frontier and 8 open-weight models, the authors find that smaller open-weight models outperform frontier models on task progress. Counterintuitively, frontier models score higher on LLM-judged spatial grounding and mind modeling yet achieve lower average progress (0.39 vs. 0.53). Correction spirals—repeated invalid remove attempts—waste turns and strongly correlate with low progress. The results challenge the assumption that strong individual reasoning translates to effective coordination, emphasizing the need to evaluate collective pragmatic competence.

## Method Summary
CRAFT simulates a 3×3 grid with up to 3 vertical layers. Three directors each see a distinct 2D projection (left, top, right wall) of a target structure and the current board state. The builder, with no direct target view, receives all directors’ messages each turn plus up to five oracle-verified moves that would achieve forward progress toward the target. The builder selects one move; if valid, the game engine applies it and updates the board. Progress is measured at turn 20 via IoU, completion percentage, and position accuracy. The study tests 15 director models (open-weight and frontier) with a fixed GPT-4o-mini builder, three random seeds per structure, and 20 held-out complex targets. A communication failure is diagnosed when at least one correct oracle candidate exists but the builder does not choose it. LLM judges evaluate spatial grounding (SG) from directors’ private thoughts, mind modeling (MM) from public messages, and pragmatic sufficiency (PS) from the collective output per turn.

## Key Results
- Open-weight models (Mistral-7B: 0.63; Qwen-7B: 0.62) surpass most frontier models; the best frontier model (GPT-4.1-Mini) reaches 0.68.
- Frontier models score higher on SG and MM but have lower average progress (0.39) than open-weight models (0.53).
- Correction spirals (excessive remove attempts) are a dominant failure; frontier models exhibit a remove gap up to +0.47.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Multi-Sender Bounded Pragmatic Speaker
**Claim**: Director agents in CRAFT function as bounded pragmatic speakers, balancing private reasoning with a model of the builder’s joint information needs.  
**Mechanism**: Each director’s policy multiplies a base speaker distribution (from private view) and a Theory-of-Mind listener that evaluates how utterances contribute to the builder’s task progress. The joint ToM listener aggregates contributions from all directors, making the coordination problem a multi-sender Rational Speech Act model.  
**Core assumption**: The builder integrates all directors’ messages to select moves, and directors can anticipate this integration when speaking.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract]: “We formalize this problem as a multi-sender pragmatic reasoning task.”  
- [section]: Section 3.1 and Theorem 3.3 derive the joint policy: π⋆(u1,u2,u3|z⋆,c) ∝ ∏i Sbasei(ui|z⋆i,ci) · LToMjoint(z⋆|u1,u2,u3,c).  
- [corpus]: Weak support; neighboring papers (e.g., AgentsNet) study multi-agent coordination but not the BPS formalization.  
**Break condition**: When directors ignore the joint listener—for example, by repeating information already conveyed by others—the collective output fails to specify a unique correct move, resulting in a communication failure.

### Mechanism 2: Oracle-Verified Candidate Isolation
**Claim**: Providing the builder with oracle-verified moves isolates director communication quality from builder execution capability, enabling precise credit assignment of failures.  
**Mechanism**: At each turn, the game engine enumerates all physically valid moves that make verified forward progress toward the target. The builder must select one of these candidates based solely on director messages. A turn is labeled a communication failure if at least one correct oracle candidate exists but the builder does not select it.  
**Core assumption**: The builder’s selection ability is not the limiting factor when correct moves are present; thus, failures trace to director underspecification rather than builder incompetence.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract]: “the builder uses oracle-verified candidates to isolate communication failures.”  
- [section]: Section 4.3 defines the builder agent and oracle; Definition 4.1 formalizes communication failure.  
- [corpus]: Not strongly supported; neighboring papers address multi-agent coordination but not oracle isolation.  
**Break condition**: When the oracle set lacks any correct move (e.g., due to irrecoverable board states from earlier errors), a communication failure cannot be diagnosed because no correct candidate was available for selection.

### Mechanism 3: Correction Spiral Dynamics
**Claim**: Frontier models exhibit a “correction spiral” where directors repeatedly issue remove instructions targeting wrong layers, consuming turns without progress.  
**Mechanism**: Directors leveraging their unique wall view detect discrepancies between board and target and issue corrections (removals). However, due to layer miscounting or span ambiguity, these instructions often specify invalid layers. The builder, following consensus, attempts the same failing remove repeatedly, creating a spiral of wasted turns.  
**Core assumption**: Unique perspective utilization drives over-removal; this behavioral pattern correlates with lower task progress.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
- [abstract]: “Correction spirals—excessive remove attempts—emerged as a key failure, especially in frontier models.”  
- [section]: Section 6.1 analyzes remove gap and shows over-removal patterns; Figure 5 plots remove rates; Section 6.2 identifies MM5 (unique perspective) as a mediator (Table 7); Figure 6 shows a three-turn deadlock example.  
- [corpus]: Neighbors like “AgentsNet” study coordination but do not document this specific failure mode.  
**Break condition**: When directors fail to update their layer specification after a failed removal or neglect to issue the prerequisite remove for the target block, the spiral persists across multiple turns.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept**: Pragmatic Communication (Gricean Maxim of Quantity)  
  **Why needed here**: Directors must decide what information to include or exclude based on what the builder already knows from other directors. Violating the maxim of quantity leads to redundancy (wasting tokens) or underspecification (builder confusion).  
  **Quick check**: If two directors already mention the same block, should the third director also mention it? Explain why or why not based on Grice’s maxims.

- **Concept**: Theory of Mind in Multi-Agent Systems  
  **Why needed here**: Each director must model the builder’s mental state—specifically, how the builder integrates all messages (the joint ToM listener). Without accurate ToM, directors cannot calibrate utterances to the builder’s current knowledge.  
  **Quick check**: In CRAFT’s BPS framework, what does the “joint ToM listener” LToMjoint represent, and how does it differ from a single-listener ToM?

- **Concept**: Partial Observability and Information Asymmetry  
  **Why needed here**: Each director sees only one wall; the builder sees none. Success requires each director to surface the unique information only they possess and to reconcile conflicting instructions.  
  **Quick check**: How many grid positions are visible to all three directors? How does this asymmetry drive the need for communication?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Directors output private `` and public `<message>` → Builder selects a move from oracle‑provided candidates → Game Engine validates and updates board state → Oracle generates next set of verified candidates → LLM Judges score director outputs post‑hoc from logs.

- **Critical path**:  
  1. Directors read current board state and their private target view.  
  2. Directors generate think (private reasoning) and message (public instruction).  
  3. Builder receives all messages plus oracle candidates; selects a move.  
  4. Game engine applies move (if valid), updates board, computes new progress.  
  5. Oracle computes next set of candidates for the next turn.  
  6. Judges score director outputs post‑hoc from trajectory logs.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - **Fixed builder vs. model‑matched builders**: Using a fixed GPT‑4o‑mini builder isolates director effects but may not reflect heterogeneous coordination dynamics.  
  - **Oracle builder vs. free builder**: The oracle isolates communication quality but may overestimate performance; a free builder introduces execution noise that confounds diagnosis.  
  - **LLM‑as‑judge vs. human evaluation**: Judges scale to thousands of turns but may exhibit biases; human evaluation is gold‑standard but expensive.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - **Layer error**: Move targets a vertical layer that is currently occupied or invalid.  
  - **Span error**: Large block placement/removal omits the second cell endpoint.  
  - **Wrong position**: Block placed at incorrect grid coordinate due to frame‑of‑reference mismatch.  
  - **Remove gap**: Difference between attempted and oracle‑prescribed remove rates; positive gap indicates over‑removal (correction spiral).  
  - **No‑oracle turn**: When no verified forward move exists, board state may be irrecoverable.

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Baseline evaluation**: Run each director model on 20 held‑out structures with the oracle‑builder interface, logging full trajectories.  
  2. **Judge scoring**: Compute per‑turn SG, MM, and PS scores using the provided LLM‑judge prompts (three runs for SG/MM, two for PS).  
  3. **Correlation & mediation**: Correlate judge metrics with task progress (turn 20); test whether remove gap mediates the relationship between unique perspective utilization (MM5) and progress.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1: Does providing directors with direct multimodal input (e.g., 3D renderings of their partial views) improve collective coordination in CRAFT, and do any gains generalize to novel structures or require additional training?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "CRAFT is evaluated in a text-only setting and does not consider models with direct access to visual inputs (Li et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). While multimodal inputs may improve individual spatial grounding, it remains unclear whether such gains transfer to multi-agent coordination, where communication and conflict resolution are critical."  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper finds that higher individual spatial grounding scores (measured by SG judges) do not correlate with task progress, suggesting that improving perceptual inputs alone may not address the core coordination challenge. Without multimodal experiments, it is unknown whether visual inputs would change this pattern or introduce new failure modes.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Running CRAFT with directors receiving raw visual inputs (e.g., rendered 2D projections) instead of textual descriptions, comparing coordination performance against the text-only baseline across multiple model families, and testing generalization to held-out structure complexities.

### Open Question 2: What specific mechanism—in training data, objective, or architecture—causes frontier models to exhibit "correction spirals" (over-removal), and can we decouple high-quality reasoning from this maladaptive behavior?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] From Figure 5 and Section 6.1: Frontier models show remove gaps up to +0.467, while well-performing open-weight models track oracle removals closely. The mediation analysis in Section 6.2 shows that unique perspective utilization (MM5) predicts over-removal, which in turn destroys progress, but the root cause of this chain is not identified.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper identifies the behavioral signature but cannot distinguish between competing explanations: (a) frontier models' stronger ToM reasoning leads them to notice more unseen errors and thus over-correct; (b) their instruction tuning makes them overly literal in following "remove" directives; (c) their longer context windows cause them to incorporate outdated board states. Without targeted ablations, the cause remains speculative.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments varying model scale, RLHF/instruction-tuning intensity, and context management, while measuring (a) directors' awareness of previous failed commands, (b) sensitivity to board-state changes, and (c) calibration of uncertainty in their private reasoning (think blocks). Prompting interventions that force turn-level state tracking could also isolate whether the failure is representational or strategic.

### Open Question 3: What component(s) of "collective pragmatic competence" enable successful coordination in CRAFT, given that individual communication quality (SG, MM) does not predict group performance?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit/inferred] The paper concludes that "individual pragmatic competence is necessary but not sufficient for collective task success" and notes that only the group-level Pragmatic Sufficiency (PS) score distinguishes successful turns. However, PS is an outcome metric, not a diagnostic of what directors should do differently. The multi-sender BPS theory requires modeling a joint ToM listener, but no model achieved this.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper shows that even models with high SG and MM scores (e.g., GPT-4.1-Mini) perform poorly, indicating that the translation from good individual reasoning to good collective communication is non-trivial. The failure is not underspecification alone—PS scores remain low even when directors mention correct blocks—suggesting a deeper issue of information integration across minds.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Fine-grained analysis of successful trajectories (from top-performing open-weight models) to extract behavioral patterns that PS captures but SG/MM miss, such as temporal coordination (e.g., deferring to others' unique information) or meta-communication about conflicts. Training or prompting studies that explicitly encourage directors to synthesize others' messages before speaking, and measuring whether such interventions boost PS without degrading SG/MM.

### Open Question 4: In heterogeneous director teams (mixing frontier and open-weight models), does coordination performance reflect "social loafing," complementary strengths, or catastrophic failure, and how do communication dynamics differ from homogeneous teams?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "We also do not explore settings with heterogeneous director models...Such configurations could provide further insight into how differences in alignment algorithms, pretraining, and post-training data influence collaborative performance...While our controlled setup reduces variability...it limits conclusions about joint optimization in heterogeneous multi-agent systems."  
- **Why unresolved**: All experiments used homogeneous director teams, so we do not know whether the observed disconnect between individual competence and coordination holds when models with different capabilities interact. For instance, may a frontier model director compensate for an open-weight model's errors, or do mismatched communication styles cause spirals? The paper's finding that unique perspective utilization drives over-removal raises the possibility that heterogeneous teams might experience even worse coordination if stronger reasoners over-correct based on weaker partners' messages.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic evaluation of mixed teams (e.g., 1 frontier + 2 open-weight, or all-pair combinations) on CRAFT, measuring whether performance aggregates linearly, synergistically improves (if members specialize), or degrades (if communication protocols diverge). Analysis of turn-by-turn dialogue could reveal whether certain model types dominate the conversation, fail to adapt to partners' styles, or develop implicit protocols.

## Limitations
- Exact inference parameters (temperature, top_p), prompt formatting details, and hardware specifications are unreported, hindering exact replication.
- Evaluation is restricted to 20 held-out structures; results may not generalize to larger or differently distributed targets.
- The fixed GPT-4o‑mini builder isolates director communication but artificially suppresses builder execution errors via the oracle interface, possibly overstating director responsibility.
- LLM‑as‑judge metrics (SG, MM, PS) lack human validation and may exhibit biases or high variance.
- The proposed multi‑sender BPS mechanism remains a conceptual framework without direct empirical ablation (e.g., removing the ToM component).

## Confidence
- **[High]** CRAFT benchmark design and oracle‑verified candidate isolation: clearly defined and logically sound.
- **[Medium]** Performance gap (smaller open‑weight models outperform frontier models): observed in experiments but dependent on undisclosed settings and a limited test set.
- **[Medium]** Correction spirals as a dominant failure mode: pattern identified and quantified, though causal attribution is not fully proven.
- **[Low]** BPS mechanism as explanatory theory: intuitive but not directly tested; empirical support is indirect.
- **[Medium]** Oracle isolation enabling precise credit assignment: valid when oracle candidates exist, but builder limitations may still influence outcomes.
