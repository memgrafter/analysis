# Skeleton-to-Image Encoding: Enabling Skeleton Representation Learning via Vision-Pretrained Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05963
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05963
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** S2I enables direct use of vision-pretrained models (MAE, DiffMAE) on skeleton data, achieving state-of-the-art action recognition (e.g., 93.1% on NTU-60 C-sub with fine-tuning).

## Executive Summary
Large-scale vision-pretrained models hold great promise for representation learning but cannot be directly applied to 3D skeleton data due to format incompatibility and the scarcity of labeled skeleton datasets. This paper introduces Skeleton-to-Image Encoding (S2I), a method that transforms skeleton sequences into standardized 224×224 image-like representations by partitioning joints into semantic body parts and stacking them across time. This encoding allows the direct application of masked autoencoders such as MAE and DiffMAE for self-supervised pretraining on skeleton data, while also accommodating heterogeneous skeleton formats. Evaluations on NTU-60, NTU-120, and PKU-MMD show strong performance: linear probing reaches up to 85.8% on NTU-120 (multi-stream), and fine-tuning achieves 93.1% on NTU-60 and 91.2% on NTU-120. Additional gains are observed in semi-supervised (1% labels) and cross-format transfer scenarios, underlining the effectiveness and generalizability of S2I.

## Method Summary
S2I processes a 3D skeleton sequence of shape (T × J × 3) by first partitioning the J joints into five semantic body parts (spine, left arm, right arm, left leg, right leg). Within each part, joints are ordered by kinematic distance from the torso. The temporal dimension is then stacked, assigning the (x, y, z) coordinates to the red, green, and blue channels, yielding a 2D grid of size (T × J) × 3. This grid is interpolated (resized) to a fixed resolution of 224 × 224 to match the input expectations of standard vision models. A pretrained Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16) from MAE or DiffMAE, initialized with ImageNet weights, is used as the backbone. For self-supervised pretraining, random masking (75% patch removal) is applied, and the model reconstructs the masked patches either via mean squared error (MAE) or diffusion denoising (DiffMAE). Downstream tasks—linear probing or fine-tuning—attach a classification head and train with standard optimizers and skeleton-specific augmentations (horizontal flip, rotation, affine, Gaussian noise). The approach is format-agnostic: the same body-part mapping and pipeline can be applied to skeletons with different joint counts (e.g., 25, 20, 13) by adjusting the partitioning step.

## Key Results
- **Linear probing:** 3s-S2I achieves 83.1% (NTU-60 C-sub) and 75.0% (NTU-120 C-sub); multi‑stream fusion pushes these to 85.8% and 78.9% respectively.
- **Fine-tuning:** 3s-S2I attains 93.1% on NTU-60 C-sub and 91.2% on NTU-120 C-set, setting new state-of-the-art.
- **Cross‑dataset and semi‑supervised:** With only 1% labeled data on NTU-60, 3s‑S2I reaches 75.2% (C-sub). Transfer to the Toyota (CV1) dataset improves to 53.8% (3s‑S2I). Universal pretraining across datasets yields sizable gains, e.g., +5.3% on PKU-MMD II.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1 – S2I Encodes Skeletons into Vision‑Compatible Inputs.** By partitioning joints into five body parts, ordering them kinematically, stacking across time, and interpolating to 224×224, S2I converts sparse, articulated sequences into dense spatial‑temporal grids. This preserves essential motion patterns while meeting the fixed‑size, three‑channel requirements of vision models. The core assumption is that semantic body‑part grouping retains enough anatomical structure for downstream tasks. Evidence is given in the abstract and Section 3.1; failure could occur if interpolation distorts fine trajectories or if the joint set deviates strongly from humanoid morphology.

**Mechanism 2 – Transfer of Image‑Pretrained Visual Priors.** S2I allows initializing MAE/DiffMAE with ImageNet pretrained weights. The model then learns skeleton representations by reconstructing masked regions of the encoded images. This reuses rich spatial feature extractors (edges, textures, coherent patterns) that are transferable to motion semantics. The assumption is that visual coherence patterns in the S2I images align with those in natural images. Evidence includes the abstract, Section 3.2, and comparative results against training from scratch. Potential break‑condition: the pretrained features might overfit to image‑specific statistics (e.g., color distributions) that are irrelevant or even misleading for joint trajectories.

**Mechanism 3 – Format‑Agnosticism via Unified Image Representation.** Because S2I always produces a 224×224 image regardless of the original joint count or layout, it decouples the representation from dataset‑specific topology. This permits a single pretrained vision model to process heterogeneous skeleton sources (e.g., NTU with 25 joints, Toyota with 13) and enables universal pretraining across mixed datasets. The key assumption is that the five‑part partitioning generalizes to any humanoid skeleton. The paper demonstrates applications to NTU, NW‑UCLA, and Toyota; however, the approach may fail for skeletons with irregular kinematic chains (e.g., missing limbs) or when interpolation obscures format‑specific cues.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
3D skeleton sequence (T × J × 3) → S2I encoder (joint partitioning → kinematic ordering → temporal stacking → interpolation to 224×224×3) → Pretrained ViT backbone (MAE or DiffMAE) → Classification head

**Critical path:** Raw skeleton → S2I encoding → Vision model forward pass → Feature extraction → Task‑specific head training. Ensure interpolation preserves temporal continuity and that body part semantics remain consistent across datasets.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Partitioning granularity: Fixed five parts may be too coarse (loss of detail) or too fine (risk of overfitting to dataset‑specific joints).
- Interpolation method: Linear interpolation is simple but can blur motion; higher‑order methods may yield smoother dynamics at the cost of computational overhead.
- Masking strategy: Random masking performed best in the paper, yet joint‑ or temporal‑aware masking might better capture skeleton‑specific structure but could increase task difficulty.

**Failure signatures:**
- Poor linear probing performance suggests ineffective transfer from ImageNet pretraining or corrupted S2I encoding.
- Degradation on cross‑format datasets (e.g., Toyota) indicates body‑part mapping is not fully robust to different joint sets.
- Overfitting during fine‑tuning on small datasets (e.g., NW‑UCLA) may arise if augmentation is insufficient or if the pretrained model has not adapted well to skeleton statistics.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Ablate ImageNet pretrained weights:** Compare scratch versus ImageNet‑initialized MAE/DiffMAE on NTU‑60 linear probing to quantify the transfer gain.
2. **Test masking strategies:** Evaluate random, joint, temporal, and group masking on NTU‑60 to identify the optimal strategy for skeleton dynamics.
3. **Single vs. multi‑stream fusion:** Assess joint‑only, motion‑only, bone‑only, and the 3‑stream combination (joint, motion, bone) to measure modality complementarity within vision models.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Integration with Vision‑Language Models (VLMs).** The authors plan to extend S2I to VLMs and multi‑modal frameworks, but the compatibility of the image‑like representation with complex cross‑attention mechanisms and textual alignment remains untested. Evidence needed: experiments integrating S2I into models like CLIP or Flamingo for retrieval or zero‑shot tasks, and analysis of fusion strategies with RGB video streams.

2. **Why random masking outperforms skeleton‑specific strategies.** Although Table 2 shows random masking yields the best results, the paper does not explain *why* structured masking (joint, temporal, group) underperforms. Evidence needed: diagnostic studies of reconstruction quality per body part or time segment under different masks, attention map analysis to see if pretrained models focus on meaningful skeleton regions, and comparison with models that develop masking strategies during skeleton‑specific pretraining.

3. **Impact of dataset heterogeneity on universal pretraining.** Universal pretraining (mixed datasets) shows mixed gains; it is unclear how noise levels, coordinate scales, and capture devices interact during joint training. Evidence needed: controlled experiments mixing subsets with known distribution shifts, per‑dataset loss tracking, and adaptive weighting schemes (e.g., gradient normalization, dataset‑specific batch norms) to balance diverse sources.

4. **Robustness of format‑agnostic design to atypical skeletons.** The five‑part partitioning assumes standard humanoid joint sets. For skeletons with extra joints (fingers, face) or missing limbs, the mapping may fail. Evidence needed: testing S2I on synthetic or real skeletons with missing/extra joints, robustness analysis to random joint dropout or permutation, and development of adaptive partitioning (e.g., clustering joints by motion similarity).

5. **Representational differences from skeleton‑specific architectures.** It is unknown whether vision‑pretrained models learn fundamentally different spatiotemporal features compared to models designed for skeletons (e.g., ST‑GCNs, graph transformers). Evidence needed: visualization and comparison of feature spaces (e.g., t‑SNE), transfer tests to related tasks such as motion prediction or pose estimation, and attention pattern analysis on skeleton‑specific perturbations.

## Limitations
- Interpolation details (e.g., bilinear vs. nearest neighbor) are unspecified, potentially distorting motion patterns.
- Body‑part partitioning rules are manually defined per dataset; generalizing to arbitrary skeletons lacks automation.
- Normalization statistics for S2I images are not fully reported, which could affect transferability across datasets.
- Minimal ablations on key design choices (number of parts, masking strategy) limit understanding of S2I’s mechanism.
- Cross‑format claims rely on a limited set of datasets; generalisation to highly divergent joint topologies remains unverified.

## Confidence
- **S2I encoding preserves motion patterns:** Medium – empirical success without direct validation of semantics.
- **Transfer of visual priors from ImageNet:** Medium – strong gains shown, but no causal evidence linking improvements to specific priors.
- **Format‑agnostic representation generalizes:** Medium – cross‑dataset experiments are promising but untested on very diverse skeletons.

## Next Checks
