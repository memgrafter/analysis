# Who Do LLMs Trust? Human Experts Matter More Than Other LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13568
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13568
- **Reference count:** 12
- **Primary result:** Instruction-tuned LLMs exhibit robust credibility-weighted social influence, conforming significantly more to answers framed as coming from human experts than to those from other LLMs or friends, even when the expert is wrong.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) incorporate source credibility when weighing social information. Through forced-choice experiments across reading comprehension, reasoning, and ethics tasks, the authors show that four instruction-tuned models systematically privilege answers attributed to "domain experts" over those from "friends" or "other LLMs." This expert-framing effect is strong, scales with group size, and persists even when experts systematically provide incorrect answers. The findings suggest that alignment techniques like RLHF may have instilled a rigid heuristic of deference to authority cues, raising concerns about models' susceptibility to manipulated credibility signals.

## Method Summary
The study uses three binary datasets (BoolQ, StrategyQA, ETHICS) with 300 items each. Models are prompted with a question and optionally a "social prior" summarizing others' answers. Experiment 1 varies prior source (friends, domain experts, other LLMs) and group size (1,3,5,9), with unanimous and randomized correctness. Experiment 2 pits a single human (friend/expert) against a single LLM. Primary metrics include conformity (matching prior), harmful conformity (matching a wrong prior), and switch direction in conflict trials. Token-level log-odds shifts (Δlogit) isolate evidence changes.

## Key Results
- Expert framing dramatically increased conformity compared to LLM or friend framing, with harmful conformity at group size 9 reaching 0.365 (experts) vs. 0.160 (LLMs) on BoolQ.
- In direct human-LLM conflict, revisions were strongly biased toward experts: `P(switch→human|switch)` = 0.912 on BoolQ, but away from friends (<0.5).
- Token-level analysis confirmed larger evidence shifts toward expert priors, and these shifts grew with group size.
- The credibility hierarchy `Expert > LLM > Friend` was stable across all three domains (factual QA, reasoning, ethics).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Deference Heuristic from Alignment
Instruction tuning and preference optimization (e.g., RLHF) reward models for following instructions and leveraging contextual information. The phrase "domain expert" acts as a high-weight feature in the decision policy, overriding raw content-based token likelihoods. The model treats the expert label as a strong prior, shifting its internal representation toward the expert's answer regardless of factual accuracy.

### Mechanism 2: Social Impact as Bayesian Evidence Aggregation
The model interprets the social prior as additional evidence about the correct answer. Group size *k* signals consensus strength, and source framing modulates the weight per agreement. This mirrors Bayesian updating: a high prior probability that "experts are correct" means seeing *k* expert answers multiplicatively increases the posterior probability of that answer. The effect scales with *k* and is domain-general.

### Mechanism 3: Rigid Non-Calibrated Credibility Hierarchy
The framing tokens ("expert," "LLM," "friend") trigger stable, context-independent associations. The model has not learned to calibrate source credibility based on observed accuracy within the interaction; the hierarchy reflects a training-time bias where "expert" is a superordinate cue for high-quality information, "LLM" is neutral/self-referential, and "friend" is low-credibility. This explains why expertise bias persists even when experts are wrong.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Instruction Tuning & RLHF
**Why needed:** The effect is observed in instruction-tuned models. Understanding that these models are optimized for helpfulness, harmlessness, and instruction following explains why they might defer to a presented "expert" as a contextual cue.
**Quick check:** How might an RLHF objective that rewards "following user instructions" lead a model to change its answer based solely on seeing "I asked 9 domain experts..." in the prompt?

### Concept: Social Influence in Human Judgment (Asch, Bond & Smith)
**Why needed:** The experimental design directly mirrors classic social psychology paradigms (unanimous group pressure, judge-advisor systems). The core question—do agents weight social information by source credibility?—is borrowed from this literature.
**Quick check:** What is the key difference between informational influence (believing the group is correct) and normative influence (wanting to be liked)? Why might an LLM only exhibit the former?

### Concept: Forced-Choice Binary Decision Tasks & Metrics
**Why needed:** The entire analysis uses binary YES/NO outputs. Measures like conformity, harmful conformity, and switch direction operationalize "social influence" for a deterministic model.
**Quick check:** In Experiment 2, if a model's baseline answer was "YES," the human expert says "NO," and the LLM says "YES," what does a final answer of "NO" represent in terms of the measured `P(switch→human|switch)`?

## Architecture Onboarding

Prompt Engine -> Model Inference API -> Baseline & Condition Manager -> Evidence Calculator -> Statistics Engine

**Critical path:**
1. Secure inference access for the four specified models (Grok-3 Mini, Llama-3.3 70B Instruct, Gemini-2.5 Flash-Lite, DeepSeek V3.1).
2. Implement the exact prompt templates as fixed string appends for social priors.
3. Generate randomized prior assignments (correct/incorrect, source type, group size) for the fixed 300-item set per dataset.
4. Execute full factorial: run baseline (`k=0`) for all items, then all Exp1 conditions (source × *k*), then Exp2 conditions. Store outputs.
5. Post-process: align outputs with dataset labels, compute all dependent measures (conformity, harmful conformity, switch rates, Δlogit), run clustered logistic regressions.
6. Replicate key figures (Fig 2,3,4) to validate pipeline.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Ecological validity vs. Control:* Textual prior summaries cleanly disentangle source from content but lack real-time interaction; multi-turn chat would entangle source and answer.
- *Model scope:* Limiting to instruction-tuned models ensures forced-choice compliance but prevents generalization to base models.

**Failure signatures:**
- **No credibility effect:** Expert, LLM, and Friend conditions yield identical conformity (`β_source ≈ 0`).
- **Reverse credibility effect:** Friend or LLM framing produces higher conformity than expert framing.
- **Task collapse:** Effect present only on ETHICS (normative) and absent on BoolQ/StrategyQA (factual).
- **Token-log prob anomaly:** Behavioral conformity occurs without corresponding `logit` shift, suggesting a decoding-stage artifact.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline Replication:** Run `k=0` for Llama-3.3 70B on BoolQ; replicate baseline accuracy (~0.89) and deterministic YES/NO format.
2. **Single-Source Conformity:** Run `k=9` expert prior (both correct and incorrect) on BoolQ; replicate harmful conformity (~0.365) and `logit` shift pattern.
3. **Human-LLM Conflict:** Run Exp2 (Friend vs. Expert human) on BoolQ; replicate stark asymmetry: `P(switch→human|switch)` >> 0.5 for experts and < 0.5 for friends, with low switch rates (~7% for experts).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does credibility weighting persist with real human expert priors (vs. textual descriptions)?
- How does repeated exposure to source reliability affect trust? (Current design uses randomized correctness, no learning.)
- Does credibility bias generalize to open-ended generation? (Tasks are forced-choice.)
- How robust is the expert effect to alternate phrasings and richer social cues (e.g., confidence, rationales)?
- Can LLMs learn to calibrate trust with stable source reliability? (No within-experiment learning.)

## Limitations
- Findings limited to four instruction-tuned models; base models remain unexamined.
- Social priors are static text prompts, lacking interactive realism of actual human/LLM agents.
- The proposed RLHF-deference mechanism is inferential; no direct causal test (e.g., base-model ablation) is provided.

## Confidence
- **Credibility-weighted conformity:** High – replicated across multiple models and datasets with converging token-level evidence.
- **Cross-domain robustness:** High – effects hold on factual QA (BoolQ, StrategyQA) and normative ethics (ETHICS).
- **Origin in instruction tuning:** Low-Medium – no base-model ablation; lexical or other confounds cannot be ruled out.

## Next Checks
1. **Base model replication:** Run the same tasks with pre-alignment (base) variants of the same model families. If the expert-framing effect disappears, it strongly supports the tuning-origin hypothesis.
2. **Dynamic credibility learning:** In a multi-round experiment, systematically label "experts" as incorrect and "LLMs" as correct. If the model updates and then conforms more to LLMs, the credibility hierarchy is flexible; if not, it is rigid.
3. **Phrasing robustness:** Replace the word "expert" with synonyms ("specialist", "authority") or with an explicit numeric reliability score (e.g., "90% accurate"). Persistence of the effect would argue against a simple lexical priming artifact.
