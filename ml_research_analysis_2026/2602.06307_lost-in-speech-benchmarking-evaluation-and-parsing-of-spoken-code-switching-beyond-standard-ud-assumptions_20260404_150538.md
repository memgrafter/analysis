# Lost in Speech: Benchmarking, Evaluation, and Parsing of Spoken Code-Switching Beyond Standard UD Assumptions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06307
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06307
- **Reference count:** 8
- **Primary result:** Standard UD parsing fails on spoken code-switching due to disfluencies; the authors introduced SpokeBench (taxonomy + gold benchmark), FLEX-UD (ambiguity-aware metric), and DECAP (agentic parsing), achieving up to 52.6% LAS improvement and 76.2 FLEX-UD score over baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses parsing of spoken English-Spanish code-switching (CSW), where disfluencies and non-canonical structures break standard Universal Dependencies (UD) assumptions and cause brittle performance. The authors contribute: (1) a linguistically grounded taxonomy of spoken CSW phenomena derived from the Miami Corpus; (2) SpokeBench, a gold-standard benchmark of 126 sentences stratified by these phenomena; (3) FLEX-UD, an evaluation metric that tolerates linguistically plausible variation while penalizing catastrophic structural errors; and (4) DECAP, a decoupled agentic parsing framework that uses specialized LLM-based agents to isolate spoken-phenomena handling from core UD assignment. On SpokeBench, DECAP improves LAS by up to 52.6% over existing parsers and achieves a FLEX-UD score of 76.2, substantially higher than bilingual Stanza (30.4) and the BiLingua LLM pipeline (70.7). FLEX-UD reveals qualitative robustness gains masked by rigid metrics like LAS.

## Method Summary
DECAP is a modular, instruction-grounded parsing pipeline consisting of four sequential LLM agents (GPT-4.1, deterministic, temperature=0). The pipeline processes raw tokenized input with language tags: (1) **SPH** tags disfluencies (repetition, ellipsis, fillers) and proposes tokenization edits (e.g., splitting contractions, creating dotted MWEs); (2) **LSR** applies conservative cross-linguistic normalizations (expanding contractions, confirming MWEs) while respecting SPH’s tags; (3) **Core** assigns UD relations (UPOS, HEAD, DEPREL) under constraints (single root, map `reparandum` via `dep` to `spoken_anchor`), annotating only dotted MWE nodes; (4) **V/R** validates global UD constraints (acyclicity, single root), computes confidence, and applies FLEX-UD penalty `P`. No fine-tuning occurs; all agents use prompts from Appendix E. Evaluation uses the new FLEX-UD metric, which scores component matches (ID, UPOS, HEAD, DEPREL) and multiplies by `(1−P)` where `P` is the sum of catastrophic error penalties (e.g., missing dotted MWE, invalid heads). Baselines include a bilingual Stanza parser and the BiLingua LLM pipeline.

## Key Results
- DECAP improves LAS by up to **52.6%** over existing parsers on spoken CSW.
- DECAP achieves **FLEX-UD score 76.2** vs. BiLingua (70.7) and traditional bilingual parser (30.4) on SpokeBench.
- Largest gains occur on **repetition, ellipsis, and discourse** categories, reducing catastrophic structural errors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** A linguistically grounded taxonomy of spoken CSW phenomena (SpokeBench) enables precise diagnosis of parsing failures by structurally isolating non-canonical features that violate standard UD assumptions.  
**Mechanism:** The taxonomy categorizes disfluencies (repetition, ellipsis, fillers) and cross-linguistic complexities (enclisis, contractions) that cause brittle parsing. By mapping parser errors to specific phenomena categories, it transforms a monolithic “low LAS” symptom into actionable, phenomenon-level failure modes (e.g., “fails on reparandum attachment,” “struggles with fragmented roots”). This categorization informed the design of DECAP’s specialized agents.  
**Core assumption:** The structural sources of parsing degradation in spoken CSW are taxonomically consistent and can be enumerated, allowing for modular intervention rather than holistic model scaling.  
**Evidence anchors:** The taxonomy is data-driven from the Miami Corpus; SpokeBench is explicitly stratified by these categories.  
**Break condition:** If new spoken CSW corpora reveal prevalent disfluency types not in the taxonomy, DECAP’s SPH agent would lack detection rules, causing unhandled noise to propagate.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** FLEX-UD, an ambiguity-aware evaluation metric, reveals qualitative improvements in parser robustness that are masked by rigid metrics (LAS/UAS) by tolerating linguistically plausible variation and penalizing only catastrophic structural errors.  
**Mechanism:** FLEX-UD decomposes scoring into components (ID, UPOS, HEAD, DEPREL) and applies a severity multiplier `P` based on catastrophic errors (e.g., missing required dotted MWE, invalid head references that create cycles). This contrasts with LAS, which treats all label mismatches equally. By separating “acceptable ambiguity” from “genuine failure,” it provides a diagnostic signal aligned with linguistic plausibility.  
**Core assumption:** In spoken CSW, a single “gold” UD tree is often artificially forced; structural ambiguity is an inherent property of the data, not an annotation error.  
**Evidence anchors:** The metric’s design is grounded in the paper’s own annotation disputes, where “acceptable variation” was distinguished from “substantive errors” during adjudication.  
**Break condition:** If the definition of “catastrophic” vs. “minor” error does not generalize beyond the SpokeBench annotation guidelines, FLEX-UD’s penalty weights may misalign with linguistic plausibility in new contexts.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** DECAP’s decoupled agentic architecture improves robustness by isolating spoken-phenomena handling (SPH), language-specific normalization (LSR), core UD assignment (Core), and global validation (V/R), preventing disfluencies from contaminating syntactic structure assignment.  
**Mechanism:** Instead of a monolithic parser, DECAP uses a pipeline of LLM-based agents with narrow, instruction-grounded responsibilities. SPH tags disfluencies and proposes tokenization edits without assigning syntax. LSR conservatively normalizes while respecting SPH’s tags. Core assigns UD relations under hard constraints (e.g., attach `reparandum` tokens via `dep` relation to intended head). V/R enforces global UD validity and computes confidence/penalties. This separation ensures, for example, that repetition does not cause the core parser to create multiple roots or cycles.  
**Core assumption:** The complexity of spoken CSW can be modularized: disfluency detection/reconstruction, cross-linguistic normalization, and canonical syntax assignment are separable sub-tasks amenable to specialized LLM prompting.  
**Evidence anchors:** Table 2 shows DECAP’s FLEX-UD score (76.2) >> BiLingua (70.7) >> traditional (30.4), with largest gains on repetition and ellipsis—precisely the phenomena SPH handles.  
**Break condition:** If the modular separation causes information loss (e.g., SPH misses a subtle repair that only Core could infer) or if downstream agents ignore upstream constraints, the decoupling could harm performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Universal Dependencies (UD) canonical assumptions  
  **Why needed:** DECAP explicitly works *around* standard UD assumptions (single root per sentence, one token = one node, complete clauses). Understanding these assumptions is necessary to see why spoken CSW breaks them (e.g., ellipsis means missing arguments, repetition means duplicate tokens with distinct roles).  
  **Quick check:** What UD principle is violated when a speaker says “I will I will not buy” and the parser creates two separate roots for the two “I will” sequences?

- **Concept:** Disfluency typology in conversational speech  
  **Why needed:** The paper’s taxonomy (repetition, ellipsis, break of thought, fillers) is the bedrock of SpokeBench and DECAP’s SPH agent. Without distinguishing these, any parser treats all “uh” or repeated words as canonical tokens, leading to attachment errors.  
  **Quick check:** How does a “break of thought” (e.g., “you have you gotta show tu tía”) differ from simple ellipsis in terms of parser challenge?

- **Concept:** Ambiguity-aware evaluation design  
  **Why needed:** FLEX-UD’s core innovation is moving beyond exact-match LAS. It requires understanding that in spoken CSW, multiple dependency structures can be equally valid (e.g., where to attach a discourse marker). This concept challenges the assumption that a single gold tree exists.  
  **Quick check:** Why might attaching the discourse marker “then” to the verb “buy” vs. to the pronoun “I” both be defensible in a code-switched utterance, and how should a metric treat this?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  SPH -> LSR -> Core -> V/R  
  SPH: Detects spoken phenomena, proposes tokenization edits, labels reparanda/fillers.  
  LSR: Applies conservative normalization (expands contractions, confirms MWEs), adds lemmas/language confidence.  
  Core: Assigns UD relations (UPOS/HEAD/DEPREL) under SPH/LSR constraints, enforces single root.  
  V/R: Validates global UD constraints, computes confidence/penalty, remaps to sheet IDs.

- **Critical path:** Strict sequential dependency: SPH → LSR → Core → V/R. Each stage consumes the structured JSON of the previous. V/R is the final arbiter of structural validity.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Modularity vs. LLM Cost:** Decoupling improves interpretability and error isolation but requires 4 LLM calls per sentence (vs. 1 for a monolithic pipeline). This is a deliberate tradeoff for robustness in low-resource settings where retraining is infeasible.
  - **Conservative vs. Aggressive Normalization:** LSR is instructed to be conservative (“respect SPH authority,” “rarely create MWEs”). This prevents over-correction but may miss some normalizations that could aid Core parsing.
  - **Prompt Complexity:** Each agent has a narrow, high-precision prompt. This reduces scope confusion but requires careful prompt engineering to handle edge cases without cross-agent contradiction.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **SPH failure:** Missed `reparandum` labeling → Core attaches repeated token as independent syntactic node → catastrophic head error.
  - **LSR failure:** Over-splitting a contraction not flagged by SPH → token count mismatch → Core’s `HEAD_ID` references invalid `proposed_ID` → V/R must repair.
  - **Core failure:** Ignores `spoken_anchor` → `reparandum` attached to wrong head → structural error.
  - **V/R failure:** Fails to detect a cycle created by Core → invalid parse output (multiple roots persist).

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Component isolation test:** Run each DECAP agent in isolation on SpokeBench. Compare SPH’s `spoken_label` accuracy against manual annotation; check LSR’s contraction split accuracy; verify Core’s adherence to `spoken_anchor` constraints.
  2. **Ablation over agent sequence:** Disable one agent at a time (e.g., SPH→Core skipping LSR). Measure FLEX-UD score drop to quantify each agent’s contribution. Expected: SPH ablation causes largest drop on repetition/ellipsis.
  3. **FLEX-UD calibration:** Take sentences where LAS is identical between DECAP and BiLingua but DECAP’s parse is linguistically more plausible (per guidelines). Verify that FLEX-UD scores DECAP higher while LAS unchanged.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How does DECAP's performance and cost scale with longer, multi-turn conversational speech containing more overlapping spoken phenomena and language switches?  
  **Basis:** The paper evaluates on single-sentence excerpts (SpokeBench, 126 sentences). Limitations note SpokeBench is “intentionally small and curated” and extending to larger corpora would strengthen generalizability. Agentic pipeline cost/latency for full dialogues are not discussed.  
  **Why unresolved:** Full conversations introduce complexities like coreference across turns and dynamic language alternation. Cost of multiple LLM calls per utterance is prohibitive for practical deployment.

- **Open Question 2:** To what extent are the linguistic categories and annotation guidelines in SpokeBench language-pair independent, and can the taxonomy be automatically induced for other language pairs without expert-led, data-driven refinement?  
  **Basis:** The taxonomy was derived from English-Spanish utterances by trained linguists. Limitations note phenomena are “common in many bilingual settings, but distribution and interaction may vary across language pairs.”  
  **Why unresolved:** Applicability beyond English-Spanish is untested. The manual, linguist-intensive process for creating SpokeBench is not scalable. Universal coverage of the nine categories remains unproven.

- **Open Question 3:** Can the decoupled architecture of DECAP be replaced or augmented by a single, fine-tuned model that learns to handle spoken phenomena implicitly, and would such a model match DECAP's performance without the inference overhead of multiple LLM agents?  
  **Basis:** DECAP is framed as an “instruction-grounded framework” that “enables adaptation to new languages and spoken phenomena without retraining.” Limitations state reliance on prompt quality and underlying models.  
  **Why unresolved:** No comparison to a fine-tuned model exists due to data scarcity. It is unclear if modular gains come at unacceptable cost in speed or if a sufficiently large fine-tuned model could learn the same distinctions.

- **Open Question 4:** Does the FLEX-UD metric's design, which tolerates “linguistically plausible” variation, inadvertently encourage parsers to produce structurally underspecified or non-committal analyses for genuinely ambiguous spoken input, and how should this trade-off be formally evaluated?  
  **Basis:** FLEX-UD is introduced to “tolerate linguistically defensible variation” and distinguish catastrophic errors from benign alternatives. The paper shows DECAP gains in part by reducing catastrophic errors.  
  **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze whether higher FLEX-UD scores correlate with human-rated interpretative adequacy. A parser could game FLEX-UD by producing maximally generic parses that avoid penalties but are useless.

- **Open Question 5:** What is the minimum viable set of spoken-phenomena handling rules (SPH's output) that must be fixed *before* core syntactic parsing to achieve the majority of DECAP's performance gain, and can these rules be learned from data?  
  **Basis:** DECAP's modularity isolates “spoken-phenomena handling” from “core syntactic analysis.” Performance gains are attributed to decoupling, but no ablation replaces SPH outputs with gold labels or automatic predictions.  
  **Why unresolved:** It is unclear which specific SPH decisions (e.g., correct `reparandum` labeling, contraction splitting) are most critical. If only a subset drives gains, a lighter-weight, potentially learnable preprocessing module could replace SPH.

## Limitations
- Code and exact evaluation details (FLEX-UD weights, baseline setups) are not publicly available, hindering faithful reproduction.
- All claims are evaluated solely on SpokeBench (English-Spanish); generalizability to other language pairs or speech domains is unknown.
- DECAP’s decoupled agents may suffer error propagation if SPH misses phenomena or if downstream agents ignore constraints.

## Confidence
- **SpokeBench taxonomy diagnostic utility on Miami Corpus:** High
- **SpokeBench as a universal spoken CSW typology:** Medium
- **FLEX-UD metric (valid on SpokeBench):** Medium
- **DECAP framework (on SpokeBench):** High
- **DECAP for broader spoken CSW (due to potential unmodeled phenomena):** Medium
