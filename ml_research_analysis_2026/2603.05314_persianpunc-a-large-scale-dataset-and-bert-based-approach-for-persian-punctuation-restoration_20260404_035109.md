# PersianPunc: A Large-Scale Dataset and BERT-Based Approach for Persian Punctuation Restoration
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.05314
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05314
*   **Reference Count:** 12
*   **Primary Result:** A fine-tuned ParsBERT model achieves a macro-averaged F1 score of 91.33% and a Full Sentence Match rate of 61.80%, outperforming GPT-4o (85.96% macro F1) on a large-scale Persian punctuation restoration benchmark.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the underexplored problem of restoring punctuation to Persian text, particularly from ASR outputs. The authors introduce PersianPunc, a large-scale dataset of 17 million sentences created by aggregating and filtering diverse formal and informal Persian text sources. They frame the task as token-level sequence labeling and fine-tune the monolingual ParsBERT model on a 989k-sample subset. The resulting system demonstrates strong token-level accuracy (macro F1 ~91.3%) while being computationally efficient, significantly outperforming large language models like GPT-4o in both accuracy and inference cost. The work highlights a specific "over-correction" failure mode in LLMs where they undesirably edit non-punctuation tokens.

## Method Summary
The authors formulate punctuation restoration as a sequence labeling problem. For each token in a punctuation-free input sentence, the model predicts one of five classes (EMPTY, COMMA, QUESTION, PERIOD, COLON) corresponding to the punctuation mark that should follow that word. The model is built by adding a linear classification head onto the pre-trained monolingual ParsBERT encoder. A critical preprocessing step involves normalizing punctuation to Persian standards and aligning labels such that only the first subword of a multi-subword word receives a label; continuation subwords are ignored in the loss calculation. The model is trained with a cross-entropy loss using AdamW on a carefully filtered 989k-sample subset of the larger PersianPunc corpus, which requires sentences to contain at least two punctuation marks to focus learning on non-trivial cases.

## Key Results
*   The ParsBERT-based model achieves a **macro-averaged F1 score of 91.33%** and a **Full Sentence Match (FSM) rate of 61.80%** on the held-out test set.
*   It **outperforms GPT-4o (85.96% macro F1) and GPT-4o-mini (79.54% macro F1)**, demonstrating that smaller, task-specific models can surpass large proprietary LLMs on this specialized task.
*   Analysis reveals LLMs suffer from **"over-correction,"** altering non-punctuation tokens in ~5% of cases, whereas the BERT-based model does not make such edits.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Dataset Diversity and Focused Filtering
A large-scale, multi-source dataset with rigorous filtering is necessary to learn robust Persian punctuation patterns. By aggregating formal (encyclopedic, medical) and informal (social media, blogs) corpora, the model is exposed to a wide range of punctuation conventions. The filtering criterion (sentences must contain at least two punctuation marks) creates a focused benchmark that emphasizes the most challenging and impactful restoration decisions, excluding trivial single-clause utterances. The core assumption is that punctuation usage varies systematically by domain and register; a model trained on a narrow corpus will fail on out-of-domain text.

**Break condition:** If the filtering criteria systematically exclude valid low-punctuation patterns present in target ASR outputs or specific deployment domains (e.g., poetry), model performance will degrade on those inputs.

### Mechanism 2: Efficient Monolingual Sequence Labeling
Framing the task as token-level sequence labeling and fine-tuning a monolingual model (ParsBERT) is computationally efficient and effective. The model learns to predict a punctuation class for each token position based on contextual embeddings from ParsBERT, which provides strong initial Persian linguistic knowledge. Using a dedicated classification layer on top of the encoder avoids the redundancy and cost of full generative modeling. Assigning labels only to the first subword of a WordPiece token aligns predictions with word-level punctuation placement, a crucial detail for morphologically complex Persian.

**Break condition:** If the subword-label alignment algorithm is flawed, predictions will be systematically shifted, corrupting token-level evaluation and real-world usability.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** Token-Level Sequence Labeling
    *   **Why needed:** Converts a text-generation-style problem (inserting punctuation) into a per-token classification task, which is simpler, more efficient, and aligns well with the sequential nature of punctuation placement.
    *   **Quick check:** For a sentence "من دوست دارم، کتاب می خوانم." the token for "دوست" (if not split) should be labeled EMPTY, the token for "کتاب" should be labeled COMMA, and the token for "خوانم" should be labeled PERIOD.
*   **Concept:** Monolingual Pre-trained Model (ParsBERT)
    *   **Why needed:** Provides a strong, language-specific foundation of syntax, semantics, and morphology, which is critical for understanding Persian sentence structure to predict where punctuation logically fits.
    *   **Quick check:** Compare the embeddings or contextual representations from ParsBERT vs. a multilingual model (e.g., mBERT) for ambiguous Persian sentences; ParsBERT should show more coherent contextual understanding.
*   **Concept:** WordPiece Tokenization & Subword Labeling
    *   **Why needed:** Standard tokenizers split rare or complex words into subwords. The model must not assign a punctuation label to a continuation subword, as the punctuation conceptually belongs to the whole word, not a piece of it.
    *   **Quick check:** For a word like "می‌خواهم" split into `["می", "##خواهم"]`, only the first subword `"می"` should receive the label corresponding to the punctuation following the full word.
*   **Concept:** Macro-Averaged F1 Score
    *   **Why needed:** Punctuation classes are imbalanced (few question marks/comma vs. many periods/empty). Macro-F1 gives equal weight to each class's performance, preventing the majority class from dominating the metric and hiding poor performance on rare but important marks.
    *   **Quick check:** Calculate per-class precision and recall, then average them. A high macro-F1 indicates the model is not ignoring minority punctuation types.
*   **Concept:** Full Sentence Match (FSM)
    *   **Why needed:** Measures end-to-end usability. A real-world ASR system needs the entire punctuated sentence to be correct; token-level accuracy does not guarantee a usable output. It is a stringent, application-relevant metric.
    *   **Quick check:** For each sentence in the test set, compare the model's predicted punctuation sequence (as a string of marks) to the gold sequence. Count the exact matches.
*   **Concept:** Dataset Filtering for "Focused Benchmark"
    *   **Why needed:** By requiring sentences to have ≥2 punctuation marks, the dataset deliberately excludes extremely simple sentences. This focuses model capacity and evaluation on the harder, more ambiguous cases where punctuation restoration is most valuable.
    *   **Quick check:** Analyze the distribution of punctuation counts in the 17M raw corpus vs. the 989k training subset. The filtered subset should show a right-shifted distribution (more punctuation per sentence).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Raw Punctuated Persian Text -> Normalization & Filtering -> Tokenization (ParsBERT WordPiece) -> Punctuation Label Alignment (label to first subword only) -> ParsBERT Encoder + Linear Classifier Head -> Per-Token Punctuation Class Predictions

**Critical Path:** The alignment step is the critical path. A mistake here (e.g., assigning labels after punctuation removal, or to the wrong subword) corrupts all downstream training and evaluation, regardless of model architecture.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
*   **BERT vs. LLM:** The authors trade the broad, zero-shot capability of an LLM for the efficiency, control, and specialized accuracy of a fine-tuned BERT. The BERT approach avoids LLM over-correction and is suitable for real-time deployment.
*   **Sequence Labeling vs. Generation:** Sequence labeling is chosen over text generation to simplify the problem, reduce hallucination risk, and improve computational efficiency. The cost is the inability of the model to *insert* words or make other textual edits (a feature, not a bug, per the over-correction finding).
*   **Monolingual vs. Multilingual:** A monolingual model (ParsBERT) is preferred over a multilingual one for presumably better grasp of Persian-specific morphology and syntax, though this is not explicitly ablated.

**Failure Signatures:**
1.  **Label-Token Misalignment:** Predicted punctuation appears on the wrong words in the output. Often caused by incorrect handling of the subword labeling rule or preprocessing order (removing punctuation before vs. after tokenization).
2.  **Poor Comma F1:** The paper notes commas are particularly challenging. A model with low comma precision/recall will have a mediocre macro-F1 despite good period/question performance.
3.  **High FSM Error Rate despite Good Token F1:** Indicates errors are distributed such that few sentences are perfectly punctuated, a critical failure for end-use. Could be due to error propagation through a sentence.

**First Three Experiments:**
1.  **Alignment Verification Test:** Manually label 20 sentences. Implement the described preprocessing (normalize, tokenize punctuated text, assign label to first subword of each word, remove punctuation for input). Print token-label pairs. Verify 100% alignment matches manual annotation.
2.  **Split Sanity Check:** Train a small model on a known, fixed 100k-sample subset. If possible, compare validation macro-F1 against the paper's reported value on their 10k validation set for a comparable data slice. A large gap indicates an unreported split or preprocessing difference.
3.  **Domain Shift Probe:** Take the trained model (or a quick retrain) and evaluate it on a held-out domain not in the training mix (e.g., classical Persian poetry or very short telegram-style text). A significant drop in FSM would confirm the break condition hypothesis related to the dataset's filtering and source diversity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can prosodic features from Persian speech be effectively integrated into punctuation restoration models to improve performance on authentic ASR outputs?
**Basis in paper:** Explicitly stated in future work: "incorporating prosodic information from Persian speech could improve punctuation restoration."
**Why unresolved:** The current model uses only text-based features, ignoring acoustic cues (pauses, intonation) that strongly correlate with punctuation.
**What evidence would resolve it:** A comparative study of a multimodal model (text + prosodic features like pause duration, pitch) vs. the current text-only model on a held-out set of real ASR transcriptions with audio.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Does the dataset's filtering criterion (requiring ≥2 punctuation marks) create a distribution that misrepresents the difficulty of real-world ASR post-processing, where many segments may be simple single-clause utterances?
**Basis in paper:** Inferred. The filtering is acknowledged as creating a "focused benchmark" that excludes simple sentences. The authors note sentence boundaries are often detected separately in ASR.
**Why unresolved:** The 61.80% FSM suggests error, but it's unclear how much stems from architecture vs. training on a dataset that purposely excludes the simplest cases.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluating the model on a *stratified* test set that includes a representative proportion of simple (single-punctuation or no-punctuation) sentences, and reporting performance degradation on this subset.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What is the precise linguistic or architectural cause of the "over-correction" behavior observed in GPT-4o for Persian, and can it be mitigated without sacrificing the model's strong token-level F1?
**Basis in paper:** Explicitly documented: "GPT-4o exhibits over-correction in approximately 5% of samples, making undesired edits...This over-correction behavior is particularly problematic..." Inferred: No word *additions*, only deletions/substitutions.
**Why unresolved:** The phenomenon is documented but not analyzed. Unknown if it stems from decoding strategy, prompt insufficiency, or training data properties.
**What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study on GPT-4o's outputs: (1) categorize types of over-corrections, (2) test alternative, more constrained prompts/decoding parameters, (3) compare over-correction rates on Persian vs. other languages.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Can a single model jointly and effectively perform Persian punctuation restoration and Zero-Width Non-Joiner (ZWNJ) insertion, and would this multi-task learning improve the accuracy or robustness of either task?
**Basis in paper:** Explicit future work: "extending the model to jointly handle punctuation restoration and Zero-Width Non-Joiner (ZWNJ) insertion."
**Why unresolved:** The current model is single-task. ZWNJ insertion is a separate, frequent normalization issue. The potential interaction—where contextual understanding benefits both—is hypothesized but untested. There's also a risk of negative transfer.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Train a ParsBERT-based multi-task model with shared encoder and separate heads for punctuation and ZWNJ. Evaluate on a test set annotated for *both* tasks against a single-task baseline.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** How robust are the reported performance metrics (Macro F1: 91.33%, FSM: 61.80%) to variations in test-set size, domain composition, and annotation consistency, given the single 1,000-sentence test set?
**Basis in paper:** Inferred. Authors state: "Our evaluation is limited to 1,000 test sentences due to resource constraints...More extensive evaluation...would strengthen our findings." Test set is a 1K stratified sample from a 1M subset, not the full 17M.
**Why unresolved:** With only 1,000 test sentences, confidence intervals are unknown. The stratified sampling may not capture full diversity. Annotation consistency (especially for flexible commas) is not measured.
**What evidence would resolve it:** (1) Compute confidence intervals via bootstrapping on the existing test set. (2) Create and evaluate on an additional, larger test set (5k-10k sentences) sampled from the *full* 17M dataset. (3) Conduct an inter-annotator agreement study on a subset of the test set.

## Limitations
*   **Subword-label alignment underspecified:** The exact algorithm for handling punctuation between subwords of a single word and masking continuation subwords (e.g., using `ignore_index=-100`) is not detailed, risking implementation variance.
*   **Unreported train/val/test split details:** The random seed and exact stratification weights used to sample the 989k/10k/1k splits from the 17M corpus are absent, preventing faithful reproduction of the reported metrics.
*   **Generalization constrained by filtering:** The requirement for sentences to have ≥2 punctuation marks may create a distribution that does not fully represent the spectrum of ASR outputs, which can include many simple, low-punctuation segments.

## Confidence
*   **High:** The existence, scale, and design (multi-source, filtering) of the PersianPunc dataset are clearly described and supported.
*   **Medium:** The model architecture (ParsBERT fine-tuning, sequence labeling) and hyperparameters are specified, but the unreported subset sampling strategy introduces uncertainty around the exact reproducibility of the 91.33%/61.80% figures.
*   **Medium-Low:** The comparative claims against LLMs (superiority, over-correction) are based on a limited evaluation of only two proprietary models without cost benchmarking or testing of open-weight alternatives.
*   **Medium:** The reported over-correction phenomenon in LLMs is documented anecdotally (~5%) but lacks a rigorous error analysis to diagnose its root cause.
