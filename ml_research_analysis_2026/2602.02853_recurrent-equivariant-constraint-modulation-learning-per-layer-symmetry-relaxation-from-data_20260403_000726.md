# Recurrent Equivariant Constraint Modulation: Learning Per-Layer Symmetry Relaxation from Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02853
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02853
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces RECM, a recurrent mechanism that automatically learns per-layer relaxation of equivariant constraints, achieving lower MSE on N-body (3.7 vs. ~4.0) and motion capture (~1.0 reduction) and improving precision‑recall trade‑off on molecular conformer generation.

## Executive Summary
Equivariant neural networks enforce strict symmetry constraints, which can be too restrictive for tasks with only approximate symmetries. Manual tuning of per‑layer relaxation is costly and dataset‑specific. This paper proposes Recurrent Equivariant Constraint Modulation (RECM), where each layer’s relaxation weights are learned via a recurrent update driven by its input‑target distribution. The relaxation is theoretically upper‑bounded by the layer’s symmetry gap, guaranteeing full recovery for perfectly symmetric data while allowing necessary flexibility otherwise. Experiments on N‑body simulations, motion capture, and molecular conformer generation show RECM consistently outperforms prior relaxation methods, improving accuracy and achieving better precision‑recall trade‑offs.

## Method Summary
RECM augments an equivariant layer with parallel unconstrained candidate branches and a modulation controller. The controller maintains a recurrent state $h_t$ per layer, updated by an exponential moving average of a learned function $l_\theta(z,y)$ that compares the layer’s input‑target pair to its group‑symmetrized version. Relaxation weights $\alpha_{i,t}$ (for each unconstrained branch) and $\beta_t$ (for the equivariant branch) are derived from $h_t$ via bounded linear maps and nonlinearities. During training, gradients flow to all parameters; at inference, branches with negligible $\alpha_i$ are pruned. The update rule ensures $h_t$ converges under mild conditions, and the expected relaxation is bounded by $2B\, W_1(p, p_G)$, linking relaxation to the symmetry gap.

## Key Results
- On N‑body simulation (exact symmetry), RECM reduces MSE to 3.7 vs. ~4.0 for prior relaxation.
- On motion capture trajectory prediction (approximate symmetry), MSE improves by ~1.0 over best baseline.
- On molecular conformer generation (GEOM‑Drugs), applying RECM to ETFlow and DiTMC increases precision (e.g., ETFlow median: 82.0%→82.02%) while maintaining recall, yielding higher F1‑score than both strict equivariant and fully unconstrained models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** RECM uses a recurrent state $h_t$ to dynamically control per‑layer relaxation parameters $\alpha_{i,t}$, $\beta_t$ during training.
- **Mechanism:** The state $h_t$ is updated via an exponential moving average of a learned function $l_\theta(z_{t-1}, y_{t-1})$ applied to the previous layer’s input‑target pair. The relaxation weights are non‑linearly transformed from $h_t$ (e.g., via GeLU). This creates a feedback loop where the optimization dynamics of the layer itself inform its future constraint modulation.
- **Core assumption:** The learnable update function $l_\theta$ is bounded and Lipschitz, and the input distribution $p_t$ converges to a fixed $p$. This ensures $h_t$ converges to a stable value $h^*$.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “each layer’s relaxation weights are learned via a recurrent update rule driven by the symmetry properties of its input‑target distribution.” [section 4.1] Equations (1) and (2) define the recurrent $h_t$ update and its use to compute $\alpha_{i,t}$, $\beta_t$. Neighboring work on constraint relaxation provides context but does not detail RECM’s recurrent mechanism.
- **Break condition:** Convergence of $h_t$ (Lemma 4.1) fails if $l_{\theta_t}$ does not converge uniformly or if $p_t$ does not converge in Wasserstein distance, e.g., due to unstable training or non‑stationary data.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The limiting relaxation level is theoretically upper‑bounded by the layer’s symmetry gap (the Wasserstein distance between its input‑target distribution and its group‑symmetrized version).
- **Mechanism:** The update function $l_\theta(z,y)$ is defined as $r_\theta(z,y) - \frac{1}{|C_G|}\sum_{g\in C_G} r_\theta(\rho_{\text{in}}(g)z, \rho_{\text{out}}(g)y)$. Its expectation over $p$ is bounded by $2B\, W_1(p, p_G)$, where $B$ is the Lipschitz constant of $r_\theta$ and $p_G$ is the symmetrized distribution. Since $|\alpha_i|$ is bounded by $|w_{\alpha_i}^T h^*|$, this propagates the symmetry gap bound to the relaxation weights.
- **Core assumption:** The group $G$ is compact with a finite topological generating set $C_G$, representations are continuous and unitary, and $r_\theta$ is Lipschitz.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “the learned relaxation is upper‑bounded by the layer’s symmetry gap.” [section 4.2] Theorem 4.2 formally states $|\mathbb{E}[l_\theta]| \le 2B\, W_1(p, p_G)$ and conditions for lower bound. Related work on symmetry gaps discusses the concept but not RECM’s bound.
- **Break condition:** The bound relies on Kantorovich‑Rubinstein duality, requiring $l_\theta$ to be Lipschitz. If $r_\theta$ is not properly normalized or the group action is not measure‑preserving, the guarantee may fail.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** RECM automatically reconciles relaxation levels across layers without task‑specific prior knowledge, recovering full equivariance for symmetric layers while allowing flexibility for approximate symmetries.
- **Mechanism:** Each layer’s $h_t$ evolves based only on its local input‑target pairs $(z_t, y_t)$. For a layer with fully symmetric distribution ($p = p_G$), Theorem 4.2 forces $\mathbb{E}[l_\theta]=0$, leading $h^*\to 0$ and thus $\alpha_i\to 0$ (full equivariance). For non‑symmetric $p$, $\mathbb{E}[l_\theta]$ can be non‑zero, allowing non‑zero $\alpha_i$ (partial relaxation).
- **Core assumption:** The update function $r_\theta$ is a universal approximator for bounded Lipschitz functions, ensuring that for any non‑zero symmetry gap, parameters exist to achieve $\mathbb{E}[l_\theta] > 0$.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “without requiring any prior knowledge about the task‑dependent target relaxation level.” [section 4.2] Lemma 4.3 shows $p \neq p_{C_G}$ iff $p$ is not fully invariant, linking non‑zero relaxation to non‑symmetric distributions. [section 5.1] Figure 2 empirically shows $\alpha$ converging to zero on fully symmetric (rotated) data and to non‑zero on non‑symmetric (aligned) data.
- **Break condition:** If the network’s capacity is insufficient (e.g., $r_\theta$ not expressive enough), it might fail to realize the required $\mathbb{E}[l_\theta]$ even for a non‑zero symmetry gap, leading to under‑relaxation. The paper assumes sufficient capacity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Group Equivariance**  
  Why needed: RECM modulates constraints on equivariant layers; understanding that a layer $f_{\text{eq}}$ must satisfy $f_{\text{eq}}(\rho_{\text{in}}(g)z) = \rho_{\text{out}}(g) f_{\text{eq}}(z)$ is foundational.  
  Quick check: If a linear layer $W$ is SO(3)-equivariant with $\rho_{\text{in}}=\rho_{\text{out}}=\text{ROT}$, what constraint must $W$ satisfy? (Answer: $W R = R W$ for all rotation matrices $R$.)

- **Symmetry Gap / Invariance Gap**  
  Why needed: The symmetry gap ($W_1(p, p_G)$) is the key quantity bounding RECM’s relaxation; it quantifies how much a layer’s input‑target distribution deviates from exact symmetry.  
  Quick check: If a distribution $p(z,y)$ is perfectly SO(3)-invariant, what is $W_1(p, p_G)$ and what does RECM converge to? (Answer: Zero; $\alpha_i \to 0$.)

- **Recurrent State Convergence**  
  Why needed: The guarantee that $h_t$ converges to $h^*$ (Lemma 4.1) underpins the entire mechanism; it links the adaptive process to a stable target.  
  Quick check: What two main conditions on $l_{\theta_t}$ and $p_t$ are needed for $h_t$ to converge almost surely? (Answer: $l_{\theta_t}$ bounded/Lipschitz and converges uniformly; $p_t$ converges in $W_1$.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Equivariant base layer ($f_{\text{eq}}$) and unconstrained candidate branches ($f_{\text{un},i}$) feed into the modulation controller (MLP $r_\theta$ and recurrent state $h_t$) which outputs relaxation weights $\alpha_{i,t}$ and $\beta_t$. The final output is $\beta_t f_{\text{eq}}(z) + \sum_i \alpha_{i,t} f_{\text{un},i}(z)$. Pruning removes terms with $|\alpha_i| < \epsilon$ at inference.  
Plain text diagram: Input → $f_{\text{eq}}$ and $f_{\text{un},i}$ → Modulation Controller ($r_\theta$, $h_t$) → $\alpha$, $\beta$ → Output $= \beta·f_{\text{eq}} + \sum \alpha_i·f_{\text{un},i}$; pruning deletes small $\alpha_i$ branches.

### Critical Path
1. Forward pass: For each layer $j$, compute $z_j$ and current $\alpha_{j,t}, \beta_{j,t}$ to produce output $\beta_{j,t} f_{\text{eq}}(z_j) + \sum_i \alpha_{i,j,t} f_{\text{un},i}(z_j)$.
2. Loss computation: $L(f(x), y)$.
3. Backward pass: Gradients flow to parameters of $f_{\text{eq}}$, $f_{\text{un},i}$, $w_{\alpha,i}$, $w_{\beta}$, and $r_\theta$.
4. State update: $h_t$ is updated without gradients via the EMA equation using previous step’s $l_\theta(z_{t-1}, y_{t-1})$.
5. Repeat for next iteration/minibatch.

### Design Tradeoffs
- More unconstrained branches ($K$) and larger $h_t$ dimension increase capacity to break symmetry but add parameters; pruning mitigates inference cost.
- A more complex $r_\theta$ (deeper MLP) can better approximate the symmetry gap signal but may destabilize $h_t$ convergence; a 16‑dim hidden layer was found sufficient.
- Larger generating set $|C_G|$ yields a tighter bound to $p_G$ but increases compute per step; accuracy is robust to its size.

### Failure Signatures
- **Under‑relaxation on non‑symmetric tasks:** All $\alpha_i$ converge near zero despite performance benefit; inspect $h_t$ trajectory – likely $r_\theta$ lacks expressivity to detect the gap.
- **Oscillating or drifting $\alpha_i$ / $h_t$:** $h_t$ fails to converge; may require lower learning rate for $r_\theta$ parameters to satisfy Lemma 4.1.
- **Excessive retained parameters on symmetric tasks:** Many $\alpha_i$ remain large; check that $||w_{\alpha,i}|| \le 1$ is enforced and $l_\theta$ is correctly specified.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Ablation on controlled symmetry (ModelNet40):** Train VN‑PointNet+RECM on randomly rotated (fully symmetric) vs. aligned (non‑symmetric) point clouds. Verify $\alpha_i \to 0$ only for symmetric case and that final accuracy exceeds fixed‑schedule baselines.
2. **Comparison on exact vs. approximate symmetry tasks:** Apply RECM to SEGNN (N‑body, exact symmetry) and EGNO (motion capture, approximate). Check that RECM matches or beats prior constraint relaxation methods (ES, ACE‑exact/appr) with a single training run.
3. **Scalability and precision‑recall trade‑off (GEOM‑Drugs):** Apply RECM to ETFlow/DiTMC on molecular conformer generation. Verify precision increases (approaching equivariant model) while recall remains competitive (better than strict equivariant), yielding higher F1‑score. Prune and report final active parameter ratio.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does the convergence guarantee require stationary layer distributions?**  
   Basis: Lemma 4.1 assumes $(z_t,y_t)$ distribution converges; joint training makes it non‑stationary.  
   Why unresolved: Proof does not account for evolving distributions in deep networks.  
   What evidence would resolve: Generalize convergence proof to non‑stationary multi‑layer case, or show robustness via constant LR experiments.

2. **How does choice of generating set $C_G$ affect RECM’s optimization and bound tightness?**  
   Basis: Theory allows any topological generating set; paper uses fixed rotations but no study of alternatives.  
   Why unresolved: $C_G$ might impact gradient variance and practical convergence; no guidance on selection.  
   What evidence would resolve: Compare performance with different $C_G$ sizes and elements; analyze relation between $C_G$ properties and bound.

3. **Do learned $\alpha_i$ scale with the quantitative symmetry gap $W_1(p,p_G)$, or is the bound $2B W_1$ loose?**  
   Basis: Theorem 4.2 bounds $|\mathbb{E}[l_\theta]|$ by $2B W_1$, but $\alpha_i$ may be smaller.  
   Why unresolved: Without known correlation, cannot predict relaxation needed from data symmetry.  
   What evidence would resolve: Compute $W_1$ for intermediate layers and correlate with $\alpha_i$; synthetic tasks with controlled gaps.

4. **Can RECM be extended to non‑compact groups (e.g., translations) lacking a finite Haar measure?**  
   Basis: Theory assumes compact $G$ with Haar; applications focus on rotations (compact). Non‑compact symmetries not covered.  
   Why unresolved: Haar measure needed for averaging; non‑compact groups require alternative.  
   What evidence would resolve: Develop theory for non‑compact groups; implement RECM‑like modulation for translation equivariance in CNNs.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees rely on assumptions (bounded Lipschitz $r_\theta$, convergence of $p_t$ and $l_{\theta_t}$, compact group) that may be violated during unstable training.
- The bound linking relaxation to symmetry gap can be loose, and estimating the gap from data is non‑trivial.
- Empirical validation is confined to three domains; generalizability to other symmetry groups and larger models is unknown, and the pruning threshold $\epsilon$ remains heuristic.

## Confidence
- Recurrent convergence & automatic per‑layer modulation: **High** – proofs are rigorous under assumptions, and Figure 2 shows convergence.
- Relaxation upper‑bounded by symmetry gap: **High** – Theorem 4.2 is sound; empirical trend in Figure 2 aligns with theory.
- Empirical superiority over baselines: **Medium** – improvements are consistent but confined to specific models/datasets; broader validation needed to exclude dataset bias or tunable hyperparameters.

## Next Checks
1. Compute empirical symmetry gap (e.g., via sliced Wasserstein distance) on test distributions for each layer and correlate with learned $\alpha_i$ to quantify bound tightness.
2. Ablate generating set size $|C_G|$ and $r_\theta$ capacity across all tasks to assess robustness of $h_t$ convergence and final performance.
3. Extend RECM to two new symmetry groups ($O(3)$ including reflections, and $Scale$ for similarity transforms) to test applicability beyond rotations.
