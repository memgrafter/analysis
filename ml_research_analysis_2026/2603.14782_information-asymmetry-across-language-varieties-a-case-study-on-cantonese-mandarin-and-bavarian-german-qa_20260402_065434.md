# Information Asymmetry across Language Varieties: A Case Study on Cantonese-Mandarin and Bavarian-German QA

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14782  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14782  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** LLMs exhibit severe knowledge gaps for facts appearing only in local Wikipedia editions; providing the local context or a translated version dramatically improves performance.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) command knowledge from predominantly high‑resource languages, yet local language varieties (e.g., Cantonese, Bavarian) are underrepresented. This study builds WILOVA‑QA, a QA dataset where correct answers exist exclusively in local Wikipedia lead sections but not in standard Mandarin or German counterparts. Closed‑book experiments reveal extremely low BERTScore (~17‑26), confirming that LLMs fail to recall such facts from parametric memory. When the local context is supplied, performance rises sharply (gains up to +32.7). Translating the local context into the standard language yields additional improvements. The work demonstrates that information asymmetry on the web directly shapes LLM knowledge gaps and that local Wikipedia editions are an underutilized resource.

## Method Summary
The authors created WILOVA‑QA by first extracting aligned article pairs from local and standard Wikipedia editions. A local‑heavy filtering step ensured that the lead section of the local edition contained unique information absent from the standard lead. Human annotators then wrote questions and answers in the standard language based solely on the local lead content, verifying that the answers did not appear in any part of the standard article. The resulting dataset covers two language families: Cantonese–Mandarin (yue‑cmn) and Bavarian–German (bar‑deu). For evaluation, the study used five prompt scenarios: question‑only (closed‑book), +standard, +local, +standard+local, and +local(translated). Answers were generated by several LLMs (Llama3.1, Qwen2.5, gpt‑oss) via vLLM and assessed with lexical (ROUGE‑L, chrF++) and semantic (BERTScore, LLMaJ) metrics. The translation condition was applied only to Cantonese due to reliable Mandarin MT.

## Key Results
- Closed‑book QA yields very low BERTScore (17–26), indicating failure to recall facts unique to local editions.
- Providing the local Wikipedia lead (+local) substantially improves performance: e.g., Qwen2.5‑72B gains +32.68 (yue‑cmn) and +13.32 (bar‑deu).
- Translating the local context to the standard language (+local(translated)) often yields further modest gains (e.g., +2.18 over +local for yue‑cmn).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Parametric knowledge gaps
LLMs rarely encounter local‑only facts during pretraining because crawls are dominated by high‑resource standard language content. Consequently, the model’s internal weights do not encode such information. Evidence: closed‑book BERTScore ~17–26 consistently across models.

### Mechanism 2: Effective use of local context
When the local lead section is provided, the model can directly read the answer even if it did not store it internally. This shows that LLMs can comprehend and extract information from the local variety. Evidence: large gains with +local across all tested models.

### Mechanism 3: Cross‑lingual transfer via translation
Translating the local context into the standard language aligns the information with the model’s dominant language distribution, easing comprehension. Evidence: +local(translated) outperforms +local for Cantonese, where MT quality is high.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Information Asymmetry  
  **Why needed:** It explains why some facts appear only in local Wikipedia editions, creating knowledge gaps for LLMs.  
  **Quick check:** What does information asymmetry refer to in multilingual Wikipedia?  
  **Answer:** The uneven coverage of facts across language editions, where certain details are present only in some local versions.

- **Concept:** Cross‑lingual Context Retrieval  
  **Why needed:** The study tests whether LLMs can answer questions using context in a different language variety.  
  **Quick check:** What does the “+local” prompt scenario evaluate?  
  **Answer:** The model’s ability to answer questions when relevant information is provided in the local language variety.

- **Concept:** QA Evaluation Metrics (ROUGE‑L, chrF++, BERTScore, LLM‑as‑a‑Judge)  
  **Why needed:** Multiple metrics capture lexical overlap, semantic similarity, and human‑like correctness, providing a robust assessment.  
  **Quick check:** Why use both overlap‑based metrics and LLM‑as‑a‑Judge?  
  **Answer:** Overlap metrics are fast and language‑agnostic; LLM‑as‑a‑Judge adds nuanced correctness judgment, complementing automated scores.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Wikipedia dumps → Alignment script → Local‑heavy filtering → Manual QA annotation → Prompt templates → LLM inference (vLLM) → Generated answers → Evaluation (ROUGE‑L, chrF++, BERTScore, LLMaJ) [Translation: Local context → Google Cloud Translate → Standard context].

**Critical path:**  
1. Preprocess Wikipedia: extract and align local/standard articles, filter to local‑heavy pairs.  
2. Annotate QA: identify unique local facts, craft questions/answers in standard language, verify asymmetry.  
3. Prepare prompts: generate five context conditions for each QA pair.  
4. Run inference: obtain answers from all models.  
5. Evaluate: compute similarity metrics and LLMaJ correctness.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Lead section vs. full document: Leads are concise and reduce noise but may omit some unique facts; document‑level annotation was added to probe out‑of‑context retrieval.  
- Translation scope: Only Cantonese had reliable MT; Bavarian translation was omitted, limiting cross‑lingual comparison.  
- Model diversity: Testing small (8B) and large (120B) models reveals scaling trends but increases compute cost.  
- Metrics: Overlap metrics are fast but may not reflect semantic correctness; LLMaJ is more accurate but slower and introduces judge bias.

**Failure signatures:**  
- No gain with +local → annotation errors, poor local comprehension, or corrupted context injection.  
- Large discrepancy between BERTScore and LLMaJ → over‑reliance on surface matching.  
- High variance across seeds → prompt sensitivity or unstable generation.  
- Translation degradation → check if +local(translated) underperforms +local.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Closed‑book QA (question‑only) – establishes baseline parametric knowledge.  
2. Open‑book with local/standard contexts – tests direct context utilization.  
3. Translation‑augmented QA – measures benefit of converting local context to standard language.

## Limitations
- Dataset scope limited to two language pairs and lead‑section facts; generalizability to other varieties or domains is unknown.  
- Translation experiments only for Cantonese; Bavarian results are absent due to lack of reliable MT.  
- Evaluation relies heavily on automatic metrics with limited human validation.

## Confidence
- Claim 1: LLMs lack parametric knowledge of local‑only facts → **High** confidence (low closed‑book scores).  
- Claim 2: Supplying local context enables correct answers → **High** confidence (consistent large gains).  
- Claim 3: Translating local context yields further improvement → **Medium** confidence (modest gains, only validated for one pair).

## Next Checks
1. Scale to diverse varieties: replicate for at least three additional language pairs (e.g., Moroccan Arabic→MSA, Finnish→Swedish) using the same local‑heavy filtering.  
2. Noisy retrieval simulation: integrate WILOVA‑QA into a RAG pipeline with full Wikipedia documents and ranked distractors to test robustness.  
3. Human‑LLM judgment correlation: conduct a human evaluation of 500 answers and correlate with LLMaJ scores to quantify judge reliability.
