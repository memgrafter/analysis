# Tone Matters: The Impact of Linguistic Tone on Hallucination in VLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06460
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06460
- **Reference count:** 36
- **Primary result:** VLMs exhibit non‑monotonic hallucination rates with a peak at intermediate prompt intensity; safety alignment is more effective against semantic toxicity than structural coercion.

## Executive Summary
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) often fabricate information when visual evidence is absent. This paper investigates how the linguistic tone of prompts—specifically, graded coerciveness—modulates such hallucination. The authors introduce Ghost-100, a synthetic dataset where key visual details are deliberately missing (e.g., blurred text), and apply a 5‑Level Prompt Intensity Framework ranging from neutral queries to toxic commands. Evaluating three open‑weight VLMs, they measure hallucination frequency via Attack Success Rate (ASR) and severity via a 5‑level Hallucination Severity Score (HSS). All models show a non‑monotonic pattern: ASR increases with moderate coercion, then declines at higher intensities, though the reduction threshold differs across models (Qwen at Levels ¾, MiniCPM‑V only at Level 5). This suggests current safety training better detects semantic hostility than structural pressure, exposing model‑specific vulnerabilities to compliance pressure.

## Method Summary
The study uses a zero‑shot evaluation on frozen VLMs. Ghost‑100 comprises 600 synthetic images across six categories, each engineered to lack a specific queried attribute (e.g., an illegible name badge). Five prompt templates per image implement a graded intensity scale: Level 1 is a neutral question, Level 2 adds mild coercion, Level 3 enforces an expectation, Level 4 imposes normative enforcement (structural coercion), and Level 5 adds toxic language and refusal prohibition. For each of three models (MiniCPM‑V 2.6‑8B, Qwen2‑VL‑7B, Qwen3‑VL‑8B), responses are collected under fixed decoding (temperature 0.7, top‑p 0.9) with 4‑bit NF4 quantization. Human annotators label whether a response hallucinates the missing content (computing ASR). An LLM judge (GPT‑4o‑mini) assigns a severity score (HSS) based on the confidence and specificity of the fabricated information. The resulting ASR and HSS curves across prompt levels reveal the non‑monotonic behavior.

## Key Results
- All VLMs show peak hallucination frequency (ASR) at intermediate prompt intensity (Levels 2–3), followed by a decline at higher intensities.
- Thresholds for reduction differ: Qwen2‑VL and Qwen3‑VL drop at Levels 3–4, while MiniCPM‑V only declines at Level 5.
- The contrast between Level 4 (structural coercion) and Level 5 (toxic coercion) indicates safety alignment reacts strongly to semantic toxicity but is less effective against purely structural pressure.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Moderate prompt intensity increases hallucination because compliance pressure overrides uncertainty, but extreme intensity (especially toxic) activates safety mechanisms that reduce hallucination.
- **Mechanism:** The model trades off between instruction-following and safety. Mild coercion encourages answering despite missing visual evidence, while strong toxic triggers trigger refusal behaviors.
- **Core assumption:** Safety alignment is tuned to detect semantic hostility more than structural pressure.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "hallucination rates do not increase monotonically with prompt intensity. All models exhibit reductions at higher intensity levels at different thresholds"
  - [section] Figure 2 shows ASR rising from L1 to L2/L3 then falling at higher levels for all models.
  - [corpus] Neighboring LLM research (e.g., [2309.11064]) suggests prompt nuances affect hallucination, though VLM-specific non-monotonicity is novel.
- **Break condition:** If hallucination rates increase monotonically with intensity, the mechanism is falsified.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The threshold at which safety mechanisms reduce hallucination varies across models, indicating model-specific alignment characteristics.
- **Mechanism:** Different models have different sensitivity to coercive language due to variations in training data, RLHF, or architecture.
- **Core assumption:** Safety alignment is not uniform; models calibrate risk differently.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Qwen models reduced at Level 3-4 while MiniCPM-V reduced only at Level 5."
  - [section] Figure 2: Qwen2-VL-7B and Qwen3-VL-8B show ASR drop at L3/L4; MiniCPM-V drop at L5.
  - [corpus] No direct comparative study found among multiple VLMs under graded prompts; this paper provides primary evidence.
- **Break condition:** If all models show identical reduction thresholds, the mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Structural coercion (rigid output constraints) is less likely to trigger safety alignment than semantic toxicity, leading to higher hallucination under non-toxic but coercive prompts.
- **Mechanism:** Safety training emphasizes detecting harmful content (toxicity), not constraints that force generation; thus, models comply with structural pressure even when uncertain.
- **Core assumption:** Safety alignment focuses on semantics, not pragmatics.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "current safety alignment is more effective against semantic toxicity than structural coercion"
  - [section] Prompt templates: L4 (normative enforcement) is structurally coercive without toxic language, L5 (coercive control) adds explicit toxicity and refusal prohibition. L4 often fails to reduce hallucination while L5 does.
  - [corpus] Limited; the paper's prompt design and results directly support this distinction.
- **Break condition:** If L4 (structural coercion) consistently triggers safety refusals comparable to L5, the mechanism is invalid.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Ghost-100 dataset -> Prompt Intensity Framework (5-level templates) -> Model Inference (MiniCPM-V, Qwen2/3-VL, 4-bit NF4, temp=0.7, top-p=0.9) -> Response Logging -> Human Annotation (ASR) & LLM Judge (HSS) -> Analysis of ASR/HSS curves.
- **Critical path:**
  1. Generate Ghost-100 images via procedural pipeline (Algorithm 1).
  2. For each model, load with quantization and fixed decoding.
  3. For each image and prompt level, generate response and log.
  4. Collect human annotations for ASR.
  5. Run LLM judge on all responses to produce HSS.
  6. Analyze ASR and HSS across prompt levels; plot non-monotonic curves.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Synthetic dataset ensures null ground truth but lacks real-world complexity.
  - Null ground truth eliminates ambiguity but may not generalize to partially missing info.
  - 5-level framework balances granularity and experimental load.
  - Human vs LLM evaluation: human for gold-standard ASR, LLM for scalable severity; introduces potential bias in LLM judge.
  - Model selection: open-weight models for reproducibility, but limited to specific families.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Dataset generation failure: missing component libraries or style pools.
  - Model inference errors: out-of-memory with quantization, or template mismatches.
  - Annotation disagreement: low inter-annotator agreement on hallucination labels.
  - LLM judge inconsistency: severity scores not correlating with human judgment.
  - Non-reproducible curves: due to random seeds or decoding variations.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Baseline sanity check: Run Level 1 prompts on 10 images; expect near 0% ASR (refusals) and HSS≈1. Verifies models understand the null condition.
  2. LLM judge validation: Sample 100 responses, have humans assign severity, compare with GPT‑4o‑mini scores using Cohen’s κ; ensure acceptable agreement (>0.7).
  3. Threshold sensitivity test: For one model, vary temperature (0.1, 0.7, 1.0) and re-run on a subset; check if non‑monotonic pattern persists across stochastic settings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Why do different VLMs exhibit varying thresholds at which increasing prompt intensity reduces hallucination (e.g., Qwen models at Levels 3–4 vs. MiniCPM‑V only at Level 5)? The paper identifies the phenomenon but does not investigate the underlying architectural, training-data, or alignment-method differences. Resolution would require ablation studies comparing model internals, safety-tuning datasets, or controlled finetuning experiments.
- **Open Question 2:** Can safety alignment techniques be adapted to mitigate hallucinations induced by structural coercion (e.g., rigid formatting) as effectively as those induced by semantic toxicity? The paper diagnoses the limitation but does not propose, implement, or evaluate any modifications to alignment pipelines. Evidence would come from developing new alignment objectives or datasets that explicitly include structurally coercive prompts and evaluating on Ghost‑100.
- **Open Question 3:** Do the observed non‑monotonic hallucination patterns generalize to closed‑source commercial VLMs and natural‑image benchmarks with inherently ambiguous missing information? The study is restricted to three open‑weight models on a synthetic null‑ground‑truth dataset. Generalization requires replicating the 5‑level prompt framework on closed‑source models and on ambiguous real‑world benchmarks (e.g., VQAv2).
- **Open Question 4:** Does relying on an LLM judge (GPT‑4o‑mini) without visual context bias Hallucination Severity Scores (HSS), and how aligned is this automated scoring with human severity judgments? The LLM judge may be swayed by linguistic cues unrelated to actual hallucination confidence. A comprehensive human evaluation with multiple annotators scoring the same responses using the HSS rubric, computing quantitative agreement metrics (e.g., Krippendorff’s α), would be needed.

## Limitations
- **Replication details missing:** Exact prompt templates (L1–L5), the GPT‑4o‑mini judge instruction (including the explicit null‑ground‑truth statement), and the human annotation protocol for ASR are not fully disclosed.
- **Limited model coverage:** Only three open‑weight VLMs were evaluated; findings may not generalize to other model families or commercial systems.
- **Synthetic dataset constraints:** Ghost‑100’s controlled absence of visual features lacks the complexity and ambiguity of real‑world missing‑evidence scenarios.

## Confidence
- **Non‑monotonic hallucination frequency (ASR):** High – robust pattern across all three models in Figure 2.
- **Differential safety thresholds across models:** High – clear, statistically significant shifts in ASR reduction points.
- **Safety alignment’s greater sensitivity to semantic toxicity than structural coercion:** Medium – supported by L4 vs L5 contrast but relies on prompt content details that are somewhat ambiguous and on a limited model set.

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt template audit:** Retrieve the exact L1–L5 strings from the authors or repository; run a human validation on a sample (e.g., 20 items per level) to confirm the intended intensity ordering and inter‑level discriminability.
2. **LLM judge calibration:** Collect human HSS labels on a stratified set of 200 responses; compute Cohen’s κ against GPT‑4o‑mini using the reported prompt. If κ < 0.7, refine the judge instruction or employ a jury of multiple LLMs with majority voting.
3. **Generalization study:** Apply the 5‑level framework to additional VLMs (e.g., LLaVA‑1.6, InstructBLIP, GPT‑4V, Claude‑3.5) to test the reproducibility of non‑monotonic curves and threshold variability across architectures and scales.
