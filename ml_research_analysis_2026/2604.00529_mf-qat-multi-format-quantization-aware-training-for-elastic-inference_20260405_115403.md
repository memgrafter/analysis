# MF-QAT: Multi-Format Quantization-Aware Training for Elastic Inference

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2604.00529
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00529
- **Reference count:** 23
- **Primary result:** Multi-format QAT matches or nears the performance of precision-specific single-format QAT across all evaluated formats, including unseen intermediate bit-widths, enabling a single checkpoint to serve multiple precisions at runtime via Slice-and-Scale conversion.

## Executive Summary
Standard quantization-aware training (QAT) optimizes a model for a single numeric format, limiting deployment flexibility across diverse hardware. This paper introduces Multi-Format QAT (MF-QAT), which trains a single model to be robust across multiple quantization formats (e.g., MXINT2–8, MXFP4–8). The core method sequentially trains on increasing precision formats using straight-through estimators, and stores a high-precision (anchor) checkpoint. At inference, a Slice-and-Scale conversion transforms the anchor into any lower precision without retraining. Across six large language models, MF-QAT achieves performance comparable to single-format QAT at each trained precision and generalizes to intermediate bit-widths, with accuracy typically within 1% on key zero-shot tasks. The conversion incurs negligible loss, offering an elastic inference pipeline from a single 8-bit checkpoint.

## Method Summary
MF-QAT extends QAT to multiple target numeric formats within a single training run. The model maintains full-precision master weights. In each training step, the master weights are first quantized to an anchor format (MXINT8 or MXFP8) using standard block-wise scaling. Then, a Slice-and-Scale operation converts the anchor quantized weights to a sampled target format (e.g., MXINT2, MXINT4, etc.). The forward pass uses the low-precision converted weights; the backward pass employs straight-through estimators for both quantization and conversion, updating the master weights to minimize loss under the composite quantization noise. Training proceeds sequentially over increasing bit-widths (e.g., 2→4→6→8 for MXINT) to stabilize learning. After training, only the anchor (8-bit) checkpoint is stored. At runtime, the system loads the anchor and applies the same Slice-and-Scale conversion to the requested target precision, enabling elastic inference without additional checkpoints.

## Key Results
- Multi-format QAT achieves accuracy that matches or closely approaches single-format QAT across all trained precisions (e.g., MXINT2,4,6,8) on language modeling and zero-shot tasks.
- The approach generalizes to unseen intermediate bit-widths (e.g., MXINT3,5,7) with minimal degradation, typically within 1% average 0-shot accuracy drop.
- Slice-and-Scale conversion from the anchor checkpoint to lower precisions yields negligible additional error compared to direct quantization, allowing efficient storage and on-demand precision scaling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Sequential increasing-precision training stabilizes multi-format learning
- **Claim:** Training sequentially on increasing precision formats (2→4→6→8) prevents higher-precision representations from destabilizing when later exposed to lower-precision quantization noise.
- **Mechanism:** Starting with low-bitwidth quantization forces the model to learn robust coarse representations that can withstand aggressive quantization. Subsequent training with higher bitwidths refines these representations without losing earlier robustness because the coarser quantization bins subsume finer granularity.
- **Evidence:** Section 3.2 explains the rationale; abstract mentions the core method; break condition: decreasing order would cause overfitting to higher-precision values.
- **Break condition:** Training in decreasing precision order would overfit to higher-precision weight values, leading to performance collapse at lower precisions.

### Mechanism 2: Straight-through estimators enable gradient propagation through multiple quantization operations
- **Claim:** STE allows simultaneous gradient flow through quantization and Slice-and-Scale conversion, enabling the full-precision master weights to adapt to multiple discretization manifolds.
- **Mechanism:** During a forward pass, weights are quantized to the anchor and then converted to the target format; backward pass treats both operators as identity, propagating gradients to update the master weights.
- **Evidence:** Section 3.5 and 2; corpus support from Attn-QAT.
- **Break condition:** Without STE, quantization would block gradient flow, preventing multi-format learning.

### Mechanism 3: Slice-and-Scale mathematically preserves high-precision reconstruction when down-converting
- **Claim:** For a well-calibrated anchor representation, SS conversion (bit-slicing and scale adjustment) yields a valid lower-precision representation with minimal reconstruction error limited to rounding.
- **Mechanism:** MXINT: divide integer elements by 2^Δe and round, multiply shared scale by 2^Δe; MXFP: explicit division and requantization. The product of scale and integer is preserved.
- **Evidence:** Section 3.3 equations; Section 4.3 shows SS matches direct quantization; Section 3.4 distinguishes MXFP handling.
- **Break condition:** If anchor not trained for multi-format robustness, SS conversion would incur errors akin to naive PTQ.

## Foundational Learning

### Quantization-Aware Training (QAT)
- **Why needed:** MF-QAT directly builds on QAT; understanding STE and noise simulation is prerequisite.
- **Quick check:** What operator substitutes for quantization in the backward pass of QAT, and why is it necessary? (Answer: Straight-through estimator, to allow gradient flow.)

### Microscaling Formats (MXINT/MXFP)
- **Why needed:** MF-QAT operates within the MX format specification, which uses block-wise shared scaling factors distinct from per-tensor or per-channel quantization.
- **Quick check:** How does a microscaling format represent a tensor, and what are its three defining parameters? (Answer: block size, element bitwidth, shared scale per block.)

### Block-wise Scaling
- **Why needed:** Slice-and-Scale relies on independent per-block scales to convert each block without cross-block interference.
- **Quick check:** Why is block-wise scaling crucial for Slice-and-Scale? (Answer: Because conversion adjusts each block's scale independently based on its own anchor values.)

### Straight-Through Estimator (STE)
- **Why needed:** STE is the enabling trick for gradient-based training through non-differentiable quantization operations.
- **Quick check:** What does STE do in the backward pass, and what is assumed about the quantization error's gradient? (Answer: It passes gradients unchanged (identity), assuming the error's gradient is negligible.)

### Post-Training Quantization (PTQ)
- **Why needed:** The evaluation uses PTQ to convert trained checkpoints to target formats, isolating the training procedure's effect.
- **Quick check:** Why use PTQ for evaluation instead of the exact formats used during training? (Answer: To test generalization to arbitrary precisions, including unseen intermediates.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Quantization modules (QA, QA→t) -> Training loop (samples target formats, computes loss with quantized weights, updates master via STE) -> Checkpoint handler (stores only anchor weights) -> Inference converter (applies Slice-and-Scale at runtime) -> Evaluation harness (applies PTQ to compare baselines)

**Critical path:**  
1. Implement MXINT/MXFP quantization and Slice-and-Scale functions per paper equations.  
2. Integrate STE into training step where forward uses converted weights.  
3. Verify single-format QAT baselines before multi-format cycling.  
4. Build anchor storage and runtime conversion; validate SS fidelity against direct quantization.  
5. Evaluate on language modeling and zero-shot tasks.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Training schedule: 1 epoch per format (paper) vs more epochs; more epochs may improve accuracy but risk overfitting for >2B models.  
- Anchor precision: MXINT8/MXFP8 as storage/efficiency trade-off; lower anchors reduce storage but may lose information for accurate SS to very low precisions.  
- Format sampling: Uniform cycling as in paper; focusing more on lower precisions could harm higher precision performance.  
- Block size: Fixed at 64; smaller blocks may improve fidelity but affect hardware compatibility.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. Multi-format QAT underperforms single-format at trained precisions: indicates insufficient training, poor LR, or STE implementation error.  
2. SS conversion degrades accuracy more than direct PTQ: suggests anchor lacks robustness or SS math error, especially for MXFP.  
3. Unseen intermediate precisions (e.g., MXINT5) suffer large drops: model not format-agnostic; need more training diversity.  
4. Training instability when switching formats: loss spikes may be STE gradient explosion or LR issues.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate single-format QAT brittleness: Train Llama-3.2-1B with single-format QAT for MXINT2 and MXINT8 separately. Evaluate across MXINT2-8. Expect sharp performance drop when testing out-of-training precision.  
2. Validate multi-format QAT claim: Train with multi-format QAT (2→4→6→8) on same model. Evaluate across all MXINT2-8, including unseen 3,5,7. Expect performance to track the best single-format at each precision.  
3. Test Slice-and-Scale conversion fidelity: For an MXINT8 anchor, implement SSMXINT to MXINT2-7. Compute per-layer MSE against direct quantization from full precision. Expect SS MSE close to direct MSE, with small gap increasing as target precision decreases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scaling to larger models:** Does MF-QAT work for >10B parameters and diverse architectures? The paper tests up to 7B and notes overfitting issues.
2. **Joint weight and activation quantization:** Can the approach be extended to quantize activations? The paper only considers weight-only quantization.
3. **Training schedule robustness:** How does alternative order (decreasing, random) or simultaneous training compare to the proposed increasing-order sequential schedule?
4. **Generalization to non-language domains:** Does MF-QAT apply to vision transformers or CNNs? The evaluation focuses on LLMs and multimodal models.

## Limitations
- MXFP exponent max (`emax`) values for E2M1/E3M2/E4M3 are not explicitly defined, hindering precise reproduction of MXFP experiments.
- Training data is limited to 128 examples from WikiText-2, increasing risk of overfitting and reducing statistical reliability, especially for larger models.
- Anchor format per model (MXINT8 vs MXFP8) is not specified, influencing storage and conversion details.
- Rounding behavior for signed integers in Slice-and-Scale MXINT conversion may need careful handling (symmetry).

## Confidence
- **Multi-format robustness:** Medium. Results across six LLMs are compelling but depend on unresolved specification details and small-data training.
- **Slice-and-Scale fidelity:** Medium. The mathematics appear sound, but empirical validation is incomplete without correct MXFP `emax`; implementation errors could affect outcomes.
- **Sequential training order:** Medium. The increasing-order rationale is plausible and supported by a break condition, but lacks systematic ablation of alternative schedules.

## Next Checks
1. **SS conversion validation:** For random tensors, compute MSE between SS-converted weights (anchor MXINT8/MXFP8 → lower precisions) and directly quantized weights. Discrepancies >1e-5 indicate implementation errors, especially for MXFP exponent alignment.
2. **Training order ablation:** Run MF-QAT with decreasing precision order (8→6→4→2) on Llama-3.2-1B. A significant performance drop at MXINT2 (>2% relative) versus the increasing order would confirm the claimed mechanism.
3. **Data scaling experiment:** Train MF-QAT on Llama-2-7B using the full WikiText-2 train split (~36K sentences). If multi-format performance degrades relative to single-format QAT more than in the paper, the 128-example regime is insufficient.
