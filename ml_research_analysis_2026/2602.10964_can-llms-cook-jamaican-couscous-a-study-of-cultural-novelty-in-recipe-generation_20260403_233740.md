# Can LLMs Cook Jamaican Couscous? A Study of Cultural Novelty in Recipe Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10964
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10964
- **Reference count**: 13
- **Primary result**: LLM-generated culturally adapted recipes show no statistically significant correlation between textual novelty (via Jensen-Shannon divergence) and any cultural distance metric (geographic, linguistic, religious, value-based), while human adaptations do. LLMs overproduce certain novelty types and fail to preserve culturally core ingredients.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates whether large language models (LLMs) can generate culturally adapted recipes (e.g., transforming a Moroccan dish into a Jamaican version) in a manner that mirrors human cultural borrowing. Using the GlobalFusion dataset and a suite of Jensen-Shannon divergence–based novelty metrics, the authors find that LLMs fail to modulate novelty according to cultural distance. Unlike humans, LLMs exhibit no correlation between generated recipe divergence and established cultural distance measures. Instead, they inflate lexical novelty—particularly "New Surprise" and "Difference"—misinterpret creativity prompts, lose culturally salient signals in intermediate layers, and default to generic ingredients. The work highlights a fundamental gap in LLMs' ability to perform grounded, culturally aware transformation.

## Method Summary
The study uses the GlobalFusion dataset, which pairs human recipes across 130 countries for 500 dishes. For each dish, the origin country’s recipes serve as a reference corpus, and variations for target countries are treated as cultural adaptations. Eight LLMs generate recipes via zero-shot prompts that include the dish name, target nationality, and a novelty/tradition keyword (e.g., "novel," "traditional"). Outputs are filtered for quality and preprocessed (lemmatization, POS filtering). Novelty is quantified by comparing the token distribution of each generated recipe’s instructions to the reference corpus using five Jensen-Shannon divergence metrics: Newness, Uniqueness, Difference, New Surprise, and Divergent Surprise. Primary analysis computes Pearson correlations between these metrics and four cultural distance measures (Inglehart-Welzel values, linguistic, religious, geographic). Parallel analyses examine layer-wise representation changes (Logit Lens), ingredient grounding (overlap, recall, TF-IDF country attribution), and title nationality attribution.

## Key Results
- LLM-generated recipe novelty shows no significant correlation with any cultural distance metric; human adaptations do.
- LLMs overproduce "New Surprise" and "Difference" regardless of prompt keywords, misinterpreting creativity directives as lexical injection.
- Middle-layer representations compress cultural signal, and final outputs fail to recover it, leading to flattened divergence.
- LLMs exhibit poor ingredient grounding: low recall of culturally core ingredients, overuse of generic items (onion, salt), and high title nationality misattribution.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: LLMs overproduce textual novelty because they interpret creativity prompts as directives for lexical/semantic injection rather than culturally grounded transformation.  
**Mechanism**: Prompt keywords like "novel" or "original" trigger the model to prioritize token-level novelty metrics (new word appearances, atypical co-occurrences), inflating statistical divergence without meaningful cultural repositioning. The model lacks a grounded model of how novelty functions within specific culinary traditions.  
**Evidence**: Abstract notes models "inflate novelty... by misunderstanding notions such as creativity and tradition." Section 4.2.2 shows keywords have minimal impact on textual divergence overall, but "novelty" and "originality" consistently increase Newness while decreasing Uniqueness and Difference.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: Cultural information is weakly encoded in LLM internal representations, leading to a loss of culturally specific divergence in middle layers that is not recovered later.  
**Mechanism**: During forward passes, representations for recipes from different cultures become less distinguishable in middle layers on metrics like Difference and New Surprise. Final-layer and output reconstructions prioritize recipe format compliance over cultural nuance, resulting in outputs with diminished cultural signal.  
**Evidence**: Section 4.3.1 observes "information compression in the early and middle layers" for key metrics; LLM-generated divergence magnitudes remain substantially lower than human references. Figure 7 shows narrower layer-wise divergence gaps for LLMs vs. humans.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: LLMs fail to ground generation in culturally core ingredients, defaulting to globally high-frequency generic items, which erodes the material basis for cultural difference.  
**Mechanism**: The next-token prediction objective favors high-probability, context-agnostic ingredients that appear frequently across cuisines in training data. This leads to low recall of specific, culturally salient ingredients and a substitution strategy that homogenizes outputs.  
**Evidence**: Section 4.3.3 states LLMs "systematically fail to recover many ingredients that humans consider essential" and "expand without grounding and default to generic, or stereotypical ingredients." Table 4 shows top ingredients are globally ubiquitous items like "Salt taste" and "Onion." Figure 9 indicates lower ingredient preservation recall for LLMs vs. humans.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) for Textual Novelty**  
  **Why needed**: Core metric for quantifying "cultural divergence" between a generated recipe and a reference corpus. Asymmetric PMI-based variants (Newness, Difference, etc.) capture different novelty dimensions.  
  **Quick check**: How does JSD measure "Cultural New Surprise" differently from plain "Newness," and why might one correlate with cultural distance while the other does not?

- **Concept: Cultural Distance Metrics**  
  **Why needed**: Independent variables (geographic, linguistic, religious, value-based) used to test correlation with LLM-generated novelty. They ground analysis in established social science constructs.  
  **Quick check**: Why might a model trained on multilingual text fail to show correlation with linguistic distance if evaluation uses only English recipes?

- **Concept: Ingredient Grounding & TF-IDF Country Attribution**  
  **Why needed**: Concrete probe for cultural representation at the material level. Low TF-IDF cosine similarity between a recipe’s ingredients and its target country indicates poor grounding.  
  **Quick check**: What does high ingredient overlap but low recall of reference ingredients suggest about LLM ingredient selection?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
GlobalFusion Dataset Loader -> Prompt Engine -> LLM Generator (vLLM) -> Text Processor (spaCy) -> Metric Engine (JSD computation) -> Correlation Analyzer.  
Parallel paths: Ingredient Analyzer (overlap/recall/TF-IDF) and Layer Probe (Logit Lens re-encode -> layer-wise metrics).

**Critical path**: Data loading and pairing -> prompt construction for each dish/country/keyword -> zero-shot generation -> preprocessing -> novelty metric computation -> correlation analysis. Layer and ingredient analyses run as parallel post-processing on generated outputs.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Layer depth vs. computational cost: Analyzing all layers is prohibitive; sampling embedding, middle, and final layers is a practical compromise that may miss nuanced trajectories.
- Prompt complexity vs. control: Adding persona/expert prompts increases realism but adds noise; the study uses simplified prompts to isolate cultural signal.
- English-only vs. multilingual generalizability: Results are limited to English recipes, potentially underrepresenting multilingual model capacities and masking cross-lingual cultural cues.

**Failure signatures**:
1. Low ingredient usage in instructions (<<20% match to provided ingredient list) as in Table 1.
2. High frequency of generic ingredients (onion, salt) in top-20 lists (Table 4).
3. Country attribution error rate >25% in recipe titles (Figure 8).
4. Flat or inverted correlation coefficients for New Surprise/Difference across cultural distances (Figure 3).

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline Cultural Correlation**: For a single model (e.g., llama3-70b), generate recipes for all dish/country pairs. Compute all five novelty metrics and four cultural distances. Report Pearson r and p-value for each metric/distance pair. Expected: Only Newness may show weak correlation; others near zero or inverted.
2. **Keyword Sensitivity Test**: For the same pairs, generate 7 recipes per pair using each keyword from {novelty, uniqueness, newness, difference, surprise, creativity, originality}. Average novelty metrics per keyword. Perform ANOVA to test keyword effect. Expected: Minimal effect; "novelty"/"originality" increase Newness only.
3. **Layer-wise Divergence Collapse**: For 2–3 dish/country pairs, generate human and LLM recipes. Apply Logit Lens to get token projections at embedding, middle (~0.5 depth), and last three layers. Compute Difference and New Surprise per layer. Plot gap between origin-country and variation recipes. Expected: Gap is small/compressed in middle layers for LLM; human gap persists/increases later.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: What specific mechanisms within transformer architectures cause the observed compression and weak recovery of cultural signals in middle layers? The paper identifies the phenomenon but does not isolate whether this stems from attention pattern biases, feed-forward layer limitations, or training objectives that flatten cultural nuances.
- **Open Question 2**: Does the inability of LLMs to modulate novelty based on "creativity" vs. "tradition" prompts stem from a fundamental misalignment between prompt semantics and the model's internal concept of novelty? Could richer contextual prompts, few-shot examples, or supervised fine-tuning on culturally adapted recipes elicit better control?
- **Open Question 3**: To what extent do ingredient grounding failures reflect dataset biases in GlobalFusion (uneven country representation) versus inherent model limitations? The analysis assumes human references are culturally definitive but may itself be biased toward dominant cuisines or online recipe availability.
- **Open Question 4**: Why do multilingual LLMs not exhibit better cultural alignment despite exposure to diverse linguistic contexts? Does the monolingual (English-only) evaluation framework mask potential cross-lingual cultural adaptations? Might generating recipes in native languages yield different patterns?
- **Open Question 5**: Can architectural modifications (e.g., adapter layers for cultural embeddings, retrieval-augmented generation with cultural knowledge bases) explicitly preserve and modulate cultural information during generation, thereby improving correlation between novelty metrics and cultural distance?

## Limitations
- Exact ε thresholds for novelty metrics and the leave-one-out procedure are undisclosed, preventing exact replication.
- Mapping of the 130 GlobalFusion countries to the Inglehart-Welzel, linguistic, and religious distance sources is not provided; handling of missing distances is unspecified.
- Evaluation is restricted to English recipes, limiting cultural representativity and potentially understating multilingual model capacities.

## Confidence
- LLMs show no cultural-distance correlation: **High** (consistent across 8 models, robust statistics).
- LLMs overproduce textual novelty & misread creativity prompts: **High** (keyword analysis across metrics).
- Middle-layer cultural compression: **Medium** (layer sampling coarse; full trend inferred).
- Poor ingredient grounding & title misattribution: **High** (TF-IDF, recall, and attribution rates clear).
