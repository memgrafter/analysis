# DynHD: Hallucination Detection for Diffusion Large Language Models via Denoising Dynamics Deviation Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16459
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16459
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** DynHD achieves state-of-the-art hallucination detection, improving average AUROC by 12.2% over TraceDet on LLaDA-8B-Instruct and reaching 84.3% AUROC on Dream-7B-Instruct, while maintaining higher inference efficiency than multi-sample methods.

## Executive Summary
DynHD is a method for detecting hallucinations in Diffusion Large Language Models (D-LLMs). It addresses two key challenges: the uneven informativeness of tokens in fixed-length D-LLM outputs and the importance of modeling uncertainty evolution over denoising steps. The approach first filters out non-semantic tokens to obtain a clean uncertainty signal, then learns a query-conditioned reference trajectory for normal (factual) denoising dynamics, and finally detects hallucinations by measuring the deviation of observed dynamics from this reference. Experiments show significant improvements over prior trajectory-based methods and demonstrate superior efficiency.

## Key Results
- DynHD improves average AUROC by 12.2% over TraceDet on LLaDA-8B-Instruct and achieves 84.3% AUROC on Dream-7B-Instruct.
- It maintains higher inference efficiency than multi-sample consistency methods.
- Ablations confirm the necessity of token filtering, temporal attention, and rebound regularization.

## Method Summary
DynHD introduces a novel approach to hallucination detection in Diffusion LLMs by leveraging the dynamics of uncertainty across denoising steps. The core idea is that factual generations follow a characteristic pattern of uncertainty decay, while hallucinations exhibit deviations such as stagnation or rebound. The method operates in two stages: first, it constructs a semantic-aware evidence vector from the token-level entropy trajectory by filtering out non-informative tokens (e.g., control markers, stopwords) and computing statistics (mean, max, top‑k) over the remaining semantic positions. Second, it learns a reference trajectory for factual samples using a generator network \(g_\theta\) that predicts expected evidence at each step conditioned on the query and timestep. The detector then measures deviation between observed and reference evidence, using temporal attention to focus on informative steps and regularization to penalize path deviation and late‑stage entropy rebounds.

In detail, the system takes as input the question \(q\), final response \(r\), and the full entropy trajectory \(T\) collected during generation. The evidence construction module produces a sequence \(E = \{a_t\}\) where each \(a_t\) is a 3‑dimensional vector summarizing the entropy distribution over semantic tokens at step \(t\). The pre‑trained reference generator (trained only on factual samples) yields \(\hat{E} = \{\hat{a}_t\}\). The detector then forms composite features \(x_t = [a_t; \hat{a}_t; \Delta a_t]\) and applies a learned temporal attention to weight steps, aggregating them into a final representation classified by an MLP. Additional losses on path deviation and rebound, with an adaptive margin derived from factual scores, encourage the model to keep factual samples near the reference and flag large divergences as hallucinations. The pipeline adds minimal overhead beyond the D‑LLM’s generation cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
DynHD’s effectiveness stems from three core mechanisms:

### Mechanism 1: Semantic-Aware Evidence Construction
- **Claim:** Filtering out non-semantic tokens from fixed‑length D‑LLM outputs improves hallucination detection by reducing noise in uncertainty signals.
- **Mechanism:** A rule‑based filter (\(I_{ignore}\)) removes five classes of non‑informative tokens (control markers, lexical noise, task boilerplate, stopwords, subword fragments). Only remaining “semantic” positions \(K_t\) contribute entropy to evidence construction.
- **Core assumption:** Fixed‑length D‑LLM generations contain a minority of semantically meaningful tokens; aggregating entropy over all tokens drowns hallucination signals.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions “information density imbalance”; Section 3.1 defines \(I_{ignore}\) and Eq. 3 for \(K_t\); related work lacks spatial filtering.
- **Break condition:** If all tokens were equally informative, filtering would discard valid signals. Ablation in Table 3 (w/o Token Filtering) shows performance drop, supporting necessity.

### Mechanism 2: Learned Reference Trajectory
- **Claim:** Learning a query‑conditioned reference trajectory for uncertainty evolution provides a dynamic baseline against which to measure generative stability.
- **Mechanism:** A generator \(g_\theta\) predicts reference evidence \(\hat{a}_t = g_\theta(q, e_t)\) at each step \(t\), trained only on factual samples (\(y=0\)) via SmoothL1 loss between predicted and actual evidence.
- **Core assumption:** Factual generations exhibit a learnable, question‑specific “normal” pattern of uncertainty decay; hallucinations deviate in shape (stagnation, rebound) rather than absolute magnitude.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “learn expected evolution trajectory”; Section 3.2 defines \(L_{ref}\); cross‑dataset generalization (Table 2) shows transferred dynamics.
- **Break condition:** If factual denoising dynamics were entirely idiosyncratic, a learned reference would be meaningless. Cross‑dataset results suggest the assumption holds.

### Mechanism 3: Deviation Scoring with Late‑Stage Emphasis
- **Claim:** Hallucinations are detected by measuring deviation from the reference trajectory, with emphasis on late‑stage dynamics (stagnation, rebound) using adaptive temporal weighting and regularization.
- **Mechanism:** For each step, construct composite feature \(x_t = [a_t; \hat{a}_t; \Delta a_t]\). Temporal attention learns weights \(\omega_t\) prioritizing steps with high deviation. Regularization losses \(L_{path}\) (cumulative deviation) and \(L_{reb}\) (rebound penalty) encourage factual samples to stay near reference and penalize late uncertainty increases.
- **Core assumption:** Hallucinatory denoising exhibits characteristic morphological deviations (non‑monotonic decay, late‑stage entropy increases) distinguishable from factual convergence.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “measuring discrepancy between observed and reference trajectories”; Section 3.2 defines deviation scoring; Figure 5b visually separates trajectories; ablation of \(L_{reb}\) hurts performance (Table 3).

## Foundational Learning
To grasp DynHD, readers should understand:

### Diffusion Denoising for Text
- **Why needed:** DynHD relies on the multi‑step iterative remasking process of D‑LLMs. Fixed‑length sequences are generated via \(T\) steps of entropy‑controlled token refilling; thus a trajectory (not just final output) exists.
- **Quick check:** Why does a D‑LLM’s fixed‑length output contain many non‑semantic tokens, and how does that differ from AR‑LLM decoding?

### Entropy as Uncertainty Signal
- **Why needed:** Token‑level predictive entropy is the raw evidence DynHD processes. High entropy indicates model uncertainty about a token’s identity at step \(t\).
- **Quick check:** In a diffusion step, why might a semantically critical token have higher entropy than a preposition, and how does that relate to hallucination risk?

### Trajectory‑Based vs. Output‑Centric Detection
- **Why needed:** DynHD’s novelty is exploiting denoising dynamics. This contrasts with AR‑LLM methods that use single‑pass output uncertainty or multi‑sample consistency.
- **Quick check:** Why would a hallucination in a D‑LLM potentially show correct content at intermediate steps but wrong content at the final step?

## Architecture Onboarding
The DynHD detector consists of the following components, processed in this order:

**Input:** Question \(q\), final response \(r\), full denoising trajectory \(T\) (entropy fields \(S_t\) for all steps \(t\)).  
**Semantic‑Aware Evidence Construction:** Apply \(I_{ignore}\) filter to get valid token positions \(K_t\) per step, then compute 3‑stat evidence \(a_t\) = [mean, max, top‑k average entropy].  
**Reference Evidence Dynamics Generator:** Pre‑trained \(g_\theta\) (MLP) takes query embedding and step embedding to predict \(\hat{a}_t\).  
**Deviation‑Based Detector:** Build composite feature \(x_t = [a_t; \hat{a}_t; \Delta a_t]\). Use learned temporal attention weights \(\omega_t\) to aggregate projected \(z_t\) into representation \(h\), then MLP classifier. Also compute path‑deviation \(s_{path}\) and rebound \(s_{rebound}\) with adaptive margin \(\hat{m}\).  
**Regularization:** Losses \(L_{path}\) and \(L_{rebound}\) combined with classification loss.

**Critical path:** \(T\) → (filtering & stats) → \(E\) → (predict \(\hat{E}\)) → (compute deviation & \(\omega_t\)) → \(h\) → \(\hat{y}\). Reference generator \(g_\theta\) is frozen during detector training.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Filtering strictness:* More aggressive \(I_{ignore}\) reduces noise but risks removing subtle semantic tokens. Hand‑crafted rules may need adaptation.
- *Reference generator capacity:* Simple MLP may fail to capture complex dynamics; more expressive models could overfit.
- *Temporal attention:* Learnable weights adapt to informative steps; fixed weighting is simpler but less effective.

**Failure signatures:**
- *Poor spatial filtering:* If \(I_{ignore}\) is incomplete, evidence remains noisy; performance collapses (Table 3).
- *Misaligned reference:* If \(g_\theta\) is poorly trained, \(\hat{E}\) doesn’t represent “normal,” leading to high deviation scores for factual samples.
- *Ignoring late dynamics:* Without \(L_{reb}\), detector misses rebound pattern; AUROC drops (Table 3).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Reproduce ablation “w/o Token Filtering”: Remove \(I_{ignore}\) and expect ≥10% AUROC drop (per Table 3) to validate spatial filtering necessity.
2. Visualize reference trajectories: Plot \(a_t\) vs \(\hat{a}_t\) for factual/hallucinated samples; factual should follow reference closely, hallucinated diverges (like Fig. 5b).
3. Inspect temporal attention weights \(\omega_t\): Average weights across test set; expect monotonic increase toward final denoising steps (\(t \rightarrow 0\)). A flat distribution suggests attention failure.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How universally applicable are the rule‑based filtering rules (\(I_{ignore}\)) across diverse languages, domains beyond QA, and text types with different structural conventions?
- **Basis in paper:** Filtering mechanism (Sec 3.1) is defined using five English‑centric linguistic dimensions. Evaluated only on English QA datasets; generalizability to other contexts is untested.
- **Why unresolved:** “Non‑informative” token definition likely language‑ and task‑dependent. Rules that filter structural padding in English QA may remove meaningful tokens elsewhere (e.g., punctuation in poetry, code delimiters), harming robustness.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic evaluation on multilingual benchmarks (e.g., TyDi QA) or diverse generation tasks (summarization, code) with adapted rules. Comparative AUROC would show if hand‑crafted rules need per‑context engineering or a learned filter is necessary.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How robust is the learned reference evidence dynamics generator (\(g_\theta\)) to distribution shifts in factual data, and does its reliance on training only on factual samples (\(y=0\)) create a brittle reference space?
- **Basis in paper:** \(g_\theta\) trained exclusively on factual samples via \(L_{ref}\). Paper does not discuss covariate shift (e.g., new question topics) or adversarial factual samples with unusual dynamics.
- **Why unresolved:** If factual training set is narrow, reference may be too specific, causing high deviation scores for valid but unconventional factual generations → false positives.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Stress‑test \(g_\theta\) on factual samples from a held‑out dataset with different topic distributions (e.g., procedural vs. entity‑based). Compare deviation score distributions for in‑distribution vs. out‑of‑distribution factuals to detect overfitting.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the theoretical justification and empirical stability of the adaptive factual margin (\(\hat{m}(\tau)\)) used in dynamical regularizers, particularly its sensitivity to the quantile parameter (\(q\)) and EMA decay (\(\beta\))?
- **Basis in paper:** \(\hat{m}(\tau)\) updated via EMA using a quantile of path/rebound scores from factual batches (Eq. 9). Treated as a hyperparameter; no analysis of \(q\), sensitivity, or convergence.
- **Why unresolved:** Quantile \(q\) sets the “acceptable” deviation boundary; wrong value could over‑penalize factuals or miss subtle hallucinations. EMA stability under non‑stationary score distributions is unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation varying \(q\) and \(\beta\) across seeds/datasets, tracking \(\hat{m}(\tau)\) variance and correlation with validation AUROC. Identify robust defaults or need for more principled margin setting.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the deviation‑based detection signal from DynHD be effectively combined with complementary hallucination signals (e.g., output‑based uncertainty or latent probes) to achieve further performance gains?
- **Basis in paper:** Experiments compare DynHD as standalone against other standalone baselines; no exploration of hybrid ensembles with, e.g., semantic entropy or CCS scores.
- **Why unresolved:** Different signals may capture orthogonal failure modes; fusion could improve robustness across hallucination types.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Simple combination: train a logistic regression on concatenated features of DynHD’s score and top baseline scores (TraceDet, Semantic Entropy). Significant AUROC improvement would indicate complementary value.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How does DynHD’s performance scale with denoising step count (\(T\)) and sequence length (\(l\)), and does its efficiency advantage hold for high‑resolution diffusion (large \(T\)) or longer generations?
- **Basis in paper:** Complexity O(\(T \cdot l\)) dwarfed by D‑LLM’s O(\(T \cdot l^2\)). Experiments use \(T\)=128/64, moderate \(l\). No evaluation on \(T\)>256 or \(l\)>512.
- **Why unresolved:** Constant factors of top‑k sorting and temporal attention may become non‑trivial at high \(T\)/\(l\). Detection efficacy might degrade: longer sequences could have more structural tokens challenging filtering; longer trajectories may exhibit more complex dynamics beyond the reference generator’s capacity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying \(T\) and \(l\) (e.g., 64,128,256,512) keeping model fixed, measuring both AUROC and added latency. Establish practical scalability limits and whether assumptions hold at higher resolutions.

## Limitations
- **Implementation Ambiguity:** Critical hyperparameters (MLP architectures, embedding dimensions) and exact \(I_{ignore}\) token lists are unspecified, risking reproduction fidelity.
- **Generalization Risk:** Hand‑crafted spatial filtering may not transfer to other D‑LLM tokenizers or generation setups (e.g., non‑English tasks).
- **Reference Generator Simplicity:** Using a shallow MLP for \(g_\theta\) may fail to capture complex, query‑specific dynamics, limiting cross‑dataset efficacy.

## Confidence
- **Spatial Filtering Mechanism:** High (supported by strong ablation results)
- **Learned Reference Trajectory Mechanism:** Medium (supported by cross‑dataset results but depends on unverified reference quality)
- **Deviation & Late Dynamics Mechanism:** High (backed by visual evidence and ablation of rebound loss)
