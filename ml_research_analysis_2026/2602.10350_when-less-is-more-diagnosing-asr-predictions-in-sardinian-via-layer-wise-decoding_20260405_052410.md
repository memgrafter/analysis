# When Less Is More? Diagnosing ASR Predictions in Sardinian via Layer-Wise Decoding

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10350
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10350
- **Reference count**: 30
- **Primary result**: Intermediate layers (Layer 22) of a pretrained Wav2Vec2 model achieve lower phoneme error rate (35.40%) than the final layer (36.73%) for low-resource Sardinian ASR, with 53 identified regressive errors where correct intermediate predictions degrade in the final layer.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether intermediate transformer layers in a pretrained speech model yield more accurate phoneme-level predictions than the final output layer for low-resource Sardinian automatic speech recognition (ASR). Using a Wav2Vec2 model, the authors applied layer-wise decoding by truncating the top encoder layers, decoding phoneme sequences from each remaining layer, and evaluating with phoneme error rate (PER). They found that an intermediate layer (Layer 22) produced the best PER (35.40%), outperforming the final layer (36.73%). Notably, intermediate layers reduced substitution errors and overgeneration. Fine-grained error analysis revealed 53 cases where correct predictions at intermediate layers turned into errors in the final layer, suggesting that deeper abstraction may discard phonetic details crucial for fine-grained discrimination. These results challenge the assumption that deeper layers always improve downstream performance and point to potential improvements for low-resource ASR by leveraging intermediate representations.

## Method Summary
The authors used a pretrained Wav2Vec2 base model (24 encoder layers) trained on multilingual speech. For each depth \(L\) from 1 to 24, they truncated the encoder after layer \(L\) and processed the same Sardinian test audio through the feature extractor and the first \(L\) layers. The resulting hidden states were passed through a linear projection to the phoneme vocabulary and decoded via CTC to produce phoneme sequences. These predictions were compared to gold phoneme transcriptions using PER. To identify regressive errors, they aligned the predictions from all layers and tracked which phonemes were correctly predicted at intermediate layers but became substitution or deletion errors at the final layer.

## Key Results
- Optimal PER at Layer 22 (35.40%) vs. final Layer 24 (36.73%).
- Intermediate layers around the optimum reduced substitution errors and instances of overgeneration (insertions).
- 53 regressive errors were identified: phonemes correctly predicted by intermediate layers became erroneous in the final layer output.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The superiority of intermediate layers is attributed to a hypothesized “over-abstraction” effect in deeper layers: as the transformer encoder progresses, it discards fine-grained phonetic details in favor of higher-level linguistic abstractions, which can harm phoneme-level discrimination for certain sounds. However, the study does not provide direct empirical evidence (e.g., probing classifiers or attention analysis) to confirm this mechanism.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Raw Audio -> CNN Feature Extractor -> Transformer Encoder (24 layers, truncated at L) -> Linear Projection -> CTC Decoder -> Phoneme Sequence.

**Critical path**: The main inference flow for a given layer depth \(L\) passes the input through the feature extractor and the first \(L\) encoder layers, then through a fixed linear classifier, and finally through CTC decoding to produce the phoneme prediction. The evaluation loop repeats this for each \(L\) and computes PER; a secondary path aligns predictions across layers to detect regressive errors.

**Design tradeoffs**: 
- Deeper layers provide more contextual abstraction but risk losing phonetic detail; shallower layers retain acoustic cues but may lack sufficient context for discrimination.
- Using a fixed pretrained model (without fine-tuning on Sardinian) leverages cross-lingual transfer but may not optimize for Sardinian phonology.
- Truncating layers reduces computational cost but might discard useful representations; the paper shows an optimal intermediate depth.

**Failure signatures**: 
- PER increases after a certain layer depth, indicating degraded phonetic information.
- Presence of many deletion or substitution errors that are not regressive may signal general model inadequacy.
- Regressive errors (correct intermediate to incorrect final) specifically point to over-abstraction in deeper layers.

**First experiments**:
1. Baseline evaluation with the full 24-layer Wav2Vec2 model to establish standard PER.
2. Systematic layer-wise truncation (L = 1,...,24) to measure PER as a function of depth and locate the optimum.
3. Fine-grained error alignment across layers to count regressive errors and characterize their phonetic properties.

## Limitations
- The study’s scope is extremely narrow, involving only the Wav2Vec2 architecture and a single low-resource language (Sardinian); results may not generalize to other models or languages.
- The proposed “over-abstraction” mechanism for regressive errors is not empirically validated (e.g., via probing or attention visualization).
- External validation is lacking: the work has received no citations and only a few loosely related papers, suggesting it remains an underexplored niche.

## Confidence
- **Intermediate layers yield lower PER than final layer**: Medium (directly supported by PER comparison, but limited to one model/language).
- **53 regressive errors exist**: High (fine-grained observational count from error alignment).
- **Findings generalize to other low-resource ASR setups**: Low (no cross-model or cross-linguistic evidence).

## Next Checks
- Replicate the layer-wise decoding protocol on two other pretrained speech models (e.g., HuBERT, Whisper) using the same Sardinian corpus to test consistency of the intermediate-layer advantage.
- Conduct the same analysis on two additional low-resource languages from different families (e.g., Basque, Navajo) to determine whether optimal layer depth correlates with phonological complexity or data scarcity.
- For the 53 regressive errors, perform phonetic feature clustering (e.g., manner/place of articulation, vowel height) to identify whether specific sound classes are disproportionately affected by final-layer degradation.
