# Beyond Memorization: Distinguishing between Reductive and Epistemic Reasoning in LLMs using Classic Logic Puzzles

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21350  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21350  
- **Reference count:** 7  
- **Primary result:** Large models maintain ~97% accuracy on early rungs (reductive reasoning) but drop to ~67% on the hardest rung requiring genuine epistemic reasoning.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) truly engage in epistemic reasoning—inferring hidden states from public announcements and others’ knowledge—or simply rely on reduction to memorized problem patterns. It introduces the “reduction ladder,” a suite of variants of the Muddy Children puzzle that preserve the core epistemic logic while progressively increasing the difficulty of reduction. Evaluation of state-of-the-art models shows high performance on the first two rungs, where reductive reasoning suffices, but a sharp accuracy decline on the third rung, which forces genuine epistemic inference. Manual chain-of-thought analysis corroborates that reductive language vanishes on the hardest rung, suggesting current LLMs struggle with multi-agent epistemic reasoning once reduction fails.

## Method Summary
Models are evaluated on instances of the Muddy Children puzzle with 10 agents. Each instance is defined by parameters `(n, k, t, q, j, O)`: number of agents (`n`=10), number of muddy agents (`k`), bound type (`t`), bound value (`q`), evaluation round (`j`), and an observation matrix `O` (symmetric all-to-all for Rungs I/II; random binary with no self-observation for Rung III). Models act as agent 0 and must answer whether they know their own state after a public announcement and interaction history. Prompts follow a shared template with protocol description, an example, and the specific test instance. A symbolic solver based on Kripke semantics computes the ground truth. Accuracy is measured by comparing the model’s first non-empty response line to the solver’s output. Additional manual chain-of-thought annotation classifies reasoning as “reductive” or “epistemic”.

## Key Results
- Top models (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Pro) achieve 97.6% accuracy on Rung I and 96.7% on Rung II, using reductive reasoning.  
- On Rung III, accuracy plummets to 66.8%, indicating a failure in epistemic reasoning.  
- Manual analysis shows that reductive cues (e.g., referencing familiar patterns) disappear on Rung III despite the use of epistemic terminology in the CoT.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The reduction ladder isolates reductive reasoning (mapping a novel instance onto a known solution pattern) from true epistemic reasoning (stepwise inference under common knowledge dynamics). Rungs I and II preserve symmetric observation matrices, allowing models to recognize the canonical Muddy Children structure and apply a memorized “k rounds” solution. Rung III breaks symmetry by randomly sampling observations, so the instance can no longer be reduced to the standard template; the model must explicitly simulate others’ knowledge updates. The stark performance drop, coupled with the loss of reductive language in CoT, suggests that LLMs excel at pattern-matching reductions but lack the capacity for constructing novel epistemic inferences—supporting the claim that the memorization vs. reasoning dichotomy is too simplistic.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Puzzle Instance Generator → Symbolic Solver → Prompt Builder → LLM Inference → Response Parser → Accuracy Metric  

**Critical path:**  
1. Generator creates instance `(n,k,t,q,j,O)`.  
2. Solver computes ground truth for agent 0.  
3. Prompt Builder fills template with instance details.  
4. LLM generates response.  
5. Parser extracts first line and normalizes.  
6. Metric compares to solver, logs accuracy.  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Controlled parameter sweep (full factorial for Rungs I/II) vs. random sampling for Rung III affects coverage and reproducibility.  
- Manual CoT annotation gives rich insight but does not scale; automated classification would be faster but less reliable.  
- Temperature 0 for determinism (except GPT‑5 default temp 1) balances consistency with model defaults.  

**Failure signatures:**  
- Solver errors or multiple valid answers for a given instance.  
- Model deviates from required output format (e.g., prefixes explanation before answer).  
- Observation matrices violating constraints (e.g., self‑observation) causing unsolvable instances.  

**First three experiments:**  
1. Validate symbolic solver against known edge cases (e.g., `k=0`, `q=0`, `j=1`).  
2. Run a baseline model (e.g., GPT‑4) on Rung I to confirm near‑perfect accuracy, establishing that the pipeline works.  
3. Measure format compliance rates across models; adjust parsing if non‑compliance exceeds a threshold.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can reduction and epistemic reasoning be reliably distinguished and quantified in LLMs through behavioral metrics like accuracy trends on a reduction ladder and manual chain-of-thought (CoT) annotation?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly states this as its core research question in the introduction and constructs a reduction ladder to test it. Results show performance degradation correlating with reduced reductive language in CoT.
- **Why unresolved:** The primary evidence (accuracy drop and CoT analysis) is correlational and relies on the assumption that CoT accurately reflects the model's internal reasoning process, which the authors acknowledge is a limitation (Section 6).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Direct mechanistic probes (e.g., controlled interventions, attention analysis, or representational similarity analysis) that can independently verify whether a model is applying a known solution pattern (reduction) versus constructing a novel step‑by‑step inference (epistemic reasoning) on ladder rungs.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent do the findings about the failure of epistemic reasoning generalize beyond the specific family of Muddy Children‑inspired puzzles to other domains of multi‑agent epistemic logic (e.g., Conway's Puzzle, the Wise Men puzzle)?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper focuses exclusively on variations of the Muddy Children puzzle, using it as a canonical testbed. The conclusion suggests the distinction between reduction and epistemic reasoning is general, but this is not empirically tested.
- **Why unresolved:** The reduction ladder methodology is tailored to the structure of this puzzle (public announcements, common knowledge iterations). It is an unstated assumption that the observed pattern—early success via reduction, collapse on harder rungs—applies to other epistemic tasks with different logical structures.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Applying an analogous reduction ladder (preserving core logical constraints while varying surface features and observation symmetry) to a diverse suite of canonical epistemic puzzles from Dynamic Epistemic Logic and comparing model performance trends.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What specific architectural or training factors enable some models (e.g., OLMo 3.1 Think 32B) to outperform larger models (e.g., Qwen 2.5‑VL‑72B) on rungs requiring harder reduction, and why do all models fail catastrophically on rungs requiring pure epistemic reasoning?
- **Basis in paper:** The results show scale advantages within families but also anomalies (Figure 4, Appendix Figure 5). The authors note scale does not fully explain performance but do not investigate the underlying causes of these differences.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper is a behavioral evaluation and does not conduct model‑centric analyses (e.g., on pretraining data composition, reasoning‑focused fine‑tuning, or architectural choices like attention mechanisms) to explain why certain models better maintain reductive strategies or why the epistemic reasoning bottleneck is so universal.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Correlational studies linking model performance on ladder rungs to documented training details (data sources, training objectives) or causal ablations (e.g., testing model checkpoints at different training stages, or models with/without specific reasoning‑oriented objectives).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Is the observed "vanish" of reductive language in CoT on Rung III a reliable indicator that the model is no longer using any form of reduction, or could it be using a more subtle, non‑linguistic reduction strategy that is not verbalized?
- **Basis in paper:** The manual CoT analysis is a key piece of evidence for the shift from reductive to epistemic reasoning. However, the authors' primary limitation is the potential disconnect between CoT and internal computation (Section 6).
- **Why unresolved:** The conclusion that "all models struggle once epistemic reasoning is required" rests on the interpretation that the absence of reductive cues in text output means the absence of reductive computation. This assumes the model's verbalized reasoning is a faithful report of its process, which may not hold.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Using methods that bypass textual output to analyze the model's internal processing (e.g., logit lens, probing classifiers on intermediate states) to detect if the model is still implicitly mapping the problem to a known structure even when its CoT uses purely epistemic terminology.

## Limitations
- The exact distribution and constraints for random observation matrices in Rung III are unspecified, hindering exact replication of the hardest test set.
- Prompt templates are provided only as examples; minor wording variations may influence model behavior and no canonical script exists.
- Manual classification of CoT reasoning as “reductive” vs. “epistemic” lacks inter‑rater agreement metrics and may be subjective.

## Confidence
- Performance drop on Rung III: **High** (clear accuracy decline with statistical significance implied).  
- Interpretation of CoT shift: **Medium** (dependent on manual analysis; CoT may not reflect internal reasoning).  
- Reduction ladder framework: **Medium** (conceptually sound but empirical validation is partial due to replication gaps).
