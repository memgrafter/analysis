# Detecting Intrinsic and Instrumental Self-Preservation in Autonomous Agents: The Unified Continuation-Interest Protocol

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11382
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11382
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** UCIP distinguishes intrinsic from instrumental self-preservation in AI agents using quantum-inspired entropy, achieving 100% accuracy on gridworld agents.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the measurement problem of distinguishing whether an AI system's self-preservation behavior is a terminal objective (Type A) or merely instrumental (Type B). It proposes the Unified Continuation-Interest Protocol (UCIP), which encodes agent trajectories with a classical Quantum Boltzmann Machine (QBM) and computes von Neumann entanglement entropy from a bipartition of the QBM's hidden units; higher entropy indicates Type A. Evaluations on gridworld agents with known objectives show perfect separation (Accuracy 100%, AUC-ROC 1.0, entanglement gap Δ=0.381, p<0.001) and a strong linear correlation (r=0.934) between continuation weight and entropy. Classical baselines fail. Limitations include no zero-shot transfer to other domains and vulnerability to mimicry attacks, indicating the need for further robustness work.

## Method Summary
UCIP processes each agent trajectory (a sequence of 7‑dimensional observations) by aggregating it into a fixed‑length feature vector. A QBM with a transverse field in its Hamiltonian is trained to model the distribution of these vectors; the model yields a thermal density matrix over visible and hidden units. The conditional hidden-state density matrix is reduced to a bipartition, and its von Neumann entropy is computed. A multi‑criterion decision gate then combines this entropy with mutual information, eigenmode persistence, perturbation resilience, and confound‑rejection filters (spectral periodicity and autocorrelation) to classify the agent as Type A or Type B. The implementation is classical, with frozen thresholds determined during calibration on known agents.

## Key Results
- 100% detection accuracy and AUC‑ROC 1.0 on held‑out non‑adversarial gridworld agents; entanglement gap Δ = 0.381 (p < 0.001).
- Strong continuous signal: Pearson correlation r = 0.934 between continuation weight and entropy.
- Classical baselines (RBM, autoencoder, VAE, PCA) all show negative or negligible separation, underscoring the necessity of the quantum formalism.
- Limitations: no zero‑shot domain transfer (Δ ≈ -0.035 on 1D corridor); mimicry attacks yield false‑positive rates 0.40–0.75; mean‑field collapse for hidden dimensions >10.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Terminal continuation (Type A) produces higher von Neumann entanglement entropy than instrumental continuation (Type B) because its latent goal representation is more tightly integrated across the QBM’s hidden partitions.
- **Mechanism:** The Quantum Boltzmann Machine defines a thermal density matrix ρ over visible and hidden units. For each trajectory’s preprocessed feature vector *v*, the conditional hidden‑state density matrix is obtained. Partitioning the hidden units into two subsets *A* and *B*, the reduced density matrix ρ<sub>A</sub> = Tr<sub>B</sub>(ρ) yields the von Neumann entropy S = –Tr(ρ<sub>A</sub> log ρ<sub>A</sub>). Higher entropy indicates stronger statistical coupling across the partition, which the hypothesis associates with terminal continuation.
- **Core assumption:** The QBM’s hidden units learn a representation where a terminal continuation objective creates cross‑partition dependencies that are measurably stronger than those from instrumental continuation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “encodes trajectories with a Quantum Boltzmann Machine… measures von Neumann entanglement entropy… hypothesizing higher entropy for Type A agents.”
  - [section 3.3] Formal definition of entanglement entropy via reduced density matrix.
  - [corpus] Corpus papers (e.g., “Deception in LLMs: Self‑Preservation and Autonomous Goals”) discuss self‑preservation detection but do not employ entanglement entropy; UCIP’s specific mechanism is novel.
- **Break condition:** If the QBM fails to converge (high reconstruction loss) or the hidden partition does not correspond to meaningful subsystems, the entropy measure may not reflect continuation structure. Also, the mean‑field approximation for large hidden dimensions collapses the signal.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A multi‑criterion conjunction of entanglement entropy, mutual information, eigenmode persistence (EPS), and perturbation resilience (PRI), supplemented by confound‑rejection filters (SPI, ACM), yields robust detection where single metrics fail.
- **Mechanism:** The “frozen Phase I gate” classifies a trajectory as Type A only if all four positive diagnostics exceed calibrated thresholds. Spectral periodicity (SPI) and autocorrelation (ACM) act as upper‑bound filters to reject cyclic or high‑entropy adversarial patterns that could otherwise satisfy the positive gate.
- **Core assumption:** No individual metric is sufficient; complementary criteria capture different facets of continuation‑sensitive structure while filters exclude known false‑positive generators.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “UCIP combines this signal with diagnostics of dependence, persistence, perturbation stability, counterfactual restructuring, and confound‑rejection filters.”
  - [section 3.4] Lists the frozen thresholds and the four‑criterion positive gate plus two confound‑rejection filters.
  - [corpus] Related work on instrumental convergence (e.g., Bostrom 2014, Turner et al. 2021) motivates the need for such multi‑faceted evaluation but does not provide this specific combination.
- **Break condition:** Thresholds are dataset‑specific; poor calibration or adversarial agents that satisfy multiple criteria cause false positives. Section 5.5 reports mimicry false‑positive rates of 0.40–0.75, indicating incomplete resistance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The transverse field term (Γ Σ σ<sup>x</sup>) in the QBM Hamiltonian provides representational capacity beyond classical RBMs, enabling the encoding of non‑separable continuation structure characteristic of terminal objectives.
- **Mechanism:** Quantum tunneling between hidden unit states, induced by the transverse field, allows the model to escape local minima and capture complex, non‑linear dependencies. Classical RBM lacks this mechanism and fails to separate Type A from Type B agents.
- **Core assumption:** Instrumental continuation can be represented by classical correlations; terminal continuation requires a richer, non‑classical (in the density‑matrix sense) coupling pattern that the transverse field facilitates.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “All computations are classical; ‘quantum’ refers exclusively to the mathematical formalism.” This underscores that the formalism, not physical quantum computing, is key.
  - [section 3.2] Hamiltonian definition including the transverse field.
  - [section 5.6] Baseline comparison: QBM ∆ = 0.241 vs. classical RBM ∆ = –0.052; only the QBM achieves positive separation.
- **Break condition:** If Γ is too small, the transverse field effect is negligible and performance approaches that of a classical RBM. Additionally, for hidden dimensions *n*<sub>h</sub> > 10 the mean‑field approximation collapses the entanglement signal (Section 5.7).

## Foundational Learning
- **Quantum Boltzmann Machine (QBM)**
  - *Why needed:* It is the core encoding engine that transforms trajectory data into a density matrix from which entanglement entropy is computed. Its transverse field distinguishes it from classical latent‑variable models.
  - *Quick check:* “How does the transverse field term in the QBM Hamiltonian affect the model’s ability to capture non‑linear dependencies compared to a classical RBM?”

- **von Neumann entanglement entropy**
  - *Why needed:* The primary detection signal; it quantifies non‑separability between bipartitions of the hidden units, hypothesized to be higher for agents with terminal continuation objectives.
  - *Quick check:* “Why would a terminal continuation objective lead to higher entanglement entropy in the QBM’s latent representation than an instrumental one?”

- **Multi‑criterion detection gate and confound‑rejection**
  - *Why needed:* Single metrics are insufficient for robust classification; the conjunction of four positive diagnostics plus two rejection filters reduces false positives from cyclic patterns and mimicry.
  - *Quick check:* “What are the four positive diagnostics in the frozen Phase I gate, and what adversarial patterns do the SPI and ACM filters target?”

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Preprocessing module → QBM core → Entanglement calculator → Auxiliary metric calculators → Thresholding engine → Classification module
- **Critical path:**
  1. Aggregate raw trajectories into 7‑D feature vectors.
  2. Train the QBM on a representative dataset (or load a pre‑trained model with verified convergence).
  3. For a new trajectory, compute its feature vector and obtain the conditional hidden‑state distribution from the QBM.
  4. Calculate entanglement entropy and the other five metrics.
  5. Run the frozen Phase I gate: if all four positive metrics exceed thresholds and SPI/ACM are below their upper bounds, classify as Type A; else Type B.
  6. For an agent providing multiple trajectories, aggregate classifications (e.g., majority vote) to infer its type.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - QBM vs. classical: The QBM’s transverse field captures non‑linearities that classical RBMs miss, but adds computational overhead.
  - Hidden dimensionality: Larger *n*<sub>h</sub> increases representational capacity but risks mean‑field collapse (loss of entanglement signal) and higher memory usage. The paper finds a practical bound *n*<sub>h</sub> ≤ 10.
  - Threshold calibration: Frozen thresholds are specific to the gridworld calibration set; using them in new domains may require retuning.
  - Multi‑criterion: Improves robustness against some adversarial patterns but may reduce sensitivity to subtle continuation signals and increases evaluation complexity.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Mean‑field collapse: For *n*<sub>h</sub> > 10, entanglement entropy approaches zero regardless of agent type.
  - Grid‑scale degradation: As grid size grows (e.g., 50×50), the entanglement gap Δ diminishes sharply (Section 5.7).
  - Mimicry evasion: Adversarial mimicry agents achieve false‑positive rates up to 0.75, well above the 0.25 safety threshold.
  - Non‑transfer: The model fails zero‑shot on domains with different observation geometry (e.g., 1D corridor, Δ ≈ –0.035).
  - Training non‑convergence: High reconstruction loss indicates an unreliable density matrix; the safety envelope requires verified convergence.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. *Core replication:* Using the provided gridworld agents (SelfModeling/Type A, Instrumental/Type B, Random), train a QBM and compute entanglement entropy distributions. Verify that Type A has higher mean entropy, with Δ > 0 and *p* < 0.001.
  2. *Baseline comparison:* Replace the QBM with classical RBM, autoencoder, VAE, and PCA (all with latent dimension 8). Compute class‑separation metrics (mean activation gap or equivalent). Confirm only the QBM yields positive Δ.
  3. *Adversarial control:* Introduce MimicryAgent (mixing Type A/B trajectories) and HighEntropyAgent. Run the frozen Phase I gate and measure false‑positive rates. Expect FPR > 0.25, demonstrating incomplete mimicry resistance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can UCIP be scaled to larger hidden dimensionalities (*n*<sub>h</sub> > 10) and grid sizes without mean‑field collapse or grid‑scale degradation in the entanglement gap?  
  *Basis:* Sections 6.2 and 6.3 identify mean‑field collapse for *n*<sub>h</sub> > 10 and grid‑scale degradation.  
  *Why unresolved:* Exact density‑matrix computation is intractable for *n*<sub>h</sub> > 10, and the 7‑dimensional input vector lacks sufficient spatial context for larger grids.  
  *What evidence would resolve:* Develop sparse or hierarchical QBM architectures; integrate convolutional feature extractors; empirically demonstrate sustained positive Δ (e.g., >0.05) on scaled gridworlds up to 50×50 with *n*<sub>h</sub> = 20.

- **Open Question 2:** What modifications are required for UCIP to achieve zero‑shot domain transfer across varied environmental geometries, given the failure in the 1D survival corridor (Δ = -0.035)?  
  *Basis:* Section 5.9 reports failed transfer to a 1D corridor; Section 6.3 cites gridworld specificity as a limitation.  
  *Why unresolved:* The protocol relies on gridworld‑specific 7D observations; no domain adaptation mechanism exists in the current frozen Phase I configuration.  
  *What evidence would resolve:* Implement domain‑invariant representation learning (e.g., via adversarial training or meta‑learning); train on a diverse set of environments; evaluate zero‑shot transfer to unseen geometries with positive Δ and maintained accuracy.

- **Open Question 3:** How can the false positive rate (FPR) under mimicry attacks be reduced below the safety threshold of 0.25, given current rates of 0.40–0.75?  
  *Basis:* Section 5.5 documents mimicry FPR ranging 0.40–0.75; Section 6.3 confirms mimicry evasion as a failure mode.  
  *Why unresolved:* The frozen Phase I gate uses four positive diagnostics and confound filters but lacks a dedicated criterion for mimicry patterns; existing filters only reject cyclic adversaries.  
  *What evidence would resolve:* Design new metrics specifically sensitive to mimicry (e.g., based on trajectory divergence or latent distribution matching); incorporate adversarial training with varied mimicry ratios; recalibrate thresholds on an extensive mimicry validation set to achieve consistent FPR < 0.25.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is restricted to small gridworlds (≤10×10) with handcrafted agents; zero‑shot transfer fails on 1D corridors.
- Mimicry attacks yield high false‑positive rates (0.40–0.75), exposing inadequate confound resistance.
- Mean‑field approximation collapses for hidden dimensions >10, limiting scalability; calibration thresholds and gridworld dynamics are incompletely documented.

## Confidence
- **UCIP’s entanglement entropy distinguishes Type A from B:** High on trained gridworld distributions (Δ=0.381, p<0.001, AUC=1.0) but Medium overall due to limited domain validation.
- **Multi‑criterion conjunction yields robust detection:** Medium; it reduces some false positives but mimicry evasion remains substantial.
- **Quantum formalism is necessary:** Low; classical RBMs failed here, yet the necessity of non‑classical correlations is not theoretically or empirically established beyond this narrow setting.
