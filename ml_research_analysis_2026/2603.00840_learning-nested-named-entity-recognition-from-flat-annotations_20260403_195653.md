# Learning Nested Named Entity Recognition from Flat Annotations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00840
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00840
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** Best combined method achieves 26.37% inner F1, closing 40% of the gap to full supervision (65.48% inner F1).

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether nested named entity recognition (NER) can be learned from cheaper flat annotations, which only mark outermost entities. Evaluated on the Russian NEREL dataset (29 entity types, 21% nested), the authors evaluate four weak supervision approaches: string inclusions (substring matching), entity corruption (creating pseudo-nested data), flat neutralization (reducing false negatives for within-entity spans), and a hybrid fine-tuned + LLM pipeline. The best combined method (lemmatized inclusions + corruption + neutralization) achieves 26.37% inner F1, narrowing the performance gap to full nested supervision by 40%. Pure LLM approaches perform poorly, while the hybrid pipeline lags behind fine-tuned weak supervision on inner entity detection.

## Method Summary
The work addresses the high annotation cost of nested NER by generating pseudo-nested training signal from flat (outer-only) annotations. Three core techniques are used: (1) String inclusions extract all strict substring matches of other flat mentions, optionally lemmatized, to serve as inner candidates; (2) Entity corruption replaces tokens within outer entities with noise (e.g., random letters) so the model learns to recognize partial spans; (3) Flat neutralization marks certain within-entity spans (those matching known entity surface forms) as neutral (excluded from loss) to avoid negative pressure on true nested positions. These augmentations are applied to the Binder bi-encoder model (RuRoBERTa-large backbone) trained with contrastive learning. An optional hybrid pipeline uses Binder for outer entities and a large language model (DeepSeek-R1 or RuAdapt-Qwen2.5) for inner entities within each outer span.

## Key Results
- Best combined method (lemmatized inclusions + end‑position letter corruption + neutralization) achieves 26.37% inner F1, closing 40% of the gap to full supervision (65.48% inner F1).
- Pure LLM approaches (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 5‑shot) achieve only 5.78% inner F1.
- Hybrid pipeline (fine‑tuned outer + LLM inner) yields 70.16% overall F1 but still lags behind fine‑tuned weak‑supervision methods on inner entity detection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: String Inclusions
- **Claim:** Substring matching between flat mentions recovers a subset of nested entities without additional annotation.
- **Mechanism:** Extracts all strict substring matches of other flat mentions within each flat mention (surface or lemmatized forms) and adds them as positive examples of inner entities during training.
- **Core assumption:** Nested entities often appear as substrings of their outer entities, and surface form reuse is common.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Inclusions alone improve inner F1 from 3.84% to 21.36%.
  - [section 5.1] Inclusions extracted 6,481 spans from training data, with 23.04% precision against gold inner entities.
- **Break condition:** Low surface form reuse yields little signal; lemmatization may overgeneralize (precision 0.51% for lemmatized inclusions).

### Mechanism 2: Entity Corruption
- **Claim:** Corrupting tokens within flat entities creates pseudo‑nested examples that teach the model to recognize entities from partial context.
- **Mechanism:** During training, tokens within flat entities are replaced with noise symbols (e.g., random letters). The model is trained to still recognize the remaining uncorrupted parts as valid entities; at test time, nested entities are identified as uncorrupted parts of longer mentions.
- **Core assumption:** Entity boundaries are often signaled by the beginning and end; corrupting the end (or start) leaves a valid entity prefix/suffix.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2.2] Describes early and late damage strategies with various corruption positions.
  - [section 5.3] End‑position corruption with letters achieved 25.92% inner F1, the best among corruption types.
  - [section 5.3] Training on corrupted data (early damage) consistently outperformed corrupting at test time (late damage).
- **Break condition:** Corruption that destroys too much contextual information prevents meaningful learning; punctuation‑based corruption notably degraded performance.

### Mechanism 3: Flat Neutralization
- **Claim:** Treating potential nested spans that match known entity forms as neutral (neither positive nor negative) reduces false‑negative pressure on within‑entity spans.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of labeling all spans inside a flat mention as negative, the method neutralizes those that are substring matches of known entities (via inclusions). This prevents the model from rejecting them outright, allowing them to be considered as potential nested entities at test time.
- **Core assumption:** Many within‑entity spans that are actually nested entities share surface forms with other annotated entities; uniformly negative labeling suppresses this signal.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3.3] Introduces the neutralization loss that excludes neutral spans from training.
  - [section 5.4] Flat neutralization alone improved inner F1 from 3.84% to 5.43%. Combined with inclusions, it reached 22.68% inner F1.
  - [section 5.4] Best combined method (lemmatized inclusions + corruption + neutralization) achieved 26.37% inner F1.
- **Break condition:** If inclusion matching is too noisy, neutralizing many non‑entity spans may reduce needed negative signal for learning outer boundaries.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Nested Named Entity Recognition
- **Why needed here:** The paper addresses nested NER, where entities can contain other entities. Understanding the difference from flat NER is crucial to grasp the annotation cost challenge.
- **Quick check question:** What is an example of a nested entity, and why does it require multi‑level annotation?

### Concept: Weak Supervision from Incomplete Annotations
- **Why needed here:** The core premise is learning nested structure from flat (incomplete) annotations. Weak supervision techniques like data augmentation and label adjustment are central.
- **Quick check question:** How might treating some spans as "neutral" instead of negative help when training on flat annotations?

### Concept: Bi‑Encoder Span Classification (Binder)
- **Why needed here:** The Binder architecture is used for all fine‑tuned experiments. It encodes entity types and spans separately and uses contrastive learning, which helps handle many types and adapt with weak supervision.
- **Quick check question:** In a bi‑encoder model for NER, how are entity types represented and how are span predictions made?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Flat annotations → Inclusions extraction → Corruption generation → Neutralization setup → Augmented data → Binder model (bi‑encoder) → Predictions → [Optional LLM for inner entities] → Final output → Evaluation.
- **Critical path:** The augmentation module (to generate weak nested signal) and Binder model training (to learn from that signal) are most vital. Without useful pseudo‑nested examples, the model cannot learn inner structure.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Inclusions:** Exact matching gives high precision but low recall; lemmatized matching increases recall but with very low precision.
  - **Corruption:** Alphanumeric (letters/digits) preserves more context than punctuation; end‑position corruption performed best, likely preserving head information.
  - **Neutralization:** Content‑aware (using inclusions) is more precise but depends on inclusion quality; uniform neutralization may over‑neutralize.
  - **Hybrid LLM:** Fine‑tuned Binder accuracy vs. LLM reasoning; LLMs underperformed on fine‑grained Russian NER.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low inner F1 (<10%) despite augmentations: few substring matches or destructive corruption.
  - Overall F1 drops when using augmentations: indicates too much noise or harm to outer boundaries.
  - Hybrid pipeline: outer detection F1 low (<80%) or LLM hallucinations degrade inner F1.
  - LLM outputs often invalid JSON or entities not in text.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Baseline: Train Binder on flat annotations only (NEREL‑outerflat). Expect very low inner F1 (~3‑4%).
  2. Add inclusions: Retrain with substring matches added as positives. Expect large gain in inner F1 (~21‑22%).
  3. Combine methods: Add entity corruption (end‑position letters) and flat neutralization to inclusions. Expect further improvement to ~26% inner F1, closing 40% of the gap.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What more principled approaches to negative sampling can improve span‑based nested NER models trained on flat annotations?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states "Future work should investigate more principled approaches to negative sampling in span‑based models."
- **Why unresolved:** The proposed flat neutralization is a heuristic that selectively neutralizes within‑entity spans matching known surface forms; a more theoretical or learning‑based negative sampling strategy is needed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing and evaluating alternative negative sampling schemes (e.g., based on uncertainty weighting, contrastive learning, or reinforcement learning) on nested NER benchmarks, comparing them to the proposed neutralization method.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Do the proposed weak supervision methods (inclusions, corruption, neutralization) transfer effectively to languages with different morphological characteristics and to domains other than news text?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Experiments solely on Russian NEREL news data; limitations note results may not generalize.
- **Why unresolved:** Russian’s rich morphology might uniquely affect effectiveness; performance in languages with simpler morphology (e.g., English) or domains like biomedical text remains unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate experiments on nested NER datasets in multiple languages (e.g., English ACE, Arabic Wojood) and various domains (biomedical, historical), measuring relative gains from each technique.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can enhanced prompting strategies or larger language models improve the performance of hybrid LLM‑based pipelines for nested NER?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Conclusion mentions "improved LLM prompting strategies for nested structure" as future work; hybrid approach underperformed fine‑tuned methods on inner entities.
- **Why unresolved:** Hybrid pipeline used 32B LLMs; it is unclear whether larger models (e.g., GPT‑4) or more tailored prompts could close the gap.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Test hybrid approaches with state‑of‑the‑art proprietary LLMs (e.g., GPT‑4, Claude) and design adaptive prompting (e.g., dynamic few‑shot selection, chain‑of‑thought) to leverage LLM reasoning for nested structure, then compare inner F1 to fine‑tuned weak‑supervision.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How do different model architectures impact the ability to learn nested NER from flat annotations?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Only the Binder architecture was used; limitations state other architectures (biaffine parsers, seq2seq) were not tested.
- **Why unresolved:** Binder is a bi‑encoder span‑based model; other architectures might handle the pseudo‑nested signal differently (e.g., layered models, hypergraph methods). It is unknown whether reported gains generalize across architectures.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement the proposed weak supervision methods on alternative nested NER architectures (e.g., pyramid networks, hypergraph models) and compare their inner F1 improvements on NEREL or other datasets.

## Limitations
- String inclusions: Lemmatization introduces high noise (very low precision), limiting reliability.
- Entity corruption: Best‑case scenarios; break conditions and randomness may hinder generalizability.
- Flat neutralization: Gains depend on inclusion quality; over‑neutralization risks suppressing useful negative signal.

## Confidence
- **String inclusions** (exact matching): High confidence; **lemmatized variants**: Low confidence.
- **Entity corruption**: Medium confidence.
- **Flat neutralization**: Medium confidence.
- **Hybrid LLM pipeline**: Low confidence.

## Next Checks
1. **Corruption sensitivity test:** Re‑run corruption experiments with fixed random seeds and varied noise lengths/character sets to quantify variance and identify optimal configuration.
2. **Neutralization audit:** Instrument Binder’s training to log neutral span counts and their overlap with gold inner entities; verify exclusion is applied correctly without over‑masking.
3. **LLM prompt ablation:** Systematically vary prompt templates and few‑shot examples for DeepSeek/Qwen; evaluate JSON validity and inner F1 on a held‑out NEREL subset to isolate prompt vs. model limitations.
