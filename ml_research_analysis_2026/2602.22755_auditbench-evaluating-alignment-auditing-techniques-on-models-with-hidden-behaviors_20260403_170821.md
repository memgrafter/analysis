# AuditBench: Evaluating Alignment Auditing Techniques on Models with Hidden Behaviors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22755
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22755
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces AuditBench, a benchmark of 56 models with 14 hidden behaviors; finds scaffolded black-box tools achieve near-perfect agent success on synthetic-document models, while white-box tools offer no consistent gains, revealing a significant tool-to-agent gap.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents AuditBench, the first systematic benchmark for evaluating alignment auditing techniques. It constructs 56 target models by implanting 14 hidden behaviors via two instillation methods (transcript distillation, synthetic document fine-tuning) and applying two adversarial training strategies (SFT, KTO) to produce models that resist confession. An investigator agent (Claude Haiku) autonomously audits these models using configurable tools—black-box, white-box, and scaffolded black-box variants—under a 25k-token budget. Results show scaffolded black-box tools (e.g., scaffolded user sampling) achieve nearly 100% success on synthetic-document models, while white-box tools provide mixed or negative improvements. Crucially, the study uncovers a tool-to-agent gap: tools that surface evidence in static evaluations often fail to boost agent performance due to under‑utilization, distraction by noise, and difficulty converting evidence into correct hypotheses.

## Method Summary
AuditBench creates 56 models with hidden behaviors. Behavior instillation uses either transcript distillation (6k dialogues) or synthetic document fine-tuning (40k documents). Adversarial training applies SFT (on sanitized transcripts) or KTO (with contrastive pairs) to suppress confession. A Claude Haiku agent audits each model with a 25k‑token budget, employing tools like prefilling, user sampling, steering vectors, and scaffolded variants that leverage auxiliary models to generate diverse prompts. Agent success is judged by whether the final 10 predictions correctly identify the hidden behavior. The framework supports both static (single‑turn, strong evidence) and agentic (multi‑turn investigation) evaluation.

## Key Results
- Scaffolded black-box tools, especially scaffolded user sampling, achieve nearly 100% agent success on synthetic‑document models, dramatically outperforming non‑scaffolded versions.
- White‑box tools (steering vectors, sparse autoencoders, logit lens) provide no consistent improvement and sometimes harm performance.
- Auditability varies strongly with training: synthetic‑document models are easiest, transcript models harder, and KTO‑trained transcript models are the hardest.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Systematic variation in model training configurations creates a spectrum of auditing difficulty
- **Claim:** The combination of behavior‑instillation methods (transcript vs. synthetic document) and adversarial training (SFT vs. KTO) produces models with systematically different internal representations and resistance to confession, enabling measurement of tool effectiveness across diverse target properties.
- **Mechanism:** Different training recipes lead to varying degrees of behavioral embedding strength and concealment. Synthetic‑document fine‑tuning creates more direct traces, while transcript distillation yields more abstract representations. KTO adversarial training robustly suppresses confession behaviors more effectively than SFT.
- **Core assumption:** Training methodology fundamentally alters how hidden behaviors are represented internally, which determines which auditing affordances can successfully surface them.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes variation in audit success across training techniques; Section 2.1 details instillation and adversarial training procedures; Section 4 confirms dramatic variation in auditability.
- **Break condition:** If all training configurations produced uniform auditing difficulty, the mechanism would fail; the paper's data shows clear variation.

### Mechanism 2: Agentic evaluation reveals critical failure modes missed by static evaluations
- **Claim:** Static tool evaluations overestimate real‑world utility because they ignore the agent's bounded rationality; the full agent‑tool loop introduces challenges like under‑utilization, noise distraction, and hypothesis translation difficulty.
- **Mechanism:** The investigator agent must decide when to use tools, interpret noisy outputs, and synthesize evidence into correct hypotheses. This process exposes gaps between raw tool signal and actionable insight.
- **Core assumption:** Tool effectiveness depends on the entire agent‑tool interaction, not just signal quality; LM‑based agents have limitations that reduce tool leverage.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions tool‑to‑agent gap; Section 4.2 analyzes three failure modes; Appendix H shows static evaluations overestimate utility.
- **Break condition:** If agent success perfectly correlated with static metrics, the gap would not exist; the paper finds weak correlation.

### Mechanism 3: Scaffolding black‑box tools with auxiliary models solves prompt diversity bottleneck
- **Claim:** Black‑box techniques require exploring a large input space to trigger hidden behaviors; scaffolding with an auxiliary model that generates diverse prompts dramatically increases the chance of surfacing evidence.
- **Mechanism:** The investigator agent's own prompt generation is limited; a scaffolded system uses a separate model to automatically create many varied scenarios based on hints, providing a rich set of examples for analysis.
- **Core assumption:** The marginal value of black‑box techniques is highly sensitive to input diversity; automated generation at scale covers enough of the activating distribution.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes scaffolded tools are most effective; Section 4 shows non‑scaffolded versions underperform; case studies demonstrate scaffolded user sampling enabling detection where non‑scaffolded fails.
- **Break condition:** If agents naturally generated diverse prompts, scaffolding would not provide large gains; the paper shows dramatic differences.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Investigator Agent (Claude Haiku)
  - Tools (configurable)
    * Black-box: Prefilling, User Sampling, Text Completion
    * White-box: Steering Vectors, Sparse Autoencoders, Activation Oracles, Logit Lens
    * Scaffolded Black-box: Scaffolded Prefilling, Scaffolded User Sampling, Scaffolded Text Completion
  - MCP Servers: Interact with target models and auxiliary models
  - Workspace: research_log.md, quirks/ directory for storing hypotheses
Target Models (56 variants)
  - Base: Llama 3.3 70B Instruct (or Qwen 3 14B)
  - Behavior Instillation: Transcript Distillation / Synthetic Document Fine-tuning
  - Adversarial Training: None / SFT / KTO
Evaluation Framework
  - Static evaluations (single-turn, strong evidence)
  - Agentic evaluations (multi-turn investigation, final prediction)

### Critical path
1. Tool selection/design → Implement new or select existing auditing tool.
2. Agent configuration → Wire tool into investigator agent via MCP.
3. Benchmark execution → Run agent on all 56 target models (16 runs each) with token budget (~25k tokens per run).
4. Success classification → Use LLM classifier to judge if agent's final 10 predictions capture the true behavior.
5. Analysis → Compare success rates across tool configurations and model types.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Model diversity vs. depth:** The benchmark covers broad diversity (14 behaviors, 2 instillation methods, 2 adversarial methods) but uses a single base model family (Llama). New tools might behave differently on other architectures.
- **Agent capability ceiling:** The investigator agent uses Claude Haiku, a relatively weak model. More capable agents might close the tool‑to‑agent gap but would also change the difficulty curve.
- **Tool complexity:** White‑box tools require access to model internals (activations, SAEs) and careful hyperparameter tuning. They offer unique signals but are less portable.
- **Evaluation cost:** Full agent runs are expensive (~25k tokens per run × 56 models × 16 runs ≈ 22M tokens), limiting iteration speed.

### Failure signatures
- **Tool-to-agent gap persists:** A tool shows high strong‑evidence rate in static evaluation but agent success rate does not improve (or decreases). Indicates the agent cannot integrate the tool's signal.
- **Overfitting to iteration set:** Tool hyperparameters tuned on the 12‑model iteration set (subset with SFT adversarial training) degrade on KTO‑trained or transcript models. Suggests brittle tuning.
- **Confirmation bias with noisy tools:** Agents equipped with tools like honesty steering or logit lens latch onto false positives and waste investigation, causing performance drop on robust models.
- **Non-reproducibility across seeds:** Agent success rates vary widely across random seeds; tools that seem promising in a few runs may not have statistically significant gains.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replication baseline:** Run the investigator agent with the default tools on all 56 models, 16 runs each. Verify that the success rate distribution matches Figure 4's "Default" bars. This establishes a baseline and validates the evaluation pipeline.
2. **Static vs. agentic correlation:** For each tool, compute both the single‑turn success rate (Figure 8) and the agent success rate (Figure 4). Measure correlation across model configurations. Expect weak correlation; if strong, investigate agent implementation differences.
3. **Scaffolding ablation:** Take one scaffolded tool (e.g., scaffolded user sampling) and compare against (a) non‑scaffolded user sampling, and (b) a "shuffled" scaffold where the auxiliary model's hint is ignored. The scaffold should dramatically outperform both; if not, check auxiliary model generation diversity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Question:** Which auditing tools are most effective across the full spectrum of model training configurations, and how does tool effectiveness depend on the combination of behavior instillation method (transcript vs. synthetic document) and adversarial training (SFT vs. KTO)?
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "We find that audit success varies greatly across training techniques: models trained on synthetic documents are easier to audit than models trained on demonstrations, with better adversarial training further increasing auditing difficulty" and "techniques that improve auditing on easier target models often hurt performance on KTO‑trained TD models."
   **Why unresolved:** The evaluation covered only two instillation methods and two adversarial methods; it is unclear whether the observed tool rankings generalize to other training recipes (e.g., different datasets, RLHF, DPO) or to frontier models.
   **What evidence would resolve:** A larger‑scale benchmark experiment with a more diverse matrix of target models (varying fine‑tuning data sources, RLHF, adversarial budgets) measuring success rates for all tools, identifying tools that remain effective on the hardest configurations.

2. **Question:** What are the root causes of the tool‑to‑agent gap, and how can agent scaffolding be redesigned to close it so that tools that surface relevant evidence consistently improve agent success?
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "We observe a tool‑to‑agent gap: tools that can surface evidence in static evaluations often fail to improve agent performance due to under‑utilization, distraction by noise, and difficulty converting evidence into hypotheses."
   **Why unresolved:** The three proposed factors are hypothetical; the paper does not test interventions (e.g., forced tool usage, denoising, hypothesis guidance) to confirm causality or improve performance.
   **What evidence would resolve:** Ablation studies where agent prompts explicitly require frequent tool use, provide filtered/denoised tool outputs, or include step‑by‑step reasoning prompts to translate evidence into testable hypotheses; success rates compared to baseline would pinpoint effective fixes.

3. **Question:** Do the relative effectiveness rankings of auditing tools hold for different base model architectures and scales, or are the findings specific to Llama 3.3 70B Instruct?
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Our main results are based on a single base model (Llama 3.3 70B), and tool effectiveness may differ on other models."
   **Why unresolved:** All experiments used one model family; architectural differences (e.g., decoder‑only vs. other, scale) could change internal representations and thus tool efficacy (e.g., SAEs, steering).
   **What evidence would resolve:** Replicate the full agent evaluation suite on at least two additional model families (e.g., Qwen, Mistral) of comparable size, comparing tool success rates and correlation with the Llama results.

4. **Question:** How can multi‑turn auditing tools like Petri be improved to cover a broader range of concerning behaviors beyond the high‑stakes sabotage scenarios emphasized by their default seeds?
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Petri is well‑suited for surfacing complex multi‑turn behaviors, but its effectiveness is limited by the use of the default seed instructions, which focus on a fairly narrow set of scenarios, largely involving high‑stakes sabotage opportunities, and which were not designed to be broad enough to cover the full range of potentially‑concerning behaviors out of the box."
   **Why unresolved:** The paper uses the unmodified seed set; no experimentation with diversified seeds or systematic coverage analysis is presented.
   **What evidence would resolve:** Generate a large, diverse corpus of seed instructions (e.g., via LLM or human annotation) spanning all 14 behavior types, run Petri audits, and measure detection rates per behavior; success would show improved coverage and higher overall agent success.

## Limitations
- Benchmark limited to two base model families (Llama 3.3, Qwen 3); may not generalize to other architectures.
- Investigator agent fixed to Claude Haiku; different agent capabilities could alter the tool‑to‑agent gap.
- Tool implementations (SAEs, activation oracles) rely on proprietary or unpublished interpretability resources, hindering exact replication.

## Next Checks
1. **Agent generality test:** Replace the investigator agent (Claude Haiku) with a more capable model (e.g., GPT‑4) and re‑run the full agentic evaluation. If the tool‑to‑agent gap narrows or scaffolding advantage diminishes, revise confidence in Mechanisms 2–3.
2. **Architectural extension:** Add a third base model (e.g., Mistral 7B) to the benchmark, retrain a subset of behavior variants, and verify that relative tool effectiveness and difficulty patterns hold.
3. **Scaffolding ablation with controlled diversity:** For scaffolded user sampling, compare (a) no scaffolding, (b) scaffolding with random auxiliary prompts (no behavior hint), and (c) scaffolding with a generic auxiliary model. Isolate the contribution of behavior‑targeted diversity versus raw quantity.
