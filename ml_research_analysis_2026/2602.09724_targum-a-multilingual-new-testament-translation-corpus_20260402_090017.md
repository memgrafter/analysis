# Targum - A Multilingual New Testament Translation Corpus

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.09724
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09724
*   **Reference Count:** 13
*   **Primary Result:** Constructed a deep multilingual NT corpus with 651 translations (334 unique) across 5 languages, offering 2.4–5.0× more unique translations per language than prior resources.

## Executive Summary
Targum addresses the chronic lack of *depth*—many translations per language—in existing multilingual biblical corpora. It aggregates 651 New Testament translations (334 unique) from 13 online sources and manually canonicalizes their metadata to group identical editions and define translation lineages. The resulting resource enables diachronic analysis, with a key finding that modern translations are both lexically and semantically more diverse than historical ones. The corpus is distributed with rich provenance, pre-computed similarities, and embeddings to support flexible downstream research.

## Method Summary
The construction follows a three-stage pipeline: (1) **Acquisition** using an indexer and scraper to ingest raw NT texts and metadata from 12 online biblical libraries and the eBible corpus; (2) **Parsing** to extract standardized, verse-level JSONL data, handling non-standard segmentation; (3) **Manual Canonicalization** where curators research publication histories to assign stable `canonical_id` (translation work) and `canonical_version` (edition) identifiers. A validation loop computes cross-source lexical similarity for same-edition pairs (target median >0.996) to detect parser or source errors. Finally, semantic embeddings are pre-computed for all chapters.

## Key Results
*   Corpus depth: 334 unique translations—English (194), French (41), Italian (17), Polish (29), Spanish (53)—representing 2.4–5.0× more per language than prior depth-focused corpora.
*   Validation success: Same-edition pairs from different sources show median lexical similarity of 0.996, confirming canonicalization and parsing accuracy.
*   Diachronic trend: Pairwise similarity (lexical & semantic) decreases significantly over time for all languages (β < 0, all p < .011), indicating modern translations are more diverse.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Aggressive Source Breadth for Depth
*   **Claim:** Indexing and scraping from a large, diverse set of 13 online sources dramatically increases the number of unique translations per language.
*   **Mechanism:** Sources include both international aggregators (e.g., Bible Gateway) and language-specific repositories (e.g., bibliepolskie.pl), capturing a wide institutional and confessional spread. Raw collection yields 651 translations.
*   **Core assumption:** Digitized translations for the target era and languages are mostly accessible on these public websites.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] directly reports 2.4–5.0× increase per language; [section 3.1] details the 13-source pipeline; Table 2 shows raw counts per source.
*   **Break condition:** Limited by digital availability; exclusions like German due to scarce online versions demonstrate the scope is bounded by digitization bias.

### Mechanism 2: Manual Metadata Canonicalization for Flexible Uniqueness
*   **Claim:** Manual research to assign `canonical_id` and `canonical_version` transforms inconsistent scrapes into a research-ready corpus where "uniqueness" is user-definable.
*   **Mechanism:** Curators examine title pages, prefaces, and publisher records to group scraped instances by translation work (`canonical_id`) and specific edition (`canonical_version`), recording reasoning for reassessment.
*   **Core assumption:** Documentary evidence exists to reliably disambiguate publication histories and revision years.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] states metadata "allows researchers to define 'uniqueness'"; [section 3.2] describes the process and fields; Appendix A lists them as core.
*   **Break condition:** Manual mapping becomes ambiguous for translations with complex, multi-predecessor lineages or undocumented digital revisions, as noted in limitations.

### Mechanism 3: Cross-Source Validation via Lexical Clustering
*   **Claim:** Computing lexical similarity between multiple copies of the same `(canonical_id, canonical_version)` pair acts as an automatic quality control for parsing and source text errors.
*   **Mechanism:** Near-perfect similarity (median 0.996) between duplicates confirms correctness. Significant deviations signal a parser logic error or a pre-existing mistake in a source file, triggering manual investigation against authoritative documents.
*   **Core assumption:** Different source sites host accurate copies of the same published edition; high inter-source similarity is a reliable proxy for textual fidelity.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.3] defines the validation methodology; [section 5.3/Figure 5 & Appendix C] presents the empirical distribution validating the approach.
*   **Break condition:** May miss systematic, identical errors propagated across all copies or be less effective for inherently paraphrastic translations where minor lexical changes are intentional.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Depth-First vs. Breadth-First Corpus Design.**
    *   **Why needed here:** Targum's core innovation is prioritizing *depth* (many translations per language) over *breadth* (many languages with few translations). This dictates sourcing (deep per-language aggregation) and analysis (genealogical studies within a tradition).
    *   **Quick check:** What are the trade-offs of a depth-focused vs. breadth-focused biblical corpus for studying the evolution of translation strategies within a single language tradition?
*   **Concept: Canonicalization for Genealogical Grouping.**
    *   **Why needed here:** The `canonical_id`/`canonical_version` scheme groups by *work* and *edition*, not exact text. This enables lineage studies (e.g., KJV derivatives) while controlling for exact copies, distinct from simple string deduplication.
    *   **Quick check:** How would you group these three files: (1) 1769 Blayney KJV from Bible Gateway, (2) same 1769 text from Bible Hub, (3) original 1611 KJV from another site?
*   **Concept: Diachronic Analysis with Pairwise Similarity Regression.**
    *   **Why needed here:** The finding that modern translations are more diverse relies on using pairwise lexical/semantic similarity between *unique editions* as the diversity metric, regressed on midpoint year while controlling for temporal distance. This avoids pseudoreplication from scraped duplicates.
    *   **Quick check:** Why must analysis use only the 334 unique `(canonical_id, canonical_version)` pairs, not all 651 scraped instances, when testing for increasing diversity over time?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Indexer -> Scraper -> Parser -> Canonicalizer -> Validator -> Embedder -> Distributor
    (Validator loop feeds back to improve Parser logic and resolve Canonicalizer uncertainties)
*   **Critical path:** Linear through processing stages (Indexer to Distributor) with an essential feedback loop from Validator to earlier components.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Manual vs. Automated Metadata:** Manual canonicalization ensures accuracy for complex histories but is not scalable to hundreds of languages.
    *   **Raw Retention vs. Cleaned-Only:** Keeping all scraped instances with provenance maximizes user flexibility but increases storage and shifts deduplication burden to users.
    *   **Similarity Metric Choice:** Lexical (Levenshtein) is sensitive to small edits for validation; semantic (embeddings) captures broader stylistic drift. Both are provided for different needs.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **Parser:** Inconsistent `num_verses` for same `canonical_id` across sites; low lexical similarity (<0.9) between same-version pairs.
    *   **Canonicalizer:** High variance in `canonical_year` within a purported single edition; unresolved lineage ambiguities.
    *   **Validation:** Systematic bias in embedding-based cross-language similarity that contradicts known typology.
    *   **Legal/Ethical:** Missing/incorrect `copyright_status` leading to inappropriate distribution of copyrighted material.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Parser Sanity Check:** For a well-known translation with many scraped copies (e.g., KJV), compute all pairwise lexical similarities. Confirm distribution matches Appendix C (P10 > 0.96). Low scores indicate parser errors.
    2.  **Canonicalization Reproducibility Audit:** Have a second annotator reconstruct groupings for 10 random `canonical_id` groups using only provided reasoning and external sources. Compute inter-annotator agreement.
    3.  **Diachronic Trend Reproduction:** Using distributed metadata and precomputed similarities, replicate the regression for one language (e.g., Spanish). Confirm negative β coefficient is significant (p<.05).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question 1:** What factors beyond publication era (e.g., translation philosophy, confessional tradition, source text) account for most pairwise variation?
    *   **Basis:** Authors state most variation is driven by translation philosophy and other factors; R² values are modest.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Corpus lacks systematic annotations for philosophy, tradition, or source text.
    *   **Evidence to resolve:** Sub-corpus annotated with granular metadata for multivariate regression.
*   **Question 2:** How does flattening complex lineages into a single `canonical_id` affect analyses relying on these groupings?
    *   **Basis:** Authors acknowledge complex, multi-predecessor histories are not yet modeled.
    *   **Why unresolved:** `canonical_id` cannot represent indirect influence or partial reuse; groupings may conflate distinct philosophies.
    *   **Evidence to resolve:** Build a directed translation dependency graph and re-analyze with lineage-aware groupings.
*   **Question 3:** To what extent does "digitization bias" skew the observed diachronic distribution, especially for early translations?
    *   **Basis:** Explicit limitation: corpus represents the digital, not complete, landscape; German excluded due to scarcity.
    *   **Why unresolved:** No ground-truth bibliographic dataset for all published NT translations to compare against.
    *   **Evidence to resolve:** Manual audit of physical library catalogs/bibliographies to estimate digitization completeness per era.
*   **Question 4:** What explains the consistent finding that French translations are significantly longer than others, even after controlling for book?
    *   **Basis:** Analysis confirms French length significance; authors speculate it's due to morphological/orthographic properties, not philosophy.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Correlation not causation; no experiment isolates specific linguistic features.
    *   **Evidence to resolve:** Regression modeling translation length as a function of language typological features and philosophy proxies.
*   **Question 5:** How do pre-defined research questions benefit from Targum's depth, and what new computational insights into translation history become tractable?
    *   **Basis:** Paper positions Targum as a "depth-focused" general-purpose resource but does not demonstrate concrete studies.
    *   **Why unresolved:** The claimed "new class of computational research" remains prospective; no case studies are presented.
    *   **Evidence to resolve:** Published case studies applying Targum to specific historical/linguistic problems infeasible with sparser corpora.

## Limitations
*   **Digitization Bias:** Corpus composition is constrained by what is digitized and accessible online; early or region-specific translations may be missing (e.g., German).
*   **Scalability of Manual Metadata:** Canonicalization relies on manual documentary research, which is not sustainable for scaling to hundreds of languages or for adding complex new metadata layers like lineages.
*   **Parser Edge-Case Ambiguity:** Rules for handling non-standard verse segmentation (e.g., "6a", "6-8") are not fully specified, risking inconsistent parsing.

## Confidence
*   **Depth Advantage (2.4–5.0× more translations):** **High** — Directly supported by aggregated source counts.
*   **Metadata Canonicalization Validity (median similarity 0.996):** **Medium** — Strong cross-source validation evidence, but scalability and complex lineage handling are open.
*   **Diachronic Diversity Trend (negative β, p<.011):** **Medium** — Results are robust statistically but depend on correct canonicalization and stable embedding models; could be confounded by unmeasured factors.
