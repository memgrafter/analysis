# A One-Inclusion Graph Approach to Multi-Group Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23208
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23208
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** For any target group \(g\), expected group-only error \(\le d_{\mathcal{H}|g}/(n+1)\), achieving optimal \(1/n\) rate; for simultaneous evaluation over all groups, expected maximum group error is \(O(d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}/n + d_{\mathcal{G}}\log n/n)\), with the \(\log n/n\) term proven necessary; the approach extends to the agnostic setting with \(O(\sqrt{d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}}/\sqrt{n})\) rates.

## Executive Summary
Multi-group learning seeks a predictor with low error on each group in a collection \(\mathcal{G}\), under group-realizability (each group admits a perfect hypothesis from \(\mathcal{H}\)). Prior algorithms incurred an extra \(\log n\) factor. This work extends the classical one-inclusion graph (OIG) to a multi-group bipartite matching problem. A bipartite network is built from the OIG of the group-realizable concept class, and a linear program with group-specific capacities is solved. The resulting permutation-invariant learner achieves an expected group error of at most \(d_{\mathcal{H}|g}/(n+1)\) for any target group—matching the optimal \(1/n\) rate—and an expected maximum group error of \(O(d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}/n + d_{\mathcal{G}}\log n/n)\) over all groups, with the \(\log n/n\) term shown necessary by a lower bound. High-probability and PAC guarantees are derived via aggregation, replacing the group cardinality with a multi-group covering number. The method also extends to the agnostic setting with an \(O(\sqrt{d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}}/\sqrt{n})\) rate.

## Method Summary
The algorithm constructs the one-inclusion graph (OIG) on the unlabeled sample \(U\) for the group-realizable concept class \(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^U\). Vertices represent labelings of \(U\) consistent with some \(h\in\mathcal{H}\) on every group; edges connect labelings differing at exactly one point. For each group \(g\), the capacity \(d_g\) is defined as the maximum density of the subgraph induced by edges relevant to \(g\). A linear program (5) on a bipartite network (left nodes = edges, right nodes = vertices) yields a fractional matching \(f\). The optimum is integral and prediction‑sufficient (\(f_{e,u}+f_{e,v}=1\) for each edge). Given a training set of \(n-1\) labeled points and a test point, the two OIG vertices consistent with the training labels are identified; they are adjacent via an edge \(e\), and the test label is predicted randomly according to \(f_{e,\cdot}\). For PAC guarantees, multiple predictors trained on random subsample prefixes are aggregated by majority vote.

## Key Results
- Single-group error: \(\mathbb{E}[\text{err}_g(A(S))] \le d_{\mathcal{H}|g}/(n+1)\), achieving optimal \(1/n\).
- Simultaneous error: \(\mathbb{E}[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}} \text{err}_g(A(S))] = O(d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}/n + d_{\mathcal{G}}\log n/n)\), with the \(\log n/n\) term necessary.
- Agnostic extension: \(\mathbb{E}[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}} \text{err}_g(A(S))] = O(\sqrt{d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}}/\sqrt{n})\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Extending the OIG to a multi‑group bipartite matching problem yields a learner with optimal/near‑optimal group‑wise error rates.
- **Mechanism:** Build a bipartite network where left nodes are OIG edges (restricted to group‑relevant ones) and right nodes are vertices, with each vertex carrying a group‑specific capacity \(d_g = \max_{W\subseteq V} |E_W^g|/|W|\). Solve LP (5); the resulting fractional matching defines a randomized prediction rule.
- **Core assumption:** *Group‑realizability* (each group has a perfect \(h\in\mathcal{H}\)).
- **Evidence:** The abstract describes the extension; Section 3.1 formally defines the bipartite network \(B_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathcal{G}}(U)\); related work (e.g., Asilis et al. 2024) connects OIG to bipartite matchings, generalized here to overlapping groups.
- **Break condition:** Capacities set too low → LP infeasible; group‑realizability fails → OIG no longer reflects VC‑bounded densities.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The LP’s optimum solution is *prediction‑sufficient* and integral, yielding a well‑defined learner.
- **Mechanism:** Duality shows the optimum value equals \(|E|\) (Lemma 5), forcing \(f_{e,u}+f_{e,v}=1\) for every edge. Integrality follows from total unimodularity or an integral flow construction.
- **Core assumption:** OIG combinatorial properties and the chosen capacities.
- **Evidence:** Abstract states “showing its optimum is integral and prediction‑sufficient”; Lemma 2 and Lemma 3 (Section 3.3/Appendix D) prove these; Dughmi (2025) links OIG to max‑flow, echoing the duality argument.
- **Break condition:** Fractional optimum without unit sums → cannot randomize correctly between two consistent hypotheses.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Transductive error bounds for a permutation‑invariant learner imply the stated rates (after aggregation).
- **Mechanism:** First, bound multi‑group transductive error via the matching’s capacity constraints (Lemma 4). Permutation invariance → exchangeability (Haussler et al. 1994) converts transductive to expected prediction error. For high‑probability, aggregate predictors from random prefixes via majority vote; union‑bound over groups using multi‑group covering numbers \(N_{\text{mg}}\).
- **Core assumption:** Permutation invariance; group‑realizability; finite VC dimensions.
- **Evidence:** Abstract mentions “high‑probability and PAC guarantees are derived via aggregation, yielding analogous expressions with multi‑group covering numbers.” Theorem 3 (Appendix B) formalizes the conversion; Definition 6 defines \(N_{\text{mg}}\). Aden‑Ali et al. (2023b) provided similar aggregation tools.
- **Break condition:** Non‑permutation‑invariant learner breaks conversion; many groups make \(\log N_{\text{mg}}\) dominate.

## Foundational Learning

### One-Inclusion Graph (OIG)
- **Why needed:** The OIG is the combinatorial backbone; its maximum subgraph density characterizes the VC dimension and directly provides the capacities.
- **Quick check:** *What does Lemma 1 state about the relationship between VC dimension \(d\) and the maximum subgraph density of the OIG?* → They are equal.

### Group-Realizable Concepts \(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^U\)
- **Why needed:** Encodes exactly the functions perfectly consistent with each group under \(\mathcal{H}\); guarantees are proved against this class.
- **Quick check:** *How is \(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}}^U\) defined (Definition 1), and how does it generalize classical realizability?* → It includes all labelings on \(U\) for which, for every \(g\in\mathcal{G}\), there exists \(h\in\mathcal{H}\) with zero error on \(g\cap U\).

### Multi-Group Relevant Covering Number \(N_{\text{mg}}\)
- **Why needed:** Measures effective number of groups when evaluating simultaneously, replacing \(\log |\mathcal{G}|\) with a potentially smaller term under overlap.
- **Quick check:** *Given an \(L_1(P)\) \(\varepsilon\)-cover \(\hat{\mathcal{G}}(\varepsilon)\), what is \(N_{\text{mg}}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{G},P)\) (Definition 6)?* → Minimum number of balls needed to cover \(\mathcal{G}\) in the pseudo‑metric \(d_{\text{mg}}(g,g') = 2\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{g(x)\neq h(x)} + \mathbf{1}_{g'(x)\neq h(x)}]\).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:**  
OIG Constructor → Network Builder → LP Solver → Matching Post-Processor → Learner Wrapper → Aggregation Module (for PAC).

**Critical Path:**
1. Estimate/input VC dimensions \(d_{\mathcal{H}}, d_{\mathcal{G}}, d_{\mathcal{H}|g}\).
2. For a test point, build the OIG on the combined set of \(n\) points (\(n-1\) training + test).
3. Construct bipartite network \(B_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathcal{G}}(U)\) and compute capacities \(d_g\) (typically via Lemma 5’s dual).
4. Solve LP (5) to obtain fractional matching \(f\).
5. If integral, output deterministic prediction; else use randomized rule based on \(f\).
6. For PAC guarantees, repeat steps 2‑5 for multiple random prefixes and aggregate via majority vote.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- Exact vs. approximate capacities: Exact \(d_g\) is combinatorial; upper bound \(d_{\mathcal{H}|g}\) is easier but may be loose.
- Fractional vs. integer matching: Fractional gives a natural randomized predictor; integer gives determinism but requires integrality (e.g., network flow).
- Scalability: OIG size grows exponentially with \(n\); practical implementations may use implicit or surrogate representations.
- Aggregation cost: PAC version multiplies runtime by \(O(\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon^2)\); parallelizable.

**Failure Signatures:**
- LP infeasible or optimum < \(|E|\): capacities too low or group‑realizability violated.
- High transductive error on validation: \(d_{\mathcal{H}|g}\) underestimated or groups more complex.
- Disproportionate errors in certain groups: larger \(d_{\mathcal{H}|g}\) or large \(N_{\text{mg}}\).
- Majority vote not improving PAC error: insufficient number of aggregated predictors or high variance between prefixes.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Synthetic group‑realizable data:** Use thresholds on \([0,1]\) (\(d_{\mathcal{H}|g}=2\)) and nested groups; verify empirical \(d_{\mathcal{H}|g}/n\) scaling.
2. **Real dataset with fairness groups:** Apply to UCI Adult; define groups by protected attributes; compare maximum group error of MgOIG vs. standard ERM.
3. **Agnostic regime test:** Use data where group‑realizability is broken; check emergence of \(\sqrt{d_{\mathcal{H}|g}/n}\) rates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can the one‑inclusion graph approach be extended to multi‑group learning in multiclass classification and partial concept learning? (Explicitly stated as future work.)
2. What is the computational complexity of solving the multi‑group matching linear program (5), and does it admit a more efficient combinatorial algorithm akin to Ford‑Fulkerson? (Inferred from lack of direct combinatorial method.)
3. Under what structural conditions on \(\mathcal{G}\) does the simultaneous guarantee achieve the optimal \(1/n\) rate without the \(\log n\) factor? (Lower bound shows necessity for disjoint groups; overlapping groups might improve.)
4. Is the multi‑group relevant covering number \(N^{\text{mg}}\) a necessary complexity measure, and can it be simplified or bounded more tightly by parameters like mutual VC dimension? (The PAC bound includes \(\log N^{\text{mg}}\), but its precise role and tightness remain unclear.)

## Limitations
- The OIG construction is exponential in sample size, limiting scalability; practical approximations are required for large \(n\).
- Exact computation of group capacities \(d_g\) is combinatorial; using the VC‑dimension upper bound may be loose and weaken theoretical guarantees.
- The simultaneous bound’s \(\log n/n\) term is necessary in the worst case, but its dependence on group overlap and constant factors are not fully characterized.
- The PAC version’s aggregation multiplies runtime by \(O(\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon^2)\), which can be substantial.
- The agnostic extension’s \(O(\sqrt{d_{\mathcal{H}|\mathcal{G}}}/\sqrt{n})\) rate may be improvable for overlapping groups; the role of \(N_{\text{mg}}\) in that setting is unclear.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** Optimal \(1/n\) rate for single‑group error. **Confidence:** High.
- **Claim:** Simultaneous bound with \(\log n/n\) term and matching lower bound. **Confidence:** High.
- **Claim:** Transductive‑to‑PAC conversion via permutation invariance and covering numbers. **Confidence:** High.
- **Claim:** LP optimum equals \(|E|\) and yields prediction‑sufficient matching. **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Claim:** Computational feasibility for complex \(\mathcal{H}\) and large \(n\). **Confidence:** Medium‑Low.
