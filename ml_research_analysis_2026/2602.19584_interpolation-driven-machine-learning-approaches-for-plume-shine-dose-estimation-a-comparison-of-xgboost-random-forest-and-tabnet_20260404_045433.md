# Interpolation-Driven Machine Learning Approaches for Plume Shine Dose Estimation: A Comparison of XGBoost, Random Forest, and TabNet

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19584
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19584
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: XGBoost achieved R² 0.998 and MAPE 1.00% on high-resolution plume shine dose test data using PCHIP-interpolated training.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the challenge of sparse radiation dose data by using shape-preserving interpolation (PCHIP) to densify plume shine dose datasets for 17 radionides. Tree-based (XGBoost, Random Forest) and attention-based (TabNet) machine learning models are compared within this framework. All models gain accuracy from the interpolated data; XGBoost reaches near-perfect prediction (R²=0.998, MAPE=1.00%). Interpretability reveals tree models focus on key geometry-dispersion features (release height, stability, distance), while TabNet distributes attention more broadly, explaining its relative underperformance. A web-based GUI provides interactive scenario evaluation with side-by-side physics-based reference calculations, supporting practical adoption in safety-critical radiation assessment.

## Method Summary
The authors generate reference plume shine doses using pyDOSEIA, a Gaussian plume and point-kernel photon transport code, across combinations of radionuclide (17), release height (10–200 m), atmospheric stability (A–F), and downwind distance (25–2000 m). To alleviate sparsity, PCHIP interpolation is applied along the distance dimension for each fixed radionuclide/height/stability tuple, producing a dense high-resolution dataset that preserves monotonic decay and matches original points exactly. Three ML models—XGBoost (gradient boosting), Random Forest, and TabNet (deep attention)—are trained separately on the original low-resolution and the interpolated high-resolution datasets. Preprocessing includes log10 transformation of the dose target, integer encoding of categorical features, and min‑max scaling of numerical features. Models are evaluated using R², MAPE, and sMAPE on held‑out test sets. Interpretability is conducted via conditional permutation importance for tree models and aggregated attention masks for TabNet. Finally, a Streamlit GUI enables interactive dose prediction with direct comparison to pyDOSEIA calculations, enhancing transparency for users.

## Key Results
- Training on PCHIP-interpolated high-resolution data improved accuracy for all models compared to the original sparse data.
- XGBoost achieved the best test performance on high-resolution data: R² = 0.998, MAPE = 1.00%.
- Tree-based models primarily used geometry-dispersion features (release height, atmospheric stability, downwind distance), while TabNet’s attention was more distributed across inputs, which partly explains the performance gap.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Shape-preserving interpolation via PCHIP successfully transforms sparse plume shine dose tables into dense, high-resolution datasets without introducing non‑physical artifacts, enabling ML models to learn smooth dose–distance relationships.
- Mechanism: PCHIP constructs a piecewise cubic polynomial between each pair of adjacent discrete dose values, enforcing continuity of the function and its first derivative while preserving monotonicity in intervals where the data are monotonic. This yields a continuous dose curve that exactly matches the original points and fills intermediate distances with physically plausible values.
- Core assumption: The true plume shine dose as a function of downwind distance (for fixed release height and stability) is smooth and monotonic under the Gaussian plume assumptions used by pyDOSEIA.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: “The datasets were augmented using shape-preserving interpolation to construct dense, high-resolution training data.”
  - [section 3.2]: Detailed description of PCHIP application and visual validation against ground‑truth dose values (Figs. 3–5).
  - [corpus]: Corpus neighbors do not provide strong direct evidence; while some papers discuss plume identification or interpolation techniques, none specifically validate PCHIP for radiological dose augmentation. (Weak corpus support)
- Break condition: If the dose–distance relationship were non‑monotonic due to complex terrain, deposition, or meteorological shifts, PCHIP’s monotonicity preservation could produce unrealistic local extrema. Furthermore, interpolation cannot create information beyond the range or resolution of the original sparse samples.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Tree‑based ensemble models (XGBoost, Random Forest) align better with the low‑dimensional geometry‑dispersion structure of plume shine dose than TabNet’s attention‑based deep architecture, leading to consistently higher accuracy and robustness.
- Mechanism: Tree ensembles hierarchically split on features that maximally reduce impurity; the strongest splits naturally arise on release height, downwind distance, and stability because these dominate dose variations. Gradient boosting (XGBoost) sequentially corrects residual errors, effectively capturing secondary radionuclide‑specific effects after the primary geometry‑dispersion trends are learned. TabNet, in contrast, uses a sequential attention mask that distributes feature importance more uniformly across all inputs, diluting focus on the dominant variables.
- Core assumption: The functional mapping from inputs to plume shine dose is primarily governed by a small set of geometric and meteorological features; radionuclide identity acts as a secondary conditional modifier.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: “Interpretability analysis revealed tree‑based models focused primarily on geometry‑dispersion features (release height, stability, and downwind distance), while TabNet distributed attention more broadly.”
  - [section 5.3]: Radionuclide‑wise feature importance (Fig. 9) shows tree models concentrate on release height, while TabNet spreads importance; exhaustive ablation (Fig. 10) demonstrates that three geometry‑dispersion features capture most of the predictive signal.
  - [section 5.2]: Error distributions across stability classes and radionuclides reveal TabNet’s larger variance, especially for noble gases with steep dose gradients.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor “X‑TIME: An in‑memory engine for accelerating machine learning on tabular data” notes that tree‑based methods often outperform deep learning on tabular tasks, providing indirect support. (Partial corpus alignment)
- Break condition: If the problem involved many equally important high‑order interactions or a vastly larger dataset, deep architectures with distributed attention might surpass tree ensembles. Also, if the dominant features were less informative, the hierarchical splitting strategy could become less effective.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The web‑based graphical user interface (GUI) with integrated model interpretation and direct physics‑based comparison enables practical adoption by providing transparency and real‑time validation in a safety‑critical context.
- Mechanism: The Streamlit app accepts scenario parameters (radionuclide, distance, height, stability) and returns dose predictions from all three trained ML models alongside reference pyDOSEIA calculations. By presenting results side‑by‑side, users can quickly spot discrepancies and build trust. Although the GUI does not itself display feature attributions, the underlying study’s interpretability analysis (permutation importance, attention masks) informs which features matter most, adding another layer of transparency.
- Core assumption: Emergency planners and safety analysts require immediate, understandable predictions that can be cross‑checked against established physics‑based methods to accept ML surrogates for operational use.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: “For practical deployment, a web‑based GUI was developed for interactive scenario evaluation and transparent comparison with photon‑transport reference calculations.”
  - [section 5.4]: Describes the GUI’s functionality, including interactive inputs and the integration of reference quadrature calculations.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor “V‑SHiNE: A Virtual Smart Home Framework for Explainability Evaluation” highlights the value of interactive frameworks for explainability, but direct evidence for plume dose is limited. (Weak corpus link)
- Break condition: If the GUI introduces latency (e.g., slow model loading or quadrature computation), its utility in time‑critical emergencies diminishes. Lack of explicit feature explanations within the interface may also limit deeper trust for some users.

## Foundational Learning
- **Shape-preserving interpolation (PCHIP)**
  - Why needed: It is the cornerstone of the data augmentation strategy, converting the original sparse dose tables into a dense, smooth training set that retains the physical monotonicity of dose decay with distance. Without it, ML models struggle to generalize between discrete points.
  - Quick check question: Why is it important that PCHIP preserves monotonicity in intervals where the data are monotonic, and how does this differ from standard cubic spline interpolation?

- **Inductive bias of tabular machine learning models**
  - Why needed: Understanding the inherent preferences of tree ensembles vs. attention‑based networks explains the observed performance hierarchy (XGBoost > RF > TabNet) and guides model selection for other physics‑based surrogate tasks.
  - Quick check question: How does gradient boosting’s sequential correction of residuals help it capture conditional feature interactions that a single decision tree or a uniformly attending deep model might miss?

- **Feature attribution for interpretable machine learning**
  - Why needed: In safety‑critical radiation assessment, stakeholders must know which input factors drive predictions to validate model behavior, meet regulatory standards, and debug failures.
  - Quick check question: What is the primary difference between permutation importance (used for tree models) and attention masks (used in TabNet) regarding model agnosticism and computational cost?

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map: pyDOSEIA simulations -> PCHIP interpolation (distance dimension) -> ML model training (XGBoost, Random Forest, TabNet) -> Evaluation (R², MAPE, sMAPE) -> Interpretability analysis -> Streamlit GUI deployment.

- Critical path:
  1. Generate low-resolution dataset (`DLR`) with `pyDOSEIA` across radionuclides, heights, distances, stabilities.
  2. Apply PCHIP to obtain high-resolution dataset (`DHR`); split into training/test such that original points are excluded from `Dtrain_HR`.
  3. Train each model on both `Dtrain_LR` and `Dtrain_HR`, tuning hyperparameters (manual for RF/XGB, Optuna for TabNet).
  4. Evaluate all models on `Dtest_LR` (discrete) and `Dtest_HR` (interpolated) to assess resolution sensitivity.
  5. Compute feature importances on the unified test set (`Dtest_LR ∪ Dtest_HR`) using the model‑specific method.
  6. Package trained models into the Streamlit GUI for interactive use.

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Interpolation resolution vs data size: Finer distance steps improve smoothness but increase dataset size and training time; PCHIP is computationally cheap but limited to 1‑D and cannot extrapolate beyond the original distance range.
  - Model choice: XGBoost offers top accuracy and fast inference but requires careful tuning; TabNet provides built‑in attention but is more sensitive to hyperparameters and performs worse on this task; RF is robust but less fine‑grained.
  - Interpretability depth: Permutation importance is model‑agnostic but can be biased by correlated features; attention masks are intrinsic but may not perfectly reflect true feature relevance.

- Failure signatures:
  - Poor cross‑resolution performance: Model trained on `DLR` shows large errors on `Dtest_HR` → indicates training data lacks smoothness; remedy: use `DHR`.
  - Radionuclide‑specific spikes: Excessive errors for noble gases (e.g., Kr, Xe) suggest the model struggles with steep dose gradients; consider adding derived features (e.g., plume width) or more diverse training samples.
  - Unstable permutation importance: Large variance across random restarts or radionuclides could signal overfitting or feature correlation; inspect partial dependence plots.
  - Uniform TabNet attention: Masks that are nearly uniform indicate the model is not learning to focus; may be due to underfitting, excessive sparsity regularization, or insufficient data.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. **Reproduce baseline data**: Run `pyDOSEIA` for `137Cs` with stability classes A and F, release heights 10–200 m (step 10 m), distances 25–2000 m. Verify that the resulting dose distribution matches the boxen plot in Fig. 2 (value ranges orders of magnitude).
  2. **Validate PCHIP interpolation**: For a fixed (radionuclide, height, stability), apply PCHIP to the discrete dose values and plot the interpolated curve against the original points (as in Figs. 3–5). Ensure monotonic decay and exact passage through original samples; optionally compute maximum deviation if a finer numerical evaluation is available.
  3. **Benchmark XGBoost on high‑resolution data**: Train XGBoost on `Dtrain_HR` using the hyperparameters from Table S1. Evaluate on `Dtest_HR` and confirm that metrics reach R² ≈ 0.998 and MAPE ≈ 1.00% (Table 3). If not, check preprocessing (log‑transform, scaling), random seed, and that the test set contains only interpolated points.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: What specific inductive biases cause tree-based ensembles (XGBoost, Random Forest) to outperform TabNet in plume shine dose prediction, and is this advantage persistent across varied physics‑dominated tabular problems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors explicitly compare model performance and interpretability, noting that tree-based models concentrate on geometry-dispersion features (release height, stability, distance) while TabNet distributes attention broadly (Sections 5.1, 5.3). They attribute XGBoost’s superiority to its sequential boosting capturing dominant trends and residual refinements, aligning with prior work on tabular data.
- Why unresolved: The study is observational; it does not manipulate model inductive biases (e.g., altering TabNet’s attention mechanism or tree splitting criteria) to test causality. The explanation relies on post‑hoc feature attribution without controlled experiments isolating bias effects.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic benchmarking on synthetic datasets with known low‑dimensional structure, where inductive biases are deliberately varied (e.g., constraining TabNet to focus on few features, forcing trees to use all equally), to see if performance hierarchy reverses under altered bias‑task alignment.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Could multi‑dimensional interpolation (e.g., over release height or stability) or alternative interpolation schemes beyond PCHIP further improve the physical consistency and generalization of ML surrogates for sparse radiation datasets?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The framework uses PCHIP interpolation only along downwind distance (Section 3.2), justified by its shape‑preserving property for monotonic decay. The authors do not compare other interpolation methods or extend densification to other input dimensions, implying an unstated assumption that distance is the sole sparsely sampled axis needing augmentation.
- Why unresolved: The choice is methodologically unmotivated beyond distance monotonicity; release height and stability may also have smooth relationships with dose that could benefit from interpolation. No ablation tests whether interpolating these dimensions changes model performance or physical plausibility.
- What evidence would resolve it: Train models on datasets augmented with different interpolation strategies (e.g., spline, Kriging) and dimensional scopes (1D distance‑only vs. 2D including height or stability), evaluating not just accuracy but also smoothness predictions and adherence to known physical constraints (e.g., monotonic decay with distance).

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can uncertainty quantification be integrated into interpolation‑assisted ML surrogates for radiation dose to meet safety‑critical operational needs, and does this affect model interpretability or deployment?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper focuses on point prediction accuracy (R², MAPE) and develops a GUI showing deterministic outputs (Section 5.4). Despite the safety‑critical context emphasized in the introduction, no uncertainty estimates are provided, leaving decision‑makers without confidence bounds.
- Why unresolved: Radiation emergency response requires probabilistic risk assessment. The absence of uncertainty quantification limits trust and applicability in real‑world scenarios where over/under‑prediction consequences differ. The current framework assumes perfect interpolation and model certainty.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extend XGBoost, RF, and TabNet to output predictive distributions (e.g., via quantile regression, Bayesian methods, or deep ensembles), then assess calibration reliability (e.g., coverage of true values) and usability in hypothetical decision‑making exercises, comparing how uncertainty affects model selection and user interaction in the GUI.

## Limitations
- Exact pyDOSEIA simulation parameters (e.g., dispersion coefficients, deposition velocities) are undisclosed, hindering exact reproduction of the reference dataset.
- PCHIP interpolation assumes monotonic dose decay; this assumption may be violated by complex terrain or deposition effects, potentially yielding non‑physical intermediate values.
- Feature attribution techniques (permutation importance, attention masks) have known limitations (e.g., bias with correlated features, attention not always reflecting true relevance) that may affect interpretability trust.
