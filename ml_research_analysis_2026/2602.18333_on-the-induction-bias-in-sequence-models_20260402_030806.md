# On the "Induction Bias" in Sequence Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18333  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18333  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Transformers lack an inductive bias for incremental state updates, resulting in poor data efficiency and length‑isolated solutions on state‑tracking tasks; recurrent models exhibit amortized learning and length generalization when supervision aligns with their recurrent updates.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the in-distribution data efficiency of transformers versus recurrent neural networks (RNNs) on synthetic state‑tracking tasks: modular addition and permutation composition. Using a rigorous binary‑search estimation of the minimal training set size \(N^*\) required for convergence, the authors compare three supervision formats: outcome (final result only), chain‑of‑thought (CoT: intermediate partial sums), and aligned CoT (ACoT: per‑token partial sums aligned with inputs). Key findings show that RNNs (LSTM, Dense‑SSM) dramatically outperform transformers under outcome supervision, but transformers close the gap with CoT. Crucially, RNNs prefer ACoT, becoming more data‑efficient as sequence length grows, while transformers remain length‑inefficient. The paper introduces a sharing factor \(\kappa\) to quantify weight sharing across lengths; transformers exhibit \(\kappa\) near zero (length‑isolated solutions), whereas RNNs achieve \(\kappa \gg 1\) in their preferred formats, indicating amortized learning. High \(\kappa\) strongly correlates with successful out‑of‑distribution (OOD) length generalization, suggesting that an induction bias for incremental state updates is key for state‑tracking.

## Method Summary
The study employs two algorithmic tasks: (1) modular addition over \(\mathbb{Z}_m\), requiring the sum of a sequence modulo \(m\), and (2) composition of random permutations from \(S_5\). Models are trained to produce either the final outcome, a chain of intermediate partial sums (CoT), or an aligned sequence where each partial sum immediately follows its corresponding input (ACoT). Sequence lengths \(n\) range from 2 to \(L\), with \(L \in \{5,10,20,30\}\). Three length distributions are used: Fixed (all \(n=L\)), Uniform (random \(n\in[2,L]\)), and Short‑to‑Long (curriculum). The core evaluation metric is \(N^*\), the smallest training set size (uniques) for which at least one of 15 hyperparameter configurations (3 learning rates \(\times\) 5 seeds) reaches a validation loss threshold (\(10^{-4}\) or perfect accuracy) within a fixed budget of 250 k steps. \(N^*\) is estimated via a binary‑geometric search (Algorithm 1) that adaptively narrows the dataset size range. For each task configuration (Task, Format, Distribution, Model), the authors compute \(N^*_\text{joint}\) for a uniform distribution over all lengths \(2\ldots L\), and separately \(N^*_n\) for each fixed length \(n\). The sharing factor \(\kappa\) is defined as \(\kappa = (\sum_{n=2}^{L} N^*_n) / N^*_\text{joint}\), measuring how much training on a mixture of lengths reduces total sample cost relative to learning each length independently. \(\kappa\) values are then correlated with OOD length generalization performance (e.g., length \(2L\)) to assess whether weight sharing promotes robustness.

## Key Results
- **Data efficiency gap depends on supervision.** Under outcome supervision, transformers need orders of magnitude more data than RNNs and fail on longer sequences; with CoT, transformers close the gap, while RNNs benefit only from ACoT (aligned intermediate targets).
- **Weight sharing across lengths is negligible for transformers but strong for RNNs.** Transformers consistently show \(\kappa \approx 0.28\) (modular addition) or even below 1, indicating learned solutions are isolated to specific lengths. RNNs with ACoT achieve \(\kappa \gg 1\) (e.g., 9.83 for LSTM), evidencing amortized learning.
- **High \(\kappa\) predicts OOD length generalization.** Across both tasks, configurations with \(\kappa \gg 1\) generalize robustly to sequences twice as long as those seen during training, while low‑\(\kappa\) models fail dramatically.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Recurrence Enforces Incremental Updates
Recurrent architectures compute a hidden state \(h_t\) from the previous state \(h_{t-1}\) and the current input \(x_t\). This recurrence forces the model to incorporate new information into a minimal sufficient statistic for the next step immediately, decomposing the multi‑step task into repeated application of a single‑step transition operator. In contrast, a transformer computes each output using the full context window, allowing it to recalculate the required state from all past inputs at every step and thus avoiding a commitment to a particular state representation. The paper assumes that state‑tracking tasks benefit from weight‑sharing across timesteps when the transition operator is consistent. Empirical evidence includes the abstract’s claim that transformers “lack an ‘induction bias’ for incremental state updates” and the stark performance contrast: RNNs learn efficiently under outcome supervision while transformers require vastly more data or fail entirely. A break condition for this mechanism is the presence of strict hidden‑state recurrence; if full context access is allowed at every step (as in decoder‑only transformers), the bias is absent.

### Mechanism 2: Amortized Learning via High Sharing Factor \(\kappa\)
A model that learns a shared transition operator \(f\) can apply it iteratively for any sequence length \(n\). Training on a distribution of lengths therefore teaches a single reusable mechanism, making the joint learning cost \(N^*_\text{joint}\) much lower than the sum of independent per‑length costs \(\sum N^*_n\). This yields a sharing factor \(\kappa = (\sum N^*_n) / N^*_\text{joint} \gg 1\), indicating amortized learning. Without such sharing, \(N^*_\text{joint} \approx \sum N^*_n\) (\(\kappa \approx 1\)) or even worse due to interference, leading to length‑isolated solutions. The paper’s experiments confirm this dichotomy: for Transformer+CoT, \(\kappa \approx 0.28\) (modular addition), meaning joint training is less efficient than separate training; for RNN+ACoT, \(\kappa \gg 1\) (e.g., 9.83) shows substantial amortization. The core assumption is that the computational graph for length‑\(n\) sequences can be unrolled into \(n\) repetitions of the same operation. Evidence: Section 4 defines \(\kappa\), Figure 6 shows the contrast, and Observation 4.5 reports a strong positive correlation between high \(\kappa\) and OOD length generalization. A break condition would be if high \(\kappa\) did not predict OOD success, or if a model achieved high \(\kappa\) without a reusable transition operator.

### Mechanism 3: Supervision Format Aligns with Native Inductive Bias
The format of supervision critically interacts with architecture to reveal or conceal the induction bias. Outcome supervision provides no intermediate targets. RNNs can still discover the transition operator via backpropagation through time, yielding moderate \(\kappa\). Transformers struggle immensely because they must simulate the iterative process within a single feed‑forward pass for each length, requiring vast data and failing to generalize. Chain‑of‑thought (CoT) supervision gives explicit intermediate states; this aligns with the transformer’s global computation style (autoregressive generation with re‑attention), making CoT their most efficient format, but the learned solution remains length‑isolated (low \(\kappa\)). For RNNs, CoT creates a recall bottleneck: after processing the entire input, the network must remember all \(x_t\) to output the partial sums, which is difficult given a fixed‑size hidden state, harming performance. Aligned CoT (ACoT) supervises the state at the moment of update (\(x_t \rightarrow s_t\)), directly training the transition operator \(f\) at each step. This perfectly aligns with the recurrent inductive bias, leading to high \(\kappa\) and excellent data efficiency. The paper assumes that the optimal learning signal for a model with an induction bias is one that supervises the state at the update moment; for a model without such bias, the optimal signal decomposes the task into subproblems that match its native computation. Evidence: Abstract and Section 3 compare \(N^*\) across formats; Observation 3.4 shows longer sequences improve RNN data efficiency with ACoT but not with transformers; Observation 3.5 finds that under outcome supervision, short sequences are more valuable for RNNs. A break condition would be if transformers achieved high \(\kappa\) with outcome supervision, or if RNNs showed high \(\kappa\) with CoT, forcing a revision of the interaction hypothesis.

## Foundational Learning
The paper relies on several key concepts:

- **Inductive Bias**: The paper's core thesis is that different architectures have inherent preferences for certain solution families (e.g., recurrence favors incremental updates). This explains why RNNs and transformers behave differently on the same task. Quick check: Does the architecture reuse parameters across timesteps (RNN) or allow independent per‑position computation given full context (Transformer)?

- **State Tracking & Minimal Sufficient Statistic**: The tasks require maintaining a state that evolves with each input. The paper defines an inductive bias as producing a hidden state \(h_t\) that is a “minimal sufficient statistic” for predicting \(x_{t+1}\). This formalizes the idea that the state should compactly capture all information needed for future steps. Quick check: Can the hidden state at time \(t\) alone predict the next output without re‑accessing the full input history \(x_1\ldots x_t\)?

- **Sample Complexity & Binary Search Estimation (\(N^*\))**: The primary empirical metric is \(N^*\), the smallest number of training samples that yields convergence (validation loss \(< 10^{-4}\) or perfect accuracy) within a fixed compute budget. \(N^*\) is estimated by a binary‑geometric search over dataset sizes, testing up to 15 hyperparameter configurations per size. Understanding this procedure is essential to interpret the reported data efficiencies. Quick check: Is \(N^*\) measured for a single fixed length or for a mixture of lengths? How is success defined?

### Critical Path
1. For a given combination of Task, Supervision Format, Length Distribution \(L\), and Model, run the binary‑geometric search (Algorithm 1) to estimate \(N^*\).
2. Repeat step 1 for a range of maximum lengths \(L\) (e.g., 5,10,20,30) using a Uniform distribution over lengths \(2\ldots L\) to obtain \(N^*_\text{joint}\).
3. For each individual length \(n \in \{2,\ldots,L\}\), train a model on a Fixed distribution (all sequences length \(n\)) to obtain \(N^*_n\).
4. Compute the sharing factor \(\kappa = (\sum_{n=2}^{L} N^*_n) / N^*_\text{joint}\).
5. Correlate \(\kappa\) values across all configurations with out‑of‑distribution length generalization scores (e.g., accuracy on length \(2L\)) to assess the relationship.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Model scale:** The paper tests a 6‑layer, 256‑d transformer and 1‑layer LSTMs/Dense‑SSMs; smaller variants show similar trends, but very large pretrained transformers may behave differently.
- **Task complexity:** Both modular addition (commutative) and permutation composition (non‑commutative) exhibit the same patterns, suggesting generality beyond simple abelian groups.
- **Supervision format:** Outcome, CoT, and ACoT dramatically alter \(N^*\) and \(\kappa\); the choice of format is crucial for revealing an architecture’s induction bias.
- **Length distribution:** Fixed vs. Uniform vs. Short‑to‑Long influences whether models benefit from mixed‑length training; RNNs with ACoT gain from seeing longer sequences earlier.

### Failure Signatures
- **Transformer + Outcome:** \(N^*\) explodes with length and state‑space size; often fails even at the maximum dataset size (Table 1 dashes).
- **Transformer + CoT/ACoT:** Low \(\kappa\) (~0.28 for CoT) indicates length‑isolated solutions; OOD generalization is near zero (Table 2).
- **RNN + CoT:** Recall bottleneck causes high \(N^*\) and \(\kappa \approx 1\), with poor OOD performance similar to transformers.
- **RNN + ACoT:** This is the successful regime: moderate or decreasing \(N^*\) as length grows, \(\kappa \gg 1\), and strong OOD generalization (scores ~1.0).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **What inherent property of transformer attention prevents length‑invariant state representations?** The paper demonstrates low \(\kappa\) but does not identify which specific attention mechanism (e.g., content‑based addressing, lack of recurrence) is responsible. Resolving this could involve replacing attention with simpler weight‑sharing mechanisms or conducting representational similarity analyses across lengths.
- **What causes the recall bottleneck in RNNs under CoT supervision?** The paper attributes RNN failure with CoT to a recall bottleneck but does not test whether this stems from limited hidden‑state capacity or is inherent to generating intermediate outputs after processing the whole sequence. Evidence could come from scaling hidden sizes or augmenting RNNs with external memory.
- **Is the \(\kappa\)–OOD generalization correlation robust across diverse tasks and realistic sequence distributions?** The correlation is established only on two synthetic tasks with uniform random sequences. Extending the analysis to other algorithmic benchmarks (e.g., SCAN, robotics planning) or naturalistic data would test the generality of the finding.

## Limitations
- **Scope limited to synthetic tasks and narrow model zoo.** Results may not generalize to natural language, large pretrained transformers, or more complex sequential problems.
- **Strong dependence on supervision format.** The dramatic swings in performance and \(\kappa\) across outcome, CoT, and ACoT indicate that conclusions are conditional on the learning signal; the underlying theoretical reason for this interaction remains unclear.
- **Reproducibility ambiguities.** Key implementation details (loss function, Dense‑SSM parameterization, tokenization of CoT/ACoT) are not fully specified, requiring assumptions for replication and potentially affecting numeric results.

## Confidence
- **High confidence:** RNNs dramatically outperform transformers on in‑distribution data efficiency for outcome supervision; the performance reversal with CoT/ACoT is consistently observed.
- **Medium confidence:** The measured sharing factor \(\kappa\) values (e.g., \(\kappa\approx0.28\) for Transformer+CoT, \(\kappa\gg1\) for RNN+ACoT) and their strong correlation with OOD length generalization.
- **Low confidence:** The broad theoretical claim that transformers fundamentally lack an inductive bias for incremental state updates, making state‑tracking inherently challenging (this interpretation is indirect and based on performance plus \(\kappa\)).
