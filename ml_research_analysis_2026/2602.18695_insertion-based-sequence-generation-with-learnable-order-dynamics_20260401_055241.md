# Insertion Based Sequence Generation with Learnable Order Dynamics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.18695
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18695
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Learnable order dynamics in discrete flow matching drastically improve exact match accuracy on hard graph traversal (7.4% → 88.1%) and boost molecule validity/quality (98.9%→99.8% validity, 39.5%→62.1% quality) over uniform order.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces LFlexMDM, an insertion-based sequence generation model that learns data-dependent generation orders within a continuous-time Markov chain framework. By parameterizing per-token insertion and unmasking rates with Kumaraswamy cumulative distribution functions, the model enables joint training of a variable-length masked diffusion generator and a target order network without costly trajectory simulation. The method projects target rates defined on an auxiliary fixed-length space onto the generator’s variable-length action space via a weak intertwining construction. Empirical results on star graph traversal and small-molecule generation show that learning a structured order yields substantial gains in accuracy, validity, and quality, though with a noted trade-off in diversity. Training stability is achieved by freezing the unmasking target rate, which leaves sufficient flexibility for the generator to learn relative ordering through insertion dynamics and confidence-based selection.

## Method Summary
LFlexMDM combines Discrete Flow Matching (DFM) with learnable insertion schedules. A target network ϕ ingests a clean padded sequence z¹ and predicts per-position Kumaraswamy parameters (shape b, fixed a) that define insertion and unmasking hazard rates. For a timestep t, two independent particles zₜ are sampled from these rates in an auxiliary space and then projected onto the generator’s variable-length state (xᵗ, sᵗ). The generator network θ, conditioned on (xᵗ, sᵗ), produces per-gap insertion rates and per-masked-position unmasking rates and token distributions. The loss matches θ’s rates to the projected target rates via a Bregman divergence, with gradients for ϕ estimated by REINFORCE leave‑one‑out using the two particles (single autograd pass). Training stabilizes by freezing the unmasking target (bᵤₘ=1) and adding regularization that discourages extremely concentrated or uniform schedules. At inference, τ‑leaping samples events according to θ’s rates; optional confidence‑based gating selects which masked positions to unmask based on the product of unmasking rate and predicted token probability.

## Key Results
- On hard star graphs, LFlexMDM achieves a near-perfect negative correlation (‑0.91) between generation order and junction distance, far surpassing FlexMDM (‑0.22), and boosts exact match accuracy from 7.4% to 88.1%.
- On de novo small‑molecule generation (SAFE dataset), validity increases to 99.8% (from 98.9%) and quality to 62.1% (from 39.5%) over uniform order dynamics, with a slight diversity decrease.
- Freezing the unmasking target rate dramatically stabilizes training; both rates trainable leads to loss divergence, while freezing bᵤₘ preserves expressivity through the insertion schedule and confidence‑guided unmasking.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Learnable order dynamics via parameterized hazard rates enable the generator to adapt insertion/unmasking schedules to data patterns.
- **Mechanism**: Define per‑position insertion and unmasking hazard rates λᵢ⁽ⁱⁿ⁾(t) = (b⁽ⁱⁿ⁾ⁱ a t^{a‑1})/(1‑tᵃ) and similarly λᵢ⁽ᵘᵐ⁾(t) from the derivatives of Kumaraswamy CDFs F(t)=1‑(1‑tᵃ)ᵇ. A target network ϕ predicts b values for each position given the clean sequence. The generator network θ predicts rates to match these targets via a Bregman divergence loss.
- **Core assumption**: The joint distribution of insertion/unmasking times across positions factorizes and can be sampled efficiently (e.g., by inverse transform). Also, the per‑position factorization of conditional probabilities holds (positional independence).
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [abstract] "They parameterize per‑token insertion and unmasking rates using Kumaraswamy CDFs, enabling joint training of a variable‑length masked diffusion generator and target order networks without numerical simulation."
  - [section 3.1.1] "We parameterize b_{ϕ,i}^{in}(z^1) and b_{ϕ,i}^{um}(z^1) by a transformer... set a_in = a_um = a to be a constant, which yields the following closed‑form expressions for the hazard rates."
  - [corpus] Neighbor paper "Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion‑Insertion Processes" also uses insertion‑based diffusion but does not learn data‑dependent schedules; LFlexMDM’s novelty is the learnable rates for order control.
- **Break condition**: If Kumaraswamy parameters become extreme (e.g., b → ∞ or 0) the hazard rates degenerate, causing instability; if factorization assumption is violated in complex dependencies across positions, learned orders may not generalize.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Projected rate matching on an auxiliary alignment space allows a variable‑length insertion generator to be trained as if it operated on fixed‑length aligned sequences.
- **Mechanism**: The auxiliary space Z̄ = (V ∪ {[D],[M]})ᴸ includes [D] placeholders and alignment via position tuples. Target rates are defined on Z̄ with insertion ([D] → [M]) and unmasking ([M] → token). The generator lives on X (variable‑length). They are linked by weak intertwining projection: target conditional rates in Z̄ are projected onto X via a Bayes decoder that marginalizes over latent alignment and dropped positions. Under per‑position independence this yields a mixture of per‑position projected rates.
- **Core assumption**: The encoder‑decoder mapping f_contract and its inverse preserve necessary structural information; projected rates generate the same marginal path on X as intended. The position‑wise factorization remains valid after projection.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [section 2] "Projected DFM: ... construct a conditional rate on an auxiliary space Z, ... then project the rate onto the space of interest X creating weakly intertwined CTMCs."
  - [section 3] "Define the auxiliary space of partial sequences as Z := ⋃_{k∈[[L]]} Vᵏ × Sₖ."
  - [appendix D.3.1] Derived projected rates R^{π,i}_{um} and R^{π,i}_{in} used in the loss.
- **Break condition**: If alignment information in the auxiliary space does not correctly map to the original sequence (e.g., errors in [D] representation), projected rates may not match the generator’s action space, causing optimization mismatch.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Training stability and improved final performance are achieved by freezing the unmasking target rate (setting bᵤₘ = 1) and adding schedule regularization.
- **Mechanism**: Allowing bᵤₘ to be trainable causes unstable loss dynamics. Fixing bᵤₘ = 1 ensures a constant unmasking hazard, stabilizing the ratio of insertion to unmasking rates. The generator still learns relative ordering through token prediction network K_θ and the insertion rates b_in. Regularization penalties encourage the mixture CDFs across positions to be uniform and avoid extreme concentration at endpoints.
- **Core assumption**: Even with fixed bᵤₘ, the generator can learn the desired order because conditioned unmasking probabilities K_θ(i|xᵗ) can vary across positions and time, and confidence‑based selection during inference can further shape the effective order.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 5.1.1] "Interestingly, the performance of the generator improves when the unmasking target rate bum is fixed (/snowflake) as opposed to trainable (ὒ5)... Figure 9 shows the loss comparison... motivates freezing b_um."
  - [appendix D.5.2] Introduces schedule regularization L_reg(ϕ).
  - [section 5.1.1] "Fixing bum still leaves enough room for learning of insertion order... can be elicited even in the unmasking steps by confidence‑based position selection."
- **Break condition**: Over‑regularization may force insertion rates toward uniform, preventing data adaptation; under‑regularization may allow degenerate schedules; in tasks where unmasking timing is critical, a fixed bᵤₘ might limit expressivity.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Discrete Flow Matching (DFM)
- **Why needed**: DFM provides the continuous‑time Markov chain framework with rates that generate a probability path from noise to data. It supplies the training objective (rate matching) that LFlexMDM adapts to its projected and learnable setting.
- **Quick check**: How does the Kolmogorov Forward Equation relate the rate matrix to the time evolution of the marginal probabilities in a CTMC?

### Concept: Per‑position independence assumption
- **Why needed**: The rate matrix for sequences grows exponentially. The assumption that conditional marginals factorize per position yields a sparse mixture where only one token changes per jump. This makes training and sampling tractable for both target and generator rates.
- **Quick check**: In variable‑length sequences with auxiliary alignment, why does the per‑position assumption still hold after projection onto the original space?

### Concept: Kumaraswamy distribution parametrization
- **Why needed**: It offers a flexible two‑parameter family CDF F(t)=1‑(1‑tᵃ)ᵇ with closed‑form inverse and hazard rate λ(t)= a b t^{a‑1}/(1‑tᵃ). For fixed a it simplifies the likelihood pₜ(zᵢ|z¹) and enables efficient sampling in the auxiliary space, critical for the single‑backward‑pass training scheme.
- **Quick check**: Derive the hazard rate function λ(t) from the Kumaraswamy CDF and explain why it is convenient for gradient‑based optimization.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Data (clean sequence z¹) -> Target network ϕ (predicts b_in, b_um) -> Sample t, draw two zₜ from Kumaraswamy marginals -> Project each zₜ to (xᵗ, sᵗ) via f_contract -> Generator network θ (predicts λ_in, λ_um, K_um) -> Compute projected target rates R^{π,ϕ} -> Loss (unmatching + insertion + regularization) -> Single autograd pass -> Gradient updates for θ and ϕ.

### Critical path
1. Sample batch of data sequences z¹.
2. Forward through ϕ to get b_in, b_um.
3. For each, sample t and two copies of zₜ (for RL‑2).
4. Project each zₜ → (xᵗ, sᵗ).
5. Forward (xᵗ, sᵗ) through θ to get λ^θ, K^θ.
6. Compute L_um, L_in, L_reg; combine to L_total.
7. Backward: Compute ∇_θ L and ∇_ϕ L with stop‑gradient as needed (single autograd pass).
8. Parameter updates (Adam).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Flexibility vs stability**: Trainable bᵤₘ yields a larger hypothesis space but can destabilize optimization. Freezing bᵤₘ simplifies and often boosts final performance.
- **Network capacity**: Target network ϕ can be separate or share backbone with θ. Sharing reduces memory but may cause interference; separate performs better on hard tasks.
- **Inference‑time decisions**: Confidence‑based unmasking selection (top‑k by product of λ_um and K_max) improves order fidelity but reduces diversity; nucleus sampling affects validity/quality tradeoff.
- **Auxiliary size**: Larger models for ϕ capture more complex order patterns but require more compute and risk overfitting.

### Failure signatures
- Training loss diverges or oscillates when both b_in and b_um are unconstrained (see Fig 6). Remedy: freeze b_um or increase regularization.
- Validation exact match on graph tasks remains low (<10%) if the generator does not learn proper order indicators (check correlation plots).
- On molecule generation, validity drops sharply if insertion rate network produces extreme rates; monitor length distribution.
- Shared backbone causes performance degradation on tasks requiring distinct dynamics (hard star graph) – monitor separation of losses.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Star Graph Medium** (baseline): Train LFlexMDM with separate ϕ, frozen b_um, no confidence. Expected exact match ~93%, token accuracy ~98%. Confirms basic pipeline and advantage over FlexMDM (~89%).
2. **Star Graph Hard** (order learning): Same setup, compute correlation between normalized generation order and distance from junction. Expect mean correlation ~ -0.91 (vs -0.22 for FlexMDM). Also check exact match >87%.
3. **SAFE Molecule Generation**: Train on SAFE, sample 1000 molecules with nucleus p=0.2, confidence enabled. Expect validity ~99.8%, quality ~62.1%. This confirms learned order improves molecular validity and quality over FlexMDM (98.9% and 39.5%).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can we achieve stable joint training of both insertion and unmasking rates without sacrificing flexibility?
- Do learnable order dynamics yield benefits in sequence generation domains beyond the two studied (star graphs, molecules)?
- How can we maintain the high validity and quality from learned order dynamics while preserving output diversity?

## Limitations
- Tokenization details for star graphs and molecule vocabulary are unspecified, leading to ambiguous reproduction.
- Generator gap representation and confidence‑based unmasking score/selection policy are omitted, impairing replication of inference gains.
- Hyperparameters (Kumaraswamy a, DDiT size, training schedule) are missing, forcing arbitrary choices that may affect performance.
- Core assumptions (positional independence after projection, correct alignment mapping) may not hold for complex sequences, risking breakdown.

## Confidence
- **Learnable order via Kumaraswamy CDFs**: Medium. Equations provided but instability at parameter extremes and factorization assumption threaten validity.
- **Projected rate matching**: Medium. Appendix derivation exists; potential misalignment between auxiliary and generator spaces could invalidate the link.
- **Freezing b_um for stability**: High. Strong empirical evidence (Figure 9) and clear rationale; over‑regularization is the main risk.
- **Overall performance gains (star graphs, molecules)**: Low. Dependent on numerous unspecified design choices; trend plausible but absolute numbers unreliable.

## Next Checks
1. **Gap Representation Ablation**: On star graphs, compare the proposed gap‑averaging against alternatives (e.g., explicit [GAP] tokens) by measuring generation order correlation; this isolates the impact of the generator's gap encoding.
2. **Confidence Selection Isolation**: For SAFE molecules, generate samples with nucleus sampling alone versus adding confidence gating; quantify changes in validity and quality to determine the isolated contribution of confidence‑based unmasking.
3. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity Sweep**: Systematically vary Kumaraswamy constant a (e.g., 1, 2, 5), DDiT hidden dimension (256, 512, 1024), and regularization weight; plot exact match (graphs) and validity (molecules) to identify robust configurations and report variance.
