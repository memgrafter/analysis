# LuxBorrow: From Pompier to Pompjee, Tracing Borrowing in Luxembourgish

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10789  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10789  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** A borrowing-first pipeline (LuxBorrow) distinguishes lexical borrowings from code-switching in Luxembourgish news, finding LU as stable matrix language but with 77.1% of articles mixing donor languages; token-level analysis identifies 25,444 borrowings, mostly morphologically/orthographically adapted and overwhelmingly French-sourced.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces LuxBorrow, a method to disentangle lexical borrowing from code-switching in Luxembourgish (LU) multilingual news text. The pipeline first gates sentence-level language identification to focus on Luxembourgish-dominant sentences, then resolves token-level borrowings using a curated loanword registry, morphological/orthographic adaptation rules, and local context features. Applied to a large corpus of RTL.lu articles (1999–2025), it finds that while Luxembourgish remains the stable matrix language, most articles incorporate foreign elements. However, mixing intensity is low, and borrowings—particularly morphologically adapted French loans—show strong diachronic growth. The authors advocate for borrowing-centric evaluation metrics over document-level mixing indices for multilingual NLP.

## Method Summary
LuxBorrow is a hybrid pipeline. Sentence segmentation is followed by language identification (OpenLID) with a length-adaptive confidence threshold; only sentences classified as Luxembourgish proceed. Within these, tokens are lemmatized/normalized (spellux) and matched against a manually curated loanword lexicon (7,796 entries). Matches are further filtered using compiled morphological/orthographic adaptation patterns and contextual features (foreign run-length, local LU density in a ±3 token window). Borrowings are identified as lexically listed, adapted items in short foreign spans with high local LU density; longer foreign spans are flagged as code-switches. The system outputs token-level labels and computes both document-level mixing (CMI) and borrowing-centric metrics (borrowed token/type rates, donor entropy, assimilation ratios).

## Key Results
- Luxembourgish is the matrix language in all articles, but 77.1% mix in donor languages (primarily French, German, English).
- Token-level analysis identifies 25,444 borrowing instances: 63.8% show morphological adaptation, 35.9% orthographic adaptation, and 0.3% are unadapted lexical forms.
- Diachronically, code-switching increased 37% (1999–2004 vs. 2020–2025), while morphologically adapted borrowings grew 13.2× from a small base, with French supplying 97–99% of borrowings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Sentence-level gating focuses token-level borrowing detection on LU sentences.
The pipeline uses OpenLID with a length-adaptive posterior threshold (base 0.50, raised to 0.80 for short sentences) to filter sentences. Only those labeled LU enter token-level analysis. This reduces noise by assuming most borrowings are integrated into LU syntax, not in pure foreign or heavily mixed sentences. Evidence: Corpus shows LU as dominant matrix language (92.5% of tokens); pipeline design explicitly restricts token detector to LU sentences.

### Mechanism 2: Lexicon-driven pattern matching with contextual cues distinguishes entrenched borrowings from code-switches.
Within LU sentences, tokens are lemmatized and matched against a curated loanword registry. Candidates are evaluated using morphological/orthographic adaptation rules and local context features (foreign run-length, LU density). Short foreign spans with high LU density are classified as borrowings; longer spans become code-switches. This operationalizes the distinction that borrowings are lexically listed and integrated, while code-switches are spontaneous and retain donor-language structure. Evidence: Identification of 25,444 borrowings with detailed adaptation breakdown demonstrates scalable pattern application.

### Mechanism 3: Borrowing-centric metrics provide more actionable evaluation for multilingual NLP than document-level mixing indices.
Instead of summarizing overall multilingualism per document (e.g., CMI), the approach quantifies only the subset of tokens identified as borrowings. Metrics include borrowed token/type rates, donor entropy among borrowings, and assimilation ratios. This isolates the integrated foreign forms that most directly challenge downstream tasks like normalization or MT, rather than稀疏的code-switches. Evidence: Median CMI rises modestly (3.90 to 7.00) despite increased mixing, while morphological borrowings—a small but growing share (0.73% of tokens)—are tracked sensitively by borrowing-centric metrics.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Sentence Segmentation & LID (OpenLID with adaptive threshold) -> Token preprocessing (tokenization, lemmatization via spellux) -> Borrowing resolver: loanword lexicon lookup + morphological/orthographic pattern matcher + contextual feature calculator (foreign run-length, LU density window) -> Statistical analysis module (computes CMI, entropy, M-index, borrowing-specific metrics) -> Output: token-level labels (Native, FR_LOAN, DE_LOAN, EN_LOAN) and aggregated summaries.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Input article → split into sentences.  
  2. For each sentence: OpenLID → if LU (or Other) keep; discard others from token-level analysis.  
  3. For LU sentences: tokenize, lemmatize, normalize each token.  
  4. Check against loanword lexicon; if not found, label Native.  
  5. If found, apply adaptation pattern rules to confirm classification; compute context features.  
  6. Combine lexicon match, pattern, and context to assign final borrowing label or code-switch.  
  7. Aggregate results per article, per domain, per time period.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  Rule-based vs. ML: Rules offer interpretability and work with limited data but may have lower recall and require manual maintenance.  
  Sentence-level gating: Improves precision by assuming borrowings reside in LU sentences, but risks missing borrowings in multilingual sentences where LU is not dominant per LID.  
  Compact lexicon: Keeps system lightweight but may not cover all borrowings, especially novel or domain-specific ones.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  Many tokens labeled Native despite being borrowed → likely lexicon coverage gaps or missing adaptation patterns.  
  High proportion of unadapted lexical borrowings (0.3%) might indicate over-reliance on adaptation rules; some borrowings may enter without adaptation.  
  Inconsistent CMI values in small domains (e.g., CSV Süden with n=1) highlight sensitivity to sample size; metrics unstable for low-volume sections.  
  Error propagation: sentence LID errors cascade to token stage; no recovery mechanism.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Sentence LID validation:** Manually annotate a sample of sentences; measure OpenLID accuracy before/after length-adaptive threshold; quantify impact on downstream borrowing detection.  
  2. **Borrowing detector evaluation:** Create a gold standard by manually labeling random LU sentences. Compute precision, recall, F1 for the rule-based detector; perform error analysis to identify missing lexicon entries or misapplied patterns.  
  3. **Metric stability test:** Compute borrowing token/type rates and assimilation ratios on bootstrapped samples per period to assess confidence intervals; compare against document-level CMI to demonstrate robustness to article count fluctuations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What is the accuracy of the LuxBorrow pipeline in distinguishing lexical borrowings from code-switches, and how does it compare to human annotation?  
  Basis: Limitations state the pipeline is not anchored in a fully manually annotated gold standard, introducing uncertainty. Future Work mentions creating a gold standard to quantify remaining error.  
  Why unresolved: Without a gold standard, precision/recall remain unknown.  
  Evidence needed: Manual annotation of a subset with inter-annotator agreement, followed by error analysis against pipeline output.

- **Open Question 2:** How do borrowing and code-switching patterns differ across language registers (e.g., news vs. social media vs. spoken)?  
  Basis: Future Work proposes adding conversational and social media data to capture rarer adaptation patterns underrepresented in edited news.  
  Why unresolved: Current corpus is limited to professional journalism, which may suppress spontaneous mixing.  
  Evidence needed: Collection and analysis of comparable informal-register data using the same pipeline.

- **Open Question 3:** Does incorporating an explicit borrowing detection feature improve performance on downstream NLP tasks for Luxembourgish?  
  Basis: Future Work plans to evaluate impact of an `is_loan` feature in tasks like normalization, NER, ASR, and MT.  
  Why unresolved: It is unknown whether borrowing signals provide actionable information beyond existing features.  
  Evidence needed: Controlled experiments adding the `is_loan` tag to models for each task, measuring accuracy improvements.

- **Open Question 4:** To what extent are the borrowing patterns from RTL news generalizable to other Luxembourgish language domains?  
  Basis: Limitations note LuxBorrow reflects editorial choices of a single media outlet, not all Luxembourgish use.  
  Why unresolved: Single-source, journalistic corpus may have genre-specific biases.  
  Evidence needed: Replication on diverse corpora (academic, literary, online forums) to compare key metrics.

## Limitations
- The loanword registry (7,796 entries) and exact pattern rules are unreleased, creating a black box for token-level matching.
- Sentence-LID gating parameters (length-adaptive threshold specifics, cutoff N) and contextual feature thresholds are qualitatively described, not quantified.
- No manually annotated gold standard exists to measure precision/recall of the borrowing detector.

## Confidence
- **High:** Diachronic trends (code-switching ↑37%, morphological borrowings ↑13.2×) and donor dominance (FR 97–99%) rely on aggregate counts from a large corpus (259k articles). The borrowing-first conceptual distinction is well-supported.
- **Medium:** Pipeline design (sentence gate → lexicon + patterns → context) is logical and internally consistent, but performance hinges on unreleased components.
- **Low:** Exact token-level counts (25,444 borrowings), adaptation breakdowns (63.8% morph., 35.9% ortho.), and specific metric values depend on unreleased thresholds and lexicon.
