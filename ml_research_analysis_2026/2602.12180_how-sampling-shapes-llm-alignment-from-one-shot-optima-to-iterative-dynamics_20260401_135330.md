# How Sampling Shapes LLM Alignment: From One-Shot Optima to Iterative Dynamics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12180
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12180
- **Reference count:** 8
- **Primary result:** Sampling distributions and reference policy choices critically affect alignment method stability and convergence; fixed sampling fails ranking axioms while instance-dependent sampling can satisfy them, and iterative self-reinforcement may cause oscillations or collapse.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the choice of sampling distribution and reference policy influences preference-based alignment methods such as Identity Preference Optimization (IPO) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). The authors show that in one-shot settings, fixed sampling distributions violate fundamental ranking axioms (Condorcet‑top, Smith‑top, order‑preserving), whereas adaptive instance‑dependent sampling can meet these criteria.

Under structured preferences—particularly strongly transitive ones—skewed or on‑policy sampling amplifies policy concentration, yielding extreme outputs. In iterative alignment where sampling and reference depend on previous policies, the dynamics exhibit persistent oscillations under cyclic preferences or entropy collapse under strongly transitive preferences. The authors derive parameter conditions for convergence (e.g., small α+βλ∥P−½11⊤∥₂) and validate their findings on real‑world data (HelpSteer), identifying self‑reinforcement as a primary cause of instability and collapse.

## Method Summary
The study formalizes alignment as optimizing a policy π over a discrete set of K responses given a fixed preference matrix P (with P_ij = Pr(response i preferred to j)). Two methods are analyzed: IPO, whose closed-form solution is π* ∝ π_ref ⊙ softmax(β P μ); and DPO, which solves an implicit first‑order condition before applying a softmax. In one‑shot settings, the sampling distribution μ is fixed. For iterative alignment (MRS‑IPO/DPO), the authors introduce mixing parameters α and λ: the reference policy becomes π_ref^t ∝ π_t^α π_ref^{1−α}, and the sampling distribution becomes μ_t = λ π_t + (1−λ) π_0, creating a self‑reinforcing loop. Theoretical analysis uses matrix algebra, majorization theory, and dynamical systems. Empirical validation uses the NVIDIA HelpSteer dataset: for each prompt with ≥4 responses, 4 are sampled, and pairwise preferences are derived from randomly chosen attribute scores (helpfulness, correctness, etc.). The preference matrix is classified as cyclic or strongly transitive, and the alignment dynamics are simulated across varying (α, β, λ).

## Key Results
- Fixed sampling distributions in IPO violate basic ranking axioms (Condorcet‑top, Smith‑top, order‑preserving); only instance‑dependent μ can satisfy them.
- Under strongly transitive preferences, skewed or on‑policy sampling amplifies policy concentration, leading to extreme outputs; iterative self‑reinforcement then causes oscillations (cyclic preferences) or entropy collapse (strongly transitive preferences).
- Convergence is guaranteed when α + βλ∥P−½11⊤∥₂ is small; otherwise self‑reinforcement leads to instability or collapse.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Sampling Determines Ranking Compliance
- **Claim:** Fixed sampling distributions in Identity Preference Optimization (IPO) fail basic social choice axioms (Condorcet‑top, Smith‑top, order‑preserving), but instance‑dependent sampling can satisfy them.
- **Mechanism:** The IPO solution is π* ∝ π_ref ⊙ softmax(β P μ). Whether the ranking of π* respects a desirable property depends on the weighted preference vector P μ. A fixed μ (e.g., uniform) does not necessarily preserve order relations that the preference structure P imposes. An adaptive μ that places more mass on responses that are strongly preferred under P can achieve the desired ranking guarantees.
- **Core assumption:** The preference matrix P is fixed and the sampling distribution μ has full support.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “proper instance‑dependent sampling can yield stronger ranking guarantees, while skewed on‑policy sampling can induce excessive concentration under structured preferences.”  
  - [Section 3.1] Proposition 4 (no fixed μ satisfies Condorcet‑top, Smith‑top, order‑preserving) and Theorem 5 (there exists an instance‑dependent μ achieving these).
- **Break condition:** If the sampling distribution is fixed and does not reflect the underlying preference ordering (e.g., uniform), the ranking axioms are violated for some P.

### Mechanism 2: Iterative Self‑Reinforcement Causes Oscillations or Collapse
- **Claim:** In iterative alignment where both the sampling distribution and reference policy depend on the previous learned policy, the dynamics can exhibit persistent oscillations under cyclic preferences or entropy collapse under strongly transitive preferences.
- **Mechanism:** The mixed reference/sampling IPO (MRS‑IPO) update is π_{t+1} = softmax(log π_{t}^{(ref)} + β P μ_t) with μ_t = λ π_t + (1−λ) π_0 and π_{t}^{(ref)} ∝ π_t^α π_ref^{1−α}. This creates a self‑reinforcing loop. Under cyclic P (e.g., rock‑paper‑scissors), the Jacobian at the uniform fixed point has eigenvalues with modulus > 1 when α² + a² β² λ²/3 > 1, causing a rotational field and non‑convergent oscillations. Under strongly transitive P, a positive minimum gap δ ensures θ_i^t − θ_{i+1}^t grows linearly with t when βλ/(1−α) is large, forcing the policy to concentrate on the top action (ε‑collapse or exact collapse when α=1).
- **Core assumption:** The preference structure is either cyclic (non‑transitive) or strongly transitive; parameters α∈[0,1], λ∈[0,1), β>0.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “persistent oscillations under cyclic preferences or entropy collapse toward deterministic policies under strongly transitive ones.”  
  - [Section 4.1] Proposition 12 (explicit instability for K=3 rock‑paper‑scissors) and Theorem 13 (global convergence when α + βλ/2‖P−½11⊤‖₂ < 1, contrasting the collapse regimes).  
  - [Section 4.2] Theorem 14 (ε‑collapse for large βλ/(1−α) and full collapse when α=1).  
  - [Section 5] Experiments on HelpSteer data (Figures 1–4) validate oscillations and collapse.
- **Break condition:** Oscillations occur when the spectral radius condition (e.g., α² + a²β²λ²/3 > 1 for a 3‑cycle) exceeds 1. Collapse occurs when βλ/(1−α) exceeds a threshold inversely proportional to the minimum preference gap δ.

### Mechanism 3: Skewed Sampling Amplifies Policy Concentration
- **Claim:** Under strongly transitive preferences, a sampling distribution that is more aligned with the true ordering (i.e., places more mass on higher‑ranked responses relative to a baseline) yields a more concentrated (extreme) learned policy. This effect compounds in iterative alignment.
- **Mechanism:** For ST P with order 1 ≻ … ≻ K, the logit gap θ_i − θ_j = (P_i − P_j)ᵀ μ. If μ dominates μ′ in the majorization order (μ−μ′ is non‑increasing) and P_i ≻ P_j, then θ_i(μ) − θ_j(μ) > θ_i(μ′) − θ_j(μ′) > 0 (Theorem 10). Thus shifting probability mass toward preferred responses increases separation, making the policy more skewed.
- **Core assumption:** Strongly transitive preference matrix; the variation between two samplings satisfies a majorization‑type ordering.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “skewed or on‑policy sampling can amplify policy concentration, yielding more extreme outputs.”  
  - [Section 3.2] Theorem 10 (majorization implies larger logit gaps) and Corollary 3.2 (concrete BT example with head–tail separation).
- **Break condition:** When the sampling becomes highly on‑policy (λ → 1 in MRS‑IPO) and the preference is ST, the amplification drives the policy toward the top action, especially combined with large β.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Full‑Support Distributions
- **Why needed here:** The theoretical guarantees (uniqueness of IPO/DPO optima, non‑degeneracy of Jacobian, etc.) rely on the interior of the probability simplex: every response must have positive probability in μ and π_ref to avoid boundary issues that could break identifiability or invertibility of critical matrices.
- **Quick check question:** “What could go wrong if μ assigns zero probability to a non‑optimal response in the population objective?”  
  *Answer:* The corresponding row/column may be ignored, potentially giving non‑unique optimizers or violating transitivity assumptions.

### Concept: Strong Transitivity (ST) and Strongly Transitive (SST)
- **Why needed here:** ST ensures that the preference ordering is preserved across all full‑support samplings, allowing clean comparison of concentration. SST (strict gap per row) guarantees positive δ for collapse analysis and order‑preservation for any μ.
- **Quick check question:** “How does ST differ from ordinary transitivity? Give an example of an ST preference matrix.”  
  *Answer:* ST requires P_{i,k} ≥ max(P_{i,j}, P_{j,k}) for i<j<k; ordinary transitivity only needs P_{i,j} ≥ ½ for i<j. An example: a Bradley–Terry model with well‑separated scores.

### Concept: Mixing Parameters α and λ
- **Why needed here:** They control the degree of self‑reinforcement: α governs how much the reference policy tracks the current policy, λ controls the mixture of on‑policy vs. off‑policy sampling. These parameters determine whether the iterative dynamics converge, oscillate, or collapse.
- **Quick check question:** “What is the effect of setting α = 1 and λ = 1 under strongly transitive preferences?”  
  *Answer:* The update reduces to π_{t+1} ∝ π_t ⊙ softmax(β P π_t), which causes entropy collapse to the top action (Theorem 14).

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
The MRS‑IPO/DPO system comprises:
- **Static inputs**: preference matrix **P** ∈ ℝ^{K×K}, initial reference policy π_ref, baseline off‑policy distribution π_0.
- **Parameters**: α (reference mixing), λ (sampling mixing), β (inverse temperature).
- **State per round** t: current policy π_t.
- **Derived quantities**: mixed reference π^{(t)}_ref ∝ π_t^α π_ref^{1−α} (element‑wise); mixed sampling distribution μ_t = λ π_t + (1−λ) π_0.
- **Update**:
  - For IPO: compute v_t = P μ_t, then π_{t+1} = softmax(log π^{(t)}_ref + β v_t).
  - For DPO: find θ such that (P − Q(θ))ᵀ μ_t = 0 (Q is BT matrix from θ), then π_{t+1} = softmax(log π^{(t)}_ref + β θ).

### Critical path
1. Initialization: π_1 ← uniform (often).
2. For t = 1,2,…:
   a. Construct μ_t ← λ π_t + (1−λ) π_0.
   b. Construct π^{(t)}_ref ← normalize(π_t^α ∘ π_ref^{1−α}), where ∘ is element‑wise multiplication.
   c. If using IPO: compute v = P μ_t and logits z = log π^{(t)}_ref + β v.
      If using DPO: solve for θ from the first‑order condition (implicit) and set z = log π^{(t)}_ref + β θ.
   d. Set π_{t+1} = softmax(z).
3. Repeat until convergence or fixed horizon.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Stability vs. adaptability**: Larger α and λ increase responsiveness to the current policy but push the system toward instability or collapse. Smaller β reduces step size, improving stability but slowing learning.
- **Off‑policy mixing**: λ < 1 injects off‑policy data (π_0) to break pure self‑reinforcement and can prevent both oscillations and collapse, at the cost of slower adaptation.
- **Reference smoothing**: α = 0 (fixed reference) is safest; α near 1 couples the reference tightly to the current policy, which can cause collapse if βλ is not sufficiently small relative to preference gaps.

### Failure signatures
- **Cyclic preferences**: Persistent non‑convergent oscillations; policy probabilities rotate. Detectable by time‑averaged variance not decaying to zero.
- **Strongly transitive preferences**: Entropy H(π_t) drops rapidly to near zero; probability concentrates on a single response (policy collapse). Detectable by monitoring H(π_t) across iterations.
- **DPO‑specific**: More sensitive to βλ; may oscillate or collapse even when IPO appears stable due to implicit solver and stronger curvature effects.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Illustrative trajectories** (Section 5.2, Figures 1,2): Choose one cyclic and one ST matrix. Vary α or βλ individually and plot π_t over iterations. Observe convergence (small α,βλ), oscillations (cyclic with large parameters), or collapse (ST with large parameters).
2. **Aggregated amplification** (Section 5.3, Figures 3,4): Over many cyclic matrices, compute cycle strength (time‑averaged variance of π_t) vs. (α,βλ); over many ST matrices, compute final entropy vs. (α,βλ). Verify that increasing α or βλ amplifies oscillations/collapse.
3. **Finite‑sample robustness** (Section E.4): Repeat (2) but add noise: for each preference pair, sample n binary comparisons to estimate P̂. Check that trends persist; note that individual trajectories may vary in phase/amplitude but the regime boundaries remain similar.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
#### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How to design alignment pipelines with principled, adaptive control over sampling and reference updates to avoid instability and collapse?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion explicitly identifies “how to design alignment pipelines with principled, adaptive control over sampling and reference updates” as a key open problem.
- **Why unresolved:** Current analysis assumes fixed mixing parameters (α, λ); adaptive strategies remain uncharacterized both theoretically and empirically.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Formal convergence guarantees for adaptive update rules; systematic experiments comparing adaptive regimes to fixed ones on diverse preference datasets.

#### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How robust are the stability and collapse conditions to finite‑sample estimation error in the preference matrix P?
- **Basis in paper:** Theoretical results assume a known P, while noisy experiments suggest deviations, but no probabilistic bounds are provided.
- **Why unresolved:** Stability thresholds (e.g., α+βλ∥P−½11⊤∥₂ < 1) depend directly on P; finite‑sample variability could invalidate them in practice, an effect not quantified.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Probabilistic convergence analyses under random P estimates; sensitivity bounds linking sample size to condition violation rates; scaling studies across noise levels.

#### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do instability and entropy collapse phenomena extend to other preference‑based alignment methods beyond IPO/DPO, such as RLHF or NLHF?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper claims broader applicability but only analyzes IPO and DPO; RLHF/NLHF involve different algorithmic steps not covered.
- **Why unresolved:** RLHF introduces separate reward modeling and policy gradients; NLHF uses a different aggregation rule. Their iterative dynamics are unknown and may differ qualitatively.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical characterizations of iterative RLHF/NLHF dynamics showing similar oscillatory/collapse regimes; reproduction of findings with generative models.

#### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can alternative reference update mechanisms (e.g., exponential moving average, delayed refresh) prevent entropy collapse in strongly transitive settings?
- **Basis in paper:** Collapse analysis assumes a specific linear mixture (α) for the reference policy; alternative forms might alter the dynamics but are unexplored.
- **Why unresolved:** The collapse condition depends on βλ/(1−α) under linear mixing; more sophisticated anchoring could change scaling but remains speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extension of collapse results to general reference update families; empirical comparison of different update rules on collapse severity and performance.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes full‑support sampling/reference, exact preference matrix P, and clean ST/cyclic structures; real‑world noisy preferences may violate these assumptions.
- DPO’s numerical solver details are underspecified, harming reproducibility.
- Experiments rely on a single dataset (HelpSteer); generalizability to other domains or preference structures remains unknown.
