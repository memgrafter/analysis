# From Reviews to Requirements: Can LLMs Generate Human-Like User Stories?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.28163
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28163
- **Reference count:** 24
- **Primary result:** LLMs (especially Gemini 2.0 Flash with two-shot prompting) can generate user stories from app reviews that match or exceed human experts in readability, understandability, and specifiability, but consistently struggle with independence and uniqueness.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether Large Language Models (LLMs) can automatically convert unstructured mobile app store reviews into well-formed agile user stories. Using a dataset of 1,000 health app reviews, the authors generate stories with three LLMs (GPT-3.5 Turbo, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Mistral 7B Instruct) under zero-, one-, and two-shot prompting conditions. Quality is assessed via a fine-tuned RoBERTa classifier using the QUS criteria and human evaluators using the RUST framework. Results show that few-shot prompting significantly improves overall quality, with top LLM performance rivaling human experts on several dimensions. However, all LLMs underperform humans in producing independent and unique stories, highlighting a key limitation for backlog generation. The work demonstrates the potential of LLMs to automate requirements elicitation from user feedback while underscoring the need for human oversight to ensure diversity and actionability.

## Method Summary
The study uses the Mini-BAR dataset of 1,000 preprocessed English health app reviews (length 40-320 characters, emoji-handled) as input. Three LLMs—GPT-3.5 Turbo, Gemini 2.0 Flash, and Mistral 7B Instruct—are prompted to convert each review into a canonical user story ("As a [role] I want [feature] so that [benefit]") using zero-, one-, and two-shot strategies (increasing example pairs). Quality is evaluated through two lenses: (1) an automated fine-tuned RoBERTa-base classifier trained on the UStAI dataset, which scores outputs across nine QUS criteria (e.g., atomic, minimal, independent) and provides an overall quality probability; (2) human evaluation via a web platform where three agile-experienced raters score 100 stories per condition using the RUST framework's 1-5 Likert scales for readability, understandability, specifiability, and technical aspects. Statistical analysis (Kruskal-Wallis, pairwise tests) compares model and prompt performance against a human expert baseline.

## Key Results
- Few-shot prompting significantly improves overall automated quality scores; Gemini 2.0 Flash with two-shot achieves the highest score (~0.663) and RUST mean (4.53), matching or exceeding human experts in readability, understandability, and specifiability.
- All LLMs consistently score lower than human experts on independence and uniqueness (e.g., human independence: 0.32 vs. LLM best: ~0.28), indicating a core weakness in generating a non-redundant backlog.
- Model-specific behavior is observed: Mistral 7B Instruct shows an inverse trend where non-canonical output increases with more shots, while larger models (GPT-3.5, Gemini) improve monotonically.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Few-shot prompting causally improves LLM-generated user story quality by providing structural exemplars that guide format adherence and content abstraction.
- **Mechanism:** Example pairs in the prompt act as in-context learning demonstrations, allowing the model to infer the canonical template and appropriate granularity. This reduces hallucination and off-template generation by steering the model's latent knowledge of user story syntax.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "especially when few-shot prompting is used"; [section 3.2 & 4.2] Table 3/Figure 3 show two-shot significantly outperforms zero-shot (p<0.0001).
- **Break condition:** If reviews are too noisy or examples are misaligned (e.g., different domain), guidance fails, leading to irrelevant or malformed stories.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Model architecture and scale determine sensitivity to prompt strategy; larger instruction-tuned models (Gemini, GPT-3.5) improve with more shots, while a smaller model (Mistral 7B) may degrade.
- **Mechanism:** Larger models have greater capacity to interpret and replicate demonstrated patterns without overfitting to surface features. Smaller models may mimic lexical patterns from examples rather than underlying structure, increasing non-canonical output.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.1] Figure 2 shows Mistral 7B's non-canonical output increases with shots; [section 4.2] Table 2 shows quality gains for Gemini/GPT-3.5 from zero→two-shot, not for Mistral.
- **Break condition:** Using a small, base model without instruction tuning likely fails to benefit from few-shot prompting for this structured task.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** LLMs excel at linguistic/structural quality but fail at conceptual independence/uniqueness because they optimize for local review-to-story transformation without global backlog awareness.
- **Mechanism:** Generation is per-review and stateless; the model lacks a memory or constraint system to track previously generated stories, leading to semantic redundancy when reviews share themes. Independence is a global backlog property, while LLM generation is a local process.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "they still struggle to produce independent and unique user stories"; [section 4.2] Table 2 shows human scores for Independence (0.32) & Unique (0.33) are higher than any LLM (~0.26-0.28, ~0.29-0.31).
- **Break condition:** If the input review corpus has high semantic diversity, local generation might coincidentally produce independent stories; failure is likely with thematic overlap.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Prompt Engineering (Few-Shot Learning)**
  - **Why needed:** The primary lever for improving quality is prompt strategy; understanding how exemplars condition output is key to applying the method.
  - **Quick check:** If adding two well-chosen example pairs improves format adherence, what is a likely risk of adding ten poorly-chosen examples?
- **Concept: User Story Quality Frameworks (QUS & RUST)**
  - **Why needed:** These multi-dimensional criteria define "high-quality" stories; the paper's evaluation depends entirely on them.
  - **Quick check:** A story is grammatical and follows the template but describes the same feature as two other stories. Which QUS criterion is violated?
- **Concept: Dataset Preprocessing for Noisy User-Generated Text**
  - **Why needed:** Input is raw app reviews; preprocessing (length, emoji filters) is critical to provide coherent, context-rich inputs to the LLM.
  - **Quick check:** Why might discarding a review with "more emojis than tokens" be a reasonable heuristic before LLM generation?
- **Concept: In-Context Learning (ICL)**
  - **Why needed:** Explains how LLMs use provided examples to adapt to a task without parameter updates; central to the few-shot mechanism.
  - **Quick check:** If the ICL examples all use the role "user" but the test review describes a "developer," what potential issue might arise?
- **Concept: Automated vs. Human Evaluation Alignment**
  - **Why needed:** The study relies on a fine-tuned RoBERTa for automated scoring; understanding its alignment with human judgment (RUST) is crucial for trusting results.
  - **Quick check:** If the RoBERTa classifier strongly correlates with grammaticality but weakly with "actionability," what might this mean for the overall quality score?

## Architecture Onboarding
Input Preprocessor -> Prompt Builder -> LLM Gateway -> (Automated Evaluator *or* Human Platform) -> Analyzer

- **Critical path:** Preprocessed Review → Prompt Builder → LLM Gateway → (Automated Evaluator *or* Human Platform) → Analyzer. The human evaluation path is slower but provides the key benchmark.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Speed vs. Depth:** Automated evaluation (RoBERTa) allows rapid iteration but may miss nuanced aspects captured by human RUST scores.
  - **Model Size vs. Cost:** Gemini 2.0 Flash & GPT-3.5 Turbo are larger/expensive but perform best; Mistral 7B is cheap but shows odd behavior (non-canonical increase with shots).
  - **Per-Review vs. Batch:** Current design generates one story per review statelessly; a batch-aware design could enforce independence but increases complexity.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High rate of non-canonical outputs (missing "As a", "so that").
  - Low automated scores on Independence/Uniqueness despite high overall quality.
  - High variance in human RUST scores across evaluators for the same story (indicates ambiguity).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Prompt Sensitivity Sweep:** For one model (e.g., GPT-3.5), generate stories from 50 reviews using zero, one, two, three, and five-shot prompts. Compute average QUS score and % canonical. Validate the paper's finding that two-shot is optimal.
  2. **Model-Architecture Contrast:** Run the same two-shot prompt on all three models. Specifically check for the Mistral 7B inverse trend (non-canonical % increase with shots) documented in Figure 2.
  3. **Evaluation Alignment Check:** Take 50 stories from Exp 1. Have them scored by the RoBERTa classifier and by 2 human evaluators using RUST. Calculate correlation between automated overall score and human mean score. The paper assumes alignment; this experiment tests that assumption directly.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Cross-domain and cross-lingual generalization:** To what extent can LLM-generated user stories generalize across different software domains and languages? The study is limited to English health app reviews.
- **Improving independence and uniqueness:** What specific prompting techniques or post-processing methods can most effectively improve the independence and uniqueness of LLM-generated user stories? Few-shot prompting alone did not close the gap with humans.
- **Integration into agile workflows:** How can LLM-generated user stories be effectively integrated and maintained within real-world agile development workflows and toolchains? The research stops at generation and static evaluation.
- **Backlog evolution resilience:** To what degree do LLM-generated user stories retain their quality and independence after iterative refinement and prioritization in a dynamic product backlog? The study evaluates initial quality in isolation.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility blockers:** No public fine-tuned RoBERTa checkpoint or exact UStAI data splits; human eval protocol details (screening, rubric anchors) are underspecified.
- **Domain specificity:** Findings may not generalize beyond health app reviews (Mini-BAR) due to domain-specific language and user expectations.
- **Corpus maturity:** Neighboring work has low citations/FMR, suggesting limited independent validation of review-to-story generation pipelines.

## Confidence
- **Few-shot prompting improves quality:** High confidence (statistically significant results, p<0.0001; replicated across models).
- **LLMs struggle with independence/uniqueness:** Medium confidence (consistent gap vs. humans, but mechanism inferred from per-review design; not explicitly ablated).
- **Model-specific few-shot sensitivity (Mistral anomaly):** Medium confidence (observed in data, but small sample may not capture full variance).

## Next Checks
1. **Evaluate RoBERTa-human alignment:** Correlate automated QUS scores with human RUST ratings on 100 stories. Low correlation would undermine the primary automated metric.
2. **Prompt example quality ablation:** Test whether *misaligned* few-shot examples (different domain/template) degrade quality versus *aligned* ones, validating the exemplar guidance mechanism.
3. **Cross-domain transfer test:** Apply the best-performing two-shot prompt to a non-health app review dataset (e.g., Google Play reviews) to assess generalizability beyond Mini-BAR.
