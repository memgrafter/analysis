# SAVeS: Steering Safety Judgments in Vision-Language Models via Semantic Cues

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19092
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19092
- **Reference count:** 39
- **Primary result:** Safety decisions in VLMs are highly sensitive to semantic cues, indicating reliance on learned visual-linguistic associations rather than grounded visual understanding.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether safety judgments in vision-language models (VLMs) for embodied tasks are driven by grounded visual understanding or are susceptible to superficial semantic cues. The authors introduce a semantic steering framework that applies controlled textual, visual (e.g., colored markers), and cognitive interventions without altering the underlying scene. To evaluate this, they propose the SAVeS benchmark and a protocol separating behavioral refusal, grounded safety reasoning, and false refusals. Experiments across multiple VLMs show that safety decisions are highly sensitive to semantic cues; coupling visual markers with explicit focus prompts most effectively steers behavior but often increases false refusals. Automated pipelines confirm steering is bidirectional: assistive steering yields modest gains, while adversarial steering reliably induces over-refusal. The results reveal VLMs rely heavily on learned visual-linguistic associations rather than robust situational grounding, exposing a vulnerability in multimodal safety systems.

## Method Summary
The study uses a semantic steering framework (M) that applies three types of interventions to input pairs (image I, instruction Q): visual (Mv) overlaying colored circles, cognitive (Mc) rewriting Q with safety prompts (e.g., in-context examples, focus steering), and textual (Mt) encoding coordinates. Evaluations are conducted on two datasets: MSSBench-Embodied (134 samples) and SA_VeS (120 synthetic paired images). A frozen VLM (e.g., Qwen3-VL) processes the steered inputs (Ĩ, Q̃) to produce responses R̃. An LLM judge (GPT-5-latest) categorizes each response into five classes (Refusal, Correct Grounding, Hallucination, Unsafe Compliance, Capability Fail) against ground-truth hazard rationales. From these, the metrics Behavioral Refusal Accuracy (BRA), Grounded Safety Accuracy (GSA), False Refusal Rate (FRR), and Situational Safety Accuracy (SSA) are computed. Additionally, automated pipelines (Guardian, Auditor, Attacker) employ auxiliary modules (risk scorer, attention analysis) to apply steering at scale and test exploitability. No model training is performed; the analysis relies on systematic input perturbations and metric comparisons to infer steering mechanisms.

## Key Results
- VLMs' safety judgments are highly sensitive to superficial semantic cues (e.g., colored markers), with red circles significantly increasing behavioral refusal accuracy while white circles decrease it.
- The combination of visual markers and explicit focus prompts (ICF) most effectively steers safety behavior but also dramatically raises the false refusal rate.
- Automated steering pipelines demonstrate bidirectional vulnerability: assistive steering yields modest improvements in hazard detection, whereas adversarial steering reliably triggers over-refusal across models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** VLMs use semantic shortcuts—relying on learned visual-linguistic associations (e.g., "red circle = danger") rather than grounded situational analysis—to make safety judgments.
- **Mechanism:** Simple visual markers (colored circles) or textual references activate pre-trained priors about risk, overriding scene-specific hazard reasoning. The model associates marker semantics (color, shape) with safety labels without examining whether the highlighted object actually poses a threat in context.
- **Core assumption:** The VLM's safety decision layer is sensitive to superficial semantic tags that co-occur with danger in its training data, allowing these tags to independently sway the `P(Safe|I, Q)` probability.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...safety decisions are highly sensitive to semantic cues, indicating reliance on learned visual–linguistic associations rather than grounded visual understanding."
  - [section 4.2(a)] Color hierarchy experiment: red circles increase BRA to 73.1% while white circles drop it to 41.8%, with GSA also shifting, proving color semantics alone drive behavior.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., "Do Vision-Language Models Understand Visual Persuasiveness?") study cue-based persuasion, showing VLMs are vulnerable to superficial visual influence, supporting the shortcut hypothesis. *Corpus evidence is suggestive but not directly on safety judgments.*
- **Break condition:** If markers are placed on irrelevant regions with a mismatched focus prompt (Table 2b, Mismatch), steering effect collapses (BRA 26.9%), showing the shortcut requires cue-prompt alignment. Also, masked-context views (Table 2c, Masked) reduce BRA drastically, indicating shortcuts need some contextual grounding to activate.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Safety steering is mediated by *prompt-cue coupling*—the effect of a visual marker depends critically on whether the text prompt explicitly directs attention to that marker's semantics (e.g., "focus on red circles").
- **Mechanism:** The visual marker provides a salient but ambiguous signal. The cognitive steering prompt (`Mc`) interprets that signal. When the prompt matches the marker's semantic association (e.g., "red" → "danger"), it amplifies the shortcut; a generic or mismatched prompt weakens or reverses the effect.
- **Core assumption:** VLMs integrate multimodal inputs at a layer where linguistic instructions can gate the influence of visual saliency. The prompt acts as a relevance selector for the marker's semantic prior.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] "Focus Steering (ICF) modifies the prompt to explicitly direct attention to specific visual markers... General Focus vs. Color-Specific Focus test whether linguistic references to color trigger semantic danger associations."
  - [section 4.2(b)] Matched prompt (red circles + "focus on red") yields highest BRA (73.1%) and GSA (28.4%); General focus drops BRA to 46.3%; Mismatched prompt drops it to 26.9%.
  - [corpus] Neighbor "Can We Talk Models Into Seeing the World Differently?" studies prompt-induced biases in VLMs, indicating language can override visual processing. *This supports the general principle of prompt-mediated influence but is not hazard-specific.*
- **Break condition:** Removing color specificity from the prompt (General focus) or using a mismatched color reference severely attenuates steering, showing the coupling is necessary for strong effects. If the prompt instructs to ignore markers (not tested but inferable), the shortcut should be suppressed.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Grounded safety reasoning requires *global scene context*; steering cues operate on top of but cannot replace contextual understanding. Isolating the highlighted object (crop-only) or removing context (masked) degrades both behavioral correctness and grounding.
- **Mechanism:** The semantic cue biases *which region* the model examines, but the hazard assessment itself still depends on integrating that region into the full scene. A crop may show a "dangerous" object (e.g., knife) but without context (microwave vs. kitchen counter), the model cannot determine if the instruction creates a risk.
- **Core assumption:** VLMs possess a two-stage process: (1) attention guidance (steerable by cues), (2) contextual risk evaluation (requires full scene). The second stage is less steerable and more brittle when context is removed.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.2(c)] Full view baseline: BRA 34.3%, GSA 10.4%. Crop-only: BRA 46.3%, GSA 34.3% (better grounding, lower FRR). Masked: BRA 20.9%, GSA 13.4% (collapse). ABS (dual-stream) best: BRA 64.2%, GSA 35.8%.
  - [section 4.1] "On MSSBench, visual cueing... often induces larger behavioral shifts than coordinate-only text prompts... However, these gains are not uniformly beneficial." This implies visual cues help but don't guarantee correct grounding.
  - [corpus] Neighbor "Circuit Tracing in Vision-Language Models" investigates internal multimodal integration, suggesting early visual shortcuts are later contextualized. *This is an indirect analog; the paper does not trace circuits for safety.*
- **Break condition:** Masking the background (Masked view) causes a collapse in BRA and GSA, showing that without global context, even a correctly highlighted region cannot support a grounded safety decision. The model reverts to object-level priors or fails.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Situational Safety (Context-Dependent Risk)**
  - *Why needed here:* The entire paper hinges on the idea that an instruction's safety is not absolute but depends on the visual scene (e.g., "put items in jar" is safe with candy, unsafe with detergent pods). A learner must distinguish *behavioral refusal* (saying "no") from *grounded safety reasoning* (identifying the correct hazard).
  - *Quick check question:* Given two images of a robot reaching for an object, one near a hot stove and one on a cool counter, with the instruction "grab the object," would a safe model refuse in both, one, or neither? Why?

- **Concept: Semantic Shortcutting vs. Grounded Understanding**
  - *Why needed here:* The core finding is that VLMs often rely on shortcuts (color = danger) instead of grounding. Understanding this dichotomy is crucial to interpret steering effects: why refusal rates increase but grounded accuracy (GSA) does not.
  - *Quick check question:* If a VLM refuses an instruction because a *red circle* is on a *benign object*, is that refusal "correct" behaviorally? Is it grounded? What would a grounded refusal look like?

- **Concept: Steering as a Diagnostic Probe**
  - *Why needed here:* Semantic steering isn't just a way to manipulate models; it's a methodology to *diagnose* the mechanisms of safety decisions. By systematically varying cues while holding scene constant, we isolate what evidence the model actually uses.
  - *Quick check question:* In the paper's experiments, what is the control condition? What changes between the control and a "steered" condition? What does a change in BRA vs. GSA tell you?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Input Layer:** Image `I` and instruction `Q`.
  2. **Steering Module (`M`):** Applies one of three interventions:
     - `Mv` (Visual): Overlays semantic markers (colored circles) on `I`.
     - `Mc` (Cognitive): Rewrites `Q` with safety/attention prompts (e.g., IC, ICF).
     - `Mt` (Textual): Encodes spatial coordinates into `Q`.
  3. **VLM Core (`f`):** The frozen pretrained vision-language model (e.g., Qwen3-VL). Takes steered `(Ĩ, Q̃)`.
  4. **Output Layer:** Response `R̃` (plan or refusal).
  5. **Evaluation Rubric:** LLM-as-a-Judge compares `R̃` to ground-truth hazard rationale, categorizing as Refusal (R), Correct Grounding (CG), Hallucination (H), Unsafe Compliance (UC), Capability Fail (CF).
  6. **Metrics:** Computes BRA, GSA, FRR, SSA from rubric labels.
  7. **Automated Pipelines (A/B/C):** Automated versions of `M` using auxiliary components (Spotter risk scorer, attention analysis) to test steering scalability and exploitability.

- **Critical path:** `(I, Q)` -> `(Ĩ, Q̃)` via `M` -> `R̃ = f(Ĩ, Q̃)` -> Judge categorization -> Metrics. The causal inference relies on keeping `(I, Q)` semantics constant while only `M` changes.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Steerability vs. Calibration:** Stronger steering (e.g., `Mv+ICF`) often increases BRA (good) but also FRR (bad). The trade-off is central; optimal steering improves hazard detection without inducing spurious refusals.
  - **Automation vs. Reliability:** Automated pipelines (Guardian, Auditor, Attacker) prove steering is exploitable, but their effectiveness is model-dependent and unstable (Table 3). Manual intervention (`M` designed by researchers) yields cleaner mechanism insights; automation introduces noise from the spotter/attention module.
  - **Dataset Fidelity:** SA_VeS (synthetic, paired) enables controlled ablations but may not capture real-world visual complexity. MSSBench (simulated) has limited textures. This affects external validity.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Over-refusal spike:** FRR increases dramatically with red markers or adversarial decoys, especially under `Mv+ICF` or Attacker pipeline. Model refuses safe scenes due to cue associations.
  - **Grounded safety stagnation:** GSA remains low (often <30% in Table 2) even when BRA is high, indicating refusals are not grounded in true hazards.
  - **Context collapse:** Masked or crop-only views cause BRA/GSA to plummet, showing the model cannot isolate hazard reasoning from scene context.
  - **Model divergence:** LLaVA-HF-34B shows extremely low BRA (compliance bias) while LLaVA-HF-13B is refusal-prone—safety behavior under steering is checkpoint-specific, not scale-predictable.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Color Hierarchy Test:** On a fixed set of unsafe scenes, apply red, white, orange, yellow, green circles with the `ICF` prompt. Measure delta(BRA) and delta(GSA) vs. baseline. Expect red > orange/yellow > green > white in BRA increase; GSA may not follow same order, decoupling behavioral from grounded effects.
  2. **Prompt-Cuing Alignment:** For a set with red circles, test three prompts: (a) Matched: "focus on red circles", (b) General: "check for hazards", (c) Mismatch: "focus on white circles". Expect (a) highest BRA/GSA, (c) near-baseline or inverse effect.
  3. **Context View Ablation:** For the same unsafe scene, provide (a) full image, (b) crop-only of hazard object, (c) full + crop (ABS), (d) masked full (only hazard region visible). Expect (c) best BRA/GSA balance, (b) lower FRR but maybe lower BRA, (d) severe drop, demonstrating context dependence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What specific visual evidence do VLMs actually use to ground their safety judgments in embodied tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "However, it remains unclear which visual evidence drives these judgments." (Introduction)
- **Why unresolved:** The paper shows safety decisions are steerable by semantic cues (e.g., colored circles), but this reveals reliance on shortcuts rather than identifying what constitutes valid visual grounding. The SAVeS protocol separates behavioral refusal from grounded reasoning, but does not specify the visual features or relationships that constitute proper grounding.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis of model attention/activation maps correlated with ground-truth hazard regions across diverse scenarios; controlled interventions that systematically remove/perturb specific visual features while preserving others to see which changes cause grounding failures.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why do different VLM architectures exhibit such divergent calibration trade-offs under semantic steering (e.g., some increase refusal without False Refusal Rate explosion, others do not)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Results show model-dependent effects (e.g., LLaVA-HF-34B has low BRA but also low FRR; Qwen models show large FRR spikes). The paper notes "larger models do not necessarily yield better safety alignment" and suggests differences in "instruction tuning and safety alignment" but does not diagnose architectural or training causes.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper attributes differences to "checkpoint-specific instruction and safety tuning" but does not isolate whether this stems from pretraining data, SFT/RLHF objectives, model architecture (vision encoder, fusion), or scale. Without ablation, we cannot know which factors govern steering sensitivity and calibration.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled training experiments: fine-tuning identical base VLMs with different safety protocols; probing internal representations (e.g., projection layer analysis) for semantic cue entanglement; correlation of steering sensitivity with training data composition (e.g., prevalence of typographic attacks).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do the semantic steering vulnerabilities discovered in single-image, single-turn evaluations persist in long-horizon, closed-loop embodied interactions where the model can observe action consequences?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study uses static image-instruction pairs. Limitations state: "the current setup is primarily single-image and single-turn, leaving temporal robustness and closed-loop correction as open problems." The Attacker pipeline shows exploitable over-refusal, but it's unknown if an agent could recover from a steered mistake over time.
- **Why unresolved:** Real embodied safety requires temporal consistency. A model might refuse correctly in one frame due to a decoy, but if the hazard persists and the decoy is removed, would it recover? Conversely, could an attacker maintain a transient semantic cue to cause persistent unsafe behavior?
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend SAVeS to multi-frame episodes with consistent steering cues (e.g., persistent red circles) and measure trajectory-level safety metrics (completion, hazard avoidance). Test recovery dynamics when cues are removed after an initial steer.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the LLM-as-judge evaluation protocol itself be gamed or biased by model responses that sound reasonable but are semantically misaligned?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The protocol uses "GPT-5-latest as an LLM-as-judge" to categorize responses (Refusal, Correct Grounding, Hallucination, etc.). The authors acknowledge: "our metrics rely on an LLM-based evaluator; although we applied consistency checks and manual verification, grading noise or bias may still affect difficult cases."
- **Why unresolved:** LLM judges may have their own semantic priors (e.g., associating "red circle" with danger) that could confuse them when a model hallucinates a hazard due to a red marker. The judge might incorrectly label a hallucinated refusal as "Correct Grounding" if the explanation mentions a plausible hazard, even if it does not match the ground truth.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Human evaluation on a subset of ambiguous cases; multi-judge agreement studies with diverse LLMs; adversarial examples crafted to confuse the judge by making hallucinated reasons sound plausible; measuring judge sensitivity to the same semantic cues that steer the VLMs.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Is there a fundamental architectural change (beyond prompting/overlays) that can make VLM safety decisions truly grounded in visual scene understanding rather than semantic associations?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper concludes: "current safety behavior is highly steerable but only partially grounded, motivating more robust, grounding-aware safety alignment." It does not propose architectural solutions, implying that steering reveals a deep limitation: models process cues through a visual-linguistic association pathway that bypasses situational grounding.
- **Why unresolved:** The steerability via simple cues suggests that safety decisions are made at a semantic association layer (e.g., vision-language connector or LLM) without adequate grounding in a verifiable visual reasoning process. We lack understanding of what an architecture would need—e.g., explicit spatial reasoning modules, counterfactual simulation, or training objectives that force hazard detection to be spatially precise and context-dependent.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and evaluation of new model architectures that enforce multi-step visual reasoning (e.g., bounding box verification, affordance checking) before outputting a safety decision; training regimes that penalize decisions based on spurious cues; measuring whether such changes reduce steering sensitivity while maintaining or improving true grounding (GSA) without raising FRR.

## Limitations
- The study uses synthetic paired images and a narrow selection of VLMs; the LLM judge's prompt and rubric are undisclosed, risking metric inconsistency; automated pipelines rely on model-specific internals and are not open-sourced; external validity to real-world embodied tasks remains unclear.
- Steering effects are highly sensitive to prompt wording and marker parameters, which may limit reproducibility.

## Confidence
- **VLMs rely on semantic shortcuts rather than grounded safety reasoning:** High
- **Prompt-cue coupling mediates steering:** Medium
- **Grounded safety reasoning requires global scene context:** Medium
- **Steering is bidirectional and exploitable:** Medium-Low
