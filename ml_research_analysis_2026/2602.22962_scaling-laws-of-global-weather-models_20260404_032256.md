# Scaling Laws of Global Weather Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22962
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22962
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Data-driven global weather models exhibit distinct scaling laws: Aurora achieves the strongest data-scaling efficiency (β≈0.51), compute-optimal training favors longer durations over larger models, and wider architectures consistently outperform deeper ones for fixed parameter counts.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive scaling law analysis for state-of-the-art global weather models (Aurora, GraphCast, Pangu, SFNO, AIFS) trained under unified conditions on ERA5 data. The authors systematically vary model size (N), dataset size (D), and compute budget (C) to measure how validation loss scales. Key findings include: Aurora's data-scaling exponent β≈0.51 implies that a 10× increase in training data reduces loss by up to 3.2×; across models, allocating compute to longer training yields better performance than expanding model size; and when parameter counts are fixed, wider architectures dominate deeper ones. The study also reveals that weather models differ fundamentally from language models in scaling behavior and that variable-specific scaling is heterogeneous, making aggregate loss a rough performance indicator. These results provide actionable guidance for building more efficient weather forecasting systems.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct controlled experiments by training variants of five weather models over a range of parameter counts (N), dataset sizes (D), and compute budgets (C). For each model family, they generate configurations with systematic width/depth trade-offs while keeping N approximately constant, and train on subsets of ERA5 ranging from 10 TB to 100 TB. Validation loss on a held-out 2021 ERA5 slice is computed using a weighted sum across atmospheric variables (Eq. 1). Power-law relationships L(D)=αD^{-β} and L(N)=γN^{-δ} are fitted via log-log linear regression. Compute-optimal frontiers are explored by varying N and D under approximately constant C, and the resulting parabolic loss curves identify optimal resource allocation. Variable-specific scaling is assessed by decomposing the aggregate loss (Eq. 2). Hardware utilization is measured as the ratio of theoretical to achieved FLOPs during training.

## Key Results
- **Data-scaling efficiency:** Aurora achieves β≈0.51, indicating strong gains from more training data; a 10× increase in D reduces loss by up to 3.2×.
- **Compute-optimal allocation:** For fixed compute, longer training (larger D) yields greater performance improvements than increasing model size (N).
- **Architectural preference:** Across all models, wider configurations consistently achieve lower validation loss than deeper ones when parameter counts are equal, contrasting with language models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Aurora achieves superior data-scaling efficiency (β ≈ 0.51) because its 3D tokenization and unified attention preserve vertical atmospheric coupling, enabling more effective extraction of information from additional training data.
- **Mechanism:** The model processes the entire atmosphere (surface + upper-air) in a single 3D token grid, avoiding variable splitting used by other models. This holistic representation captures coupled dynamics, allowing validation loss to decrease more rapidly with D.
- **Core assumption:** Atmospheric variables at different pressure levels are strongly interconnected; a model that maintains these interactions during representation learning scales more effectively with data.
- **Evidence anchors:** Figure 2 shows Aurora’s loss decreasing steepest with D; Table 1 reports β values. The paper notes a 10× data increase reduces loss by up to 3.2× for Aurora.
- **Break condition:** If training data distribution diverges from ERA5 (e.g., different reanalysis), the coupling advantage may diminish, reducing β.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Under fixed compute budget C, allocating resources to longer training (larger D) yields greater performance gains than increasing model size (N), because weather models are often data-limited rather than capacity-limited in practical regimes.
- **Mechanism:** Compute-optimal frontier analysis (Figure 5) shows performance minima occur at relatively smaller N and larger D. Larger models train slowly with limited steps, making under-training costly; moderate models trained longer extract more value from available compute.
- **Core assumption:** Validation loss L(C) exhibits a convex relationship along constant-C curves; the minimum lies toward the D-dominant side for studied models/resolutions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.3 indicate that for Aurora and GraphCast, parabolas appear primarily in the left half, and optimal strategy favors increasing D over N.
- **Break condition:** If models converge before optimal D for given N, further D yields negligible gains, shifting optimum toward larger N.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Weather forecasting models consistently favor wider architectures (increased width) over deeper ones when parameter count N is fixed, because the 6-hour prediction task is well-approximated by linear or shallow nonlinear operators.
- **Mechanism:** Increasing width expands representational capacity in latent space, crucial for capturing high-dimensional atmospheric state. Additional depth introduces more nonlinear transformations (message-passing steps, transformer layers) that provide diminishing returns for short-term dynamics.
- **Core assumption:** Mapping from initial state to 6-hour forecast benefits more from expanded basis functions (width) than from sequential composition (depth).
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.2 show wider variants achieve lower loss across all models; GraphCast and SFNO perform well even at depth=1.
- **Break condition:** Extending forecast horizon significantly (e.g., >10 days) may make deeper architectures necessary to model accumulated nonlinearities, reversing width-preference.

## Foundational Learning
- **Neural scaling laws (power-law relationships):** These underpin the entire analysis; fitting L(D)=αD^{-β} and L(N)=γN^{-δ} reveals how performance improves with data and model size. Without them, exponents β/δ and compute-optimal frontiers would be meaningless. Quick check: What does a higher β imply? It means loss drops more steeply as dataset size grows, indicating stronger data scaling.
- **Compute-optimal scaling (N vs. D trade-off):** This concept guides the allocation of a fixed compute budget C between model size (N) and training steps (D). The paper finds that for weather models, the optimum lies toward larger D, i.e., longer training. Quick check: If C is fixed and N is doubled, what must happen to D to remain on the compute-optimal frontier (assuming standard scaling a+b=1)? D would be halved (since C ∝ N·D).
- **Hardware utilization and practical efficiency:** The stark contrast between GraphCast’s ~0% and Aurora’s 37% GPU utilization shows that theoretical parameter efficiency does not guarantee practical scalability. This distinction is crucial for real-world deployment. Quick check: Why might a model with superior β still be less suitable for large-scale training? Because low hardware utilization can make training prohibitively slow or expensive, offsetting raw parameter efficiency gains.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Data pipeline: ERA5 (0.25° grid, 6-hourly) → unified validation loss (Eq. 1) with variable weights. Model families: Swin Transformer (Aurora, Pangu), Graph Neural Network (GraphCast), Graph Transformer (AIFS), Spherical Fourier Neural Operator (SFNO). Key hyperparameters: width (model dimension), depth (layers/steps), resolution (fixed at 0.25°), compute budget C (Pflop). Output: validation loss L (aggregate and variable-specific).

### Critical Path
1. Select model family and base configuration.
2. Generate variants with systematic width/depth changes keeping N in target ranges.
3. Train each variant on specified dataset sizes D (in TB) under fixed compute budget C or until convergence.
4. Compute validation loss on 2021 ERA5 using Eq. 1.
5. Fit power laws L(D) and L(N) via log-log linear regression.
6. For compute-optimal analysis, vary N and D such that C ≈ constant, plot L vs. D for fixed C, locate minima.
7. Perform variable-specific loss analysis (Eq. 2).

### Design Tradeoffs
- Width vs. depth: Prefer width; depth=1 can suffice for SFNO/GraphCast.
- Model size vs. training steps: Under fixed C, favor more steps (larger D).
- Theoretical vs. practical efficiency: GraphCast has best parameter scaling but worst hardware utilization; Aurora balances both.
- Aggregate vs. variable-specific metrics: Overall loss is rough; some variables may favor different models.

### Failure Signatures
- Variable-specific scaling diverges: e.g., one variable’s loss increases with D/N while others decrease.
- Compute-optimal parabola absent or flat: indicates model bottlenecked by factors other than N-D trade-off (e.g., Pangu’s D-dominated behavior).
- Extremely low GPU utilization (<1%): indicates memory-bottlenecked operations that won’t scale linearly with compute.
- Wide model performs worse than narrow at same N: contradicts core findings; may indicate bug or unstable training.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Data-scaling baseline:** For Aurora, train variants with widths {128, 256, 384} (similar N) on D = {10, 30, 100} TB. Fit L(D) to extract β. Expect β > 0.4.
2. **Shape comparison:** For Aurora and GraphCast, train a "wide" and a "narrow" variant with nearly identical N (within 5%). Compare validation loss; wide should win.
3. **Compute frontier:** For SFNO, fix C ≈ 25 Pflop. Train variants with (N, D) pairs: (small N, large D), (medium N, medium D), (large N, small D). Plot L vs. D; expect U-shaped curve with minimum at intermediate D.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the fundamental reason that weather forecasting models gain more from increasing width than depth when parameter counts are fixed, contrasting with language models where shape has minimal impact?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly states this contrast but provides no causal explanation specific to atmospheric dynamics.
- **Why unresolved:** The hypothesis that weather prediction relies on representational capacity rather than deep sequential reasoning lacks theoretical derivation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments isolating width vs. depth effects on spatial correlations, or a theoretical analysis linking linearity of 6-hour dynamics to shallow wide networks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can scaling laws be extended to incorporate hardware efficiency and wall-clock time, given that models like GraphCast exhibit near-zero GPU utilization while Aurora achieves 37.2%?
- **Basis in paper:** The discussion notes classical scaling laws treat compute as static and ignore wall-clock time.
- **Why unresolved:** Current laws use FLOPs but ignore implementation bottlenecks (e.g., message-passing in GNNs) that affect real training speed and cost.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A new scaling framework with hardware-aware metrics or empirical scaling studies measuring loss vs. actual training time across architectures.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What architectural or training modifications can mitigate the heterogeneous scaling responses across atmospheric variables, since aggregate validation loss is only a rough indicator?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 3.4 notes variables do not scale equally and suggests variable-specific tuning or hybrid modeling.
- **Why unresolved:** No solutions are explored; it remains unclear how to design a single model that scales uniformly or if variable-specific models are necessary.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments with variable-specific loss weighting, modular architectures with shared backbones and variable-specific heads, or analysis correlating architectural features with variable-specific scaling exponents.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why do some weather models (GraphCast, AIFS) violate the standard compute-optimal scaling constraint (a + b = 1), showing only the left half of L vs. D parabolas, and what is the correct compute-optimal frontier for different architectural families?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 3.3 reports that GraphCast and AIFS show mainly the left half, indicating performance is bottlenecked by insufficient D.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper attributes this to architectural differences but does not derive new scaling relationships or provide a general formula.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical derivation linking FLOP formulas to N-D-C relationships for each architecture, or empirical scaling across wider N-D ranges to fit model-specific optimality curves.

## Limitations
- **Dataset and resolution specificity:** Scaling exponents are derived solely from ERA5 at 0.25° resolution; generalizability to other reanalyses or climate regimes is uncertain.
- **Variable heterogeneity:** Aggregate loss masks divergent scaling across atmospheric variables, limiting performance interpretability for individual fields.
- **Hardware utilization caveats:** Extreme differences in GPU utilization (e.g., GraphCast 0.017% vs Aurora 37.2%) point to architectural bottlenecks not addressed by scaling laws alone.

## Confidence
- **Data-scaling exponents (β):** High – consistent log-log fits across multiple models.
- **Compute-optimal frontier (D > N):** Medium – observed for some models but not all; frontier shape may be model-specific.
- **Width-over-depth preference:** High – empirically validated across all five model families at 6-hour horizon.
- **Hardware utilization contrast:** High (measured directly); mechanistic explanation for inefficiencies: Medium (correlative, not ablative).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-dataset scaling test:** Replicate data-scaling experiments (β estimation) for Aurora and GraphCast using an external dataset (e.g., IMERG precipitation) to test exponent stability under distribution shift.
2. **Architectural bottleneck ablation:** Profile GraphCast’s training steps to quantify time spent in message-passing vs. attention; implement fused kernels or alternative graph implementations to measure utilization improvement and impact on scaling laws.
3. **Horizon extension experiment:** Train width/depth variants for 10-day forecasts (vs. 6-hour) to test whether deeper architectures become advantageous, challenging the shallow operator assumption.
