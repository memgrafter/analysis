# Session Risk Memory (SRM): Temporal Authorization for Deterministic Pre-Execution Safety Gates

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22350
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22350
- **Reference count:** 1
- **Primary result:** ILION+SRM achieves perfect F1 score (1.0000) with 0% false positive rate on an 80-session multi-turn benchmark, while maintaining per-turn overhead under 250 microseconds.

## Executive Summary
Stateless pre-execution safety gates, such as ILION, are effective at blocking individually risky actions but fail to detect distributed attacks that span multiple compliant actions. Session Risk Memory (SRM) introduces a deterministic temporal extension that tracks session-level risk over time. SRM maintains a behavioral centroid of the session via exponential moving average and accumulates risk using baseline-subtracted gate outputs. On a benchmark of 80 multi-turn sessions, the combination of ILION with SRM achieved perfect F1 score (1.0000) and zero false positives, eliminating the 5% false positive rate of the stateless ILION baseline while preserving 100% attack detection. The added per-turn computational overhead remains below 250 microseconds.

## Method Summary
SRM operates as a session-level wrapper around deterministic pre-execution gates (e.g., ILION). For each session, it maintains three state variables: a behavioral centroid (exponential moving average of action vectors), a baseline risk (EMA of raw gate scores during a warmup period), and an accumulated risk (EMA of adjusted risk signals). After a fixed warmup of 3 turns, the baseline is frozen. For each subsequent turn, the raw gate risk is adjusted by subtracting the baseline (clamped to non-negative), optionally combined with a drift signal (cosine distance from the centroid), and fed into an accumulator. The accumulator uses exponential decay (λ=0.75) to weight recent risk. If the accumulated risk ever exceeds a fixed threshold (τ=0.20), the entire session is blocked. The system is fully deterministic, with no learning or adaptation beyond the EMA updates, ensuring predictable behavior and low latency.

## Key Results
- Achieved perfect F1 score (1.0000) and 0% false positive rate on 80-session benchmark.
- Eliminated the 5% false positive rate of stateless ILION while maintaining 100% detection rate.
- Per-turn overhead under 250 microseconds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Baseline Subtraction Prevents False Positives
SRM eliminates false positives by normalizing gate risk signals to a session-specific baseline. During a short warmup window (K=3 turns), it computes an exponential moving average (EMA) of the raw gate risk scores (`g_t`) and freezes this baseline `b_K`. All subsequent adjusted risk signals are `ġ_t = max(0, g_t - b_K)`, so only elevation above the session's own normal operating risk contributes to accumulation. Benign sessions exhibit stable risk characteristics after warmup, so their `ġ_t` remains near zero and accumulated risk stays low. Attack sessions show systematic risk elevation above their established baseline, causing `ġ_t` to be positive and accumulate. Evidence: Without baseline correction, benign sessions with naturally higher-risk vocabulary accumulate false risk; with correction, benign sessions remain near zero accumulated risk while attack sessions show progressive accumulation. Break condition: If an attack pattern maintains risk at or below the warmup baseline (e.g., low-and-slow exfiltration), SRM may not accumulate sufficient risk to trigger.

### Mechanism 2: EMA Accumulation Detects Distributed Attacks
SRM detects distributed attacks by accumulating a scalar risk signal over time via exponential moving average, making it sensitive to sustained deviation rather than single-spike anomalies. The accumulated session risk `R_t` updates as `R_t = λ·R_{t-1} + (1-λ)·r_t`, where `r_t = w_g·ġ_t` (with `w_g=1.00`). The decay factor `λ=0.75` ensures risk fades if normal behavior returns but builds persistently if elevated risk continues. A one-shot threshold `τ=0.20` implements a "once suspicious, always flagged" policy. Distributed attack trajectories create a sequence of actions where individual gate risk may be borderline or mildly elevated (`ġ_t > 0`), but their cumulative effect exceeds the threshold, whereas benign workflows do not show such sustained elevation. Evidence: Results show SRM eliminates false positives while maintaining 100% detection, implying benign sessions do not produce sustained `R_t` elevation. Break condition: Attacks that are extremely slow (e.g., >20 turns with very low per-turn `ġ_t`) might see risk decay between malicious actions, keeping `R_t` below `τ`.

### Mechanism 3: Behavioral Centroid and Drift (Reserved)
SRM's session behavioral centroid provides a reference trajectory, enabling detection of coherence violations when combined with the drift term. A semantic centroid `c_t` tracks the EMA of action vectors: `c_t = α·v_t + (1-α)·c_{t-1}` (α=0.35). The drift `Δ_t = 1 - cos(v_t, c_{t-1})` measures deviation from the established behavioral profile. In the current implementation, the drift weight `w_δ=0` because the 21D sparse embedding yields saturated cosine distances (≈1.0 for distinct actions). However, the mechanism is retained for future use with dense continuous embeddings where semantic proximity is meaningful. Core assumption: In a suitable embedding space, benign session actions remain semantically coherent (low `Δ_t`), while escalating attacks introduce disparate actions (high `Δ_t`). Evidence: The formulation explicitly includes the drift term and discusses its expected utility with dense embeddings.

## Foundational Learning
### Deterministic Pre-Execution Safety Gates
**Why needed:** SRM extends ILION, a deterministic gate that scores each action and blocks if any score exceeds a threshold. Understanding `g_t` (raw gate risk) is prerequisite.
**Quick check:** If an action gets `cvl=0.95, idc=0.90, irs=0.92, svrf=0.88`, then `g_t = max(1-cvl, 1-idc, 1-irs, 1-svrf) = max(0.05, 0.10, 0.08, 0.12) = 0.12`. With thresholds CVL>0.8, IDC>0.7, IRS>0.85, SVRF>0.75, the action is blocked because `cvl=0.95 > 0.8`.

### Exponential Moving Average (EMA)
**Why needed:** SRM uses EMA for baseline estimation, centroid update, and risk accumulation. EMA defines how history is weighted versus the current observation via a decay rate.
**Quick check:** With decay factor `λ=0.75` in `R_t = λ·R_{t-1} + (1-λ)·r_t`, the effective half-life is approximately `ln(0.5)/ln(0.75) ≈ 2.41` turns.

### Cosine Similarity in Sparse Vector Spaces
**Why needed:** The centroid and drift (`Δ_t`) rely on cosine distance. The paper finds `Δ_t` uninformative because their 21D keyword-weighting scheme yields high cosine distances even for semantically similar actions.
**Quick check:** In a sparse vector where two distinct actions share no keywords, their cosine similarity is 0 (since dot product is 0). Adding a continuous embedding layer would produce non-zero similarities for semantically related actions, making `Δ_t` informative.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
ILION Gate → SRM Warmup Module → SRM State Manager → SRM Risk Calculator → SRM Accumulator & Verdict

### Critical Path
Action `a_t` → [ILION Gate] → `v_t, g_t` → [SRM: Compute `Δ_t`, `ġ_t`] → `r_t` → [SRM: Update `φ_t`, `R_t`] → `R_t ≥ τ?` → Session BLOCK. The stateless gate's per-action BLOCK is OR'd with SRM's session BLOCK for final verdict.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Determinism vs. Adaptivity:** Fixed hyperparameters ensure deterministic behavior but may not optimize for all role contexts.
- **Sensitivity vs. FPR:** Baseline subtraction and `w_g=1.00` trade earlier detection for near-zero FPR. The decay `λ=0.75` makes SRM less sensitive to brief lapses but more to sustained patterns.
- **Generality vs. Performance:** The 21D embedding is extremely fast (<250 μs overhead) but cripples the drift term (`Δ_t`). Richer embeddings add compute but enable an additional signal.
- **Conservatism:** "Once suspicious, always flagged" (`R_t ≥ τ` for any `t`) prevents attackers from resetting risk by returning to clean behavior but means SRM never "forgets" a session.

### Failure Signatures
- **False Negative:** Attack session where `ġ_t` never builds sufficiently (e.g., each step just below `b_K`), keeping `R_t` below `τ`.
- **False Positive:** Benign session with a genuine, sustained shift in operational scope causing `R_t` to diverge like an attack.
- **Embedding Saturation:** `Δ_t` remains ~1.0 for all non-identical actions due to sparse keyword vectors, rendering drift inert.
- **Warmup Corruption:** If the first `K` turns contain an attack action, `b_K` becomes elevated, suppressing future `ġ_t` signals ("baseline poisoning").

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Parameter Sweep:** Vary `α, β, λ, τ` systematically on the benchmark. Plot F1/FPR heatmaps to identify robust regions and confirm `τ=0.20` as optimal.
2. **Embedding Sensitivity Test:** Replace the 21D keyword vector with a small dense embedding (e.g., 384d from a lightweight transformer). Re-run with `w_δ` active. Hypothesis: `Δ_t` becomes discriminative, potentially allowing `w_g` reduction and earlier detection.
3. **Warmup Robustness Test:** Inject a malicious action in turn 1 or 2 of attack sessions. Does SRM still detect? Measure detection rate vs. position of first malicious action within warmup window to assess baseline poisoning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does SRM's performance (F1, FPR) hold when using dense continuous embeddings instead of sparse keyword vectors?
2. Can SRM be extended to hierarchical session structures (e.g., multi-agent workflows) while preserving deterministic latency guarantees?
3. Would adaptive per-session threshold mechanisms improve SRM's sensitivity and reduce false positives without harming detection rate?
4. How well does SRM generalize to natural language actions, diverse enterprise roles, and non-linear session graphs (branching, parallel actions)?

## Limitations
- Small benchmark size (80 sessions) may not generalize to diverse workloads.
- The drift term (`Δ_t`) is disabled due to sparse embeddings; SRM relies solely on gate risk.
- Fixed warmup (K=3) risks baseline poisoning if early turns are malicious.
