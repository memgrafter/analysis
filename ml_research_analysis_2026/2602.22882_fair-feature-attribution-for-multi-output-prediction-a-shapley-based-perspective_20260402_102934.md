# Fair feature attribution for multi-output prediction: a Shapley-based perspective

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.22882
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22882
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Under classical Shapley axioms, multi-output feature attribution must be computed component-wise; thus independent SHAP per output is theoretically justified, and multi-output training yields computational gains while preserving SHAP consistency.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates fair feature attribution for multi-output machine learning models through the lens of Shapley axioms. It proves a rigidity theorem showing that any attribution rule satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy player, and additivity must decompose across outputs, meaning joint-output explanations are impossible without relaxing an axiom. This theoretically justifies the common practice of computing SHAP independently for each output. Empirically, on a biomedical dataset with 15,931 samples and 75 features, a multi-output model trains 64% faster and uses less memory than independent single-output models, while SHAP values from both approaches show high consistency (cosine similarity >0.97, Spearman correlation 0.86–0.95). The findings suggest that multi-output learning preserves interpretability while offering computational savings.

## Method Summary
The authors extend the classical Shapley framework to vector-valued cooperative games, modeling multi-output predictors as mappings from feature subsets to ℝ^m. They prove that any value operator obeying the four Shapley axioms must act component-wise, reducing multi-output attribution to independent scalar Shapley computations per output. They also establish Lipschitz stability bounds, showing that small changes in the predictor induce bounded changes in SHAP values. To validate practical implications, they train a multi-output neural network and three single-output networks on a biomedical dataset, then compare per-output SHAP values using DeepExplainer, measuring cosine similarity and Spearman rank correlation.

## Key Results
- Rigidity theorem: Under the four Shapley axioms, any fair attribution rule for multi-output models must decompose component-wise across outputs; joint-output attribution is impossible without violating an axiom.
- Computational efficiency: A multi-output model trains 64% faster (89s vs 249s) and uses less memory than training three independent single-output models.
- SHAP consistency: Cosine similarity between multi-output and single-output SHAP values exceeds 0.97 for all outputs, and Spearman rank correlations range from 0.86 to 0.95, indicating high agreement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The classical Shapley axioms (efficiency, symmetry, dummy player, additivity) uniquely force feature attribution for any vector‑valued predictor to be computed **component‑wise** across outputs.
- **Mechanism:** Model the multi‑output problem as a vector‑valued cooperative game \(v:2^{[n]}\to\mathbb{R}^m\). Prove (Theorem 2.8) that any value operator satisfying the axioms must map each output coordinate independently; consequently (Theorem 2.9) the only admissible operator is the coordinate‑wise application of the scalar Shapley formula (2.4)-(2.5).
- **Core assumption:** The four Shapley axioms are imposed **verbatim** in the vector space \(\mathbb{R}^m\) with all equalities interpreted component‑wise. No additional ordering or scalarization is introduced on the output space.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “any attribution rule satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy player, and additivity must necessarily decompose component‑wise across outputs.”  
  - [section 2.3] Theorem 2.8 (rigidity) and Theorem 2.9 (uniqueness) formalize the breakdown.  
  - [corpus] Neighboring works (e.g., VARSHAP, RankSHAP) focus on scalar or ranking settings; none address axiomatic extension to vector‑valued games, so direct evidence is weak.
- **Break condition:** Relaxing any axiom (e.g., allowing a joint efficiency loss or non‑additive cross‑output coupling) opens the door to non‑component‑wise attribution rules.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The Shapley value operator is **Lipschitz continuous** with respect to natural norms on cooperative games; small changes in the predictor induce bounded changes in SHAP values.
- **Mechanism:** For vector‑valued games \(u,v\), Proposition 2.11 proves \(\|\Phi(u)-\Phi(v)\|_{A,\infty} \le \|u-v\|_{\Delta,\infty}\) and \(\|\Phi(u)-\Phi(v)\|_{A,\infty} \le 2\|u-v\|_{G,\infty}\). Translating to ML predictors, Proposition 2.13 gives \(\|\Phi(f;x)-\Phi(g;x)\|_{A,\infty} \le 2\|f-g\|_{L^\infty(\mu)}\).
- **Core assumption:** The value operator is **linear** (additivity) and the weights in the Shapley formula sum to 1, which yields the convex combination bound.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 2.4] Proposition 2.11 details the norm inequalities for games.  
  - [section 2.5] Proposition 2.13 transfers the bound to the ML setting.  
  - [corpus] No neighboring paper derives these stability estimates specifically for vector‑valued SHAP.
- **Break condition:** If the attribution rule is non‑linear or the game representation violates \(v(\emptyset)=0\), the Lipschitz guarantee may fail.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Training a **multi‑output** model reduces computational cost (time ≈ 64% faster, less memory) while its *per‑output* SHAP values stay highly consistent with those from independently trained single‑output models.
- **Mechanism:** Parameter sharing in the multi‑output architecture lowers total training epochs and memory footprint. Theoretically, component‑wise SHAP (Mechanism 1) implies that explanations for each output depend only on the conditional expectation of that output. If the multi‑output and single‑output predictors are close (empirically they are), the stability bound (Mechanism 2) ensures their SHAP vectors are similar. Empirically, cosine similarity > 0.97 and Spearman rank correlation > 0.86 (Table 4).
- **Core assumption:** Multi‑output and single‑output models yield sufficiently similar functions on the data distribution (no negative transfer), and the SHAP approximation (DeepExplainer) is accurate enough for the stability bound to hold approximately.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “multi‑output model trains 64% faster … SHAP values … highly consistent: cosine similarity exceeds 0.97.”  
  - [section 3.4] Table 4 reports cosine and Spearman metrics; Figure 2 visualizes agreement.  
  - [section 3.2] details the training protocol and architectures M1/M2.
- **Break condition:** Strong negative transfer (multi‑output performance drops significantly) or poor SHAP approximation could break the observed consistency.

## Foundational Learning
### Shapley Axioms & Scalar SHAP
- **Why needed here:** They provide the fairness‑based foundation for feature attribution. The extension to multi‑output relies on re‑using the scalar formulation for each coordinate.
- **Quick check:** “What does the dummy player axiom state, and what would it imply for a feature that never affects the prediction?”

### Vector‑Valued Cooperative Games
- **Why needed:** Multi‑output predictors map to \(\mathbb{R}^m\); understanding how to define \(v(S)\) and apply the axioms in this space is essential to grasp the rigidity theorem.
- **Quick check:** “If we desired a joint‑output attribution (allocating contributions across both features and outputs), which axiom(s) would we have to relax?”

### Stability of the Shapley Operator
- **Why needed:** It bridges theory and experiment, explaining why similar models produce similar SHAP vectors. Without stability, computational savings from multi‑output training might not preserve interpretability.
- **Quick check:** “Given two predictors \(f,g\) with \(\|f-g\|_{L^\infty(\mu)} = 0.05\), what is the worst‑case difference in their SHAP values according to Proposition 2.13?”

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Tabular data (75 features) -> Standardization, correlation‑based pruning, PCA (optional) -> Multi‑output model (shared trunk + two heads) or Single‑output models (independent copies) -> Training (Adam, lr=1e-3, early stopping, sum of task losses) -> DeepExplainer (100 background samples) -> Per‑output SHAP values -> Cosine similarity & Spearman correlation.

**Critical path:**  
1. Load and split dataset (70/15/15).  
2. Preprocess (scale, optionally prune).  
3. Train multi‑output M2 model.  
4. Train three independent single‑output M2 models.  
5. For a fixed test set, compute SHAP values with DeepExplainer.  
6. Compute similarity metrics per output.  
7. Summarize computational metrics (time, RAM).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Multi‑output saves time/memory by sharing parameters but may sacrifice a bit of per‑task performance (e.g., age R²: 0.62 vs 0.65).  
- DeepExplainer approximates exact Shapley values efficiently for neural nets, but the theoretical guarantees assume exact Shapley; approximation error may mildly affect consistency.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Negative transfer: Multi‑output R² / AUC drops > 0.05 relative to single‑output.  
- SHAP discrepancy: Cosine similarity < 0.9 or Spearman < 0.8, indicating divergence beyond stability bounds.  
- Training instability: NaNs, non‑convergence within 200 epochs.  
- Memory overflow: Peak RAM > system capacity during single‑output runs.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Computational benchmark:** Measure total wall‑clock time and peak RAM for M2 multi‑output vs summed single‑output runs; expect ~89 s vs ~249 s and memory saving.  
2. **Predictive sanity check:** Verify multi‑output model achieves R² > 0.6 for age, > 0.81 for MetSCORE, and AUC > 0.98 for sex (Table 1–2). Large deviations suggest broken training.  
3. **SHAP consistency validation:** On a fixed test subset (e.g., 1000 samples), compute SHAP values; calculate cosine and Spearman per output. Enforce thresholds: cosine > 0.97, Spearman > 0.86 as in Table 4. Failure indicates model divergence or explainer mis‑configuration.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do there exist Shapley-like attribution rules that allow joint-output explanations without violating fairness?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Sec. 4 (1): "It remains open to determine whether alternative formulations could capture joint dependencies or interactions between outputs."
- **Why unresolved:** Theorem 2.8 proves component-wise decomposition under classical axioms.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A new axiomatic system with a constructive rule that couples outputs while satisfying key properties.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can output correlations be integrated into SHAP estimation to improve accuracy or efficiency?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Sec. 4 (2): "Whether correlation structure can be explicitly integrated into the Shapley value estimation process."
- **Why unresolved:** Existing methods ignore output dependence; potential benefits unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An algorithm that leverages output covariance and demonstrates empirical/theoretical gains.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What are the theoretical limits and efficient approximation methods for vector-valued SHAP in high dimensions?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Sec. 4 (5): "Develop reduced-order, sampling-based, or variational approximations ... error bounds remain open."
- **Why unresolved:** Exact SHAP is intractable for high-dimensional multi-output models.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Scalable approximation with provable error bounds and preserved axiomatic properties.

## Limitations
- The rigidity theorem only holds if all four Shapley axioms are kept unchanged; relaxing any axiom may allow joint-output explanations, but the paper does not analyze which relaxations are acceptable.
- SHAP consistency results rely on DeepExplainer approximations; exact Shapley error is unknown, so consistency under exact SHAP is not guaranteed.
- Empirical evaluation uses only one biomedical dataset; generalizability to other domains or many-output tasks is not established.
