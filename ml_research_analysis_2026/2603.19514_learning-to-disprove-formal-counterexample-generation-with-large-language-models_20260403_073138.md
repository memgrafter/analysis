# Learning to Disprove: Formal Counterexample Generation with Large Language Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19514
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19514
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Fine-tuned model achieves 47–74% relative improvement in pass@1 over state-of-the-art reasoning models on formal counterexample generation benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
This paper formalizes the task of generating formal counterexamples in Lean 4, requiring an LLM to propose a concrete witness and produce a verifiable proof that disproves a given statement. To address data scarcity, the authors introduce a symbolic mutation strategy: starting from provable universal theorems, they automatically drop a necessary hypothesis (identified via proof analysis) to create unprovable existential problems that demand counterexamples. This yields 575K training instances. A two-model pipeline—with one LLM proposing counterexamples and another generating formal proofs—is trained via multi-reward expert iteration. The reward combines verification success on both the mutated statement and a simpler dropped-hypothesis statement, providing a denser signal. On three benchmarks (FOR-COUNTER, VERI-REASON, VERI-FORMALIZE), the approach significantly outperforms existing neural provers and reasoning models, demonstrating the effectiveness of synthetic data generation and reward shaping for formal disproof.

## Method Summary
The method synthesizes training data by mutating provable theorems from Mathlib, Leanworkbook, MiniF2F, and PutnamBench. A Lean 4 tactic analyzes each theorem’s proof to remove redundant hypotheses, then selects one necessary hypothesis to discard. This generates two new theorems: an existential "mutated version" that is false and requires a counterexample, and an existential "dropped-hypothesis" version that is provable. The 575K mutated pairs form a dataset. Training uses two LLMs: a Qwen3 8B model proposes a counterexample from the natural language description of the mutated statement; a Deepseek-Prover-V2 7B model then writes a Lean proof using that example. During expert iteration, each candidate is verified by the Lean prover. A multi-reward signal, `r = α·I(verify(π_M)) + (1−α)·I(verify(π_H))` with α=0.8, weights successful proofs. Both models are fine-tuned via weighted supervised learning over many iterations, with Dr.GRPO regularization to prevent overfitting to short proofs.

## Key Results
- The fine-tuned model outperforms state-of-the-art reasoning models (e.g., GPT-4.1-mini, Deepseek-R1) and neural provers (e.g., Deepseek-Prover-V2, Goedel-prover-v2) on all three benchmarks, achieving 47–74% relative improvement in pass@1.
- Multi-reward expert iteration converges faster and reaches higher final performance compared to single-reward training (α=1.0).
- The symbolic mutation strategy successfully produces a large corpus of valid counterexample problems, with a mutation ratio of 1.5–2.5 per seed theorem.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Symbolic Mutation for Scalable Data Synthesis
- **Claim:** Symbolic mutation enables scalable synthesis of valid counterexample problems from provable seed theorems.
- **Mechanism:** A formally provable, universally quantified theorem is mutated by dropping one necessary hypothesis via a custom Lean 4 `mutate` tactic. This transforms `∀ x, H₁(x) → H₂(x) → C(x)` into an existential counterexample target `∃ x, H₂(x) → C(x)` and a provable `∃ x, ¬H₁(x)`. Necessity is ensured by analyzing the original proof to remove redundant hypotheses.
- **Core assumption:** The dropped hypothesis is necessary; if redundant, the mutated statement remains provable.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states "systematically extracting theorems and discarding selected hypotheses"; Section 3.1 details the construction.
- **Break condition:** If the selected hypothesis is redundant, no counterexample exists, and all attempts fail.

### Mechanism 2: Multi-Reward Mitigates Sparse Rewards
- **Claim:** A multi-reward signal based on verifying both the mutated statement and the dropped-hypothesis statement mitigates sparse reward issues during expert iteration.
- **Mechanism:** For a candidate counterexample `x*`, proofs are generated and verified for both statements. The reward `r = r_mutated + (1−α)·r_dropped` is non-zero as long as `x*` falsifies the dropped hypothesis (often easier), providing a denser training signal.
- **Core assumption:** A valid counterexample for the mutated statement also witnesses `¬H₁(x*)`, making the dropped-hypothesis proof easier to activate.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions "rewards based on successfully proving both... mitigating sparse reward issues"; Section 3.2 explains the easier reward activation.
- **Break condition:** If `x*` falsifies `H₁` but fails the target implication, the dropped-hypothesis reward may overweight an invalid solution, introducing noise.

### Mechanism 3: Integrated Informal-to-Formal Pipeline
- **Claim:** The decoupled pipeline—informal proposal followed by formal verification—enables effective end-to-end learning.
- **Mechanism:** An LLM (`q_ϕ`) reasons on the natural language description to propose `x*`; a second LLM (`q_ψ`) generates a Lean proof using `x*`. Successful verification of both proofs yields rewards. Expert iteration cycles between inference/verification and weighted fine-tuning.
- **Core assumption:** The informal model can propose viable candidates, and the formal model can incorporate them into correct proofs; modular design allows independent optimization.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract describes "sequential pattern of informal-to-formal reasoning"; Section 3.3 and Figure 1 illustrate the pipeline; Table 2 shows significant gains over ablations.
- **Break condition:** Failure in either stage cascades: invalid proposals or proof models that ignore the provided example both cause verification failure.

## Foundational Learning
- **Formal Theorem Proving in Lean 4:** Understanding tactics, proof states, and the `mutate` tactic’s output is essential, as data synthesis and reward verification rely on Lean 4.  
  *Quick check:* For `theorem foo (x : Nat) (h : x > 0) : x + 1 > 0`, dropping `h` yields `∃ x, x + 1 > 0` and `∃ x, ¬(x > 0)`.
- **Multi-Reward & Reward Shaping:** The core innovation uses two verification signals to combat sparsity; knowing how to combine partial successes is key to replication.  
  *Quick check:* Why is proving `∃ x, ¬H₁(x)` easier than `∃ x, H₂(x) → C(x)`? The former is a direct witness; the latter requires satisfying an implication.
- **Symbolic Mutation for Data Synthesis:** The principle of transforming a valid proof by removing a necessary condition to create counterexample problems underlies the 575K dataset.  
  *Quick check:* From `∀ n : Nat, n ≥ 2 → n is_prime → n is_odd`, dropping `n ≥ 2` gives `∃ n, n is_prime → n is_odd` and `∃ n, ¬(n ≥ 2)`.

## Architecture Onboarding
Seed Theorem Provider -> Symbolic Mutation Engine -> (Counterexample Proposer & Proof Generator) -> Verifier -> Reward Calculator -> Training Loop (Expert Iteration) -> model updates -> back to Counterexample Proposer & Proof Generator.

**Critical path:** Seed collection → Mutation → Initial model inference → Verification → Reward assignment → Weighted SFT → New model → Repeat (mutated dataset fixed; models improve).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Mutation ratio vs. difficulty: High ratio increases volume but may produce trivial or impossible problems; no difficulty filtering.
- Reward balance (α): α=1.0 yields sparse signals; α=0.8 injects density but risks down-weighting the true task; fixed in paper.
- Model specialization vs. generality: Fine-tuning for counterexamples may harm general proof performance; not evaluated on standard benchmarks.

**Failure signatures:**
- Informal proposer generates mathematically invalid candidates (e.g., wrong domain).
- Proof model ignores the provided counterexample and invents its own.
- Mutation creates a problem where the dropped hypothesis was redundant, yielding zero reward.
- Overfitting to easy dropped-hypothesis proofs if reward weighting or GRPO is insufficient.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Validate Mutation Efficacy: Measure mutation ratio and execution time per seed across sources; expect ratio ~1.5–2.5, time <1s.
2. Ablate Multi-Reward: Train with α=1.0 vs. α=0.8 on a fixed validation split; track pass@k curves to confirm faster convergence and higher asymptote for multi-reward.
3. Benchmark Against SOTA: Evaluate final model on FOR-COUNTER, VERI-REASON, VERI-FORMALIZE; compare pass@1/4/9 to proprietary and open-source baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can we enhance the quality of synthetically generated counterexample problems to reduce redundancy and improve training efficiency? The current mutation strategy lacks data selection, causing redundant samples.
2. To what extent does increasing the size of the informal and formal reasoning models improve success rates? Training was limited to 7B models due to budget.
3. Can integrating external tools (e.g., combinatorial solvers) into the informal reasoning stage improve correctness and coverage? Tool-use could alleviate calculation errors but remains unexplored.
4. Why does the formal model sometimes ignore the provided counterexample and generate its own? This indicates poor instruction following, not explicitly discussed.

## Limitations
- The exact algorithm for identifying necessary hypotheses is underspecified; mutation may retain redundant hypotheses, creating invalid training problems.
- Reward implementation details (Dr.GRPO hyperparameters, smoothing, batch normalization) are missing, hindering replication of training dynamics.
- Data splits and prompt templates for baselines are not provided, potentially affecting benchmark comparisons.

## Confidence
- Symbolic mutation yields valid counterexample problems: **Medium**
- Multi-reward expert iteration mitigates sparse rewards and improves convergence: **Medium**
- Integrated informal-to-formal pipeline is effective: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Necessity detection audit:** Apply the `mutate` tactic to 1,000 held-out seed theorems; manually verify whether the dropped hypothesis is necessary by attempting to prove the original without it; report precision.
2. **Reward ablation reproducibility:** On a fixed 10K subset, run expert iteration under (a) single-reward (α=1.0), (b) multi-reward (α=0.8), and (c) a sweep of α values; track pass@k on a validation set every five iterations.
3. **Pipeline component isolation:** Replace the informal proposer with a deterministic baseline that reuses the witness from the automatically proven dropped-hypothesis statement (when available); compare final performance to the full system.
