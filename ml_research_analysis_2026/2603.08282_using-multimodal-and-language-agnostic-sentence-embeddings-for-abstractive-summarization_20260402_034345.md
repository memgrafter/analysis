# Using Multimodal and Language-Agnostic Sentence Embeddings for Abstractive Summarization

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.08282
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08282
*   **Reference count:** 0
*   **Primary result:** SBARThez-BGE with Named Entity Injection reduces named entity hallucination risk from 58.52% to 34.16% on French summarization, while enabling robust cross-lingual and speech-to-text summarization.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of hallucination in abstractive summarization by proposing SBARThez, a framework that modifies the BARThez encoder-decoder model to operate on frozen, multilingual sentence embeddings instead of token embeddings. This architectural change creates a language-agnostic and modality-agnostic semantic input space. To improve factual consistency, a Named Entity Injection (NEI) mechanism prepends extracted named entities to the decoder input. Evaluated on French text (OrangeSum), cross-lingual (WikiLingua, CrossSum), and speech (DECODA) summarization, SBARThez demonstrates strong performance, significantly reducing entity hallucinations and showing robustness across languages and modalities, particularly in low-resource scenarios.

## Method Summary
SBARThez employs a two-stage training procedure. First, a pretrained BARThez model is adapted on a large multilingual summarization corpus (MLSUM) by removing its token embedding layer and feeding projected sentence embeddings (from models like BGE-M3 or SONAR) directly into the encoder. A linear projection layer adjusts embedding dimensions to match the model's hidden size. In the second stage, the model is fine-tuned on task-specific data (e.g., OrangeSum for French text, DECODA for speech). For text summarization, an optional Named Entity Injection module uses a NER system to extract entities from the source document, formats them, and prepends them to the decoder input during both training and inference to constrain generation.

## Key Results
*   On French text (OrangeSum), SBARThez-BGE with NEI achieves ROUGE-L 20.22 and BERTScore 26.05, reducing named entity hallucination risk (NEHR) from 58.52% (baseline) to 34.16%, near the reference NEHR of 34.01%.
*   For cross-lingual summarization (WikiLingua, CrossSum), SBARThez consistently outperforms translation and LLM baselines, especially for low-resource languages, without language-specific fine-tuning.
*   On speech summarization (DECODA), multimodal fine-tuning (on both text and speech embeddings) yields a BERTScore of 35.05 on speech input, comparable to its text-only performance, and is robust to different audio segmentation methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Using frozen, pretrained sentence embeddings as encoder input abstracts away from token-level details, enabling better cross-lingual and multimodal transfer by operating in a language-agnostic semantic space.
*   **Mechanism:** The standard `embed-tokens` layer of BARThez is removed. A sequence of sentence embeddings (`v1...vn`) from a separate multilingual/multimodal model is projected (linear layer + GeLU) to the model's hidden size and fed directly into the encoder. The decoder remains token-based.
*   **Core assumption:** The embedding model positions sentences with similar meaning (across languages/modalities) nearby in vector space, and this semantic alignment is sufficient for the decoder to generate a high-quality summary.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "leverage the use of multimodal and language-agnostic sentence embeddings... applicable to both text and speech inputs and supports cross-lingual summarization."
    *   [section 3.1] Describes removing `embed-tokens` and feeding sentence vectors `V`. "our approach breaks new ground by effortlessly processing text data from various languages as well as audio inputs."
*   **Break condition:** If the sentence embedding space lacks fine-grained syntactic/discourse structure (e.g., negation, coreference), the decoder may generate summaries semantically misaligned with the source's precise meaning, despite topic similarity.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Explicitly injecting tokenized named entities from the source document into the decoder's initial input constrains generation, reducing hallucination of non-existent named entities.
*   **Mechanism:** A NER system identifies and extracts named entities. These are formatted (e.g., `(E1, T1)`) and tokenized using the seq2seq model's tokenizer. They are prepended to the decoder input tokens at training and inference.
*   **Core assumption:** The decoder can attend to these forced NE tokens while leveraging semantic context from sentence embeddings, balancing factual accuracy with abstractive paraphrasing.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "A Named Entity Injection mechanism that appends tokenized named entities to the decoder input is introduced, in order to improve the factual consistency."
    *   [section 4.2] Details NEI module and prompt format: `'(E1, T1), (E2, T2), . . . ,(En, Tn).'`
    *   [section 4.3] Quantitative evidence: NEHR drops from 58.52% to 34.16% for SBARThez-BGE with NEI, comparable to gold reference NEHR (34.01%).
*   **Break condition:** If source NER is inaccurate/noisy or the entity list is excessively long, the decoder may be forced to include irrelevant/incorrect entities, degrading quality and fluency. It may also increase extractiveness.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Training on a mixture of text and speech utterance embeddings creates a robust model where both modalities map to a shared, effective decoding space, enabling end-to-end speech summarization without cascaded ASR.
*   **Mechanism:** The model is first pre-trained on MLSUM using text embeddings. It is then fine-tuned for speech on DECODA using speech embeddings. A beneficial regime merges and shuffles both text and speech embedding sequences from their respective training sets ("Both Speech" training).
*   **Core assumption:** Speech utterance embedding models produce vectors semantically aligned with the text embedding space, allowing the same decoder parameters to condition on either modality.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "Our novel framework, SBARThez, is applicable to both text and speech inputs... multimodal training yields BertScore 35.05 on speech, comparable to text."
    *   [section 6.1] Compares training regimes. "All SBARThez variants exhibit their best performance on the speech test-set when trained with both text and speech modalities." SBARThez-BGE (Both) achieves 35.05 BertScore on speech.
*   **Break condition:** If the speech embedding model is poorly aligned with the text space (e.g., due to domain mismatch), the decoder will fail to generate coherent summaries from speech inputs, regardless of fine-tuning.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Sentence Embeddings vs. Token Embeddings**
    *   **Why needed here:** This is the foundational architectural shift. The encoder consumes dense, fixed-dimensional vectors (one per sentence) instead of a long sequence of discrete token IDs, enabling cross-lingual/multimodal transfer.
    *   **Quick check:** If a sentence embedding model is perfectly language-agnostic, what is the cosine similarity between embeddings of a French sentence and its correct English translation? What about a French sentence and a different English sentence on the same topic?
*   **Concept: Named Entity Recognition (NER) as a Pre-processing Step**
    *   **Why needed here:** The NEI mechanism is key for factual consistency. NER is applied *before* summarization, and its output is structured and injected separately.
    *   **Quick check:** Why might the authors filter NER entities with a confidence threshold (0.9) and remove duplicates before injection? What error modes does this prevent?
*   **Concept: Modality-Specific Embedding Alignment**
    *   **Why needed here:** Multimodal capability relies on text and speech utterance embeddings inhabiting a shared semantic space. The same summarization decoder must handle inputs from different upstream embedding models.
    *   **Quick check:** In the "Both Speech" training setup, what are the two distinct sources of the embedding vectors being mixed? Why is this mixing hypothesized to be beneficial compared to fine-tuning on speech embeddings alone?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Input Splitter -> [Embedding Extractors (Text/Speech)] -> Projection Layer -> Modified Encoder (no embed_tokens) -> Cross-Attention with Decoder. NEI path: Source Document -> NER -> Formatted NE Tokens -> Prepend to Decoder Input.
*   **Critical path:** `[Document/Audio]` -> `Split into Sentences/Utterances` -> `[Embedding Extractor]` -> `[Projection Layer]` -> `Modified Encoder` -> `Cross-Attention with Decoder` -> `Token Outputs`.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Abstraction vs. Fidelity:** Sentence embeddings enable transfer but risk losing token-level cues (e.g., exact number, negation). NEI trades some abstractiveness for entity fidelity.
    *   **Modality通用性 vs. Optimality:** A single model handles text and speech, but a text-specialized fine-tuned model might achieve higher ROUGE/BertScore.
    *   **Simplicity vs. Performance:** Removing the encoder's token embedding layer is simple but discards all token-level features. The projection layer is a potential bottleneck if dimensions are poorly matched.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    1.  **High NEHR:** NEI disabled, or NER fails to extract key entities.
    2.  **Poor Cross-lingual Performance for Specific Languages:** The sentence embedding model has weak coverage/alignment for the source language.
    3.  **Severe Drop on Speech vs. Text:** Speech embedding model not well-aligned with text space, or "Both Speech" fine-tuning not performed. Also, check audio segmentation quality.
    4.  **Very Low Abstractiveness Metrics (P-R1, EFC, EFD close to Source):** Model is copying excessively, possibly due to insufficient capacity, poor training data, or a failing projection layer.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Projection Layer Sanity Check:** Feed dummy sentence embeddings through the projection layer. Verify output shape and gradient flow. Test with different source dimensions.
    2.  **NEI Validation:** On a small held-out set with gold NER, manually inspect formatted NE prompt. Check tokenized sequence and model loss with/without NEI.
    3.  **Modality Comparison:** Fine-tune MLSUM-pretrained SBARThez-BGE on DECODA under: a) text transcripts only, b) speech embeddings only, c) mixed. Evaluate on speech test set. Hypothesis: (c) outperforms (b).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question 1:** Does the SBARThez framework generalize effectively to languages other than French for abstractive summarization?
    *   **Basis:** [explicit] Authors state methodology could extend but "further validation is needed."
    *   **Why unresolved:** Evaluation only on French datasets and French-summary outputs in cross-lingual settings. Performance on diverse language pairs, especially low-resource, is unknown.
*   **Question 2:** Can the sentence-embedding-based approach be adapted to other NLP generation tasks beyond summarization?
    *   **Basis:** [explicit] Authors note applicability to QA, paraphrasing, simplification "remains to be explored."
    *   **Why unresolved:** Model designed and evaluated solely for summarization. Effectiveness for tasks requiring different output structures is untested.
*   **Question 3:** How can factual consistency metrics be developed for French summarization?
    *   **Basis:** [explicit] Lack of suitable French factual consistency metrics is a key limitation; existing ones are English-focused.
    *   **Why unresolved:** They used NEHR (overly strict) and ROUGE/BertScore (not direct measures). No robust, language-agnostic metric exists for French.
*   **Question 4:** How does the language coverage of the pretrained sentence embedding model impact performance for low-resource languages?
    *   **Basis:** [inferred] SBARThez-BGE outperforms baselines on languages not seen in BGE training (e.g., Igbo, Kirundi), but performance varies. Direct correlation not analyzed.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Experiments mix effects of embedding capabilities, fine-tuning data, and target resource levels. Isolating the embedding model's role requires controlled studies.
*   **Question 5:** Can NEI be improved for domain-specific named entities in spoken dialogue?
    *   **Basis:** [inferred] The generic NER system (camembert-ner) only extracts PER/ORG/LOC, while DECODA has domain-specific entities not captured.
    *   **Why unresolved:** NEI relies on external NER, which may not suit domain-specific types. No alternative entity detection for spoken dialogues was explored.

## Limitations
*   Cross-lingual generalizability is unclear; evaluation centered on French, with limited analysis of truly low-resource or typologically distant source languages.
*   NEI robustness under real-world NER noise (imperfect extraction, domain shifts) is unverified.
*   Fine-tuning protocol specifics (epochs, LR schedules) for second-stage task-specific tuning are opaque, hindering replication.

## Confidence
*   **Claim:** NEI significantly reduces Named Entity Hallucination Risk (NEHR). **Label:** **High**. Supported by clear, large-margin quantitative result (58.52% → 34.16%) on OrangeSum.
*   **Claim:** Multimodal (text+speech) training yields robust speech summarization, closing the gap to text-only performance. **Label:** **Medium**. Table 8 shows benefit of "Both Speech" training, and BertScore (35.05) is close to text (35.07). However, relies on specific, unvalidated speech embedding alignment.
*   **Claim:** Sentence-embedding-based encoder enables effective cross-lingual transfer without language-specific fine-tuning. **Label:** **Medium-Low**. Results on WikiLingua/CrossSum are positive but lack breakdown by source language diversity. Claim leans heavily on embedding models' pre-trained alignment, which may not hold universally.

## Next Checks
1.  **Low-Resource Language Stress Test:** Replicate cross-lingual experiments using language pairs involving genuinely low-resource languages (e.g., Basque, Swahili, Burmese) not in embedding model pre-training. Measure degradation vs. high-resource pairs.
2.  **NEI Robustness to Noisy NER:** Introduce synthetic/real NER errors (low-confidence inclusion, missed entities) into OrangeSum validation. Re-run inference with NEI. Quantify delta in NEHR and ROUGE/BertScore.
3.  **Embedding Space Alignment Diagnostic:** For DECODA speech experiments, compute cross-modal similarity between *aligned* speech and text utterance embeddings (from same model family, e.g., SONAR) for a held-out sample. Report mean cosine similarity. A low value (<0.6) would explain potential performance gaps.
