# Multi-Task Anti-Causal Learning for Reconstructing Urban Events from Residents' Reports

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.11546
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11546
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Multi-Task Anti-Causal learning (MTAC) reduces MAE by up to 34.61% compared to state-of-the-art baselines on urban event reconstruction.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces MTAC, a framework for estimating latent urban event counts (causes) from biased resident reports (effects) across multiple related event types. It models a causal graph where socioeconomic confounders (Z) influence both the true events (X) and latent reporting preferences (W), while each event type also affects its corresponding W. The key innovation is the assumption that the effect of Z on W is invariant across tasks, while the effect of X on W is task-specific. MTAC learns a shared neural backbone for the invariant component and task-specific heads for the heterogeneous component, followed by joint MAP inference to disentangle X and W. Extensive experiments on real-world data from Manhattan and Newark show substantial accuracy gains, and transfer learning experiments validate the invariance assumption.

## Method Summary
The task is to infer true event counts \(X^k\) from observed reports \(Y^k\) and confounder vectors \(Z\) for multiple event types \(k\). The causal structure includes directed edges \(Z \rightarrow X^k\), \(Z \rightarrow W^k\), \(X^k \rightarrow W^k\), and \(W^k \rightarrow Y^k\); some edges are forbidden by prior knowledge (e.g., \(W \rightarrow Z\), \(X \rightarrow Z\), \(Y \rightarrow \cdot\)). The adjacency matrix \(A\) is partially learned under an acyclicity constraint \(\text{tr}(\exp(A \odot A)) - |n| = 0\). The model comprises: (1) a causal graph learner optimizing \(A\); (2) a multi-task structural equation model (SEM) with a shared MLP backbone \(\theta_W\) that maps \(Z\) to embeddings for each mechanism variable \(W_i\), and task-specific heads \(\phi_W^k\) that further condition these embeddings on \(X^k\) to produce task-specific \(W^k_i\); separate MLP decoders handle \(p(X^k|Z)\), \(p(Y^k|W^k)\), and \(p(W^k|Z)\) (via the shared backbone) and \(p(W^k|X^k)\) (via the head). All variable distributions are Gaussian. Training maximizes the joint log-likelihood of all variables plus a weighted acyclicity penalty, using Adam on batches that mix tasks. For test points, MAP estimation initializes \(X^k\) and \(W^k\) from the learned conditionals and then performs gradient ascent on the joint log-probability (with \(\theta\) and fixed graph) to refine them. Several implementation details (e.g., initial learning rate for \(A\), MAP step size, number of iterations, spatial-temporal split) are not fully specified; a Minimum Viable Reproduction Plan suggests reasonable defaults (e.g., 2-layer MLPs with hidden dimension 64, Adam optimizer, and validation‑tuned hyperparameters).

## Key Results
- **Superior accuracy**: MTAC achieves up to 34.61% lower MAE than state-of-the-art baselines across parking violations, abandoned properties, and unsanitary conditions.
- **Benefit of multi‑task learning**: Joint training reduces error compared to single‑task training for every task, confirming the advantage of sharing the invariant reporting bias module.
- **Validation of invariance**: Transfer learning with a frozen shared backbone (fine‑tuning only task‑specific heads) outperforms single‑task models, especially on the smallest dataset, demonstrating that the SES→W mechanism generalizes across tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:**  
  Reporting preferences (mechanism variables **W**) are shaped by socioeconomic status (**Z**) through psychological pathways that are invariant across different urban event types (**X**).
- **Mechanism:**  
  A task‑shared neural backbone (**θ_W**) models *p*(**W** | **Z**) identically for all tasks, capturing the invariant causal effect of confounders on reporting bias.
- **Core assumption:**  
  Psychological mediators (social identity, trust in government, etc.) influence reporting willingness regardless of the event category.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: Explicitly states the "causal mechanism linking socioeconomic confounders to reporting preferences is invariant across tasks."  
  - [section 5]: Details the 5 psychological pathways and the SES layers that are "independent of the urban event type."  
  - [corpus]: Neighbor paper *"A Bayesian Spatial Model to Correct Under-Reporting in Urban Crowdsourcing"* (Agostini et al., 2024) confirms under‑reporting correlates with neighborhood SES, supporting the existence of invariant reporting bias.  
  *Corpus evidence for this specific invariance mechanism is indirect but consistent.*
- **Break condition:**  
  If tasks exhibit fundamentally different SES‑to‑preference mappings (e.g., one event type where high education reduces reporting), the shared **θ_W** would force incorrect sharing, increasing error.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:**  
  The causal effect of the urban event occurrence (**X**) on reporting preferences (**W**) is task‑specific.
- **Mechanism:**  
  Separate task‑specific neural heads (**φ_W^k**) model *p*(**W** | **X**^k) for each event type, while sharing the **Z**‑dependent backbone.
- **Core assumption:**  
  Residents’ propensity to report varies by incident category (e.g., parking violations vs. unsanitary conditions).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: Notes "the event‑to‑preference link is task‑specific."  
  - [section 4.2]: "The causal effect of urban event occurrence is task‑specific, reflecting the heterogeneous reporting propensities of residents toward different categories of urban incidents."  
  - [corpus]: Neighbor *"The built environment and induced transport CO2 emissions"* (2023) discusses context‑specific behavioral responses, analogously supporting task‑specific effects.  
  *Corpus does not directly address urban reporting, but shows context‑specificity in related human behavior.*
- **Break condition:**  
  If all event types trigger identical reporting adjustments (unlikely per social studies), task‑specific heads add unnecessary parameters and may overfit small datasets.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:**  
  Joint MAP optimization over latent mechanism variables (**W**) and cause magnitudes (**X**) disentangles true event counts from reporting bias.
- **Mechanism:**  
  Maximizing *p*(**X**, **W** | **Y**, **Z**) balances: (1) **Y** explained by **W** (reporting model), (2) **W** consistent with shared *p*(**W** | **Z**) and task‑specific *p*(**W** | **X**^k) (multi‑task prior), (3) **X** consistent with *p*(**X** | **Z**) (cause prior).
- **Core assumption:**  
  The learned forward SEM accurately represents the true data‑generating process.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: Mentions "MAP‑based inference to reconstruct causes by jointly optimizing latent mechanism variables and cause magnitudes."  
  - [section 4.3]: Derives the objective and notes it "balances the task‑specific and task‑shared causal effects."  
  - [section 6.6 (ablation)]: Removing MAP ("w/o MAP") causes MAE to jump by up to 254.49%, proving its necessity for leveraging **Y**.
- **Break condition:**  
  If the SEM is severely misspecified (e.g., omitted key confounders), MAP may converge to a biased **X** that fits **Y** but not the true causal structure.

## Foundational Learning
- **Anti‑causal estimation**  
  *Why needed here:* The task is to infer latent causes (**X**: event counts) from observed effects (**Y**: reports), opposite to standard predictive learning.  
  *Quick check question:* Why can’t we simply train a regressor **Y** → **X** without a causal model?
- **Multi‑task shared‑private decomposition**  
  *Why needed here:* To exploit invariance in *p*(**W** | **Z**) across tasks while allowing *p*(**W** | **X**^k) to vary, enabling knowledge transfer especially for small datasets.  
  *Quick check question:* What would happen if we shared all parameters across tasks or none at all?
- **Causal discovery with acyclicity constraints**  
  *Why needed here:* To learn a valid DAG (Figure 1) from data, identifying which **Z** factors are true confounders of **X**→**Y** via **W**.  
  *Quick check question:* Why enforce *tr*(exp(**A**⊙**A**)) − |n| = 0 instead of hard DAG constraints?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map** (from Figure 2 and Section 4):  
  Inputs: **Y**^k (reports), **Z** (SES features).  
  Latent: **X**^k (event count), **W**^k (reporting preference).  
  Modules:  
  1. Causal graph learner (learns **A** with acyclicity loss).  
  2. Multi‑task SEM: shared backbone **θ_W** for *p*(**W** | **Z**), task‑specific heads **φ_W^k** for *p*(**W** | **X**^k), plus SEMs for *p*(**X** | **Z**) and *p*(**Y** | **W**).  
  3. MAP optimizer: gradient‑based refinement of **X**^k, **W**^k via joint log‑likelihood.
- **Critical path**:  
  1. Learn **A** by minimizing **L** = **L_nll** + *ω*·**L_acyc** (Eq. 10–12).  
  2. Train full SEM with fixed **A** (thresholded) across all tasks.  
  3. For new data point, initialize **X**^k = *max* *p*(**X**^k | **Z**), **W**^k = *max* *p*(**W**^k | **X**^k, **Z**), then run gradient descent on Eq. 7 until convergence.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - *Shared backbone size*: Larger **θ_W** increases capacity to model *p*(**W** | **Z**) but risks overfitting if invariance weak.  
  - *Number of **W***: More mechanism variables capture finer‑grained psychological paths (Section 6.6) but may introduce noise if insufficient data.  
  - *MAP iterations*: More steps better disentangle **X**/**W** but increase inference cost; early stopping needed.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Poor transfer learning (Table 4): if shared **θ_W** hurts performance vs. single‑task, invariance assumption likely violated.  
  - High error on smallest dataset (abandoned property) even with multi‑task: indicates **θ_W** not generalizable or **|W|** insufficient.  
  - Causal graph **A** includes edges contradicting domain knowledge (e.g., **W**→**Z**): suggests discovery failed or data confounded.
- **First 3 experiments** (from Section 6):  
  1. **Multi‑task vs. single‑task MTAC** (Table 3): Joint training reduces MAE/MSE across all tasks, confirming benefit of shared **θ_W**.  
  2. **Transfer learning with frozen backbone** (Table 4): Head‑only fine‑tuning outperforms single‑task trained models, validating invariance of SES→**W**.  
  3. **Ablation on mechanism variable count** (Table 5): Reducing **|W|** from 5 increases error, justifying multi‑dimensional psychological pathways.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the MTAC framework generalize to domains beyond urban event reconstruction, such as healthcare or environmental science, where the causal structure might differ?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The evaluation is restricted to three urban event types in two cities, and while the authors mention other applications like air pollution or clinical diagnosis, these are not empirically tested.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates effectiveness only in a specific domain, leaving open whether the assumed causal invariance and model structure hold in other contexts with different data generation processes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply MTAC to diverse domains with known causal relationships, compare performance against domain‑specific baselines, and validate the invariance assumptions through transfer learning experiments across heterogeneous tasks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How robust is MTAC to violations of the fixed causal graph structure, particularly the enforced acyclicity and forbidden edges (e.g., W→Z, X→Z)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The adjacency matrix A is partially learned but constrained by fixed edges based on prior knowledge (Equation 4), which may be incorrect in some applications.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze sensitivity to these hard constraints; incorrect priors could lead to biased causal discovery and poor estimation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct ablation studies where the fixed edges are systematically altered (e.g., allowing W→Z) and measure changes in reconstruction accuracy and causal graph recovery on synthetic and real data with known ground truth.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the assumption of task‑invariant causal effects from confounders to mechanism variables hold for a broader set of tasks beyond the three urban events studied?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The problem formulation states that "the causal effect of the confounders is shared across tasks" (Section 3), and this is validated via transfer learning within the same domain.
- **Why unresolved:** Transfer learning experiments only involve the three urban event types, which are closely related; it is unclear if invariance extends to more diverse tasks where underlying mechanisms might differ (e.g., different types of diseases or pollution sources).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design experiments with tasks from distinct domains but hypothesized shared confounder effects (e.g., different medical conditions affected by age and genetics), and test if transferring the shared backbone improves performance over single‑task training.

## Limitations
- Causal discovery hyperparameters (initialization, acyclicity weight, threshold) are unspecified, hindering reproducibility of the learned graph.
- MAP inference details (step size, iterations, convergence tolerance) are not provided, potentially affecting the quality of disentanglement for test samples.
- Spatial/temporal split strategy is unstated; random splits may leak autocorrelation and inflate reported gains.

## Confidence
- Invariant SES→preference mechanism: **Medium**
- Task‑specific event→preference effects: **Medium**
- MAP optimization necessity: **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Sensitivity analysis on shared backbone capacity**: Vary hidden dimension and number of layers of the shared backbone to test whether invariance holds or if over/under‑sharing degrades performance, particularly on smaller datasets.
2. **Cross‑city zero‑shot transfer**: Freeze the shared backbone trained on Manhattan and deploy it on a fully held‑out city (e.g., Chicago) without fine‑tuning the task‑specific heads; a significant performance drop would challenge the invariance assumption.
3. **Domain expert audit of learned graph**: Present the thresholded adjacency matrix to urban sociologists to verify whether identified confounder edges (e.g., specific SES variables → reporting bias) align with established knowledge about SES‑driven reporting behavior.
