# Breaking Data Symmetry is Needed For Generalization in Feature Learning Kernels

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00316
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00316
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** RFM fails to generalize on data invariant under a non‑singleton subgroup of the target’s symmetry group; breaking that symmetry with a few points restores generalization.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the phenomenon of grokking in the Recursive Feature Machine (RFM) algorithm using algebraic tasks such as modular arithmetic. The authors apply group theory to characterize the symmetry group of the target function, which is isomorphic to the dihedral group \(D_{2p}\). They design train‑test partitions that are invariant under subgroups of this symmetry group and study when RFM generalizes. They find that RFM attains 0% test accuracy when the training set is invariant under any non‑singleton subgroup \(H\) (e.g., all points except fixed points of a reflection). In contrast, moving even a single random training point to the test set, thereby breaking \(H\)-invariance, immediately restores high accuracy. Analysis of the learned feature matrix (AGOP) shows alignment with permutation representations of the subgroup \(H\), and initializing RFM with features encoding \(H\) confines its generalization to the \(H\)-orbit of the training set. This work highlights a critical role of symmetry breaking for generalization in feature learning kernels.

## Method Summary
The study uses the Recursive Feature Machine (RFM), a kernel method that iteratively updates a positive semi‑definite feature matrix \(M_t\) via the Average Gradient Outer Product (AGOP). For modular addition on \(\mathbb{Z}_p \times \mathbb{Z}_p\), inputs are one‑hot encoded concatenations of two group elements, and labels are one‑hot vectors of the sum. The kernel is either quadratic \((x^\top M x')^2\) or Gaussian \(\exp(-\|x-x'\|_M^2 / L)\). Each iteration: compute the training kernel matrix \(K_{M_t}\), solve \(\alpha = K_{M_t}^{-1} y\) (interpolation), form the predictor \(\hat{f}(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i k_{M_t}(x_i, \cdot)\), compute the AGOP as the empirical covariance of gradients over the training set, and update \(M_{t+1} = (AGOP)^{1/2}\). Experiments vary the train‑test split to control symmetry invariance: a random split serves as baseline; a symmetric split holds out all fixed points of a reflection (diagonal points), making the training set invariant under \(H = \{\text{id}, s\}\); further variants move a few points across the split. Test accuracy and final AGOP structure are analyzed. The reproduction plan outlines concrete steps to replicate these findings, including kernel choices, hyperparameters (\(L=2.5\), \(T=60\)), and diagnostics.

## Key Results
- RFM fails to generalize (0% test accuracy) when the training set is invariant under a non‑singleton subgroup \(H\) of the symmetry group \(D_{2p}\).
- Breaking \(H\)-invariance by moving just 1–2 random training points to the test set restores high accuracy (~98% with Gaussian kernel).
- The learned AGOP matrix aligns with the permutation representation of \(H\), and initializing RFM with \(H\)-encoding features restricts generalization to the \(H\)-orbit of the training set.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: \(H\)-invariance traps RFM
- **Claim:** RFM fails to generalize when the training set is invariant under a non‑singleton subgroup **H** of the symmetry group **G** of the target function.
- **Mechanism:** Invariance under **H** causes the Average Gradient Outer Product (AGOP) to learn features that only encode the subgroup **H**, effectively trapping the model in a symmetry‑induced local structure. The learned feature matrix aligns with the permutation representation of **H**, restricting generalization to points within the orbit of **H** acting on the training set.
- **Core assumption:** The target function’s symmetry group **G** (isomorphic to the dihedral group \(D_{2p}\)) fully characterizes the task structure, and data points with the same label lie in the same **G**‑orbit.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “We find that the learned feature matrices encode specific elements of the invariance group, explaining the dependence of generalization on symmetry.”
  - Section 3: “RFM fails to generalize only when using train‑test partitions that are invariant under the action of a non‑singleton subgroup, \(H\), of the symmetry group \(G = \text{Sym}_f \cong D_{2p}\).”
  - Corpus: Neighbor papers on symmetry breaking and grokking provide contextual support.
- **Break condition:** Moving even a single random training point to the test set—thereby breaking **H**‑invariance—allows RFM to recover generalization.

### Mechanism 2: AGOP aligns with permutation representations of \(H\)
- **Claim:** The AGOP matrix learned by RFM aligns with the permutation representation of the symmetry subgroup **H** that the training partition is invariant under.
- **Mechanism:** AGOP, as the empirical covariance of gradients, captures the directions of input transformations that preserve the training labels. If the training set is **H**‑invariant, these directions correspond exactly to the linear representations of elements in **H** (e.g., identity and a specific reflection \(sr_k\)).
- **Core assumption:** The input data is one‑hot encoded, so group actions on data points are implemented as permutation matrices, and AGOP learns a linear transformation that respects these permutations.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Section 4: “We find that the learned feature matrices align with representations of the symmetry group for the target function… learned feature matrix… only has notable stripes in the points corresponding permutation representations of the subgroup elements.”
  - Figure 2 compares learned AGOP matrices to theoretical permutation representations of subgroup elements, showing structural alignment.
- **Break condition:** Initializing RFM with a feature matrix \(M_0\) that encodes a different subgroup **H'** (not **H**) can induce generalization to points outside the **H**‑orbit (Appendix F).

### Mechanism 3: Initialization with \(H\) confines generalization to its orbit
- **Claim:** RFM generalizes only to test points that lie within the **H**‑orbit of the training set when initialized with features encoding **H**.
- **Mechanism:** If \(M_0\) encodes a subgroup **H**, the RFM dynamics remain confined to transformations that commute with **H**. Consequently, the model can only correctly classify points that are reachable from some training point via an element of **H**.
- **Core assumption:** RFM’s iterative feature updates, starting from \(M_0\), cannot escape the representation class defined by **H**; the AGOP updates preserve the subspace spanned by the representations of **H**.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Section 4, Finding 4.1: “RFM… will only classify correctly the points in \(\text{Orbit}_H(X_{tr})\).”
  - Section 4, Figure 4: Cayley tables show a perfect match between correct predictions and theoretical prediction.
- **Break condition:** Either (a) the training set itself is not **H**‑invariant, or (b) \(M_0\) does not encode the same **H** as the training partition’s invariance subgroup.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Group actions and orbits.**  
*Why needed here:* The data’s structure arises from a group action (dihedral group \(D_{2p}\)) on the input space \((a,b)\). Understanding how subgroups partition the data into orbits (e.g., fixed points vs. reflection pairs) is essential to predict generalization.  
*Quick check:* Given a reflection \(sr_k\), what is the orbit of a point \((a,b)\) under \(\langle sr_k \rangle\) if \(b = a + k\)?

**Concept: Symmetry invariance of training data.**  
*Why needed here:* A training set invariant under subgroup **H** means \(h(X_{tr}) = X_{tr}\) for all \(h \in H\). This invariance determines what the AGOP can learn.  
*Quick check:* If \(X_{tr}\) contains all points except the fixed points of reflection \(s\), is \(X_{tr}\) invariant under \(H = \{\text{id}, s\}\)?

**Concept: Permutation representations.**  
*Why needed here:* One‑hot encoded data turns group elements into permutation matrices. The learned AGOP matrix is a linear combination of these permutation representations, which can be decoded to identify the subgroup **H**.  
*Quick check:* What does the permutation matrix for \(sr_k\) look like on the concatenated vector \([e_a; e_b]\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
The RFM pipeline processes data through the following components:
1. **Input Encoder** – one‑hot encodes two group elements \((a,b)\) into a vector \(x = [e_a; e_b] \in \mathbb{R}^{2n}\).
2. **Kernel** – computes a Mahalanobis kernel \(k_M(x, x') = (x^\top M x')^2\) or \(\exp(-\|x-x'\|_M^2 / L)\) using the current feature matrix \(M\).
3. **Feature Matrix** – the positive semi‑definite matrix \(M_t\) is updated each iteration.
4. **AGOP** – given the current predictor \(\hat{f}(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i k_{M_t}(x_i, \cdot)\) with \(\alpha = K_{M_t}^{-1}y\), the average gradient outer product is computed over the training set: \(AGOP = \frac{1}{|X_{tr}|}\sum_i \nabla_x \hat{f}(x_i)^\top \nabla_x \hat{f}(x_i)\).
5. **Update** – set \(M_{t+1} = (AGOP)^{1/2}\) (matrix square root).
The process iterates for \(T\) steps. Optionally, one can initialize \(M_0\) with a specific subgroup representation.

### Critical path
1. **Data partition design** – Choose train/test split based on **H**‑invariance (e.g., exclude fixed points of a reflection).
2. **RFM training** – Run iterative kernel fitting and AGOP updates; monitor test accuracy.
3. **Diagnosis** – If test accuracy remains 0%, check whether training set is **H**‑invariant for some non‑singleton **H**.
4. **Symmetry breaking** – Break **H**‑invariance by moving a single random training point to the test set; expect immediate generalization recovery.
5. **Feature analysis** – Compare final \(M_T\) to permutation representations of **G** to identify encoded subgroup.

### Design tradeoffs
- *Kernel choice:* Gaussian vs. quadratic affects how many random points must be removed to break symmetry (Gaussian often requires fewer).
- *Bandwidth \(L\):* Not explored; may influence AGOP spectrum and convergence speed.
- *Subgroup initialization:* Using \(M_0\) encoding a specific **H** deliberately restricts generalization to \(\text{Orbit}_H(X_{tr})\), enabling controlled experiments.

### Failure signatures
- 0% test accuracy on a held‑out set that forms a subgroup orbit (e.g., all fixed points of a reflection).
- Final AGOP matrix shows stripes only on positions corresponding to a subgroup’s permutation representation (e.g., identity and one reflection).
- Test accuracy does not improve with additional iterations, only with symmetry breaking in data.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate non‑generalizing partition:** Train RFM (Gaussian kernel, \(M_0 = I\)) on all data except fixed points of a reflection \(sr_k\). Verify 0% test accuracy on held‑out fixed points.
2. **Symmetry breaking:** Starting from the same partition, move 1 random training point to test set. Retrain and confirm test accuracy jumps to ~98%.
3. **Orbit confinement:** Initialize \(M_0\) with the permutation matrix of reflection \(s\) (from a failed run). Train on a random 50% split. Visualize Cayley table: test points correctly classified iff they lie in \(\text{Orbit}_{\{id,s\}}(X_{tr})\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How sensitive is the symmetry‑breaking requirement to kernel and hyperparameter choices?** The quadratic kernel required more random points to break symmetry than Gaussian; the impact of bandwidth \(L\) remains unexplored.
- **Can “symmetry traps” be formally defined and proven?** Theoretical proofs are needed to show why AGOP converges to the permutation representation of \(H\) and how that impedes generalization.
- **Does the symmetry‑based explanation extend to non‑abelian groups?** For non‑abelian groups, symmetry groups and conjugacy classes become more complex; it is unknown whether the same \(H\)-invariance failure occurs.
- **Is there a correspondence between symmetry breaking in RFM and the lazy‑to‑rich transition in neural networks?** Connecting RFM’s kernel evolution with NTK changes and feature learning could unify grokking mechanisms.
- **Why does delayed generalization (grokking) appear specifically on algebraic tasks?** It is unclear whether a rich symmetry group is necessary for grokking, or if breaking those symmetries eliminates it.

## Limitations
- AGOP–subgroup alignment is demonstrated via visual patterns without quantitative metrics; strength and generality uncertain.
- Experiments are restricted to a single prime \(p=61\) for modular addition; unknown if results hold for other algebraic tasks or primes.
- Implementation details (Jacobian computation, matrix square root) are incompletely specified, risking reimplementation errors.

## Confidence
- **H‑invariance blocks generalization**: High
- **AGOP encodes \(H\)**: Medium
- **Symmetry breaking restores generalization**: High
- **Initialization restricts to \(H\)-orbit**: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑task & cross‑prime replication:** Repeat core experiments for primes \(p \in \{59, 67, 73\}\) and for modular multiplication; verify that \(H\)-invariance still blocks generalization and that moving \(\le 1\) point recovers accuracy.
2. **Quantitative AGOP projection:** For final AGOP matrices, compute the coefficient of determination \(R^2\) when projecting onto the linear span of all permutation matrices in \(D_{2p}\). Expect near‑perfect \(R^2\) for the identified \(H\) and negligible values for other subgroups.
3. **Alternative kernel learner test:** Replace RFM’s AGOP update with a standard kernel recursive least squares (KRLS) or a neural network with a Mahalanobis layer. If the same symmetry dependence persists, the phenomenon is not RFM‑specific.
