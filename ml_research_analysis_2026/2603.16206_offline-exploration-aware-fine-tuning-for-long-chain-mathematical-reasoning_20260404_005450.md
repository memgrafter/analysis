# Offline Exploration-Aware Fine-Tuning for Long-Chain Mathematical Reasoning
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.16206
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16206
- Reference count: 29
- Primary result: OXA achieves average +6 Pass@1 and +5 Pass@k improvements over standard SFT on six math benchmarks, elevates initial policy entropy, and gains persist through RLVR.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the lack of exploration-aware initialization in RLVR-based mathematical reasoning by proposing Offline eXploration-Aware (OXA) fine-tuning. OXA decouples fine-tuning into two complementary objectives: (1) maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) on low-confidence (high-perplexity) correct teacher trajectories to internalize novel reasoning patterns, and (2) an unlikelihood loss on high-confidence (low-perplexity) incorrect self-distilled trajectories to suppress spurious reasoning peaks. A Gaussian-guided perplexity sampling strategy selects an optimal mix of samples. Evaluated on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math across six benchmarks, OXA yields consistent gains in Pass@1 and Pass@k, increases policy entropy, and these benefits transfer to subsequent RLVR training. The approach is validated across model scales (1.5B–7B) and architectures (LLaMA, Qwen), with ablations confirming each component’s contribution and orthogonality to RLVR methods.

## Method Summary
OXA performs offline fine-tuning with two data streams. Positive samples are teacher-generated correct solutions filtered by correctness and high perplexity (using the base model); these are selected via Gaussian-guided PPL sampling and trained with MLE to teach novel reasoning patterns. Negative samples are self-distilled incorrect trajectories with low perplexity; they are trained with a token-level unlikelihood loss to reduce their probabilities, redistributing mass away from erroneous peaks. The two objectives are mixed in a single training run, producing a policy with higher entropy that better explores during RLVR. The sampling strategy controls the PPL distribution to balance novelty and learnability, and the approach is agnostic to model architecture.

## Key Results
- OXA (full) on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math: average +6 Pass@1 and +5 Pass@k over standard SFT across six benchmarks.
- OXA increases initial policy entropy, and this higher entropy decays slower during RLVR, leading to sustained exploration benefits.
- Ablations show both MLE on high-PPL correct samples and unlikelihood on low-PPL incorrect samples contribute positively; OXA is orthogonal to RLVR methods (e.g., GRPO, RLOO) and yields improvements across 1.5B–7B models and LLaMA/Qwen variants.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: By reinforcing low-confidence correct trajectories, OXA expands the model's reasoning output space, enabling generation of diverse and novel reasoning patterns that were previously unmastered.
- Mechanism: Teacher-distilled data is filtered for correctness via a verifier; high-perplexity (PPL) samples are selected using Gaussian-guided sampling (Algorithm 1) and trained with maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to increase probabilities of these under-represented correct paths.
- Core assumption: The base model's high PPL on a correct trajectory indicates it is an infrequent or novel reasoning pattern; mastering such patterns broadens the model's capability frontier without reinforcing already-mastered patterns.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "promote low-confidence (high-perplexity) correct teacher-distilled trajectories via MLE to internalize new reasoning patterns"
  - [section 3.2.1]: Details the data selection (correct + high PPL) and MLE objective; mentions "internalizes previously uncaptured reasoning trajectories"
  - [corpus]: Corpus shows related work on data selection for efficiency (e.g., Select2Reason) but not for exploration via PPL stratification; evidence is indirect.
- Break condition: If the selected high-PPL samples are actually incorrect due to verifier errors or exceed the model's learning capacity, this could degrade performance or cause instability.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Suppressing high-confidence incorrect trajectories via unlikelihood loss prevents reinforcement of spurious reasoning peaks, redistributing probability mass toward potentially correct alternatives and maintaining higher policy entropy.
- Mechanism: Self-distilled trajectories from a preliminary model are verified; low-PPL incorrect samples (high-confidence errors) are selected and trained with token-level unlikelihood loss L_UL = -log(1-p) to reduce their probabilities.
- Core assumption: High-confidence errors represent erroneous patterns that conventional training would over-reinforce; explicitly penalizing them avoids locking into incorrect peaks and encourages exploration of correct alternatives.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "suppress high-confidence (low-perplexity) incorrect self-distilled trajectories via unlikelihood loss to redistribute probability mass"
  - [section 3.2.2]: Describes negative selection and unlikelihood training; notes gradient instability requiring small α.
  - [section A.1]: Theoretical analysis shows unlikelihood gradients scale with odds ratio p/(1-p); for high-confidence errors (p≈1) this explodes, justifying small α.
- Break condition: Over-suppression may inadvertently penalize valid but less frequent reasoning steps; α set too large causes training divergence.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Gaussian-guided PPL sampling optimally balances learning novel patterns (high PPL) and training stability (avoiding too-difficult samples), outperforming fixed-interval or random sampling.
- Mechanism: Data is binned by PPL; target sample counts per bin follow a Gaussian distribution centered at µ with dispersion σ; within bins, longer responses are prioritized. This explicitly controls the PPL mix of training data.
- Core assumption: There exists a sweet spot of PPL values (e.g., 2.0–3.0) where samples are challenging enough to introduce new patterns but not so difficult as to be unlearnable; a Gaussian around this spot yields the best learning signal.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3.2.1]: "To prevent the training set from being dominated by excessively difficult learning samples, we design a Gaussian-guided PPL sampling algorithm"
  - [section A.2]: Preliminary experiments comparing fixed intervals (1.0–2.0, etc.) versus Gaussian-guided show the latter achieves superior performance on AIME24.
  - [corpus]: Corpus contains data-pruning methods for efficiency (e.g., Select2Reason) but not PPL-guided sampling for exploration; evidence is weak.
- Break condition: If µ or σ are poorly tuned for a given model scale, sampling may miss the optimal PPL region, reducing effectiveness; e.g., µ too high for a small model leads to overly hard samples.

## Foundational Learning
- Concept: Policy Entropy
  - Why needed: OXA's goal is to produce an SFT model with high policy entropy to drive exploration in RLVR. Entropy measures the spread of the output distribution; higher entropy increases diversity of sampled reasoning trajectories.
  - Quick check question: If during SFT the model's average response length increases while Pass@1 stays constant, what is the likely impact on policy entropy and why?
- Concept: Cross-Entropy vs. Unlikelihood Loss
  - Why needed: OXA combines MLE (CE loss) for positive samples and unlikelihood loss for negative samples. Their gradient dynamics differ critically, especially for high-confidence tokens, affecting training stability.
  - Quick check question: Why does the unlikelihood loss gradient for non-target tokens include an odds ratio term that can explode when the negative token probability is near 1?
- Concept: Perplexity
  - Why needed: PPL serves as a proxy for model confidence. Low PPL indicates high confidence (familiar pattern); high PPL indicates uncertainty. OXA uses PPL to stratify data into low-confidence (high PPL) and high-confidence (low PPL) groups.
  - Quick check question: If two reasoning trajectories have the same token-level accuracy but one is twice as long, how would their PPL compare assuming similar per-token confidence?

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map:
  - Input: Base pretrained model + teacher-distilled dataset (e.g., AceReason-1.1-SFT).
  - Positive pipeline: Filter teacher data with math-verify → compute PPL with base model → Gaussian-guided sampling (Algorithm 1) → select high-PPL correct samples (~50k).
  - Negative pipeline: Train a small instruction model on a subset → generate self-distilled rollouts → verify → compute PPL with base model → select low-PPL incorrect samples (~50k).
  - Training: Mix positive and negative batches → loss = L_CE (positive) + α·L_UL (negative) → update model.
  - Output: OXA-finetuned model with higher entropy and improved reasoning performance.
- Critical path:
  - Unlikelihood training is numerically delicate. The gradient magnitude for non-target tokens scales with p_xt/(1-p_xt); for high-confidence errors (p_xt → 1) this explodes. Mitigation: use small α (e.g., 1e-4), select only moderately high-confidence errors, and monitor gradients.
  - Gaussian sampling parameters (µ, σ) must be adapted to model scale; e.g., 1.5B models use µ=2.5, 7B models use µ=3.0. Mis-tuning leads to suboptimal data mixes.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Positive vs. negative sample count: Equal counts (50k each) by default; increasing positives may boost mastery of new patterns, increasing negatives may improve suppression but risk instability.
  - α: Smaller α ensures stability but weakens suppression; larger α risks divergence.
  - µ: Larger µ selects harder (higher-PPL) samples; must align with model capacity.
  - Length prioritization: Favors longer reasoning chains, potentially improving complex problem-solving but may introduce verbosity.
  - Data source: Teacher quality for positives; self-distillation model quality for negatives.
- Failure signatures:
  - Loss spikes or NaN: α too large or negative samples include near-certain errors (p≈1).
  - No Pass@1 gain: Positive sampling off (µ mis-set) or teacher data quality low.
  - Entropy does not increase: Gaussian sampling ineffective or negative suppression too weak.
  - Degradation on easy problems: Over-suppression penalized valid simple patterns.
  - Excessive length without accuracy gain: Length prioritization induced verbosity.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. **Baseline validation**: Compare OXA (OXAMLE, OXAFull) vs. SFT and SFTLP on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math across 6 benchmarks; expect OXA to show ~+6 Pass@1 average improvement.
  2. **Entropy tracking**: Measure policy entropy on a held-out set during RLVR training for each SFT variant; expect OXA models to start with higher entropy and decay slower (Figure 2(c)).
  3. **Ablation on hyperparameters**: Vary α (1e-5 to 1e-3), Gaussian µ (2.0–4.0), and compare uniform vs. Gaussian sampling; validate default settings achieve best stability/performance trade-off.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Does OXA yield consistent gains on larger language models (>7B parameters)?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "due to limited computational resources, our empirical validation was restricted to models ranging from 1.5B to 7B parameters. However, we hypothesize that the benefits of OXA will be even more pronounced in larger-scale models. ... We leave the exploration of OXA on larger model scales for future research."
- Why unresolved: The hypothesis remains untested; scaling may alter dynamics of high-PPL trajectory internalization and probability smoothing.
- What evidence would resolve it: Train OXA on models of 13B, 34B, or 70B parameters using the same mathematical benchmarks, compare Pass@k, policy entropy, and RLVR persistence against conventional SFT.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can OXA be effectively applied to other complex reasoning domains, such as code generation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "While validated on mathematics, OXA holds promise for other complex domains like code generation, which we defer to future work."
- Why unresolved: The method’s reliance on teacher-distilled trajectories and verification may behave differently in domains with less crisp correctness signals.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implement OXA on code generation tasks (e.g., HumanEval, MBPP) using distilled code reasoning data, evaluate functional correctness and diversity, and contrast with standard SFT and RLVR pipelines.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How can the computational overhead of OXA, particularly from self-distillation to obtain high-confidence errors, be reduced without sacrificing benefits?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] "OXAFull incurs additional computational overhead due to the requirement of self-distillation reasoning trajectories. This sampling process is more resource-intensive than standard SFT." (Limitations)
- Why unresolved: The overhead may hinder practical adoption; the paper does not explore efficiency trade-offs or alternatives.
- What evidence would resolve it: Propose and test alternative strategies (e.g., using earlier training checkpoints, filtering heuristics, or synthetic error generation) that approximate high-confidence errors with lower compute, measuring performance and entropy retention.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What is the optimal balance between promoting low-confidence correct trajectories (MLE) and suppressing high-confidence incorrect ones (UL) in OXA?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Full OXA sometimes achieves slightly lower Pass@1 than MLE-only but maintains competitive Pass@k, suggesting a trade-off; the UL weight α is kept small and not systematically tuned.
- Why unresolved: The interaction between the two decoupled objectives is not well understood; the fixed hyperparameters may not be optimal across model scales or datasets.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct a controlled study varying α (and possibly other sampling hyperparameters) across multiple model sizes and data distributions, measuring accuracy, entropy, training stability, and RLVR persistence.

## Limitations
- Verifier dependence: Selection relies on answer-level verification; step-level errors may lead to promoting flawed reasoning or suppressing valid patterns.
- Gaussian sampling hyperparameters: µ and σ are tuned for specific model scales; optimal values likely vary with architecture and data, but are not systematically studied.
- Computational overhead: Self-distillation for negative trajectories increases resource consumption, potentially limiting practical scalability.
