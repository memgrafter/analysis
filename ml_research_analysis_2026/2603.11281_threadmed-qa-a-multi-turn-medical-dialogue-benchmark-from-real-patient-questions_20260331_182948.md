# ThReadMed-QA: A Multi-Turn Medical Dialogue Benchmark from Real Patient Questions

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.11281
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11281
- **Reference count:** 34
- **Primary result:** Even the strongest model (GPT-5) achieved only 41.2% fully correct responses, with all models showing significant degradation by the third turn.

## Executive Summary
ThReadMed-QA introduces a multi-turn medical dialogue benchmark derived from real patient-physician interactions on r/AskDocs, comprising 2,437 threads and 8,204 question-answer pairs across up to 9 turns. Evaluating five state-of-the-art LLMs on a stratified test set revealed severe performance degradation as conversations progress, widespread error cascades, and low conversational consistency. The study exposes a substantial gap in current LLMs' ability to handle realistic, sequential medical queries and provides new metrics to diagnose failure modes.

## Method Summary
The dataset was constructed by scraping r/AskDocs, filtering for verified physician replies and linear patient-physician threads with at least two follow-ups, yielding 2,437 fully-answered threads. A stratified test set of 238 threads (948 QA pairs) was used. Models were evaluated in a zero-shot, multi-turn setting where each response was appended to the conversation history. A GPT-4o judge scored responses against physician answers using a three-point rubric (correct, partially correct, incorrect). New metrics—Conversational Consistency Score (CCS) and Error Propagation Rate (EPR)—quantified within-thread consistency and error cascades. An oracle-physician ablation replaced model prior responses with ground truth to isolate the effects of question difficulty versus context poisoning.

## Key Results
- All five models degraded significantly from turn 0 to turn 2 (p<0.001), with wrong-answer rates roughly tripling by the third turn.
- Error cascades were severe: after a wrong turn, the probability of another wrong turn was 1.9–6.1× higher than after a correct turn.
- No model answered correctly 47.4% of QA pairs; GPT-5, the best model, achieved only 41.2% fully correct responses.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Cumulative Context Complexity
Model performance degrades as medical dialogue turns increase because each turn introduces new clinical details, clarifications, or corrections, requiring dynamic state updates. Models optimized for single-turn answers struggle to maintain accurate reasoning across sequential turns, leading to anchoring on flawed prior responses. Evidence: All models show statistically significant mean score drops from turn 0 to turn 2 (p<0.001). Break condition: If future models show no significant degradation (p≥0.05) on this benchmark, this mechanism would be falsified.

### Mechanism 2: Question Difficulty Dominates
The primary driver of degradation is the increasing intrinsic difficulty of follow-up questions, not "context poisoning" from the model's own prior errors. Patient follow-ups are clinically harder, requiring integration of new information or addressing missed safety signals. Evidence: In an oracle-physician ablation (perfect prior context), GPT-5, GPT-4o, and Llama performed *worse* at turn 2, indicating question difficulty explains >100% of the observed drop. Break condition: If an oracle ablation shows significant improvement (p<0.05) for most models, context poisoning would be dominant.

### Mechanism 3: Error Cascades
Once a model produces a wrong answer, subsequent turns experience a disproportionate increase in error probability. A wrong turn contaminates the model's internal case representation, making recovery difficult without explicit correction. Evidence: EPR shows wrong rates after a wrong turn are 1.9–6.1× higher than after a correct turn. CCS reveals high volatility (e.g., Claude Haiku volatile in 27.7% of threads), indicating threads swing between fully correct and fully wrong. Break condition: If EPR amplification factors are consistently ≤1.2× and CCS approaches 1.0, the cascade mechanism is unsupported.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multi-Turn Dialogue Evaluation**  
  Standard medical QA evaluates isolated Q&A, but real consultations are sequential. Each turn depends on prior context and may alter clinical complexity. Quick check: Why might a model scoring 75/100 on turn 0 drop below 50/100 by turn 3, even if each question seems only slightly harder?

- **Concept: LLM-as-a-Judge with Clinical Rubrics**  
  Evaluation uses a nuanced three-point rubric (correct/partial/incorrect) assessing safety, urgency, and proactive information, not just factual overlap. Quick check: What clinical elements could cause a factually dense but overly reassuring answer to score 0.0 instead of 0.5?

- **Concept: Error Propagation Metrics (EPR, CCS)**  
  Aggregate accuracy masks within-thread failures. CCS measures consistency; EPR measures cascade risk. Both diagnose why multi-turn performance fails. Quick check: How could a model have a lower mean score but a higher CCS than another? What does that imply about clinical utility?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Pipeline -> Inference Engine -> Evaluation Harness -> Oracle Ablation Module  
  Data Pipeline scrapes and filters Reddit threads; Inference Engine runs multi-turn generation; Evaluation Harness scores responses with GPT-4o judge; Oracle Ablation Module replaces prior model responses with physician answers for diagnosis.

- **Critical path:** Raw thread -> filtered fully-answered subset -> stratified test sample -> multi-turn inference -> judge scoring -> metric calculation. Oracle ablation runs parallel to isolate context vs. difficulty.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  LLM-as-judge vs. human eval: scalable but loses nuance. Fully-answered subset filtering ensures ground truth but removes unanswered scenarios. Zero-shot general-domain models avoid overfitting but may underperform on lay language.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  Steep T0→T2 drop (>15 points) with high volatility (low CCS). High EPR (>40%) with low after-correct wrong rate (<10%), indicating catastrophic recovery failure. Oracle ablation worsening performance signals the model's scaffolding was net-positive.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate turn-level degradation: Confirm all models show significant (p<0.001) drop from T0 to T2.
  2. Validate CCS and EPR: Expect low CCS (<60) and high EPR amplification (>2×) for top models.
  3. Run oracle-physician ablation: Expect GPT-5, GPT-4o, Llama to show worse T2 scores (context scaffolding positive), Claude Haiku slight improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the LLM-as-judge evaluation align with human physician judgments of correctness?
2. How comprehensible are model responses to patients compared to physician alignment?
3. Do models maintain consistency across longitudinal consultations spanning multiple days?
4. How do models generalize to non-English medical dialogues?

## Limitations
- **Model & Judge Stability**: Key models and the GPT-4o judge may be deprecated or drift, threatening reproducibility; exact inference parameters are underspecified.
- **Causal Attribution**: The "question difficulty > context poisoning" conclusion rests on a single oracle ablation; generalizability is unknown.
- **Ecological Validity**: Filtering to fully-answered threads (58.1% of raw data) may bias toward simpler, resolved cases, limiting realism for unanswered follow-ups.

## Confidence
- **Turn Degradation:** High (strong, replicated statistical evidence across 5 models on real-world corpus)
- **Primary Driver = Question Difficulty:** Medium (compelling internal ablation but lacks external validation)
- **Error Cascades:** Medium (novel metrics show clear patterns but predictive power for clinical harm unverified)

## Next Checks
1. Audit Model/Judge Availability: Confirm exact model endpoints and judge stability via re-scoring a fixed subset; calibrate if drift >5%.
2. Standardize Multi-Turn Protocol: Release exact API call templates, history formatting, and judge prompts; run sensitivity analysis on judge temperature/top-p.
3. Replicate Oracle Ablation: Test the "difficulty > poisoning" hypothesis on a neighboring multi-turn medical benchmark (e.g., MedPI) to assess generalizability.
