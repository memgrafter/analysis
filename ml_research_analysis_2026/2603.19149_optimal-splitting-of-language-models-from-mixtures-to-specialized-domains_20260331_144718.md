# Optimal Splitting of Language Models from Mixtures to Specialized Domains
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19149
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19149
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A novel scaling law predicts loss when splitting compute between general pretraining and domain-specific continued pretraining, enabling optimal allocation that consistently improves zero-shot QA accuracy by 1.5–2% at equal compute.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles how best to allocate compute between general pretraining and domain-specific continued pretraining when training multiple specialized models from a common corpus. The authors propose clustering the pretraining data into domains, training a seed model on all data, then copying it to train independent models per cluster. They introduce a scaling law that accurately predicts the loss of a model of size \(N\) after \(D\) pretraining tokens and \(D'\) specialization tokens, satisfying key asymptotic desiderata. This law enables computation of the optimal split point where specialization becomes beneficial. Experiments on models up to 2.7B parameters validate the law’s predictive accuracy (MAE 0.012–0.023, \(R^2 > 0.93\)) and show that applying the optimal split yields significant zero-shot QA gains over standard pretraining at equal compute, with split models even outperforming singletons with up to double the inference cost.

## Method Summary
The method comprises three stages: (1) cluster the pretraining corpus into \(K\) domains using balanced K-means on document embeddings; (2) train a seed model on the full corpus for \(D\) tokens; (3) copy the seed \(K\) times and continue pretraining each copy on its assigned cluster for \(D'\) tokens. The core technical contribution is a scaling law \(L(N, D, D_k)\) that models the loss as a sum of a data-general term, a domain-specific term, and an irreducible gap term \(\Delta_k\) constrained by sigmoid asymptotics. The law is fitted via basin-hopping on validation losses from various \((N, D, D')\) configurations. For a fixed total compute budget \(D_T = D + K \cdot D'\), the optimal \(D\) is found by solving the condition \(K \cdot \partial L/\partial D = \partial L/\partial D'\) derived from the law. At inference, a router selects the appropriate specialist model based on the prefix embedding.

## Key Results
- The scaling law predicts loss across extrapolation scenarios (model size, pretraining tokens, specialization tokens) with MAE 0.012–0.023 and \(R^2 > 0.93\).
- Applying the optimal split yields 1.5–2% higher zero-shot QA accuracy over standard pretraining at equal compute.
- Split models outperform single models trained with up to double the inference cost.
- The optimal fraction of compute for pretraining decays with total budget, while the absolute number of pretraining tokens increases with model size.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Split model training improves performance by reducing interference between domain-specific knowledge.
- **Mechanism:** Shared pretraining learns general language patterns, while domain-specific continued pretraining adapts parameters without the constraints of other domains, preventing interference.
- **Core assumption:** Domains are semantically distinct enough that joint training introduces conflicting updates.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [Section 3.2] The phonebook memorization experiment shows that when cluster overlap is 0% or 50%, splitting at 20–40% of total steps yields higher fact accuracy compared to full pretraining.
  - [Section 6.1] Split training with optimal allocation yields 1.5–2% higher zero-shot QA accuracy over standard pretraining at equal compute.
- **Break condition:** When domain overlap is very high (e.g., 100% in phonebook), splitting provides no benefit and may harm performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The proposed scaling law accurately predicts loss across model sizes, pretraining tokens, and specialization tokens.
- **Mechanism:** The law integrates a sigmoid-based irreducible term \(\Delta\) that captures the asymptotic gap between training on the full corpus and a single domain, satisfying key desiderata (distinct asymptotes, Chinchilla recovery).
- **Core assumption:** The functional form in Equation 4.3 captures the true loss surface for the training setup and data distribution.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 4.2] The scaling law is designed to meet three desiderata: distinct irreducible losses, recovery of Chinchilla scaling when \(D\) or \(D_k = 0\), and same irreducible loss when \(D_k \to \infty\).
  - [Section 5.2] The law achieves MAE 0.012–0.023 and \(R^2 > 0.93\) across extrapolation scenarios.
- **Break condition:** If the data distribution (e.g., DCLM) or model architecture changes significantly, the fitted parameters may not generalize; the law assumes fixed hyperparameters and clustering approach.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The optimal split point derived from the scaling law minimizes loss under a fixed compute budget.
- **Mechanism:** By comparing marginal loss improvements from additional pretraining vs. additional specialization, the condition \(K \cdot \partial L/\partial D = \partial L/\partial D'\) yields the splitting point \(D_{\text{split}}\). For total budget \(D_T\), solving equation 5.1 computes optimal \(D\).
- **Core assumption:** The loss derivatives are accurately approximated by the scaling law; also assume that the total compute is the sum \(D + K \cdot D'\) (due to training \(K\) specialized models).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 5.3] Figure 4 shows that optimal pretraining fraction decays with total budget, while absolute pretraining tokens increase with model size.
  - [Section 6.1] Experimental QA results in Figure 6 show performance peaking near the predicted optimal \(t_s\), confirming the optimization.
- **Break condition:** If the scaling law is misspecified, the computed \(D_{\text{split}}\) will be inaccurate. Also, the derivation assumes \(D'\) is small (infinitesimal) for the condition; with large \(D'\), the greedy split may not be optimal.

  - Quick check: How does the proposed scaling law differ from Chinchilla’s law in terms of variables and asymptotic behavior?

- **Concept: Continued Pretraining (CPT)**
  - Why needed: Split training relies on CPT to specialize models on domain clusters after a shared phase. CPT is a key technique for adapting large models without full retraining.
  - Quick check: What are the two main risks of continued pretraining (e.g., forgetting, interference) and how does split training mitigate them?

  - Why needed: The method clusters the pretraining corpus into semantically coherent domains to define split targets. Clustering quality directly impacts specialization gains.
  - Quick check: Why is balanced K-means used, and how are document embeddings obtained in the pipeline?

  - Why needed: The core contribution is optimally splitting compute between PT and CPT. This requires understanding that total FLOPs translate to \(D + K \cdot D'\) tokens.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  Clustering module -> Scaling law estimator -> Training orchestrator -> Router
- **Critical path**:
  1. Preprocess corpus: chunk into 2048-token windows, compute embeddings.
  2. Run balanced K-means to get \(K\) cluster centroids and assignments.
  3. Train seed model on full corpus for \(D\) tokens.
  4. Copy seed model \(K\) times; train each on its cluster for \(D'\) tokens (parallel).
  5. Collect loss data for different \((N, D, D')\) values; fit scaling law.
  6. For target total compute \(D_T\) and model size \(N\), solve equation 5.2 to find \(D_{\text{opt}}\); compute \(D' = (D_T - D_{\text{opt}})/K\).
  7. Repeat steps 3–4 with optimal \(D_{\text{opt}}\) and \(D'\) to produce final specialized models.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Number of clusters (\(K\)): Larger \(K\) increases specialization but multiplies CPT compute and inference routing cost; paper finds 16 works well.
  - Compute budget distribution: Pretraining fraction decreases with larger budgets; for small budgets, splitting may not be beneficial if \(D_T < D_{\text{split}}\).
  - Clustering algorithm: Balanced K-means ensures equal cluster sizes but may sacrifice semantic purity; unbalanced clustering could be used if domain sample counts vary.
  - Model size vs. splitting: Larger models require more pretraining tokens before splitting; but split models can match larger singletons at lower inference cost.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Poor scaling law fit: High MAE (>0.1) or \(R^2 \ll 0.9\) indicates the model cannot predict loss; check data range or functional form.
  - Low router accuracy: If prefix-based retrieval R@1 is low (<0.7 for 32 tokens), routing will select wrong clusters; consider longer prefix or better embeddings.
  - No gain from splitting: If split models do not outperform full pretraining at same compute, verify cluster separation and total compute relative to \(D_{\text{split}}\).
  - CPT instability: Large drops in validation loss on general tasks indicate catastrophic forgetting; may need lower learning rate or projection-based methods.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Clustering sanity check: Cluster a small subset (e.g., 10K documents) into \(K=4–8\) clusters. Manually sample from each cluster to check semantic coherence.
  2. Scaling law data collection: Train a 350M model with \(D \in \{10, 20, 40, 80\}\)B tokens and \(D' \in \{5, 10, 20\}\)B tokens on a single cluster; compute validation loss; verify loss decreases with more tokens.
  3. Optimal split validation: Use scaling law fit from Exp2 to predict optimal \(D\) for total budget \(D_T = 100B\). Train a model at that \(D\) and compare loss against models trained with \(D = 0\) (all CPT) and \(D = D_T\) (all PT). Expect v-shaped loss curve with minimum at predicted \(D\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can split model training be applied to privacy-sensitive domains where data from different clusters cannot be mixed during training, and does this constraint affect the optimal compute split?  
  Basis: The conclusion mentions "safe data handling" as a benefit: splitting allows training on shareable data in the pretraining phase and private data in continued pretraining without mixing, but the paper does not analyze this scenario.  
  Why unresolved: Experiments assume all data can be freely used in both phases; privacy constraints could force different \(D\) or \(D'\) per domain, altering the optimization problem.

- **Open Question 2:** What is the theoretical relationship between the number of domains \(K\), their semantic similarity, and the optimal pretraining tokens \(D_{\text{split}}\)?  
  Basis: Ablations show performance varies with \(K\), and the phonebook experiment shows overlap changes the optimal split, but no model predicts optimal \(K\) given cluster similarities.  
  Why unresolved: The scaling law does not include \(K\) or inter-cluster distances; optimal split is derived per fixed \(K\). Real data have continuous similarities.

- **Open Question 3:** Does the proposed scaling law accurately predict loss and optimal splits for larger language models (>2.7B parameters) and for sparse architectures like Mixture-of-Experts?  
  Basis: Experiments are limited to dense models up to 2.7B parameters. Appendix N includes one MoE experiment but does not test the scaling law's predictions for MoE.  
  Why unresolved: Scaling laws often break at larger scales; MoE has different compute dynamics and routing overhead, potentially invalidating the additive law form.

- **Open Question 4:** How does the optimal compute allocation between pretraining and continued pretraining change when domain data quantities are highly imbalanced?  
  Basis: The method uses balanced K-means clustering and equal \(D'\) per domain in experiments. Real-world corpora have uneven domain sizes.  
  Why unresolved: The optimization assumes equal \(D'\) per domain; imbalance could favor allocating more CPT tokens to smaller domains or adjusting the splitting time per domain.

## Limitations
- Clustering implementation details (balanced K-means with depth-1 tree) are not fully specified; standard implementations may yield different clusters, affecting domain separation.
- Continued pretraining procedure (e.g., optimizer reset) is ambiguous; variations could alter stability and performance.
- The scaling law’s generalization beyond DCLM-Baseline and dense architectures up to 2.7B parameters remains untested.

## Confidence
- **Split training reduces interference and improves zero-shot QA:** Medium. Strong empirical gains (1.5–2%) are shown, but depend on clustering quality and fail when domain overlap is very high.
- **Scaling law accurately predicts loss across model sizes and token budgets:** High for the tested DCLM-Baseline regime (MAE 0.012–0.023, \(R^2 > 0.93\)), but confidence drops for out-of-distribution settings.
- **Optimal split computation minimizes loss under fixed compute:** High conditional on the scaling law’s validity; the greedy derivation is mathematically sound within the model’s assumptions.

## Next Checks
1. **Clustering reproducibility:** Implement the exact balanced K-means (depth-1 tree) as referenced in Grangier et al. (2025); compare cluster coherence and routing accuracy against standard K-means, then measure impact on QA performance.
2. **CPT optimizer reset ablation:** Train specialized models with and without resetting optimizer buffers after pretraining; evaluate validation loss on the target domain and retention of general knowledge.
3. **Scaling law extrapolation test:** Train models on a different corpus (e.g., The Pile) and/or with a varied architecture (e.g., RoPE-style position embeddings); compute prediction error of the fitted scaling law to assess generalization.
