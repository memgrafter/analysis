# Curriculum-DPO++: Direct Preference Optimization via Data and Model Curricula for Text-to-Image Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13055  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13055  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Curriculum-DPO++ significantly improves text alignment, aesthetics, and human preference over DPO and Curriculum-DPO, with gains of 4–10% on key metrics.

## Executive Summary
Curriculum-DPO++ addresses a key weakness of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO): its treatment of all preference pairs as equally difficult. The method introduces a dual-curriculum framework that organizes training data by estimated difficulty (data-level curriculum) and simultaneously grows model capacity (model-level curriculum). By starting with easy preference pairs and a small model, then progressively introducing harder pairs and expanding trainable layers and LoRA rank, the model learns more effectively without overfitting. A reward-model-free variant uses prompt embedding masking to define difficulty when no external reward model is available. Evaluations across nine benchmarks for text alignment, aesthetics, and human preference show consistent improvements, with text alignment rising from 0.7243 to 0.7603 on LCM, demonstrating the orthogonality and synergy of the two curricula.

## Method Summary
The core of Curriculum-DPO++ is the synchronized progression of a data difficulty curriculum and a model capacity expansion schedule. Preference pairs are formed by generating multiple images per prompt and ranking them; the difficulty of a pair is defined by the magnitude of the reward score difference (larger difference = easier). Pairs are sorted into batches from easiest to hardest. The model starts with only the bottleneck and innermost down/upsampling blocks trainable, and LoRA matrices with a small initial rank. After each data batch, additional layers are unfrozen and the LoRA rank is increased (multiplying by a factor δ), copying previous weights into the expanded matrices to retain learned knowledge. This ensures the model’s learning capacity grows in step with task complexity. For settings without a reward model, a masking-based difficulty estimator progressively reduces the masking ratio of the losing sample’s prompt to create a curriculum from easy to hard.

## Key Results
- Text alignment improved from 0.7243 (DPO) to 0.7603 (Curriculum-DPO++).  
- Aesthetics score increased from 6.049 to 6.707.  
- Human preference win rate rose from 0.291 to 0.324.  
- Gains are consistent across both LCM and SD backbones and multiple benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Data-level Curriculum via Reward-ranked Preference Pairs
Organizing preference pairs by difficulty (based on reward model score differences) matches sample complexity to the model’s learning stage. Easy pairs (large score gaps) are presented first, providing strong learning signals; hard pairs (small score gaps) are introduced later when the model has greater capacity. The core assumption is that the score difference magnitude correlates with human-perceived difficulty. Evidence: The algorithm explicitly sorts pairs by rank difference and splits them into ordered batches (Section 3.3). This idea builds on prior curriculum DPO works but its integration with model-level growth is novel. Break condition: a biased reward model can produce an incorrect difficulty ordering, harming training.

### Mechanism 2: Model-level Curriculum via Progressive Capacity Expansion
Dynamically increasing trainable capacity (unfreezing layers and scaling LoRA rank) synchronizes with the data curriculum. A small initial model avoids overfitting on easy examples; capacity grows as harder pairs appear, providing sufficient representational power. Two schedules operate: (i) sequential unfreezing of outer downsampling and upsampling blocks, and (ii) progressive LoRA rank growth with weight copying to preserve knowledge. Core assumption: fixed high capacity risks overfitting early, while fixed low capacity underfits later. Evidence: The paper details the schedules and shows additive improvements in ablations (Table 3). Break condition: inappropriate growth speed (too fast/slow) can cause forgetting or underfitting; hyperparameters require tuning.

### Mechanism 3: Reward-model-free Difficulty Estimation via Prompt Embedding Masking
When no reward model is available, difficulty is controlled by masking the prompt embedding of the losing sample. Higher masking ratios degrade output quality more severely, creating easy pairs; lowering the ratio gradually introduces harder pairs. The core assumption is monotonic degradation with masking ratio. Evidence: The method is described in Section 3.5 and yields improvements over baselines (Table 2). Break condition: if the model is robust to masking, degradation may not be monotonic, making the curriculum unreliable.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Data-level curriculum sampler (with reward model or prompt mask generator) -> Model-level capacity scheduler -> DPO trainer -> LoRA-updated reference model. The reference model remains frozen to provide reference denoising errors.

Critical path:
1. Generate M images per prompt using reference model, obtain reward scores (or apply masking for reward-free).
2. Sort prompts by score descending, create valid pairs (i<j), compute rank difference j-i.
3. Assign pairs to batches S_k based on difficulty intervals (Algorithm 1).
4. Initialize LoRA with r_start and set initial trainable layers G (innermost blocks).
5. For each batch k=1..B:
   - Merge S_k into active pair set.
   - Expand trainable layers G to G_new (add one down and one up block).
   - Increase LoRA rank to r_k; if k>1, enlarge matrices and copy previous weights to top-left.
   - Perform H_k DPO update steps on cumulative pairs.
6. After training, merge LoRA weights into reference model.

Design tradeoffs:
- Capacity growth speed vs. stability: Faster growth risks catastrophic forgetting; slower growth may limit learning on hard examples. Tuned via δ and layer schedule frequency.
- Reward model reliance vs. practicality: Reward model gives accurate difficulty but adds overhead and potential bias; reward-free masking removes external model but relies on heuristic.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity: B, K, r_start, r_end, δ affect performance; paper shows robustness within ranges but optimal values depend on dataset/model.
- Memory vs. performance: Progressive expansion reduces peak memory vs. full training from start, but final memory matches baseline.

Failure signatures:
- Overfitting on easy batches: training loss on early batches drops fast but later batches show no improvement.
- Underfitting on hard batches: training loss stagnates on later batches despite many iterations.
- Reward model bias: if reward scores misalign with human preference, data curriculum may misorder difficulties.
- Weight transfer errors: incorrect copying of LoRA weights during rank increase disrupts learning.

First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline verification: Replicate DPO with fixed full capacity and random pair ordering on a small dataset to confirm reported baseline numbers.
2. Isolated model-level curriculum: Train with only capacity expansion (layer unfreezing + rank growth) but random pair ordering; compare against fixed-capacity DPO to measure effect of dynamic capacity alone.
3. Combined curriculum validation: Implement full Curriculum-DPO++ and compare against data-level only (Curriculum-DPO) and model-level only; expect synergy where combined outperforms both ablations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to LLMs:** Can the dual curricula be applied to fine-tune Large Language Models for preference alignment? How should textual difficulty be defined (e.g., prompt complexity, reasoning depth)? The paper suggests this as future work but notes the need to adapt the methodology to sequential generation and transformer architectures.
2. **Theoretical capacity-difficulty relationship:** What is the theoretical link between progressive capacity growth and the risk of over/underfitting? The paper empirically validates the schedule but lacks a formal framework to guide optimal synchronization between capacity expansion and data difficulty progression.
3. **Reward-model-free validity:** Is prompt embedding masking a reliable proxy for human-perceived preference difficulty? The assumption that masking ratio monotonically controls difficulty is novel but not validated against human judgments or reward-model-based difficulty estimates.
4. **Interaction with other curricula:** How does preference-based data curriculum interact with noise-level curricula (e.g., starting from high diffusion noise) or compositional complexity curricula? Could combining orthogonal curricula yield further gains, or do they conflict?
5. **Relative contributions:** The paper claims orthogonality between data and model curricula, but the relative contributions are not fully decomposed. What is the upper bound of benefit from model-level growth when the data curriculum is optimal? A missing experiment is comparing model-level curriculum alone (random data ordering) against DPO and Curriculum-DPO.

## Limitations
- Reward model bias can misorder the data curriculum, harming performance.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity (e.g., growth factor δ, batch count B) requires careful tuning for different datasets and models.
- The reward-model-free masking assumption (monotonic difficulty with masking ratio) is unverified against ground-truth human preference difficulty.

## Confidence
- Reward model bias: Medium (acknowledged, some mitigation via reward-free variant)  
- Hyperparameter sensitivity: High (evidenced by ablations showing performance variation)  
- Reward-free assumption validity: Low (novel heuristic with limited validation)

## Next Checks
1. Baseline replication: Re-run DPO with fixed full capacity and random pair sampling on a subset of D1 to confirm the reported baseline numbers.
2. Isolated model curriculum: Train with only the model-level capacity expansion (layer unfreezing + rank growth) and random pair ordering; compare against DPO to isolate the effect of dynamic capacity.
3. Reward-free monotonicity test: For a fixed prompt set, generate images with varying prompt masking ratios; collect human difficulty ratings or use an unbiased reward model to test if masking ratio correlates with perceived preference difficulty.
