# Adapting Time Series Foundation Models through Data Mixtures

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.02840
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02840
- **Reference count**: 37
- **Primary result**: MixFT achieves the best average rank (lowest MASE) across Cloud and Gift-Eval benchmarks compared to per-dataset and shared fine-tuning methods.

## Executive Summary
Time series foundation models (TSFMs) often underperform on new domains not fully covered during pretraining. Existing fine-tuning strategies, such as applying a single LoRA module to all data or separate modules per dataset, fail to account for heterogeneous sub-domains (e.g., distribution shifts, varying dimensions) within a single dataset. MixFT addresses this by using a Bayesian Gaussian mixture model to re-partition fine-tuning data into more homogeneous sub-domain clusters in the TSFM's embedding space, then training a dedicated LoRA module on each cluster. During zero-shot forecasting, it assigns the input context to the most likely sub-domain and uses the corresponding LoRA. Experiments on Cloud and Gift-Eval benchmarks with Chronos Bolt and Moirai TSFMs show MixFT achieves superior performance, demonstrating that refining data divisions by latent sub-domain improves specialized fine-tuning and forecasting accuracy.

## Method Summary
MixFT adapts a pretrained TSFM for zero-shot forecasting through a three-stage process. First, it embeds all fine-tuning context windows using the TSFM's embedding layer to obtain latent representations. Second, it fits a Bayesian Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) to these embeddings, with diagonal covariance and Normal-Inverse-Wishart prior, to discover K latent sub-domains. Each context is assigned to the most probable component via hard assignment, creating K homogeneous sub-datasets. Third, it trains K separate LoRA modules (rank r=2, scaling α=16, dropout=0.1) on the respective sub-datasets, using AdamW (lr=5e-5), MixUp augmentation, and experience replay with pretraining data to prevent overfitting. At inference, a new context is embedded, assigned to a sub-domain using the GMM posterior, and the corresponding LoRA is applied to generate the forecast. Hyperparameters like K are selected via validation performance.

## Key Results
- MixFT achieves the best average rank (lowest MASE) across evaluation datasets in Cloud and Gift-Eval benchmarks with Chronos Bolt and Moirai, outperforming per-dataset and shared fine-tuning baselines.
- Even when LoRA selection is controlled (e.g., forced use of Arrow adapters), MixFT's data mixtures yield better specialists than per-dataset mixtures, proving the benefit of sub-domain partitioning.
- MixFT yields superior performance on most individual datasets, with very low classification entropy indicating high confidence in sub-domain assignments and poor forecasts from non-selected LoRAs confirming the efficacy of hard assignment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper proposes three key mechanisms.

**Mechanism 1**: Datasets contain multiple latent sub-domains (e.g., distribution shifts, varying dimensions) not captured by dataset boundaries, and these are discoverable in the TSFM's embedding space. MixFT fits a Bayesian GMM to the embeddings of fine-tuning context windows; each GMM component represents a probability distribution over embeddings corresponding to a distinct sub-domain. Hard assignment creates homogeneous sub-datasets. Core assumption: the TSFM's embedding space effectively separates context windows from different underlying sub-domains. Evidence: [abstract] "re-partition fine-tuning data into more homogeneous sub-domain clusters"; [section 4.1] "The Bayesian GMM is defined in the embedding space of the TSFM"; [appendix D.5.1] very low classification entropy (~0.01 bits) indicates clear separation. Break condition: if sub-domains are not linearly separable or unimodal in the embedding space, clustering fails.

**Mechanism 2**: Training separate LoRA modules on sub-domain clusters yields more specialized adapters than training on whole datasets or pooled data, by reducing interference between dissimilar distributions. Each LoRA is trained exclusively on contexts from one GMM component, ensuring it learns modifications specific to a narrower, more consistent data distribution, avoiding catastrophic interference. Core assumption: homogeneous training data per adapter leads to better specialization and less interference. Evidence: [abstract] "each set is more homogeneous, leading to fine-tuned modules focused on specific sub-domains"; [section 4.1] "This re-division... simplifies its task... reduces the possibility of destructive interference"; [section 4.2] controlled routing ablation shows MixFT-trained LoRAs outperform others even with same routing. Break condition: if clusters are too small or noisy, specialized LoRAs overfit; if partition misaligns with target distribution, specialization is misdirected.

**Mechanism 3**: At inference, hard assignment to the most probable sub-domain and using that LoRA yields better forecasts than mixing or per-dataset routing, because forecasts from out-of-distribution (OOD) LoRAs are highly inaccurate. The GMM posterior is typically peaked, making a single dominant sub-domain identifiable. Hard assignment avoids diluting forecasts with predictions from OOD LoRAs. Core assumption: the GMM posterior is low-entropy for test contexts, and OOD LoRA forecasts are significantly worse. Evidence: [abstract] "assigns the input context to the most likely sub-domain"; [section 4.2] "forecasts given when using LoRA modules of unlikely sub-domains can be inaccurate due to the OOD nature"; [appendix D.5.1] average entropy ~0.01 bits; [appendix D.5.2] Figure 4 shows poor forecasts from non-selected LoRAs and mixing worsens performance. Break condition: if posterior is flat (high entropy), hard assignment becomes arbitrary and harmful.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Pretrained TSFM -> Embedding Extractor -> Bayesian GMM Fitter -> Sub-domain Data Splitter -> LoRA Trainer -> Zero-shot Router.

**Critical path**:
1. Prepare windowed fine-tuning datasets.
2. Embed all contexts using TSFM.
3. Fit Bayesian GMM to embeddings.
4. Assign embeddings to components, create sub-datasets.
5. Train K LoRA modules in parallel on respective sub-datasets.
6. At inference: embed test context, assign component, load corresponding LoRA, forecast.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- K selection: More components allow finer specialization but risk overfitting due to less data per LoRA; chosen via validation.
- Hard vs. soft assignment: Hard assignment avoids OOD mixing but fails on ambiguous contexts; soft weighting performed worse in ablations.
- Embedding space choice: Using TSFM's intrinsic embedding is model-agnostic but may not be optimal for clustering; alternatives not explored.
- GMM complexity: Diagonal covariance reduces parameters vs. full covariance, preventing overfitting on limited embeddings.

**Failure signatures**:
- High classification entropy on fine-tuning/evaluation sets indicates GMM component overlap or poor separation.
- Per-dataset LoRAs outperform MixFT suggests sub-domain clusters are less meaningful than dataset boundaries.
- Validation vs. evaluation discrepancy for K choice indicates sub-domain structure differs between fine-tuning and evaluation.
- Poor performance on specific datasets despite overall gain implies shared sub-domain assumption violated for those datasets.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Main comparison (Table 1): Evaluate MixFT vs. Shared, per-dataset, and Base on Cloud & Gift-Eval with Chronos Bolt and Moirai. Outcome: MixFT achieves best average rank and most bolded/second-best results.
2. Controlled routing ablation (Table 2): Fix routing to Arrow for all LoRA sets to isolate effect of trained modules. Outcome: Arrow-MixFT outperforms Arrow-Datasets and MBC, proving MixFT's data mixtures yield better specialists.
3. Probabilistic model ablation (Table 5): Replace Bayesian GMM with K-means GMM or topic model. Outcome: Bayesian GMM performs best or second-best on most datasets, validating its suitability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can dataset label information be incorporated into sub-domain discovery without losing data-driven partitioning benefits? The paper notes this as future work; current Bayesian GMM ignores dataset metadata.
2. Why does hard assignment outperform soft routing based on mixture probabilities, and how can this be leveraged? Soft mixing underperforms; posterior probabilities are typically peaked, but the exact reason and improved routing strategies remain open.
3. How can MixFT adapt when evaluation data contain sub-domains not present in fine-tuning? The paper assumes sub-domains are shared; novel sub-domains could force poor assignments without a fallback mechanism.
4. What is the theoretical justification for improved performance of data compartmentalisation, and how to determine the number of compartments without a validation set? Empirical gains are shown, but no formal analysis; K selection currently requires validation.

## Limitations
- Reproduction details are ambiguous: exact embedding extraction, experience replay data source, and validation split definitions are not fully specified.
- Clustering relies on GMM assumptions (linear separability, unimodal clusters); violations could cause poor sub-domain partitioning.
- MixFT does not always beat per-dataset fine-tuning, suggesting the approach is not universally superior.

## Confidence
- **Latent sub-domains are discoverable in TSFM embedding space**: Medium (indirect evidence from performance gains).
- **Sub-domain-specific LoRAs reduce interference and yield better specialists**: Medium-High (supported by controlled routing ablation).
- **Hard assignment via GMM posterior avoids mixing OOD forecasts**: High (very low entropy, poor non-selected forecasts, mixing ablations).
