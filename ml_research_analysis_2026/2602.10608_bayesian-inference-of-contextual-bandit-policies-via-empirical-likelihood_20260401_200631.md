# Bayesian Inference of Contextual Bandit Policies via Empirical Likelihood

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10608
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10608
- **Reference count**: 15
- **Primary result**: Bayesian empirical likelihood provides calibrated uncertainty quantification for contextual bandit policy values in finite samples; HPD intervals achieve nominal coverage while Wilks intervals undercover, and flexible policy comparisons are enabled without asymptotic approximations.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a Bayesian empirical likelihood framework for inferring contextual bandit policy values from logged bandit data. The method constructs an empirical likelihood for a vector of policy values using estimating equations derived from importance-weighted rewards, then combines it with a flat prior to obtain a posterior distribution. Unlike traditional chi‑squared approximations, this approach yields accurate uncertainty quantification even for small sample sizes. The authors develop an adaptive grid sampling scheme to approximate the posterior, and a univariate formulation for efficient inference on policy differences. Simulations demonstrate that Bayesian highest posterior density (HPD) intervals attain nominal coverage while Wilks intervals suffer from undercoverage. The method is applied to an adolescent BMI dataset, where a learned policy shows a significant improvement over a randomized baseline (posterior probability 0.92 of exceeding the baseline by at least 0.10). The work enables robust, finite‑sample‑valid policy evaluation and comparison.

## Method Summary
The core method forms an empirical likelihood for the policy value vector \(\mathbf{v}\) by solving a convex dual optimization problem subject to moment constraints: \(\mathbb{E}[w_a r_a] = v_a\) for each policy and \(\mathbb{E}[w_a] = 1\) (self‑normalization). The support of the observed importance weights \(w_a\) and binary rewards \(r_a\) is restricted to a known hyperrectangle \(S\) (e.g., \(w_a \in [0, K]\), \(r_a \in \{0,1\}\)). The resulting likelihood \(L(\mathbf{v})\) is proportional to \(\exp(\max_{\beta,\tau} \sum_i \log(1 + \beta^\top (\mathbf{w}_i-\mathbf{1}) + \tau^\top (\mathbf{w}_i r_i - \mathbf{v}))\) with feasibility constraints at the vertices of \(S\). With a flat prior on \([0,1]^\ell\), the posterior is \(p(\mathbf{v}) \propto L(\mathbf{v})\).

For inference, the authors locate an adaptive high‑likelihood sub‑support where \(L(\mathbf{v}) \geq c^{-1} L^*\) (with \(c\) set by a high‑confidence Wilks threshold) and discretize it into a Cartesian product grid. Posterior probabilities (e.g., HPD intervals, policy comparisons) are computed by normalizing over the grid. To reduce computational cost for comparing a new policy to a baseline, they derive a univariate empirical likelihood for the difference \(d = v_{\text{new}} - v_{\text{baseline}}\) by projecting the estimating equations onto \(( -1, 1 )^\top\), yielding a one‑dimensional posterior.

Key computational steps include: (1) computing importance weights \(w_i = \pi(x_i,a_i)/p(x_i,a_i)\) from logged data; (2) solving the dual optimization for log \(L\) at each grid point; (3) evaluating posterior mass; and (4) integrating over regions of interest.

## Key Results
- **Finite‑sample coverage**: Bayesian HPD intervals maintain nominal coverage rates (90%, 95%) even for small \(n\) (e.g., \(n=100\)), whereas Wilks chi‑squared intervals exhibit substantial undercoverage and width variability.
- **Policy comparison**: The posterior for \(d = v_{\text{new}} - v_{\text{baseline}}\) enables direct computation of probabilities like \(P(d > \delta)\). In simulations, the univariate and joint posteriors agree, but the univariate approach is far more efficient.
- **Practical impact**: In the adolescent BMI application, the learned policy outperforms random assignment with posterior probability \(P(v_{\text{new}} > v_{\text{baseline}} + 0.10) = 0.92\), demonstrating the value of full uncertainty quantification for decision making.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The Bayesian empirical likelihood framework provides calibrated uncertainty quantification for contextual bandit policy values in finite samples.
- **Mechanism**: Jointly defines an empirical likelihood for a vector of policy values \(\mathbf{v}\) using estimating equations \(\mathbb{E}[w_a r_a] = \mathbf{v}\) and a constraint \(\mathbb{E}[w_a] = 1\) to bound the support within \([0,1]^\ell\). This yields a posterior distribution \(p_{\text{post}}(\mathbf{v}) \propto L(\mathbf{v})\) (with a flat prior) that does not rely on asymptotic chi‑squared approximations.
- **Core assumption**: The importance weights \(\mathbf{w}\) and rewards \(\mathbf{r}\) are correctly derived from the observed data and the specified behavior and target policies. The support of the joint distribution \((\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{r})\) lies within a known hyperrectangle \(S\).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "Bayesian inference method... robust to small sample sizes... accurate uncertainty measurements... flexible inferences on policy comparison with full uncertainty quantification."
  - Section 3.1: Formal definition of \(\log L(\mathbf{v})\) via optimization over all probability measures on \(S\), with constraint \(\int_S w \, dQ(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{r}) = 1\) ensuring bounded support and invariance to metric.
  - Section 4.1: Simulations show HPD intervals attain nominal coverage while Wilks intervals undercover for small \(n\).
- **Break condition**: If the hyperrectangular support \(S\) is misspecified (e.g., importance weight ranges are too narrow), the resulting posterior mass may be improperly constrained, invalidating coverage guarantees.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The adaptive grid sampling method enables accurate approximation of posterior probabilities and credible intervals for policy values or differences, even as the likelihood concentrates with increasing sample size.
- **Mechanism**: Locates a high‑likelihood sub‑support for each dimension \(v_j\) defined by \(\{v_j \mid L(\mathbf{v}) \geq c^{-1} L^*\}\) where \(L^*\) is the maximum likelihood and \(c\) is a large constant corresponding to a high‑confidence Wilks threshold. Constructs a Cartesian product grid over these intervals and normalizes the posterior density.
- **Core assumption**: The posterior mass is concentrated in a hyperrectangular region that can be captured by dimension‑wise thresholds. The chosen constant \(c\) yields a region encompassing the vast majority of posterior probability.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Section 3.3: Describes the adaptive sub‑support definition and grid approximation. "Using such an adaptive sub‑support avoids wasting time on the region in which the empirical likelihood is practically zero."
  - Appendix D: Details the choice of \(c\) (e.g., \(\exp(½ F^{-1}_{\chi^2_1}(0.9999))\)) and its correspondence to a 99.99% Wilks interval/region.
  - Section 4.1: Implicit validation—the HPD intervals computed on these grids achieve correct coverage in simulations.
- **Break condition**: For very high‑dimensional \(\mathbf{v}\) (many policies), the Cartesian product may be extremely large or miss highly correlated, non‑rectangular high‑likelihood regions, leading to computational infeasibility or poor approximation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Direct construction of empirical likelihood for a policy value difference \(d = v_{\text{new}} - v_{\text{baseline}}\) enables efficient, focused inference on policy comparison with lower computational cost than the full joint posterior.
- **Mechanism**: Formulates the empirical likelihood for \(d\) by projecting the estimating equations onto \(\mathbf{t}^\top \mathbf{w} r\) where \(\mathbf{t} = (-1, 1)^\top\), leading to a one‑dimensional dual optimization. This yields a univariate posterior for \(d\) from which probabilities like \(P(d > \delta)\) are computed.
- **Core assumption**: The quantity of interest is solely the difference (or a linear combination) of policy values. Dependencies between \(v_{\text{baseline}}\) and \(v_{\text{new}}\) are fully captured by this projection in the likelihood formulation.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Section 3.2: "If the only interest is the policy value difference, we suggest using the univariate posterior distribution for \(d\) to reduce the computational cost."
  - Section 4.2: Compares probabilities from the joint \(\mathbf{v}\) posterior and univariate \(d\) posterior, noting they "share similar values" but the univariate method is "more straightforward... at lower computational cost."
  - Section 5 (BMI application): Reports \(P(v_{\text{new}} > v_{\text{baseline}}+0.10)=0.92\) using the univariate \(d\) posterior.
- **Break condition**: If the comparison requires non‑linear functions \(g(v_{\text{new}}, v_{\text{baseline}})\) (e.g., ratios, conditional probabilities), the univariate \(d\) posterior is insufficient, and the full joint posterior must be used despite higher cost.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Empirical Likelihood**
  - **Why needed**: It is the core nonparametric tool that constructs a likelihood from estimating equations (\(\mathbb{E}[w_a r_a] = \mathbf{v}\)) without assuming a full data distribution. Its dual form enables optimization over support constraints.
  - **Quick check**: What is the role of the constraint \(\mathbb{E}[w_a] = 1\) in the empirical likelihood formulation for bandit policy values?
- **Concept: Off-Policy Evaluation via Importance Sampling**
  - **Why needed**: The entire method relies on correctly deriving importance weights \(w_a = \pi_x(a)/p_x(a)\) from the target and behavior policies to form the estimating equations. Misunderstanding this leads to incorrect \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(\mathbf{r}\) pairs.
  - **Quick check**: Why does the self‑normalized constraint \(\mathbb{E}[w_a] = 1\) correspond to the SNIS estimator?
- **Concept: Bayesian Posterior Computation via Grid Approximation**
  - **Why needed**: The posterior \(p(\mathbf{v}) \propto L(\mathbf{v})\) has no closed form. For low‑dimensional \(\mathbf{v}\), discretizing the adaptive sub‑support and normalizing is the implemented inference method.
  - **Quick check**: What is the purpose of the adaptive sub‑support, and how does it relate to Wilks' theorem?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Preprocessor -> Likelihood Engine -> Posterior Gridder -> Normalizer & Sampler -> Policy Comparator
- **Critical path**: Preprocessor computes weights and reward pairs; for a query, Likelihood Engine evaluates \(\log L\) on grid points; Posterior Gridder (if needed) determines adaptive sub‑support and constructs grid; Normalizer evaluates and normalizes posterior; Policy Comparator integrates to answer queries.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Grid resolution vs. cost*: Finer grids improve accuracy but scale exponentially with \(\dim(\mathbf{v})\). Mitigate by reducing to univariate \(d\) or pairwise comparisons.
  - *Joint vs. univariate posterior*: Joint \(\mathbf{v}\) allows arbitrary queries but is high‑dimensional. Univariate \(d\) is cheap and sufficient for simple differences but cannot answer conditional queries (e.g., \(P(v_{\text{new}} > 0.8 \mid v_{\text{baseline}} < 0.6)\)).
  - *Dual solver*: The convex dual must be solved for each grid point. Parallelization is essential; warm‑starting from nearby points can help.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Undercoverage in HPD intervals for small \(n\) if the adaptive sub‑support constant \(c\) is chosen too small, missing tail mass.
  - Numerical instability in dual optimization if \(1 + \beta^\top (\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{1}) + \tau^\top (\mathbf{w} r - \mathbf{v})\) approaches zero for many vertices—indicates \(\mathbf{v}\) is far from the true value or support \(S\) is poorly estimated.
  - Excessive memory/compute for \(\dim(\mathbf{v}) > 3\) even with adaptive grid; consider variational inference but with accuracy cost.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Replicate coverage properties**: For a known policy, generate many datasets of varying \(n\). Compute Wilks and HPD 90%/95% intervals. Verify HPD coverage approaches nominal while Wilks undercovers for small \(n\).
  2. **Validate policy difference inference**: For baseline vs. new policy, compute \(P(d > 0.10)\) from the univariate \(d\) posterior across replicates. Check that the empirical frequency of \(d > 0.10\) aligns with the posterior mean probability and that intervals have correct coverage.
  3. **Adaptive grid validation**: For a fixed \(n\) and true \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{true}}\), compute the adaptive sub‑support using the chosen \(c\). Verify that the 99.99% Wilks region is contained within the grid and that posterior mass outside the grid is negligible.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can variational inference be adapted for Bayesian empirical likelihood in contextual bandits to achieve polynomial‑time computation for high‑dimensional policy spaces while preserving finite‑sample coverage guarantees?
2. How does estimation error in the behavior policy \(p_x(a)\) affect the coverage and bias of Bayesian empirical likelihood credible intervals for policy values?
3. Does the non‑uniqueness of the maximum empirical likelihood estimator (due to boundary mass allocation) affect the uniqueness and stability of the resulting Bayesian posterior?
4. What theoretical conditions ensure that Bayesian HPD intervals from empirical likelihood achieve nominal coverage in finite samples for contextual bandit policy values?
5. Can the Bayesian empirical likelihood framework be extended to sequential policy evaluation, where inference updates as new contextual bandit data arrives, while maintaining valid coverage?

## Limitations
- The support hyperrectangle \(S\) must be correctly specified; misspecification (e.g., too narrow weight bounds) invalidates coverage guarantees.
- The adaptive grid method scales exponentially with the number of policies \(\ell\), making joint posterior inference infeasible beyond \(\ell \approx 3\) and potentially missing non‑rectangular high‑likelihood regions.
- The reported adolescent BMI application cannot be externally verified due to lack of data and incomplete methodological details.

## Confidence
- **Finite‑sample HPD coverage**: Medium. Simulations show nominal coverage vs. Wilks undercoverage for a single DGP with uniform behavior policy; generalizability unknown.
- **Computational efficiency**: Medium. Adaptive grid works for \(\ell \leq 2\); univariate \(d\) reduces cost; higher dimensions remain untested and may require approximations.
- **Practical effectiveness (BMI application)**: Low. Without the original dataset, the numeric results (e.g., 0.92) cannot be independently confirmed.

## Next Checks
1. **Support sensitivity analysis**: Vary the bounds of the importance‑weight support (e.g., empirical min/max vs. theoretical \([0, K]\)) and recompute coverage across many replicates to quantify robustness.
2. **Scalability to higher dimensions**: For \(\ell = 5\) policies, compare the adaptive grid method to a stochastic variational inference approximation against a Monte‑Carlo ground truth if feasible; assess bias in probabilities like \(P(v_{\text{new}} > v_{\text{baseline}} + \delta)\).
3. **Dual solver robustness audit**: Run the dual optimization on a dense grid of \(\mathbf{v}\) values (including boundaries) for multiple datasets; log infeasibility/NaN rates; mitigate with warm‑starts or a more robust solver.
