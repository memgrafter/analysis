# Automatic Cardiac Risk Management Classification using large-context Electronic Patients Health Records

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09685
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09685
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** A custom Hierarchical Transformer achieved F1-score 92.48% and MCC 0.758 on geriatric CVRM eligibility classification, outperforming classical ML and zero-shot LLMs.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles errors in manual administrative coding for geriatric Cardiovascular Risk Management (CVRM) by building an automated classifier from unstructured Electronic Health Records (EHRs). The authors develop a custom Hierarchical Transformer architecture designed to process long clinical narratives (up to 8192 tokens) and integrate structured medication and demographic data via late fusion. Evaluated on 3,482 Dutch geriatric patient records, their model significantly outperforms traditional machine learning (LinearSVC with TF-IDF) and zero-shot GPT-4 family models. The results demonstrate that specialized hierarchical attention mechanisms are highly effective for capturing longitudinal clinical signals, offering a robust, automated solution for clinical risk stratification in privacy-constrained, on-premise hospital settings.

## Method Summary
The researchers formulated a binary classification task to determine patient eligibility for a geriatric CVRM program using longitudinal Dutch consultation notes. Data preprocessing involved de-identification, concatenating all notes per patient, and tokenization with a BPE tokenizer to a fixed 8192-token context. Structured medication histories were mapped to ATC codes and embedded using BioLORD-2023, while age and gender were used as simple anthropometric features. The core model is an encoder-only Hierarchical Transformer (H-Transformer-1D) with rotary embeddings and hierarchical attention (block size 32), which reduces computational complexity for long sequences. After text encoding, a pooling layer (CLS token or average) produces a representation, which is concatenated with the structured feature vector and passed through an MLP classifier. The model was trained with weighted loss to handle severe class imbalance (80.61% vs. 19.39%) using stratified 5-fold cross-validation and compared against LinearSVC, ResNet-1D, and zero-shot GPT-4 prompts.

## Key Results
- The Hierarchical Transformer with CLS pooling and late fusion of medication & anthropometric data achieved the highest performance (F1: 92.48%, MCC: 0.758).
- All zero-shot LLM variants (GPT-4o, GPT-4.1) performed poorly (F1 ~34%, MCC ~0.1), highlighting the gap for unadapted generative models on this Dutch clinical task.
- The hierarchical model substantially outperformed classical baselines (LinearSVC, ResNet-1D), validating the advantage of specialized attention for long-context EHR text.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A custom Hierarchical Transformer architecture with classification token pooling captures long-range dependencies in longitudinal geriatric consult narratives more effectively than standard attention or convolutional models.
- **Mechanism:** The model processes sequences up to 8192 tokens via a hierarchical attention scheme that computes attention in a tree-structured, multi-scale fashion (block size 32), reducing the quadratic complexity of standard self-attention while preserving the ability to relate distant clinical events (e.g., a historical diagnosis influencing a current risk assessment). The CLS token aggregates global context for classification.
- **Core assumption:** The clinical signal for CVRM eligibility is distributed across many temporally distant notes and relies on integrating comorbidities, medication histories, and symptom progressions, which are efficiently modeled by hierarchical attention.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] States the architecture "outperforms both traditional methods and generative LLMs" and highlights "specialized hierarchical attention mechanisms."
  - [section 3.2] Details the H-Transformer-1D implementation with rotary embeddings and hierarchical attention layers for large-context processing.
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers focus on LLMs for EHR structuring or image-based cardiac analysis; none directly evaluate hierarchical attention for long clinical text, so corpus evidence for this specific mechanism is weak.
- **Break condition:** Performance would degrade if the effective clinical context were shorter than the hierarchical block size or if local tokens (e.g., within a single consult) overwhelmingly determined the label, making global context irrelevant.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Late fusion of structured medication embeddings (via ATC code descriptions) and anthropometric vectors (age, gender) with text-derived features improves classification robustness by providing complementary, orthogonal signals.
- **Mechanism:** The text encoder processes narrative consults independently. The final text representation (CLS token or average pooling) is concatenated with pre-computed, fixed embeddings of medication histories (using BioLORD-2023) and simple anthropometrics. This allows the classifier head to learn interactions between unstructured narrative content and structured clinical facts without forcing the text model to jointly encode them.
- **Core assumption:** Information about drug prescriptions and basic demographics is not fully redundant with what is described in free-text consults and provides a stabilizing signal, especially for patients with sparse narratives.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] Mentions evaluating a "late fusion strategy to integrate unstructured text with structured medication embeddings and anthropometric data."
  - [Table 5] Shows the H-Transformer with CLS pooling and late fusion achieves the top F1 (92.48%) and MCC (0.758), modestly outperforming its text-only counterpart in Table 4.
  - [corpus] Several neighbors (e.g., "Structured Insight from Unstructured Data," "Integrating LLMs with Human Expertise") explore multimodal EHR fusion, supporting the general premise that combining data types is beneficial, though fusion strategies differ.
- **Break condition:** Fusion would not help or could hurt if the structured variables are highly correlated with text-extracted features, are extremely noisy/missing, or if the narrative alone contains all predictive information.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** An encoder-only Transformer, trained from scratch on domain-specific data, outperforms zero-shot generative LLMs for this Dutch clinical classification task due to the necessity of adapting to local language, guidelines, and data distribution.
- **Mechanism:** The custom model is pre-trained from random initialization on the target dataset (or similar Dutch clinical text) with a simple classification objective, forcing it to internalize domain-specific terminology, abbreviations, and the implicit eligibility logic. Zero-shot LLMs rely on general-domain pre-training and a prompt with a summarized guideline extract, lacking fine-grained adaptation to the UMCU's specific documentation patterns and eligibility criteria implementation.
- **Core assumption:** The gap between general medical knowledge in LLMs and the specific, operationalized CVRM eligibility rules as documented in Dutch geriatric consults is too large for reliable zero-shot reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] Notes "general-purpose generative Large Language Models (LLMs) in a zero-shot setting" were benchmarked and "custom Transformer architecture outperforms."
  - [Table 4] Shows all zero-shot LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4.1 variants) have F1-scores ~34% and MCC ~0.1, far below the ~92% F1 of the H-Transformer.
  - [section 4] States: "in the absence of domain adaptation or fine-tuning, LLMs may lack adequate understanding of the clinical context."
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Evaluating GPT's Capability" and "Classifying Cancer Stage with Open-Source Clinical LLMs" report LLM limitations in zero-shot EHR tasks, though some use fine-tuned models.
- **Break condition:** Zero-shot LLM performance would approach the specialized model if the task were simpler (e.g., based on clear, explicit mentions in text), the prompt engineering included extensive few-shot examples from the target distribution, or the LLM were specifically instruction-tuned on Dutch clinical notes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Hierarchical Attention for Sequences**
  - **Why needed here:** Standard self-attention has O(n²) complexity, making it infeasible for the 8192-token contexts in geriatric EHRs. The H-Transformer-1D's hierarchical approach is the core innovation enabling the model to scale.
  - **Quick check question:** How does computing attention in local blocks before a global stage (e.g., block size 32) reduce computational cost while maintaining the ability to connect distant tokens?
- **Concept: Handling Extreme Class Imbalance**
  - **Why needed here:** The dataset is 80.61% Class 0 (non-eligible) vs. 19.39% Class 1 (eligible). Metrics like F1-score and MCC are used because accuracy would be misleading. Understanding imbalance is key to interpreting results.
  - **Quick check question:** Why is the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) a more informative metric than simple accuracy for this binary classification problem?
- **Concept: Late Fusion of Heterogeneous Data**
  - **Why needed here:** The system must combine free-text narratives with structured medication histories and simple demographics. Late fusion is a pragmatic design choice to avoid redesigning the core text encoder.
  - **Quick check question:** What is a key risk of late fusion compared to early (e.g., joint) fusion, and how might it limit model performance?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Pipeline (raw Dutch consults) -> Structured Data Encoder (ATC embeddings + age/gender) -> Hierarchical Text Encoder (H-Transformer-1D) -> Pooling Head (CLS or Avg) -> Fusion & Classifier (concatenation + MLP).
- **Critical path:** De-identified text concatenation per patient -> Tokenization to 8192 tokens -> Hierarchical encoding -> Pooling -> Concatenation with structured embeddings -> MLP classification.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Pooling Strategy:** CLS token (trainable, task-specific aggregation) vs. Average Pooling (parameter-free, uniform). Paper finds CLS slightly better but difference is minor.
  - **Context Length:** 8192 tokens enabled by hierarchical attention. Longer contexts increase memory and may include irrelevant early history.
  - **Fusion Timing:** Late fusion is simple but may miss deep interactions. Early fusion would require modifying the text encoder's embedding layer.
  - **Model Scale:** Custom H-Transformer is small (~few million params) vs. billions in GPT-4. Enables on-premise training but may have lower raw capability if fine-tuned.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Data Leakage:** Including future consult information in the "most recent one-year" window for a patient.
  - **Structured Data Misalignment:** Incorrect ATC mapping or missing anthropometrics causing fusion vector corruption.
  - **Pooling Failure:** CLS token not learning to aggregate relevant signals (check attention rollout). Average pooling diluting signal if key info is in few tokens.
  - **Imbalance Blindness:** Model predicting majority class always; check per-class precision/recall and MCC, not just F1.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline Replication:** Train H-Transformer (CLS) on text-only data. Expect F1 > 90%, MCC > 0.7, replicating Table 4's "H-transformer 1D †".
  2. **Pooling Strategy Ablation:** Train identical H-Transformer architectures differing only in final pooling (CLS vs. Avg). Measure variance in F1/MCC across 5 folds to confirm stability and relative ranking.
  3. **Fusion Benefit Validation:** Train H-Transformer (CLS) with and without late fusion of medication & age/gender embeddings. Expect a positive delta in F1/MCC as in Table 4 vs. Table 5.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** How would fine-tuning domain-specific clinical Large Language Models (e.g., BioBERT, ClinicalBERT) compare in performance to the proposed Hierarchical Transformer for this CVRM eligibility task?
  - **Basis:** [explicit] Conclusion states evaluating fine-tuned clinical LLMs is a future avenue; zero-shot LLMs lacked adequate context.
  - **Why unresolved:** Only zero-shot generative LLMs were benchmarked; encoder-only models pre-trained or fine-tuned on clinical corpora were not tested.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Direct comparison of clinically pre-trained/fine-tuned models (e.g., BioClinicalBERT) on the same dataset using unimodal and multimodal protocols.
- **Question:** What is the quantitative trade-off between hierarchical attention complexity and computational efficiency (inference time, memory footprint) when scaling to longer clinical histories or larger cohorts in on-premise deployment?
  - **Basis:** [inferred] Architecture designed for "acceptable computational time" in compute-constrained hospital environments.
  - **Why unresolved:** No scalability analysis or wall-clock benchmarks across sequence lengths/hardware; practical deployment ceiling unknown.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Systematic profiling of inference latency, memory, and throughput across sequence lengths (e.g., 4096 to 16384 tokens) on representative hospital hardware versus standard transformers.
- **Question:** To what extent does the model's performance depend on the specific linguistic and structural characteristics of Dutch geriatric consult notes, and how would it generalize to other languages or clinical specialties?
  - **Basis:** [explicit] Dataset is Dutch geriatric reports; general-purpose LLMs struggled with the non-English task.
  - **Why unresolved:** No cross-linguistic or cross-specialty validation; suitability to other note styles/languages unclear.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Applying the identical architecture and pipeline to publicly available EHR datasets in other languages (e.g., MIMIC-III/IV) or specialties and comparing performance.
- **Question:** Can advanced information extraction techniques significantly improve the quality of structured anthropometric data (e.g., BMI, blood pressure) from unstructured EHR text, thereby enhancing multimodal late fusion performance?
  - **Basis:** [inferred] Authors note other measures like BMI were not consistently available/structured; only age/gender used.
  - **Why unresolved:** Potential value of richer extracted features not explored; state-of-the-art clinical NER not integrated.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Integrating a dedicated clinical NER model to extract additional measurements (BP, BMI, labs) from Dutch consults and re-evaluating fusion performance against the age/gender-only baseline.
- **Question:** Does the hierarchical attention mechanism provide a statistically significant advantage over simpler pooling strategies or other long-context approximations (e.g., local window attention) for capturing longitudinal dependencies in geriatric narratives?
  - **Basis:** [inferred] Core innovation is hierarchical attention; no comparison to other efficient attention mechanisms (Longformer, BigBird) or ablation of hierarchical component itself.
  - **Why unresolved:** Advantage over other efficient attention designs not tested; gains may be from using a Transformer rather than hierarchy specifically.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Ablation replacing hierarchical layers with standard full self-attention (at comparable cost) or other variants (Linformer, Performer) within same framework; performance comparison and qualitative attention analysis.

## Limitations
- **External validity:** Results are tied to a single Dutch geriatric dataset; generalizability to other populations or EHR systems is unknown.
- **Implementation opacity:** Exact tokenizer (vocabulary/source), H-Transformer-1D hyperparameters, and preprocessing rules are unspecified, blocking faithful replication.
- **LLM evaluation scope:** Zero-shot LLM performance may reflect prompt engineering or language mismatch rather than architectural inferiority; fine-tuned or few-shot LLMs are not tested.

## Confidence
- **Hierarchical attention superiority:** Medium confidence. Internal ablation supports it, but no direct comparison to other long-context methods and corpus evidence is weak.
- **Late fusion benefit:** Medium confidence. Modest gain in Table 5 vs. text-only, but risk of overfitting with small structured feature set; no early-fusion baseline.
- **Encoder-only vs. zero-shot LLMs:** High confidence for *zero-shot* setting. Large performance gap (F1 ~34% vs. ~92%) is clear and consistent across LLM variants.

## Next Checks
1. **Baseline sanity check:** Replicate LinearSVC (TF-IDF) results first to verify data labeling and preprocessing integrity before deep model debugging.
2. **Block size sensitivity:** Train H-Transformer with varying block sizes (e.g., 16, 64, 128) to confirm the chosen 32 is optimal and not a tuning artifact.
3. **Cross-lingual LLM probe:** Translate Dutch consult excerpts to English and re-evaluate zero-shot GPT-4 to isolate language vs. architectural limitations in LLM failure.
