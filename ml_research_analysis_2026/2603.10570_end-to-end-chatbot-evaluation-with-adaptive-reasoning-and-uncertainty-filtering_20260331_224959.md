# End-to-End Chatbot Evaluation with Adaptive Reasoning and Uncertainty Filtering

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10570
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10570
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** An end-to-end framework for automatic evaluation of RAG chatbots that synthesizes test data, uses adaptive LLM reasoning with confidence filtering, achieves macro-accuracy up to 0.87, detects over 90% of incorrect labels, and reduces human review to under 30% of samples.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a cost-effective automatic evaluation framework for RAG-based chatbots. The system generates question–answer pairs directly from the knowledge base, uses an LLM-as-a-judge with three reasoning strategies (Single Prompt, Sequential Decision, Adaptive K-step Reasoning) to label responses as TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN, and applies multiplicative confidence aggregation to flag uncertain cases for human review. Evaluated on a Vietnamese news dataset, the Adaptive K-step method achieves high macro-accuracy (0.87) and efficiently triages errors, substantially reducing manual annotation while maintaining strong alignment with human judgment.

## Method Summary
The framework consists of four stages: (1) Automatic test data generation: an LLM processes each knowledge-base document to produce factual and inferential question–expected answer pairs via a fixed prompt. (2) Chatbot querying: the target RAG system is queried with the generated questions to collect its responses. (3) LLM judging: each received answer is compared to the expected answer using one of three methods—a single prompt, a sequential decision tree, or adaptive K-step reasoning with intermediate steps and confidence scores. (4) Uncertainty filtering: for adaptive reasoning, the product of per-step confidences is aggregated and thresholded (τ); samples below τ are sent for human review. This approach eliminates manual test set construction and minimizes human effort by focusing review on low-confidence judgments.

## Key Results
- Adaptive K-step Reasoning (K=5) with gpt-4o-mini as judge achieves macro-accuracy of 0.87 on a Vietnamese news dataset.
- Confidence-based filtering (τ=0.4) detects over 90% of incorrect labels while requiring human review for less than 30% of samples.
- Structured reasoning methods (Sequential and Adaptive) significantly outperform Single Prompt judging, especially on minority classes (FALSE and NOT GIVEN).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Automatic Q&A Synthesis
- **Claim:** Generating Q&A pairs directly from the knowledge base creates scalable, context-grounded test data without manual labeling.
- **Mechanism:** An LLM ingests each article and outputs question–expected answer pairs via a fixed prompt. These pairs serve as reference for evaluating the chatbot.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM can generate factually consistent questions and answers that are answerable from the source article and representative of the chatbot's coverage.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] states “generates Q&A pairs directly from the underlying knowledge base”; [section 3.1] details the pipeline.
- **Break condition:** If the LLM generates questions that drift beyond the article’s factual scope or produces low-quality pairs (e.g., ambiguous, unanswerable), the evaluation baseline becomes corrupted.

### Mechanism 2: Structured LLM Reasoning
- **Claim:** Using either a fixed decision tree (Sequential) or self-directed steps (Adaptive) for LLM judging yields more balanced, human-aligned labels than a single prompt.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of one-shot judgment, the LLM decomposes the task into discrete checks (e.g., refusal detection, content comparison, meaning change) and produces intermediate reasoning, helping handle nuanced cases and label imbalance.
- **Core assumption:** Breaking judgment into reasoning steps improves the LLM’s ability to detect partial correctness and irrelevance.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2] describes all three methods; [section 5.1] shows Sequential and Adaptive achieve higher macro-accuracy than Single Prompt across models.
- **Break condition:** For very weak judge models or extremely ambiguous responses, even structured reasoning may fail; Adaptive K-step may become unstable if K is too high relative to model capability.

### Mechanism 3: Confidence-Based Filtering
- **Claim:** Multiplicative aggregation of per-step confidences from Adaptive K-step provides a reliable uncertainty signal to triage samples for human review.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM emits a confidence (0–1) at each reasoning step; the product of all step confidences (C = ∏ cᵢ) yields an end-to-end confidence. Samples with C < τ are flagged.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM’s step-wise confidences are meaningfully calibrated; any low-confidence step undermines overall trust, so multiplicative aggregation captures joint reliability.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.3] and [section 5.2] demonstrate that with τ=0.4 and K=5, >90% of incorrect labels are detected while reviewing <30% of samples.
- **Break condition:** Poor confidence calibration (e.g., overconfident on wrong judgments) would cause the aggregated confidence to fail as a triage signal; τ may need domain-specific tuning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)**  
  Why needed: The chatbot under test is RAG-based; errors may stem from retrieval failure or generation. The three-label scheme (TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN) helps diagnose these failure modes.  
  Quick check: In a RAG system, what are the two primary sources of hallucination, and how does the evaluation framework’s three-label scheme help diagnose them?

- **LLM-as-a-Judge**  
  Why needed: The core evaluation replaces human annotators with an LLM. Understanding limitations (position bias, scoring inconsistencies) and the role of prompting (e.g., chain-of-thought) is essential.  
  Quick check: Why does requiring an LLM judge to provide step-by-step reasoning (as in Sequential/Adaptive methods) improve alignment with human judgments compared to a single-prompt score?

- **Uncertainty Quantification in LLMs**  
  Why needed: The framework’s cost-saving mechanism hinges on confidence scores to triage samples. Learners must grasp how confidences are elicited and aggregated, and why multiplicative aggregation penalizes any weak link.  
  Quick check: Explain why the product of per-step confidences (rather than the average) is used as the aggregated confidence, and what a low value indicates about the reasoning chain.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Knowledge Base -> Test Data Generator (LLM) -> Q&A pairs -> Target Chatbot -> Received Answers  
  Q&A pairs + Received Answers -> Judge LLM (with chosen method) -> Labels + per-step confidences (if Adaptive) -> Confidence Aggregator -> Threshold filter -> (High-confidence: auto-accepted; Low-confidence: human review queue)

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Ingest knowledge-base documents.  
  2. Generate Q&A pairs for each document.  
  3. Query target chatbot with each question.  
  4. Run evaluation with chosen judge method.  
  5. If Adaptive, compute aggregated confidence and apply τ.  
  6. Output final labels and review list.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Accuracy vs. cost: Single Prompt is fastest (1 LLM call) but least accurate; Adaptive K-step uses multiple steps (up to K) and is more token-intensive but yields better macro-accuracy and confidence.  
  - Automation vs. review burden: Lower τ reduces human review but increases risk of undetected errors; higher τ does the opposite. τ must be tuned per judge model and domain.  
  - Model dependence: Stronger judge models (e.g., gpt-4o-mini) benefit from Adaptive K-step even at K=3; weaker models may require Sequential Decision or higher τ.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High review rate (→100%) for moderate τ: Judge model is consistently uncertain—may indicate poor prompt optimization, inadequate K, or domain mismatch.  
  - Low detection rate of incorrect labels despite high review rate: Confidence scores are miscalibrated (overconfident on wrong judgments) or threshold too lenient.  
  - Poor performance on NOT GIVEN class: Common with Single Prompt; indicates the judge struggles to detect refusal/irrelevance. Switch to Sequential or Adaptive.  
  - Wide gap between macro-accuracy and class-wise accuracy: Imbalanced dataset; macro-average reveals weakness on minority classes that overall accuracy hides.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline comparison:** Run all three judge methods (Single, Sequential, Adaptive K=3) on a small manually labeled subset (n≈50) to establish which method and judge model yield highest macro-accuracy for the target chatbot.  
  2. **Threshold calibration:** For the best method/model pair, sweep τ (e.g., 0.1 to 0.9) on the same subset to plot error detection vs. review rate (Figure 6). Choose τ that meets desired tradeoff (e.g., >90% detection with <30% review).  
  3. **Generalization test:** Apply the configured pipeline to a different domain/language subset (e.g., English articles from the same knowledge base) to verify language-agnosticism and check for domain shift in accuracy/confidence distributions.

## Limitations
- Prompt & implementation opacity: Exact prompts for Q&A synthesis and all three judge methods are not disclosed, hindering exact replication and tuning. The threshold τ selection process is also unspecified.
- Domain/language specificity: Evaluation is conducted solely on Vietnamese news; generalization to other domains (e.g., technical Q&A) or languages is unproven, and confidence calibration may shift.
- Judge model dependency: Results are tied to specific LLM judges (e.g., gpt-4o-mini). Performance with other models, especially smaller or open-source ones, is unknown. The multiplicative confidence aggregation assumes well-calibrated step-wise confidences, which is not validated.

## Confidence
- **Framework reduces annotation cost while maintaining high agreement:** Medium-High (strong results on one dataset, but no external validation).
- **Adaptive K-step Reasoning improves labeling accuracy over single-prompt:** Medium (plausible and shown to improve, but sensitive to prompt design and judge model capability).
- **Multiplicative confidence filtering effectively triages samples:** Low-Medium (demonstrated for one setting; no calibration curves to confirm correlation across distribution).

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt audit & quality control:** Manually inspect 50–100 synthesized Q&A pairs from the Vietnamese news corpus to quantify factual drift, ambiguity, and answerability. Compute inter-annotator agreement on synthetic labels to establish a noise floor.
2. **Threshold calibration sweep:** For the best-performing judge/model (e.g., gpt-4o-mini, Adaptive K=5), run a fine-grained sweep of τ (0.1–0.9 in 0.05 increments) on a held-out human-labeled set. Plot the precision-recall tradeoff for error detection to select an operating point and verify the >90% detection claim at the reported τ.
3. **Cross-domain robustness test:** Apply the fully configured pipeline (same prompts, τ, K) to a new domain (e.g., English Wikipedia or a product FAQ corpus). Measure the drop in macro-accuracy and the shift in confidence distributions. If accuracy degrades significantly, re-calibrate τ for the new domain and report the new tradeoff curve.
