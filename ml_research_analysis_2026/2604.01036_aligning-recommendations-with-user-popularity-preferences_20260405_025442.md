# Aligning Recommendations with User Popularity Preferences

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.01036
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01036
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: SPREE reduces PCE@100 by 4–16% while preserving NDCG@100 across four datasets.

## Executive Summary
The paper identifies popularity bias in recommendation systems as a misalignment between the distribution of item popularities in a user’s historical interactions and that in the top-N recommendations. To quantify this, the authors propose Popularity Quantile Calibration (PQC), a metric that computes the calibration error between these two popularity distributions. For mitigation, they introduce SPREE, an inference-time activation steering method. SPREE first estimates a user’s popularity bias (e.g., median popularity of past items). It then identifies a global “popularity direction” in the model’s representation space—a vector along which item embeddings vary with popularity. During inference, the user’s embedding is shifted along this direction by an amount proportional to the estimated bias, thereby adjusting the nearest-neighbor retrieval toward items that better match the user’s historical popularity level. Experiments on four datasets consistently show that SPREE reduces PCE@100 by 4–16% without harming NDCG@100, and it outperforms global popularity-reduction baselines. Ablations confirm that the user-specific steering magnitude is essential; uniform reduction harms both alignment and accuracy. The work demonstrates the promise of personalized, inference-time interventions for popularity debiasing.

## Method Summary
Popularity Quantile Calibration (PQC) measures misalignment by comparing the quantiles of item popularity in the recommendation set to those in the user’s historical interactions. A lower PCE indicates better alignment.

SPREE consists of three main components: (1) a bias estimator that infers a user’s popularity preference (e.g., by computing the median popularity of interacted items); (2) a popularity direction, a unit vector in the latent space obtained via linear regression or PCA relating item embeddings to their popularity; (3) a steering module that modifies the user embedding by adding the product of the bias and the direction. This adjusted embedding is then used for standard nearest-neighbor item retrieval. The method operates entirely at inference time, requiring no model retraining.

## Key Results
- SPREE reduces PCE@100 by 4–16% while preserving NDCG@100 across all four datasets.
- SPREE outperforms global popularity reduction methods (e.g., IPR, PopSteer), which often worsen user-level calibration.
- Ablation studies show that removing the user-specific magnitude (e.g., using a fixed steering strength) eliminates the gains, underscoring the need for personalization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
SPREE works because the model’s representation space contains a direction that correlates with item popularity. Shifting the user embedding along this direction directly influences which items are nearest neighbors and thus recommended. The magnitude of the shift is tailored to each user’s historical popularity preference, ensuring that users who already prefer popular items are not over-corrected, while those who favor niche content receive appropriate adjustments. This personalized approach avoids the over-correction inherent in global methods, which apply the same reduction to all users and can harm both alignment and accuracy. By preserving the relative ordering of relevant items (as indicated by stable NDCG), SPREE maintains recommendation quality while rebalancing popularity exposure.

## Foundational Learning
- **Popularity Bias**: The systematic over-recommendation of popular items, causing a mismatch with users’ interest in less popular content. Why needed: Defines the target problem. Quick check: Compute the average popularity of recommended items and compare to user history.
- **Activation Steering**: Modifying a model’s internal activations at inference to steer outputs without retraining. Why needed: Provides a lightweight, reversible intervention. Quick check: Add a fixed vector to a user embedding and observe if recommended popularity shifts.
- **Calibration Error (PQC)**: A metric comparing quantiles of two distributions; here, historical vs. recommended item popularity. Why needed: Quantifies the degree of misalignment. Quick check: Calculate PCE@100 by measuring the difference between quantiles of the top-100 recommendations and the user’s historical popularity quantiles.
- **User-Specific Bias Estimation**: Inferring a scalar representing a user’s preference for popular vs. niche items from their interaction history. Why needed: Enables personalized steering magnitudes. Quick check: Estimate median (or other quantile) popularity of a user’s past items; correlate with optimal steering magnitude.
- **Nearest Neighbor Retrieval**: Recommendation by finding items closest to a user embedding in the latent space. Why needed: Steering relies on the assumption that small embedding perturbations can change retrieved item popularity. Quick check: Perturb a user embedding slightly and verify that retrieved item popularity changes smoothly.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Bias Estimator (from user history) → steering magnitude.  
Item catalog → Popularity Direction (global unit vector).  
User Embedding + (steering magnitude × Popularity Direction) → Adjusted Embedding.  
Adjusted Embedding → k-Nearest Neighbor search over item embeddings → Recommendations.

**Critical path**: During inference for a target user:  
1. Obtain user embedding from the trained model.  
2. Compute user’s popularity bias from their interaction history.  
3. Multiply the global popularity direction by the bias to form a steering vector.  
4. Add the steering vector to the user embedding.  
5. Perform nearest-neighbor retrieval using the adjusted embedding to generate the top-N list.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Single global direction*: Simplicity and interpretability, but may not capture complex or non-linear relationships between embeddings and popularity.  
- *Bias estimation from history*: Robust to noise when using summary statistics (e.g., median), yet discards distributional details that could inform a more nuanced magnitude.  
- *Linear steering*: Assumes a linear relationship between embedding displacement and popularity shift; may fail if the embedding manifold is highly non-uniform.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Inconsistent PCE improvements across users, suggesting unreliable bias estimation or local embedding irregularities.  
- Significant drop in NDCG after steering, indicating over-correction or interference with relevance signals.  
- No reduction in PCE despite applying steering, possibly because the identified direction does not align with popularity or the embedding neighborhoods lack popularity-calibrated regions.

**First three experiments**:  
1. **Main evaluation**: Compare SPREE against the original model and global debiasing methods on PCE@100 and NDCG@100 across multiple datasets.  
2. **Ablation of user-specific magnitude**: Replace the personalized magnitude with a constant or zero to demonstrate its necessity.  
3. **Direction validation**: Visualize item embeddings with color-coded popularity to confirm the existence of a monotonic popularity direction; test steering with a permuted direction to show degraded performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the geometric structure of the item embedding space constrain the effectiveness of activation steering for popularity alignment?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state in the limitations that "the degree of improvement is limited by the structure of the item embedding space: steering is only effective if locally popularity-calibrated regions exist since retrieval is based on nearest neighbors."
- Why unresolved: The paper does not analyze the embedding manifold properties (e.g., local density, curvature) that determine whether suitable calibrated regions exist for steering to succeed.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical studies measuring local popularity distribution variability around user embeddings across datasets, or synthetic experiments where embedding geometry is controlled to test steering efficacy boundaries.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can SPREE be extended to independently control different segments of the popularity distribution (e.g., head vs. tail) rather than only shifting the median?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that their method "targets bias around the median popularity, which restricts control to shifting the central tendency" and suggest targeting specific parts (e.g., lower or upper tails) as future work.
- Why unresolved: The current steering vector is derived from a median-based bias estimator, limiting adjustments to the overall shift without granular quantile control.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing multi-direction steering (e.g., separate vectors for low/high quantiles), evaluating on users with extreme preference distributions, and measuring changes in calibration curves across all quantiles.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Why does the predictability of user-specific popularity bias from model activations vary significantly across datasets (e.g., R² up to 0.64 on ml-20m vs. 0.18 on fs-tky)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Table 5 shows large differences in estimator performance (𝑅² and MSE) across datasets, suggesting bias signals are more or less encoded depending on data characteristics.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate which dataset properties (e.g., catalog size, sparsity, user behavior patterns) affect the encoding of bias in activations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Correlating estimator performance metrics with dataset statistics (number of users/items, interaction density, popularity distribution skew) or analyzing per-user prediction errors to identify subgroups where bias is less recoverable.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Why do globally-applied popularity reduction methods (e.g., IPR, PopSteer) often worsen user-level alignment, while SPREE’s adaptive steering improves it?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Figure 4 shows that uniform reduction methods amplify negative bias (lower tail miscalibration). The authors attribute SPREE’s success to "redistributing popularity across users rather than shifting it globally."
- Why unresolved: The exact mechanism by which user-specific steering avoids over-correction is not analyzed—e.g., whether the bias estimator correctly identifies the direction and magnitude of misalignment per user.
- What evidence would resolve it: A detailed per-user analysis comparing estimated bias vs. true bias (from history) before/after steering, and examining whether uniform steering harms users with already low or high popularity preferences.

## Limitations
- Generalization: The efficacy of SPREE’s popularity direction and bias-informed steering is validated only on four datasets from limited domains; transfer to other domains (e.g., news, social media) or non-transformer architectures remains unknown.
- Metric vs. Utility: PQC measures distributional alignment but its impact on long-term user satisfaction, diversity, or business metrics is not established; NDCG preservation is an accuracy proxy, not a guarantee of holistic improvement.
- User Heterogeneity: The method assumes a stable, inferable popularity preference from user history, but real-world preferences may be context-dependent or noisy; robustness to sparse or dynamic histories is not deeply examined.
