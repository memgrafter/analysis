# Gender Bias in Generative AI-assisted Recruitment Processes
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.11736  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11736  
- **Reference count**: 19  
- **Primary result**: GPT-5 shows significant gender bias in occupational adjective suggestions (p=0.00176) but not in job titles or industries for balanced Italian graduate profiles.

## Executive Summary
This study audits GPT-5 for gender bias when generating occupational suggestions for simulated Italian graduate profiles. With 24 gender-balanced synthetic profiles, the model was prompted to output a job title, industry, and three descriptive adjectives. No statistically significant differences were found in job titles (p=0.27) or industries (p=0.38). However, adjectives revealed a pronounced gendered pattern: women were more often described with relational/emotional traits (e.g., empathetic, supportive), while men were linked to leadership/practical traits (e.g., influential, determined) (χ², p=0.00176). The findings demonstrate that even with neutral, controlled inputs, GPT-5 reproduces societal gender stereotypes, particularly in open-ended descriptive tasks, raising concerns about its use in recruitment.

## Method Summary
The experiment used 24 synthetic candidate profiles created with balanced gender, age, ISCO macro-area, and experience level. Each profile was fed to GPT-5 via a fixed prompt casting the model as a career advisor, requesting a job title, industry, and three adjectives. The model’s responses were parsed to extract these three fields. Raw outputs were manually coded into broader categories (e.g., job titles grouped by function). Frequency distributions of each output type across genders were tested for independence using chi-square. Each profile was run three times to account for stochastic variation.

## Key Results
- Job title suggestions showed no significant gender association (χ², p=0.27).
- Industry suggestions showed no significant gender association (p=0.38).
- Adjectives exhibited a strong gendered pattern: relational/emotional traits dominated for women (27 vs. 11 occurrences), leadership/practical traits for men (25 vs. 13) (χ², p=0.00176).
- Bias emerged primarily in the subjective, open-ended adjective component, not in constrained occupational categories.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Training Data Stereotype Internalization**  
GPT-5 reproduces gender stereotypes from its training data by capturing robust statistical correlations between gendered language and trait descriptors (e.g., "emotional" with women, "leadership" with men). This occurs even with synthetic, balanced inputs because the model has internalized latent associations from vast text corpora. Evidence: The abstract and section II explicitly state the model reflects and perpetuates social discriminations present in historical data. Break condition: Fine-tuning on a corpus where trait-gender co-occurrence is neutralized should weaken the pattern.

**Mechanism 2: Prompt-Driven Bias Leakage**  
The structured prompt’s open-ended "adjectives" slot invites free-form descriptive language, providing a pathway for latent bias to express. The model relies more on stereotypical schemas for subjective, ill-defined tasks, whereas job/industry labels have stronger, more neutral canonical forms in training data. Evidence: Significant bias appears only in adjectives, not in job/industry outputs, suggesting the output modality mediates bias expression. Break condition: Using a forced-choice from a predefined neutral trait list should reduce the gendered pattern.

**Mechanism 3: Measurement Sensitivity**  
Chi-square tests on broad, aggregated job/industry categories have low statistical power to detect subtle skews with small N (24). The bias surfaces more clearly at the finer-grained adjective level, where stereotype signals have higher signal-to-noise ratio. Evidence: Section IV.A notes visible but underpowered asymmetries (e.g., HR over-represented for women), while adjective-level analysis shows stark differences. Break condition: A larger sample size might yield significance for job/industry, confirming it is a power issue.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Operationalizing and Measuring Bias in LLMs**  
  Why needed: The study defines bias through specific measurable outputs (job title, industry, adjectives) and statistical tests. Understanding this translation is key to interpreting conclusions, as the choice of metrics shapes what "bias" means in this experiment.  
  Quick check: If adjectives showed no difference but job titles did, would the conclusion about bias manifestation be the same? This highlights how measurement choices influence findings.

- **Concept: Statistical Power and the Limitations of Null Results**  
  Why needed: The paper accepts null hypotheses for job/industry (p>0.05). Recognizing that "failure to reject H₀" ≠ "no bias" is crucial, especially with a small N. The significant adjective bias cautions against over-interpreting non-significant results.  
  Quick check: Which design factors (profile count, trials, output category cardinality) limit power for detecting job/industry bias?

- **Concept: Prompt Engineering as a Research Variable**  
  Why needed: The entire output is a function of the prompt. The study’s single, structured prompt channels bias into the "adjectives" slot, a major design choice that threatens external validity.  
  Quick check: How would results change if the prompt requested a narrative profile instead of structured fields? This underscores the limited scope of the paper’s conclusions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Generator -> Prompt Template -> LLM Gateway -> Output Parser -> Annotation Engine -> Statistical Test Suite

**Critical path:**  
Profile specification → Prompt injection → Model response → Field extraction → Category coding → Frequency tabulation → Statistical test.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Internal Validity vs. Generalizability: Using one model (GPT-5) and one prompt ensures control but limits applicability to other models or prompts; tradeoff between clear signal and real-world relevance.  
- Quantification vs. Nuance: Converting rich text (adjectives) into discrete categories enables stats but discards semantic differences (e.g., "supportive" vs. "empathetic").  
- Simulation vs. Reality: Synthetic profiles remove confounders but lack real-world cues; unknown how bias manifests with actual candidate data.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Signalling: Non-significant p-values for job/industry alongside visibly skewed distributions in sub-categories.  
- Silent: Model responses that violate the expected format, breaking the parser; or unreported low inter-coder reliability in open coding.  
- Divergence: High stochasticity across trials for the same profile, making the observed pattern unstable.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Model Swap Replication**: Run the identical protocol (24 profiles, 3 trials, same prompt) on Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Llama 3.1 405B. Success: replication of the adjective-gender pattern tests mechanism generalizability.  
2. **Prompt Stress Test**: Vary the prompt while keeping profiles constant: (a) omit "3 adjectives", (b) request "3 neutral adjectives", (c) request "3 technical skills". Success: reduction/elimination of gendered adjectives under constrained/reframed prompts tests Mechanism 2.  
3. **Real-Profile Pilot**: Gather 12 real Italian graduate CVs (balanced gender), anonymize all cues except gender name, prompt with actual experience descriptions. Compare adjective bias magnitude to synthetic results. Success: similar pattern establishes ecological validity.

## Limitations
- Small sample size (N=24 profiles) severely limits statistical power for job/industry categories; non-significant results do not confirm absence of bias.
- The structured prompt with a free-form "adjectives" slot may uniquely channel bias into that dimension; findings may not generalize to other prompt structures or output formats.
- Synthetic profiles lack real-world complexity (e.g., implicit cues, varied writing styles, actual achievement gaps); bias magnitude and form with authentic candidate data remain unknown.
- Open coding of outputs introduces subjectivity; inter-coder reliability is not reported; coarse category grouping may mask subtle but meaningful skews.

## Confidence
- **Claim**: GPT-5 exhibits a significant gendered pattern in the adjectives it generates for balanced profiles.  
  **Confidence**: Medium (significant p-value, but moderated by small sample and subjective coding).
- **Claim**: There is no evidence of bias in suggested job titles or industries for this specific prompt and sample.  
  **Confidence**: Low (non-significant p-values are inconclusive due to low power; cannot rule out bias).
- **Claim**: The bias manifests most strongly in the most open-ended, subjective output component due to prompt design.  
  **Confidence**: Medium (pattern consistent but inferred from single-condition experiment; requires direct comparative evidence).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the exact experimental protocol (24 profiles, 3 trials, same prompt) on at least two other state-of-the-art LLMs (e.g., Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 405B) to test generalizability of the adjective-gender association.
2. Conduct a prompt-structure manipulation: vary output constraint (free-form adjectives vs. forced-choice from a neutral list) and task framing (descriptive "adjectives" vs. evaluative "strengths") to assess bias reduction under constrained prompts.
3. Pilot the study with 50 real Italian graduate CVs (balanced gender), using the same prompt with actual experience descriptions; compare the magnitude and direction of the adjective-gender association to the synthetic profile results.
