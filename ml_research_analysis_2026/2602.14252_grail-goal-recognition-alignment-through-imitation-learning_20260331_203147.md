# GRAIL: Goal Recognition Alignment through Imitation Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14252
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14252
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** GRAIL achieves state-of-the-art goal recognition by leveraging imitation learning to capture suboptimal and biased behavior, yielding F1-score gains up to 0.5 over baselines with efficient training (seconds per goal) and sub-second inference.

## Executive Summary
GRAIL addresses the problem of goal recognition in partially observable environments by training a separate imitation learning policy for each candidate goal. These policies learn the characteristic behavioral patterns—including suboptimalities and biases—from demonstrations. At inference, an observed partial trajectory is scored against each policy using a lightweight similarity metric, and the goal with the highest score is selected. This approach eliminates the need for planning or environment interaction during test time. Evaluations on MiniGrid and PandaReach benchmarks show that GRAIL variants significantly outperform RL-based baselines in F1-score across biased optimal, suboptimal, and noisy settings, while maintaining competitive training times and sub-second latency.

## Method Summary
GRAIL operates in two phases. Offline, for each goal \(g\) in the candidate set, it trains a policy \(\pi_g\) using an imitation learning algorithm (behavioral cloning, GAIL, or AIRL) on demonstrations \(D_g\) of agents pursuing that goal. The learned policy absorbs the goal-specific behavioral fingerprint, including any biases or suboptimalities. Online, given a partial observation trajectory \(\tau_{obs}\), GRAIL computes a score for each goal: \(S_g(\tau_{obs}) = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \|\pi_g(s_t) - a_t\|^2\) (negative average MSE). The goal with the maximum score is returned. This one-shot scoring avoids costly rollouts and does not require environment dynamics models or planners.

## Key Results
- GRAIL variants increased F1-score by over 0.5 in biased optimal settings, by 0.1-0.3 under suboptimal behavior, and by up to 0.4 with noisy optimal data compared to baselines.
- Training per goal took only seconds (BC:5s, GAIL:60s, AIRL:240s) versus ~300s for GRAQL, and inference was sub-second.
- Competitive performance in optimal scenarios and robust across varying goals and observability levels.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Per-goal imitation learning captures behavioral biases
- **Claim:** GRAIL captures systematic behavioral biases (e.g., path preferences) by learning a separate goal-specific policy via imitation learning from demonstrations, rather than assuming optimality.
- **Mechanism:** For each candidate goal, an IL algorithm (BC, GAIL, AIRL) is trained on demonstration trajectories of agents pursuing that goal. The resulting policy \(\pi_g\) internalizes the characteristic distribution of actions and states, including suboptimal or biased patterns present in the data. During inference, the observed partial trajectory is scored against each \(\pi_g\).
- **Core assumption:** The demonstrations for each goal contain a consistent, learnable behavioral fingerprint—even if noisy or biased—that distinguishes it from other goals' fingerprints.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "...capturing suboptimal and biased behavior."; [section 3.1] "By the end of this phase we possess a “bank” of K policies, each of which has directly absorbed the characteristic behavioral fingerprint of reaching its assigned goal, including any systematic biases or suboptimal quirks present in the demonstrations."
- **Break condition:** If the demonstrations for different goals are not discriminative (e.g., goals lead to highly overlapping state distributions) or if the IL algorithm fails to learn the bias due to insufficient data or model capacity.

### Mechanism 2: One-shot inference via simple scoring
- **Claim:** GRAIL's one-shot inference is efficient and effective because it uses a simple, differentiable scoring function (negative average MSE) applied in a single forward pass over the observed trajectory, avoiding costly planning or rollouts.
- **Mechanism:** Given an observed trajectory \(\tau_{obs} = (s_0, a_0, ..., s_T)\) and a learned policy \(\pi_g\), the score \(S_g(\tau_{obs}) = -\frac{1}{T} \sum_t \|\pi_g(s_t) - a_t\|^2\) is computed. The goal with the highest score is selected. This requires only evaluating \(\pi_g\) on the observed states, not simulating future states.
- **Core assumption:** The action predicted by \(\pi_g\) for an observed state \(s_t\) is a meaningful proxy for how likely that state-action pair is under the true behavior for goal \(g\). Lower MSE indicates higher likelihood.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2] "To support a lightweight, one-shot scoring method... we implement a similarity metric using Negative Average MSE."; [section 5.5] Ablation shows MSE scoring reduces latency and maintains/improves accuracy under noise compared to Wasserstein.
- **Break condition:** If the learned policies \(\pi_g\) are overly deterministic or mis-specified such that their action predictions do not correlate with the true behavior's action distribution (e.g., due to high variance in expert actions).

### Mechanism 3: Decoupled training and inference
- **Claim:** GRAIL decouples training and inference, enabling scalable and low-latency GR without requiring a model of environment dynamics or a planner at test time, unlike classical and RL-based GR.
- **Mechanism:** During offline training, per-goal policies are learned (with environment interaction only for GAIL/AIRL). During online inference, only the frozen policies are evaluated on the observed states—no planning, no reward computation, no environment interaction. This contrasts with GRAQL (requires Q-values and softmax computation per state) and DRACO (requires occupancy measure comparison).
- **Core assumption:** The learned policies are sufficiently accurate representations of the goal-directed behavior that their action predictions on observed states alone are discriminative.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "By scoring an observed partial trajectory with each learned goal-directed policy in a single forward pass, GRAIL retains the one-shot inference capability... while leveraging learned policies..."; [section 3.3] "Crucially, at inference time, all GRAIL variants perform GR without any planner calls or environment interaction."; [section 5.4] Training times are competitive and inference is sub-second; [corpus] GRAQL neighbor paper explicitly requires Q-learning and KL-divergence computation per state, implying more complex inference.
- **Break condition:** If the IL training fails to produce policies that generalize to the specific partial trajectories observed at test time (e.g., due to distribution shift in initial states or observation lengths).

## Foundational Learning
**Imitation Learning (IL)**
- **Why needed:** GRAIL's core innovation is using IL (BC, GAIL, AIRL) to learn per-goal policies from demonstrations, capturing non-optimal behavior. Understanding the trade-offs between these algorithms (BC's simplicity vs GAIL/AIRL's adversarial training) is essential for implementation.
- **Quick check:** How does the choice between BC, GAIL, and AIRL affect GRAIL's ability to handle highly suboptimal or biased demonstrations?

**Goal Recognition (GR)**
- **Why needed:** GRAIL solves a specific GR problem formulation (Definition 1 & 2). A practitioner must understand the closed-goal-set assumption, partial observability, and the shift from planning/RL-based scoring to policy-based scoring.
- **Quick check:** What are the key differences between GR as inverse planning (Ramírez & Geffner) and GR as inverse reinforcement learning (GRAQL), and how does GRAIL's IL-based approach differ from both?

**Adversarial Training (GAIL/AIRL)**
- **Why needed:** GAIL and AIRL use a discriminator-policy game to match occupancy measures. For GRAIL, this helps capture complex, potentially multi-modal behavioral distributions beyond simple cloning. Understanding the role of the discriminator and the potential for mode collapse is critical.
- **Quick check:** Why might AIRL's recovered reward be less useful for GRAIL's inference than the policy itself, and how does the discriminator in GAIL directly inform the policy update?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Per-Goal IL Trainer → Policy Bank → Scoring Module → Goal Selector

**Critical path:**
- Offline: For each goal \(g\) → Load \(D_g\) → Train \(\pi_g\) → Store in Policy Bank.
- Online: Receive \(\tau_{obs}\) → For each \(\pi_g\) in Bank → Compute score \(S_g\) → Return \(\arg\max_g S_g\).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Storage vs. Accuracy:* Per-goal policies (GRAIL) improve accuracy on biased/suboptimal data but scale linearly with \(|G|\).
- *Metric choice:* MSE is fast and stable but may not capture distributional differences as well as Wasserstein; Wasserstein is more discriminative but computationally heavier.
- *IL backend:* BC is fastest to train but may underfit complex biases; GAIL/AIRL are more expressive but require careful hyperparameter tuning and are prone to training instability.

**Failure signatures:**
- High variance in F1-score across random seeds indicates unstable training (common with GAIL/AIRL).
- BC-GRAIL performance degrades sharply if demonstrations contain high action noise (e.g., Gaussian/uniform) that isn't smoothed by a stochastic policy.
- If all \(S_g(\tau_{obs})\) values are very close (< epsilon), the system is uncertain—likely due to insufficiently discriminative policies or too short \(\tau_{obs}\).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replicate biased optimal MiniGrid results:** Train BC/GAIL/AIRL on the 2-goal (7,1) vs (7,7) biased dataset. Verify F1-score ~1.0 at 20-40% observability (Figure 4). This confirms the mechanism for capturing systematic path preferences.
2. **Ablation on scoring metric:** Train BC-GRAIL and evaluate inference using both MSE and Wasserstein on PandaReach with 0.3 Gaussian noise. Confirm MSE's latency advantage and comparable/better accuracy (Figure 5 & 6).
3. **Stress test with increasing goal set size:** In MiniGrid suboptimal setting, increase from 2 to 6 goals. Expect GRAIL variants (especially GAIL/AIRL) to degrade more gracefully than GRAQL (Table 3). This tests scalability and robustness of learned policy representations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open-set goal recognition:** How can GRAIL be extended to handle unknown or dynamically changing goals? The paper states this as future work; current approach requires all goals known in advance. Resolution would involve recognizing goals not present during training or integrating new goals without full retraining (e.g., via goal-conditioned policies or meta-learning).
- **Robustness to dynamics shifts:** How can GRAIL maintain accuracy under shifts in environment dynamics or action semantics? No evaluation on transfer across environments with modified transition probabilities or action effects. Evidence needed: train in source domain, test in target with systematic dynamics changes, report performance and adaptation techniques.
- **Vision-based inputs:** Can GRAIL scale to raw perceptual inputs (e.g., images)? Current implementations use low-dimensional state vectors; high-dimensional inputs require different architectures and may affect robustness. Evidence: adapt GRAIL with convolutional or Transformer policies and evaluate on grid-worlds with pixel observations or robotic tasks with camera data.
- **Scalability to large goal sets:** How can GRAIL avoid linear training and storage costs? Per-goal training becomes infeasible for hundreds/thousands of goals. Evidence: develop a single goal-conditioned policy that generalizes across many goals and show comparable accuracy with superlinear reduction in training/storage as \(|G|\) grows.

## Limitations
- Linear scaling with goal set size leads to high storage and training costs for large numbers of goals.
- Requires sufficient and discriminative demonstrations per goal; performance degrades with scarce or noisy data.
- Limited generalization to unseen environments or out-of-distribution goals; MSE scoring heuristic may not capture complex action distributions in high-dimensional tasks.

## Confidence
- **Bias capture via per-goal IL:** High (empirical validation on MiniGrid/PandaReach).
- **One-shot inference efficiency:** Medium (sub-second latency shown but MSE's theoretical grounding weak).
- **Decoupled architecture benefits:** High (clear comparison to planning-based baselines).
- **F1 improvements:** High on reported benchmarks but limited to specific domains.

## Next Checks
1. **Scalability stress test:** Train GRAIL on MiniGrid with 20, 50, and 100 goals; measure total training time and inference latency per trajectory to quantify \(O(|G|)\) overhead.
2. **Data scarcity robustness:** For a fixed 10-goal set, reduce demonstrations per goal to 1, 2, 5; evaluate F1 to determine minimal data requirements and failure thresholds.
3. **Cross-environment generalization:** Train GRAIL on MiniGrid Easy environments, then test on Hard environments (different layouts/object placements) without retraining; assess drop in F1 to evaluate policy transferability.
