# Agentic LLM Workflow for MR Spectroscopy Volume-of-Interest Placements in Brain Tumors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13371
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13371
- **Reference count:** 24
- **Primary result:** The agentic LLM workflow adapts to diverse natural language preferences without retraining, achieving clinically relevant metrics (e.g., lowest necrosis volume, highest tumor coverage) while maintaining VOI volumes comparable to expert placements and avoiding extreme volume changes.

## Executive Summary
Inter-operator variability in MR spectroscopy volume-of-interest (VOI) placement for brain tumorsposes a challenge, as multiple valid placements exist and preferences vary by clinical context. This paper proposes an agentic LLM workflow that decomposes the task into two steps: first, generating a diverse set of candidate VOIs using vision transformer models trained with different objective preferences (e.g., balanced coverage vs. low necrosis); second, having an LLM select the optimal candidate based on quantitative metrics aligned with a user-specified natural language instruction. Evaluated on 110 clinical cases, the approach successfully adapts to conflicting preferences—minimizing necrosis, maximizing solid tumor coverage, or minimizing missed tumor—without any model retraining. The selected VOIs achieve target metric extremes while keeping volumes stable (~12 mL), demonstrating the viability of LLM-orchestrated tool use for preference-aware medical image analysis.

## Method Summary
The workflow centers on an LLM (Qwen3-14B) acting as a high-level controller that orchestrates three domain-specific tools. First, a segmentation tool (nnUNet) processes input MRI to produce a three-label map (contrast-enhancing tumor, non-enhancing/necrotic tumor, normal brain). Second, two vision transformer-based regressors (MVP and PC-MVP) generate candidate VOIs. Both are trained on synthetic data derived from a hand-designed objective function that combines terms for desired tissue fractions (e.g., solid tumor inclusion, necrosis avoidance) and geometric penalties; the training data encodes either a “balanced” or a “low fSolidoutside” (large VOI) preference. PC-MVP further diversifies candidates by conditioning on up to 50 sampled positions within the solid tumor. Third, a metrics calculator computes quantitative scores (e.g., fVOIsolid, fSolidoutside, vVOInecrosis, volume) for each candidate. The LLM receives a prompt containing the user instruction, descriptions of the model types, and a table of all candidates’ metrics; it then reasons and selects the best-matching VOI. This separation allows adaptation to new preferences at inference time simply by changing the instruction, without retraining the generation models.

## Key Results
- Minimizing necrosis: achieved necrosis volume 0.35 ± 0.59 mL (lowest among compared methods).
- Maximizing solid tumor coverage: achieved fVOI_solid = 0.66 ± 0.23 (highest).
- Minimizing missed tumor: achieved fSolidoutside = 0.52 ± 0.22 (lowest).
- Maintained VOI volumes comparable to expert placements (~12 mL) and avoided the extreme volume swings observed in pure optimization approaches.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
The system produces a diverse pool of clinically valid VOI candidates by training vision transformer models (MVP/PC-MVP) on synthetic data generated from a multi-term objective function. This function combines Gaussian-shaped rewards for desired tissue fractions with penalties for geometric constraints (e.g., excessive volume, proximity to skull). By tuning the Gaussian parameters (`µc`, `σc`) and penalty weights, two distinct synthetic datasets are created—one reflecting a balanced expert preference and another a large-VOI preference. PC-MVP further increases diversity by conditioning predictions on multiple tumor positions. The core assumption is that the range of acceptable VOIs is broad enough that deterministic optima under different preferences collectively span the clinically valid space. Evidence: [abstract] mentions candidate generation from models with different preferences; [section 2.3, 2.4] details objective parameterization and PC-MVP conditioning. Break condition: if synthetic placements are anatomically implausible or consistently violate clinical constraints, the candidate pool becomes invalid.

### Mechanism 2
The LLM acts as a flexible decision-maker that selects the optimal VOI by interpreting natural language instructions and reasoning over automatically computed quantitative metrics. Given a prompt with the user goal (e.g., “minimize necrosis”) and a table of metrics for all candidates, the LLM maps the instruction to a correct trade-off among the numbers. This decouples preference specification from the fixed objective functions used during training, enabling adaptation without retraining. Evidence: [abstract] describes LLM selection based on metrics aligned with natural language; [section 2.1] frames the LLM as a controller; [section 3.2, Table 3] shows the workflow attains the best metric in the instructed category. Break condition: if the LLM misinterprets instructions or exhibits systematic biases (e.g., always preferring smaller volumes), selection becomes arbitrary and adaptation fails.

### Mechanism 3
Overall adaptation without retraining is achieved by combining a static, diverse candidate set with a dynamic LLM selector. The MVP and PC-MVP models are each trained once on a fixed preference; their outputs cover a range of VOI characteristics (coverage, size, necrosis inclusion). At inference, the LLM chooses the candidate that best matches a new, user-provided preference. This separation means the generation models remain unchanged while the selection adapts. Evidence: [abstract] states “adapt to preferences without retraining”; [section 4] emphasizes selection among diverse placements; [section 3.2, Figure 4] show within-case VOI variation across instructions using the same models. Break condition: if a novel preference lies completely outside the distribution of the candidate set (e.g., an extremely unusual shape), no satisfactory candidate exists, forcing a suboptimal choice.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Interface (MRI + instruction) -> Segmentation Tool (nnUNet) -> Candidate Generation Tools (MVP & PC-MVP) -> Metrics Calculator -> LLM Controller (Qwen3-14B) -> Output Formatter  
- **Critical path:** User Instruction -> LLM (plan) -> Call Segmentation Tool -> Wait for segmentation -> Call Candidate Generation Tools (MVP & PC-MVP) -> Wait for all VOIs -> Call Metrics Tool for all VOIs -> Wait for metrics -> LLM (reason over table) -> Select VOI -> Output  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  • *Candidate Set Size vs. LLM Performance:* More candidates improve coverage of preference space but may exceed the LLM’s context window or reduce its ability to discern subtle differences.  
  • *Training Diversity vs. Overfitting:* Two model types/preferences balance diversity and computational cost; adding more could increase coverage but risk invalid/noisy VOIs.  
  • *Metric Complexity vs. Interpretability:* Using 5+ metrics provides rich information but requires careful prompting to ensure correct weighting; a simpler set might be more robust.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  • *LLM hallucination/ignoring metrics:* selects a VOI with poor metrics for the given instruction (e.g., high necrosis when asked to minimize).  
  • *Invalid VOI generation:* MVP/PC-MVP produce VOIs with negative dimensions, centers outside the brain, or volumes >100 mL.  
  • *Poor segmentation:* nnUNet fails to separate tumor subregions, making all metrics meaningless.  
  • *No candidate satisfies constraint:* all candidates violate a strict user preference (e.g., all have >5 mL necrosis when <1 mL requested).  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. *Candidate Diversity Stress Test:* For 20 BraTS cases, generate all candidates from both models. Compute pairwise metric distances and confirm the set covers ≥80% of the inter‑operator range reported in Table 2.  
  2. *LLM Selection Validation (Control):* For 50 cases with known “ground truth” preference (balanced vs. large‑VOI), run the pipeline. Measure the LLM’s match rate; expect >2σ improvement over random selection.  
  3. *End‑to‑End Preference Adherence:* For 30 test cases, issue three conflicting instructions (“maximize solid tumor,” “minimize necrosis,” “minimize missed tumor”). Run the workflow per instruction. Perform ANOVA on the key metrics (fVOIsolid, vVOInecrosis, fSolidoutside); expect significant directional shifts (p < 0.01).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does the workflow perform across broader clinical contexts (different tumor types, treatment stages) and technical MR settings (field strength, coil configurations)? The evaluation is limited to adult glioma cases from two sites with specific protocols.  
- Does the LLM’s selection remain robust when faced with ambiguous instructions, edge‑case tumor anatomies, or alternative LLM architectures? The study relies on a single LLM (Qwen3‑14B) and does not analyze sensitivity to prompt engineering or model choice.  
- Can the demonstrated preference adaptation translate into improved clinical outcomes (e.g., diagnostic confidence, treatment decision accuracy) rather than just placement metrics? Downstream impact is not evaluated.  
- Will the approach generalize to VOI placement in normal brain anatomy or non‑tumor pathologies? Current models are trained exclusively on tumor data and tumor‑preferring objectives.

## Limitations
- Undocumented model specifics (exact ViT configuration, loss weights, position‑sampling strategy for PC‑MVP) hinder faithful replication.  
- Missing LLM prompt template and metric descriptions raise concerns about selection reliability and reproducibility.  
- The 110 clinical cases are not publicly available, preventing independent validation on the same cohort.  
- Neighboring literature focuses on tumor segmentation/classification, not LLM‑orchestrated VOI workflows; no external validation exists.
