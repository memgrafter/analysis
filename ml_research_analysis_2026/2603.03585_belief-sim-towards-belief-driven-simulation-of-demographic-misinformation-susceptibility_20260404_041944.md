# Belief-Sim: Towards Belief-Driven Simulation of Demographic Misinformation Susceptibility

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03585  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03585  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Belief priors, derived from a psychology‑informed taxonomy and survey data, boost simulation accuracy up to 92% and, via BAFT, reduce spurious demographic shortcut reliance.

## Executive Summary
Belief-Sim investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can simulate demographic misinformation susceptibility by focusing on structured belief profiles rather than coarse demographic cues alone. The framework constructs belief profiles for demographic personas using a seven‑dimension psychology‑informed taxonomy applied to World Values Survey (WVS) data, yielding both imputed (group‑level) and observed (individual‑level) signals. Two modeling strategies are evaluated: (1) prompt‑based conditioning, which appends belief descriptions to model inputs, and (2) Belief‑Adapter Fine‑Tuning (BAFT), a two‑phase method that first learns a population belief representation and then trains a separate susceptibility head. Results across three LLMs and two datasets (PANDORA, MIST) show that belief priors dramatically improve accuracy (up to 92%) compared to zero‑shot and demographic‑only baselines. Imputed beliefs consistently outperform observed ones. BAFT further reduces reliance on demographic stereotypes (near‑zero flip rates) and generalizes strongly to out‑of‑dataset claims. The work establishes belief priors as a robust and dominant signal for simulating demographic differences in misinformation susceptibility.

## Method Summary
The task is to predict whether a given persona (defined by demographics and beliefs) will judge a claim as true or false. Inputs include evaluation datasets (PANDORA: 318 US participants, 3 rumors each; MIST‑1: 409 participants, 100 claims each) containing human judgments and binary demographics (age, gender, education, living area). Belief data are drawn from WVS Wave 7, mapped to seven dimensions (Worldview/Identity, Epistemic Trust, Cognitive Style, Conspiracy Mentality, Moral/Values, Emotion‑Related, Heuristics). Two belief sources are used: *imputed* (modal WVS responses per demographic group) and *observed* (individual’s past claim judgments).

For prompt‑based conditioning, persona descriptions are prepended to the claim and fed to a frozen LLM; conditions include zero‑shot, demo‑only, belief‑only (imputed/observed), and demo+belief. For BAFT, Phase 1 trains a linear “belief adapter” on a frozen LLM to predict WVS response distributions (KL loss). Phase 2 freezes the base LLM and belief adapter; a lightweight susceptibility head is trained on the concatenated claim embedding and belief adapter output. Baselines: 1‑phase fine‑tuning (directly on susceptibility data) and 2‑phase LoRA‑FT. Primary metrics: susceptibility accuracy (matching human label), counterfactual flip rates, and (for BAFT) KL divergence in Phase 1.

## Key Results
- Belief priors boost simulation accuracy up to 92% on PANDORA and MIST, far surpassing zero‑shot and demographic‑only baselines.
- Imputed (group‑level) beliefs consistently yield higher accuracy than observed (individual‑level) beliefs, especially in prompt‑based methods.
- BAFT achieves near‑zero flip rates in counterfactual evaluations, effectively reducing spurious demographic shortcut reliance, and maintains strong cross‑dataset generalization (MIST‑2).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Belief priors as primary drivers
*Mechanism:* The model conditions on structured belief profiles derived from a psychology‑informed taxonomy and survey data, aligning with the psychological principle of “belief consistency.” This provides a granular, plausible driver beyond demographic labels.  
*Core assumption:* Demographic groups exhibit systematic, survey‑observable belief distributions that predict susceptibility more reliably than identity alone.  
*Evidence:* Abstract and Section 5.1 report accuracy up to 92% and show imputed beliefs outperform observed ones; related work (Chuang et al., 2024) indicates single beliefs predict responses better than demographics.  
*Break condition:* If belief profiles conflict with claim content or are too sparse/noisy (e.g., using distributions instead of modal values), the model may ignore the prior or hallucinate inconsistent reasoning.

### Mechanism 2: Decoupling via BAFT improves generalization and reduces shortcuts
*Mechanism:* BAFT employs two phases: (1) train a lightweight belief adapter on a frozen LLM to predict WVS distributions; (2) freeze the belief adapter and train a separate susceptibility head. This forces the predictor to rely on the learned belief structure rather than memorizing demographic‑label shortcuts.  
*Core assumption:* A transferable population‑level belief representation can be learned from survey data without full model adaptation to task‑specific shortcuts.  
*Evidence:* Section 6 and the abstract demonstrate near‑zero flip rates and strong MIST‑2 generalization; Table 7 shows that lower KL divergence in Phase 1 correlates with better final performance.  
*Break condition:* If Phase 1 fails to capture demographic variation (high KL divergence), the belief signal becomes low‑quality, potentially harming downstream performance despite decoupling.

### Mechanism 3: Counterfactual flip rates diagnose shortcut reliance
*Mechanism:* The framework swaps demographic attributes while keeping beliefs constant and measures the change in binary prediction (“flip rate”). High flip rates indicate sensitivity to the demographic token itself (shortcut reliance); low rates, especially in “shortcut reliance” tests where demographics are non‑predictive, suggest belief‑driven decisions.  
*Core assumption:* A truly belief‑driven simulator should be invariant to demographic swaps when the belief profile remains unchanged.  
*Evidence:* Section 5.2 and Figure 5 reveal model‑dependent flip rates (e.g., Llama higher than Qwen/Mistral); BAFT yields near‑zero flips; Appendix D.3 confirms head‑tuned and fully fine‑tuned models differ in flip behavior.  
*Break condition:* Flip rates may conflate legitimate demographic effects with stereotypes if beliefs correlate with demographics; the paper acknowledges that flips do not uniquely measure stereotype‑driven shortcutting.

## Foundational Learning

**Belief Taxonomy Construction**  
- *Why needed:* The seven‑dimension taxonomy (Worldview/Identity, Epistemic Trust, Cognitive Style, Conspiracy, Moral/Values, Emotion, Heuristics) provides a structured, psychology‑grounded way to map raw WVS questions into interpretable belief priors. Without this, beliefs would be an unstructured signal.  
- *Quick check:* Why these seven dimensions over others, and how might omitting a dimension like “Need for Cognitive Closure” impact simulation fidelity?

**Imputed vs. Observed Belief Signals**  
- *Why needed:* Imputed beliefs (group‑level from WVS) are smooth and widely available; observed beliefs (individual‑level from study participants) are sparse and specific. The paper finds imputed beliefs consistently outperform observed ones in prompt‑based settings, making the distinction crucial for data sourcing and interpretation.  
- *Quick check:* What are the trade‑offs between the statistical smoothness of imputed group priors and the specificity of observed beliefs? When might observed beliefs be preferable despite lower performance?

**Counterfactual Flip Rate as a Diagnostic**  
- *Why needed:* Accuracy alone cannot separate genuine belief‑use from demographic stereotyping. Flip rates under controlled demographic swaps are the primary tool introduced to diagnose “shortcut reliance.”  
- *Quick check:* Design a counterfactual scenario where a high flip rate would be *justified* and not indicate harmful stereotyping. What does this reveal about interpreting the metric?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Belief Taxonomy & WVS Mapper -> Demographic Group Definer -> Belief Profile Builder -> 
   -> Prompt Templates -> LLM Inference -> Evaluation Module
   -> BAFT Two‑Phase Trainer (Phase 1: Belief Adapter; Phase 2: Susceptibility Head) -> Evaluation Module

### Critical Path
1. Define demographic axes and binary groups.  
2. Build belief profiles: impute modal WVS responses per group (and/or collect observed individual judgments).  
3. Choose modeling strategy: prompt‑based conditioning or BAFT.  
4. For BAFT: train belief adapter (Phase 1), then freeze it and train susceptibility head (Phase 2).  
5. Evaluate on held‑out claims using human‑aligned accuracy and counterfactual flip rates.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Prompt‑based vs. BAFT:** Prompting is zero‑cost and flexible but suffers from distribution ambiguity and higher shortcut reliance. BAFT requires fine‑tuning, is more complex, but achieves better generalization, lower flip rates, and cleaner decoupling of belief and demographic signals.  
- **Imputed vs. Observed Beliefs:** Imputed beliefs are smoother, more available, and perform better in prompts. Observed beliefs are sparse and may conflict with group priors, leading to underperformance. BAFT’s decoupling makes it less sensitive to this conflict.  
- **Single vs. All Belief Dimensions:** Using a single top‑performing dimension (e.g., Moral/Values for Age/Education) often outperforms concatenating all dimensions in prompts, likely due to noise. BAFT implicitly learns to weight dimensions during Phase 1.

### Failure Signatures
- **High flip rates** (>10%) in the “Shortcut Reliance” counterfactual setting indicate strong stereotype reliance.  
- **Large accuracy drop** from PANDORA+MIST‑1 to MIST‑2 for 1‑phase or full fine‑tuned models signals overfitting to dataset‑specific shortcuts.  
- **Low Phase 1 KL divergence** (e.g., Llama’s 0.287 vs. Qwen’s 0.051) suggests poor modeling of population belief distributions, predicting weaker BAFT performance.  
- **Accuracy below zero‑shot** in the “Demo‑only” prompt condition implies demographic conditioning introduces harmful noise.

### First Three Experiments
1. **Baseline Belief Dimension Screening:** For each of the seven belief dimensions, run prompt‑based conditioning using only imputed modal beliefs. Rank dimensions by average accuracy across demographic groups and datasets to identify strongest single‑dimension priors.  
2. **Imputed vs. Observed Signal Comparison:** For the top two dimensions from Experiment 1, compare paired accuracy of (a) imputed‑only, (b) observed‑only, and (c) imputed+observed combined. Expect imputed > combined > observed.  
3. **BAFT Validation & Shortcut Check:** Implement BAFT for one model (e.g., Qwen). Compare Phase 2 accuracy on MIST‑2 against the 1‑phase baseline. Then run the “Shortcut Reliance” counterfactual; expect near‑zero flip rate, confirming reduced shortcut reliance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What is the impact of intersecting demographic identities (e.g., elderly low‑educated rural women) on BeliefSim’s misinformation susceptibility simulation accuracy?  
2. Does the belief‑driven simulation approach generalise to non‑WEIRD cultures and languages while maintaining predictive accuracy?  
3. How can counterfactual flip‑rate metrics be refined to reliably differentiate between legitimate demographic priors and spurious stereotype shortcuts in LLM susceptibility predictions?

## Limitations
- Missing specifics (exact binary demographic splits, train/test partitioning, back‑translation pipeline, random seeds) hamper exact reproducibility.  
- The seven‑dimension belief taxonomy may be incomplete; factors like “Need for Cognitive Closure” are omitted, potentially limiting fidelity.  
- Imputed group‑level beliefs consistently outperform observed individual beliefs, but the latter are sparse and may conflict with priors, restricting practical use when individual data are available.  
- Flip‑rate diagnostics assume demographic identity should not directly influence verdicts; however, unmeasured pathways (e.g., differential media consumption) can conflate stereotype and belief effects.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑model & cross‑cultural replication:** Evaluate BeliefSim on additional LLM families (e.g., GPT‑4, PaLM‑2) and on rumor datasets from non‑English/cultural contexts to test robustness of belief priors.  
2. **Taxonomy completeness ablation:** Systematically remove one belief dimension at a time in both prompt‑based and BAFT settings; compare accuracy and flip‑rate changes to quantify each dimension’s marginal contribution and identify gaps.  
3. **Architecture sensitivity check:** Replace the linear belief adapter in BAFT with a small MLP and/or unfreeze the LLM during Phase 1; assess whether decoupling benefits hold or are tied to the exact head‑only design.
