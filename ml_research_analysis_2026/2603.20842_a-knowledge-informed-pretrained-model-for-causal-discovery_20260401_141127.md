# A Knowledge-Informed Pretrained Model for Causal Discovery

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20842  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20842  
- **Reference count:** 33  
- **Primary result:** Kode integrates weak prior knowledge through a dual-source encoder-decoder with alternating attention, a diverse synthetic pretraining corpus, and curriculum learning, achieving consistent SHD and F1 improvements over state-of-the-art baselines across synthetic (in- and out-of-distribution) and real-world datasets, even without priors.

## Executive Summary
Causal discovery aims to infer directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) from observational data, but purely data-driven methods often lack guidance while interventional approaches are expensive. This paper introduces Kode, a knowledge-informed pretrained model that softly incorporates weak prior knowledge (e.g., coarse domain constraints) into a deep learning pipeline. Kode processes observational data and prior knowledge via separate encoders, merges them using alternating attention layers that first attend sample-wise then variable-wise, and decodes a DAG with permutation and lower-triangular parameterization. A large synthetic pretraining corpus covering various graph sizes, densities, linear and nonlinear mechanisms equips the model with generalizable inductive biases, while a curriculum learning schedule gradually increases prior sparsity to stabilize training. Extensive evaluation on multiple benchmarks shows Kode outperforms prior pretrained causal discovery methods and remains robust even when prior knowledge is absent, highlighting its practical utility.

## Method Summary
Kode adopts a dual-source encoder-decoder architecture. Inputs consist of observational data *X* ∈ ℝ<sup>S×N</sup> and prior knowledge encoded as a reachability matrix *G*<sup>P</sup> ∈ {−1,0,1}<sup>N×N</sup>. Both are embedded separately (*E*<sub>*X*</sub>, *E*<sub>*P*</sub>) and concatenated. Alternating attention layers process the concatenated representation: even-numbered layers apply separate self-attention over the sample dimension for each embedding; odd-numbered layers apply joint self-attention over the variable dimension. Cross-attention then fuses knowledge into data, using the mean-pooled *E*<sub>*P*</sub> as query and *E*<sub>*X*</sub> as keys/values, yielding a latent *H*. A weak additive knowledge signal (ϵ·*q*) is injected into *H* before decoding. The decoder maps *H* to a permutation matrix *Q* (via Gumbel-Sinkhorn sampling and Hungarian assignment) and a lower‑triangular matrix Λ; the final DAG estimate is *Ĝ* = *Q*Λ*Q*<sup>⊤</sup>. Training minimizes a combined loss *L* = *L*<sub>graph</sub> + α*L*<sub>sim</sub>, where *L*<sub>graph</sub> is the negative log‑likelihood of sampled graphs and *L*<sub>sim</sub> aligns input and output similarity matrices. Pretraining uses a synthetic corpus of 300 k graphs across variable sizes *N*∈{20,30,40}, densities (ER1‑ER3), and mechanisms (linear with heteroscedastic noise, nonlinear MLPs). Curriculum learning varies the prior retention rate ρ, sampling from either Uniform(0,1) or Uniform(0, ρ<sub>max</sub>(*e*)) with ρ<sub>max</sub> linearly annealing from 0.5 to 0.2, thereby exposing the model to denser priors early and sparser priors later.

## Key Results
- Kode achieves lower SHD and higher F1 than state‑of‑the‑art pretrained baselines (e.g., BCNP, NOTEARS‑MLP) on synthetic in‑distribution tests, even with no prior knowledge.
- It generalizes to out‑of‑distribution mechanisms (e.g., deeper neural networks) and real‑world datasets (Sachs, CIPCaD‑Bench), with consistent improvements over baselines.
- The model maintains strong performance across varying prior strengths, graph densities, and variable scales, demonstrating robustness and practical utility.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Dual‑source encoder‑decoder synergy
The dual‑source architecture merges observational data and weak prior knowledge (reachability matrix) without hard constraints. Each input is embedded separately, concatenated, and processed through alternating attention layers before cross‑attention fuses them into a unified latent representation. A weak additive knowledge signal during decoding allows data‑driven correction. **Evidence:** abstract, Section 4.1; related work shows novelty of soft integration. **Break condition:** Highly inaccurate or conflicting priors may still degrade performance, especially if knowledge dominates early training; curriculum learning mitigates this but cannot fully eliminate error propagation.

### Mechanism 2: Alternating attention preserves heterogeneous semantics
Separate sample‑wise attention on data and knowledge embeddings in even layers maintains distinct semantics; odd layers then jointly attend over variables to integrate cross‑dependency information. This alternation refines representations while avoiding semantic interference. **Evidence:** abstract, Section 4.1.2. **Break condition:** For extremely large variable counts (*N* ≫ training maximum) or extremely small sample sizes, the corresponding attention may become intractable or fail to capture meaningful statistics.

### Mechanism 3: Curriculum learning stabilizes adaptation to prior sparsity
The retention rate ρ is sampled from a mixture distribution that gradually shifts from dense (Uniform(0,1)) to sparser regimes (Uniform(0, ρ<sub>max</sub>(*e*)) with ρ<sub>max</sub> annealing from 0.5 to 0.2). This schedule prevents early over‑constraining and eases the model into handling sparse priors smoothly. **Evidence:** abstract, Section 5.2; curriculum learning is a well‑established technique. **Break condition:** Too rapid annealing or inappropriate initial ρ<sub>max</sub> can hinder knowledge integration; too late introduction of sparsity may cause overfitting to dense priors.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
X (S×N) and G<sup>P</sup> (N×N) → separate embeddings *E*<sub>*X*</sub>, *E*<sub>*P*</sub> → concatenation Z → alternating attention layers (even: separate sample‑wise attention on *E*<sub>*X*</sub>, *E*<sub>*P*</sub>; odd: joint variable‑wise attention on Z) → cross‑attention (query: mean‑pooled *E*<sub>*P*</sub>, key/value: *E*<sub>*X*</sub>) → latent *H* → additive weak knowledge signal *H*′ → decoder: projection to Θ → permutation *Q* via Gumbel‑Sinkhorn/Hungarian → lower‑triangular Λ → output *Ĝ* = *Q*Λ*Q*<sup>⊤</sup>.

**Critical path:** X, G<sup>P</sup> → *E*<sub>*X*</sub>, *E*<sub>*P*</sub> → Z → alternating layers → *H* → cross‑attention → *H*′ → Θ → *Q*, Λ → *Ĝ*.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Padding vs. dynamic architectures*: Max‑variable zero‑padding ensures size‑agnostic processing but may waste computation; set‑based models avoid padding but risk scale‑dependent attention biases.  
- *Soft vs. hard knowledge integration*: Additive ε perturbation allows data‑driven correction but may dilute strong priors; hard constraints (e.g., masking) could improve accuracy but hurt robustness with noisy priors.  
- *Alternating vs. joint attention*: Separating sample and variable axes preserves semantics but may miss early cross‑dimension interactions; fully joint attention is simpler but risks semantic interference.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High SHD or low F1 on in‑distribution synthetic tests, especially with no prior, indicating weak data‑driven capability.  
- Sharp performance drop when prior strength is reduced (e.g., 100%→30% retention), suggesting over‑reliance on knowledge.  
- Inability to handle variable scales beyond training (*N* > 40) due to padding limits or attention dilution.  
- Inconsistent results across random seeds, particularly with sparse priors, due to curriculum instability.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. *In‑distribution sanity check*: Evaluate Kode on synthetic linear ER(1) graphs (*N*=20) with 0% and 100% prior; compare SHD/F1 to baselines (BCNP, NOTEARS‑MLP). Expect Kode to match or exceed baselines even without prior.  
2. *Curriculum ablation*: Disable curriculum learning (fix ρ=0.5) and train on mixed‑density data; evaluate on ER(3) sparse graphs. Expect degraded performance on sparse graphs, validating progressive sparsity scheduling.  
3. *Scale robustness test*: Test on graphs with *N*=40 (in‑distribution) and *N*=50 (out‑of‑scale) using neural‑network mechanisms; monitor F1 drop. A significant drop at *N*=50 would indicate padding or attention limitations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Mechanism diversity and OOD generalization** – To what extent does the diversity of generative mechanisms in the synthetic pretraining corpus determine out‑of‑distribution generalization performance on real‑world datasets with unknown mechanisms? The paper attributes strong OOD results to exposure to diverse mechanisms but does not isolate this factor.  
2. **Robustness to noisy or conflicting priors** – How robust is Kode’s dual‑source architecture to erroneous reachability entries, and what are the failure modes when prior strength exceeds an optimal threshold? The paper only uses priors derived from ground‑truth reachability with masking and assumes weak, soft integration.  
3. **Scalability of alternating attention** – What are the computational and memory scalability limits of the alternating attention mechanism as variable count (*N*) and sample count (*S*) grow, and does the max‑variable padding strategy introduce inefficiencies or biases at very large scales (*N* ≫ 40)? The experiments are limited to *N* ≤ 40.  
4. **Extending knowledge encoding** – Can the knowledge encoding formalism (reachability matrix ∈ {−1,0,1}) be extended to represent more nuanced or unstructured domain knowledge (e.g., textual constraints, temporal ordering) without losing the model’s ability to integrate it effectively? The current architecture assumes prior knowledge is already structured as a matrix.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter opacity:** Critical hyperparameters (embedding dimension, number of layers, α, τ, K) are not reported, hindering exact reproduction.  
- **Curriculum ambiguity:** The precise schedule π(*e*) and ρ<sub>max</sub> annealing details are vaguely defined (“monotonically increasing”), risking divergence from reported training dynamics.  
- **Implementation gaps:** Cross‑attention pooling (e.g., mean‑pooling over which dimension) and the extraction of “topo‑priors” for CIPCaD‑Bench are insufficiently specified.

## Next Checks
1. **Alternating attention ablation:** Replace alternating attention with end‑to‑end joint cross‑attention; evaluate F1 on sparse‑prior (30%) data. A significant drop would confirm the contribution of the alternating design.  
2. **Prior noise robustness:** Inject random noise (flipping reachability entries) at varying rates (0%–50%) into the prior; measure performance decay curves to quantify tolerance of the soft integration strategy.  
3. **Scale stress test:** Evaluate Kode on graphs with *N*=60/80 variables (padding to the maximum allowed size). Monitor training/inference time, memory usage, and attention weights on padded dummy nodes to identify scalability bottlenecks.
