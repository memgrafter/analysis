# Comparing Classical and Quantum Variational Classifiers on the XOR Problem

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.24220  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24220  
- **Reference count:** 16  
- **Primary result:** Successful XOR classification depends on model expressivity; a deeper VQC (L=2) and MLP achieve perfect test accuracy, but the MLP attains significantly lower binary cross-entropy and orders-of-magnitude shorter training time; real quantum hardware execution preserves the XOR decision structure but introduces spatially structured deviations in continuous predictions (mean absolute deviation ≈0.118).

## Executive Summary
This study compares classical logistic regression, a one-hidden-layer MLP, and a two-qubit variational quantum classifier (VQC) on synthetic XOR classification tasks with additive Gaussian noise. The core finding is that performance hinges on architectural expressivity: models with insufficient nonlinear capacity (logistic regression, VQC with depth L=1) fail consistently, while those with sufficient capacity (MLP with ≥4 hidden units, VQC with L=2) achieve perfect test accuracy under noisy conditions (σ=0.10, n=100). However, the MLP outperforms the VQC in predictive quality (lower binary cross-entropy) and training efficiency (orders of magnitude faster). When executing the trained VQC on real quantum hardware, the discrete classification remains correct but the continuous prediction function shows structured, spatially correlated deviations from the ideal simulator.

## Method Summary
The paper uses a synthetic binary classification dataset based on the XOR logic gate: four Gaussian clusters centered at (0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1) with isotropic Gaussian noise of standard deviation σ; labels follow XOR. Models include: (1) Logistic regression (linear), (2) MLP with one hidden layer of width h (sigmoid activations, trained with SGD), and (3) VQC with 2 qubits, angle encoding (RX(π·x)), a hardware-efficient ansatz of depth L (each layer: single-qubit RY/RZ rotations + CNOT), Pauli-Z measurement on qubit 0, and Adam optimizer. Training minimizes binary cross-entropy. Experiments vary noise level σ, dataset size n, ansatz depth L, and shot count. Key metrics are test accuracy, binary cross-entropy, training time, and, for hardware runs, per-point absolute deviation of the continuous prediction from ideal simulation.

## Key Results
- Expressivity determines success: insufficiently nonlinear models (logistic regression, VQC(L=1)) fail; sufficiently nonlinear models (MLP(h≥4), VQC(L=2)) achieve perfect test accuracy.
- MLP attains lower binary cross-entropy and training times orders of magnitude shorter than VQC, despite comparable parameter counts.
- Real quantum hardware execution preserves the global XOR decision boundary but introduces structured, spatially varying distortions in the continuous prediction function (mean absolute deviation ≈0.118 between hardware and ideal simulation).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Expressivity Bottleneck
- **Claim:** XOR classification success is causally determined by a model's architectural expressivity, not its classical or quantum nature.
- **Mechanism:** XOR requires a nonlinear decision boundary. Models with insufficient nonlinear capacity (linear classifier, VQC with L=1) cannot represent the necessary boundary geometry, leading to systematic failure. Models with sufficient capacity (MLP with h≥4, VQC with L=2) can represent it.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states LR and VQC(L=1) fail while MLP and VQC(L=2) achieve perfect accuracy; Section 5.3 accuracy plots confirm this pattern across noise levels and dataset sizes; corpus indirectly supports the expressivity role for VQCs.

### Mechanism 2: Structured Hardware Noise
- **Claim:** Real quantum hardware introduces structured deviations in the continuous prediction function, even when discrete accuracy is preserved.
- **Mechanism:** Device-level noise (gate errors, readout errors, decoherence, compilation distortions) and finite sampling distort expectation values computed by the quantum circuit. These distortions are not i.i.d. but exhibit spatial structure correlated with input geometry.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.6 describes visible local fluctuations increasing toward edges; mean absolute deviation ≈0.118 between hardware and finite-shot simulator, with a clear gap from shot noise alone.

### Mechanism 3: Training Time Overhead
- **Claim:** For the configurations studied, variational quantum classifiers require substantially longer wall-clock training time than classical MLPs.
- **Mechanism:** The per-iteration cost of simulating/executing a parameterized quantum circuit (statevector simulation, shot-based expectation estimation) is much higher per parameter than the matrix operations in a small MLP. This overhead dominates total training time.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.4 Table 5: VQC(L=2, analytic, 12 params) median train time ~943s vs. MLP(h=4, 17 params) ~0.053s; abstract notes "orders-of-magnitude shorter training time" for MLP.

- **Concept: Variational Quantum Circuit (VQC) Expressivity controlled by Ansatz Depth (L)**  
  *Why needed:* The key result is that depth L=2 succeeds where L=1 fails. This links a concrete architectural knob (depth) to functional capability (forming nonlinear boundaries).  
  *Quick check:* How does increasing the ansatz depth L in a two-qubit VQC (with alternating single-qubit rotations and CNOT) theoretically increase the class of representable quantum states and, by extension, the classical decision function derived from measurements?

- **Concept: Noise in NISQ Devices (Hardware vs. Simulation)**  
  *Why needed:* To interpret hardware results, one must distinguish between sampling noise (finite shots), ideal simulation (analytic expectation), and real-device noise (coherent errors, readout errors). The paper finds hardware noise causes structured deviations.  
  *Quick check:* What are two specific sources of noise that would cause a real quantum device's measured expectation value `<Z>` to differ from the ideal simulated value for the same circuit parameters?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  2D input (x1, x2) -> Classical MLP: Dense(4, sigmoid) -> Dense(1, linear) -> Sigmoid output  
  2D input (x1, x2) -> Quantum VQC: Angle Encoding (RX(π·x)) -> Ansatz (L layers: RY,RZ + CNOT) -> Measure Z on qubit 0 -> Map expectation m∈[-1,1] to probability p=(1+m)/2  
  Dataset Generator: Gaussian clusters around XOR corners with std σ.

- **Critical path for XOR solution (VQC):**  
  1. **Encoding:** Map 2D input to quantum state via RX rotations.  
  2. **Entangling Processing:** Apply L layers of parameterized rotations and CNOT. For XOR, L=2 is the minimum depth that can generate the required entangled, non-separable state geometry.  
  3. **Measurement:** Extract Z expectation; sign determines class.  
  4. **Decision Boundary:** The mapping from input to measured expectation must yield a 2D surface with XOR-like topology (two "islands" of class 1 in opposite corners).

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *VQC Depth (L):* L=1 is too shallow (near-linear boundary); L=2 succeeds but doubles parameters/training cost; deeper L may risk barren plateaus.  
  - *Encoding:* Angle encoding is simple but may not be optimal; alternative encodings (amplitude, IQP) change effective feature map and expressivity.  
  - *Shots:* More shots reduce sampling noise but increase wall-time per evaluation; analytic simulation ignores sampling noise entirely.  
  - *MLP Width (h):* h=1 fails; h=2 may be borderline; h=4+ succeeds—hidden units provide nonlinear basis functions.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *VQC(L=1):* Decision boundary is a single simple curve (e.g., rotated line); test accuracy ~50–55%, BCE ~0.69.  
  - *MLP(h<4):* Underfitting; cannot separate noisy clusters; accuracy degrades with noise.  
  - *Hardware execution:* Global accuracy may be perfect, but continuous score f(x) shows spatial distortion (higher error at grid edges), visible as "grainy" or "compressed" decision surfaces versus smooth simulator output.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate core accuracy comparison: Train MLP(h=4) and VQC(L=2) on Dataset B (σ=0.10, n=100) for 5 seeds; report mean test accuracy and BCE. Expect both ~1.0 accuracy, but MLP lower BCE.  
  2. VQC depth ablation: Train VQC with L=1 and L=2 on same setting; visualize learned decision boundaries (Fig 7). Expect L=1 poor separation, L=2 correct XOR geometry.  
  3. Hardware vs. Simulator inference: Evaluate trained VQC(L=2) on a 25x25 grid using ideal simulator, finite-shot simulator (1024 shots), and real IBM hardware (1024 shots). Compute per-point |f_hw - f_sim|; expect hardware deviation ~0.118 mean with spatial structure.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions. However, the limitations and proposed next checks imply several unresolved issues:

- **Scalability of Expressivity Threshold:** The identified depth threshold (L=2) for XOR may not generalize to higher-dimensional or more complex non-linear problems. How does the required ansatz depth scale with problem dimensionality and intrinsic complexity?
- **Universality of Hardware Noise Patterns:** The structured deviations observed on a single IBM device may be device-specific. Are these spatial distortion patterns consistent across different quantum processors, calibration states, and transpilation settings?
- **Training Time Bottleneck Mitigation:** The orders-of-magnitude training time overhead for VQCs is attributed to per-iteration cost. Can algorithmic improvements (e.g., gradient compression, shot-frugal optimization) or hardware advances (faster queue turnaround) close this gap for practical problem sizes?
- **Robustness to Real-World Data:** The study uses synthetic Gaussian XOR data. Do the findings on expressivity requirements, noise sensitivity, and training efficiency hold for real-world datasets with higher dimensionality, correlations, and non-Gaussian noise?

## Limitations
- **Generalizability:** Findings are constrained to a 2-qubit XOR task with specific angle encoding and hardware-efficient ansatz; performance on higher-dimensional or structurally different problems is unknown.
- **Reproducibility:** Hardware results depend on a single device's calibration state and queue conditions; exact backend and compilation transpilation settings are not fully specified.
- **Hyperparameter opacity:** VQC training details (exact epoch count, learning rate schedules, early stopping) are described as "depending on setting" without a definitive protocol, risking inconsistent convergence.

## Confidence
- **Expressivity determines success:** High (for the specific XOR configurations tested; direct ablation in Section 5.3), but Low for broader claims about quantum advantage or scaling to complex tasks.
- **Hardware introduces structured deviations:** Medium (quantified spatial effects clearly documented in Section 5.6), but evidence limited to one device and one trained parameter set.
- **VQC training time is orders of magnitude longer:** Medium (benchmark data explicit in Table 5), but implementation/stack-dependent and may not reflect optimized future runtimes.

## Next Checks
1. **Depth-expressivity boundary:** Train VQCs with fractional/continuous depth scaling (e.g., L=1, 1.5 via partial ansatz repetitions) and alternative entanglement patterns (e.g., CZ, full connectivity) on XOR; measure accuracy and visualize decision boundaries to confirm L=2 as a sharp threshold versus gradual effect.
2. **Hardware noise structure replication:** Execute the same trained VQC(L=2) circuit on ≥3 different IBM Quantum devices (e.g., `ibm_kyoto`, `ibm_nairobi`, `ibm_oslo`) on the same calendar day; compute mean absolute deviation and spatial correlation metrics against ideal simulation to test if the reported structured deviation pattern is device-specific or universal.
3. **Training time decomposition:** Profile VQC training under identical optimizer settings (Adam, lr=0.2) in three environments: (a) statevector simulator, (b) shot-based simulator (1024 shots), (c) real hardware; separately log time per gradient computation, parameter update, and circuit execution to isolate whether overhead stems from simulation complexity or network/queue latency.
