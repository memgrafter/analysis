# DPxFin: Adaptive Differential Privacy for Anti-Money Laundering Detection via Reputation-Weighted Federated Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.19314
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19314
- **Reference count**: 25
- **Primary result**: DPxFin improved accuracy over fixed-noise DP baselines by up to 3% in non-IID settings and reduced TabLeak attack accuracy from 92.9% to 58.5%.

## Executive Summary
DPxFin introduces a novel federated learning framework for anti-money laundering (AML) that adapts differential privacy (DP) noise based on client reputation. Reputation is computed each round by measuring the alignment of a client’s local model update with a temporary global aggregate; clients with higher alignment (reputable) receive less noise, preserving utility, while less aligned clients receive more noise, enhancing privacy. The server also weights aggregation by reputation, prioritizing trustworthy contributions. Evaluated on a tabular AML dataset under IID and non-IID splits with a multi-layer perceptron, DPxFin consistently outperforms fixed-DP baselines, achieving up to a 3% accuracy gain in non-IID scenarios and substantially reducing membership inference attack success (TabLeak) from 92.9% to 58.5%. This demonstrates an effective privacy-utility trade-off for sensitive financial data.

## Method Summary
DPxFin modifies standard federated learning by incorporating adaptive differential privacy driven by client reputation. At each round, the server distributes the global model and a reputation factor λk to selected clients. Each client scales its DP noise multiplier by σ′k = λk·σ (using a base σ) during local DP-SGD training, then returns a clipped and noisy update. The server aggregates all received updates into a temporary global model Wagg. For every client, it computes Euclidean distance di = ∥˜wi − Wagg∥2 between the client’s update and Wagg, normalizes distances to obtain a raw reputation repi, and then normalizes across clients to produce weights ¯repi. New λ factors for the next round are assigned via percentile thresholds (e.g., low, medium, high reputation get λ = 0.2, 0.5, 1.0). The final global update is a weighted average: Wt+1 = Σ ¯repi·˜wi. This cycle repeats, dynamically adjusting privacy noise and influence based on perceived client reliability.

## Key Results
- **Improved utility**: Up to 3% higher accuracy compared to fixed-noise DP-FedAvg in non-IID settings.
- **Strong privacy**: Reduced TabLeak attack accuracy from 92.9% (FedAvg) to 58.5% (DPxFin).
- **Balanced performance**: Maintained competitive scores across accuracy, F1, precision, and recall metrics under both IID and non-IID data distributions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Adaptive Noise Allocation
**Claim**: Assigning noise adaptively based on client reputation improves the privacy-utility trade-off.  
**Mechanism**: The server computes a reputation factor λk for each client; the client scales its differential privacy noise multiplier by σ′k = λk·σ during local training (Eq. 3). High-reputation clients (low λ) add less noise, preserving utility; low-reputation clients (high λ) add more noise, enhancing privacy.  
**Core assumption**: Clients whose model updates align closely with the global consensus are more likely to have benign, high-quality data and thus deserve reduced noise; misaligned clients may be risky and require stronger protection.  
**Evidence anchors**: [abstract] states “compute client reputation based on alignment between local and global models, then dynamically assign differential privacy noise”; [section 3.2] elaborates on the tiered noise allocation.  
**Break condition**: If reputation scores become uniform or highly volatile, the adaptive scaling may not improve utility or privacy. Additionally, if Euclidean distance poorly reflects true client quality under extreme non-IID splits, honest but heterogeneous clients could be unfairly penalized.

### Euclidean Distance as Alignment Metric
**Claim**: Euclidean distance between a client’s update and a temporary global model reliably quantifies contribution quality for reputation.  
**Mechanism**: After aggregating all received updates into a temporary global model Wagg, the server computes di = ∥˜wi − Wagg∥2 for each client, normalizes to ˜di = 1 − di/maxj dj, and sets raw reputation repi = ˜di (Eqs. 5–7).  
**Core assumption**: Updates that are vector-wise closer to the aggregate update are more consistent with the collective learning objective and thus higher quality; greater divergence indicates potential data heterogeneity or malicious intent.  
**Evidence anchors**: [abstract] implies alignment measurement; [section 3.3] explicitly defines the distance and reputation formulas.  
**Break condition**: In early training rounds, Wagg may be a poor representation of a converged model, making distances uninformative. Moreover, if model parameters have disparate scales, Euclidean distance may not correlate with true contribution quality.

### Reputation-Weighted Aggregation
**Claim**: Downweighting low-reputation clients during aggregation improves model robustness against poor or adversarial updates.  
**Mechanism**: The server normalizes reputation scores to ¯repi = repi/∑j repj and computes the new global model as Wt+1 = Σ ¯repi·˜wi (Eq. 10). Higher-weight updates come from reputable clients.  
**Core assumption**: Clients with lower reputation are more likely to provide updates that are less useful or potentially harmful (e.g., due to data heterogeneity, poor local training, or malicious intent); reducing their influence protects the global model’s integrity.  
**Evidence anchors**: [abstract] mentions “prioritising reliable client contributions”; [section 3.3] details the weighted aggregation.  
**Break condition**: Malicious clients could mimic high-reputation updates (e.g., by aligning with Wagg while embedding backdoors), exploiting the weighting. Persistent non-IID data may also cause honest clients to have low reputation, unfairly reducing their contribution.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Server components: Global Model Manager, Reputation Calculator, Aggregation Engine, Noise Factor Distributor.  
Client components: Local Trainer (DP-SGD), Update Preparator, Data Preprocessor (SMOTE, feature engineering).

**Data flow**:  
Server → (model, λ) → Client → (noisy update) → Server → (Wagg, distances, rep, new λ) → Aggregation → new global model.

**Critical path**:  
1. Initialize global model W0 and λk=1.0.  
2. Server selects clients, sends Wt and λk.  
3. Each client computes σ′k = λk·σ (or σ if first round), trains locally with DP-SGD, clips update, adds noise, returns ˜∆wk.  
4. Server averages all ˜∆wk to form temporary Wagg.  
5. For each client, compute di = ∥˜wi − Wagg∥2, then ˜di, repi, normalize to ¯repi, and assign new λi based on percentiles (Eq. 9).  
6. Aggregate final Wt+1 = Σ ¯repi·˜wi.  
7. Repeat from step 2 until convergence.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Privacy vs. utility: Lower λ for reputable clients improves utility but weakens per-client DP; tiered λ (0.2, 0.5, 1.0) is heuristic.  
- Reputation timeliness: Fresh computation each round is responsive but may be volatile; a rolling average could smooth but adds complexity.  
- Non-IID handling: Reputation mitigates heterogeneity but gains are modest (≤3%).  
- Overhead: Euclidean distance O(model size) is negligible; storing per-client λ adds minimal memory cost.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Accuracy collapse: suspect excessive noise (all λ too low) or miscomputed reputation.  
- High TabLeak accuracy (>80%): privacy failure; check λ distribution or noise clipping.  
- Reputation saturation: all λ quickly become 0.2, vulnerable to a single compromised client.  
- Convergence stall: global model accuracy plateaus early; possibly due to poor Wagg or overly aggressive noise.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Baseline replication**: Run DPxFin with σ=1.0 on the AML dataset (MLP, SMOTE, IID/non-IID) and compare test accuracy/F1 against FedAvg (no privacy) and DP-FedAvg (fixed σ). Reproduce results in Table 4 and Figure 3.  
2. **Ablation on adaptivity**: Disable adaptive noise by fixing λ=1.0 for all clients (uniform DP) and compare to full DPxFin; also vary σ to study sensitivity. Isolates the contribution of reputation-based scaling.  
3. **Attack validation**: Implement the TabLeak attack [19] on both FedAvg and DPxFin updates; measure reconstruction accuracy (Table 3). Confirm DPxFin reduces attack success from ~93% to ~59%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Is Euclidean distance the most effective metric for quantifying client-update alignment in DPxFin's reputation mechanism for financial tabular data?  
- **Basis**: Equation 5 uses Euclidean distance without comparing alternatives, despite tabular data's heterogeneous feature space.  
- **Why unresolved**: Other metrics (e.g., cosine similarity, Mahalanobis) may better capture alignment in high-dimensional financial features, potentially improving utility-privacy trade-offs.  
- **Resolving evidence**: Systematic ablation studies evaluating multiple distance metrics on the AML dataset, measuring changes in accuracy, F1-score, and TabLeak attack resilience.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How sensitive is DPxFin's performance to the fixed percentile thresholds (P70, P50) used in reputation factor assignment?  
- **Basis**: Thresholds in Equation 9 are chosen arbitrarily with no sensitivity analysis or justification.  
- **Why unresolved**: Optimal thresholds likely depend on client data heterogeneity and distribution; static values may suboptimally penalize mid-tier clients in varied non-IID settings.  
- **Resolving evidence**: Empirical experiments varying threshold percentiles across Dirichlet concentration parameters, analyzing convergence speed, final model accuracy, and reputation stability over rounds.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Does DPxFin generalize to complex model architectures (e.g., graph neural networks) and real-world financial datasets beyond the synthetic AML dataset and MLP?  
- **Basis**: Evaluations are limited to MLP on one synthetic dataset; the conclusion notes broader applicability as future work.  
- **Why unresolved**: Real financial data involves noise and graph-structured transactions; architectures like BGAT may leverage relational patterns differently, affecting noise allocation efficacy.  
- **Resolving evidence**: Replication studies using real banking datasets and architectures such as FALD-BGAT [13], comparing privacy-utility trade-offs and attack robustness against DPxFin.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Can sophisticated attackers manipulate DPxFin's reputation scores by crafting malicious updates that align with the global model while still enabling data leakage?  
- **Basis**: The reputation mechanism assumes alignment correlates with trustworthiness, but no adversarial robustness analysis is provided.  
- **Why unresolved**: Adaptive malicious clients could mimic trustworthy updates to receive less noise, yet retain sensitive information, undermining the privacy guarantee.  
- **Resolving evidence**: Implement adaptive attacks where malicious clients optimize model updates for high reputation (low Euclidean distance) while maximizing leakage; measure reconstruction accuracy in TabLeak or membership inference attacks under such conditions.

## Limitations
- The reputation metric relies on Euclidean distance to a temporary global model, which may be unstable early in training or under extreme non-IID splits; its correlation with true client quality is not formally validated.
- The tiered λ assignment (0.2, 0.5, 1.0) is heuristic and lacks a formal differential privacy guarantee, leaving the overall privacy budget ambiguous.
- Evaluations are restricted to a single synthetic AML dataset and a multi-layer perceptron; generalizability to real-world financial data and more complex architectures remains unproven.

## Confidence
- Adaptive noise allocation improves privacy-utility trade-off: **High**
- Euclidean distance reliably quantifies contribution quality: **Medium**
- Reputation-weighted aggregation mitigates poor/adversarial updates: **Low**
