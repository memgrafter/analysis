# Exposía: Academic Writing Assessment of Exposés and Peer Feedback

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06536
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06536
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Open-source LLMs achieve high agreement with humans on low-expertise writing criteria but degrade on content-related dimensions, align more with lenient graders, and perform better with combined prompting across all criteria.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Exposía, a dataset from a university course linking academic writing (student proposals) with feedback (peer/instructor comments) and fine-grained human scores on 36 (exposés) and 24 (reviews) criteria. The authors benchmark open-source LLMs on this criterion-based scoring task. Key findings indicate that model performance mirrors human patterns: agreement is high on formal/academic conventions but drops on expert-level content dimensions. Furthermore, LLMs show systematic alignment with human graders who assign higher scores. Finally, prompting the model to score all criteria jointly—rather than individually—yields both higher agreement and significant computational efficiency, a crucial recommendation for classroom deployment.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates LLMs on the novel task of predicting fine-grained, rubric-based scores for academic writing (exposés) and peer reviews. The Exposía dataset provides submissions (draft/final), inline comments, reviews, and gold scores from human instructors (two groups with differing leniency instructions). Models are prompted using two strategies: single-criterion (one score per prompt) and combined (all scores in one prompt). Performance is measured using Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWA) due to skewed label distributions, with analysis stratified by rubric-defined expertise levels (0: formal, 1: academic, 2: expert). The methodology emphasizes practical deployment considerations like token usage and runtime.

## Key Results
- LLM-human agreement is high on low-expertise criteria (e.g., formal language, structure) but degrades monotonically on content-related, expert-level dimensions (e.g., research question soundness, methodology relevance).
- LLMs exhibit a bias toward scores assigned by more lenient human graders (Group 1), indicating an alignment with positivity biases in model outputs.
- Combined prompting (scoring all criteria in one pass) consistently outperforms single-criterion prompting in agreement and reduces computational cost by 1.3×–5.3× in tokens and runtime.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** LLM scoring performance degrades on criteria requiring subject-matter expertise.
- **Mechanism:** Models rely on surface linguistic patterns and general academic conventions; they lack the deep disciplinary knowledge to evaluate soundness of research questions, methodology relevance, or theoretical framing.
- **Core assumption:** Expert-level criteria depend on domain-specific reasoning that current open-weight LLMs have not fully acquired from pre-training.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "models achieve high agreement with humans on low-expertise criteria (QWA up to 0.90) but degrade on content-related dimensions"
  - [section 6.2] "Performance by expertise level... agreement decreases monotonically with expertise"
  - [corpus] The rubric-defined expertise levels (Appendix D) and criterion-level agreement tables (Tables 19, 22) show the gradient; content rubrics (Motivation, Approach, Methodology) have lower human-human QWA and larger LLM-human gaps.
- **Break condition:** If fine-tuning or retrieval augmentation specifically targets the disciplinary corpus used in the course (Computer Science/AI topics), the performance gap on expert criteria could narrow.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** LLMs systematically align with more lenient human graders.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs inherit a "positivity bias" from instruction tuning, tending to assign higher scores; this matches the behavior of Group 1 instructors who were instructed to resolve uncertainty in favor of students.
- **Core assumption:** The model's rating distribution is shifted upward relative to a stricter reference; agreement is higher with raters who have similarly shifted distributions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "LLMs align more with human graders who assign higher scores"
  - [section 6.3] "We observe a tendency for Group 1 raters to assign higher scores... LLM agreement with Group 1 is often higher than agreement with Group 2"
  - [section 5.2, G.1] Description of Group 1 (in-semester, leniency instruction) vs. Group 2 (post-semester, no leniency instruction).
- **Break condition:** If the prompting explicitly includes calibrated examples or rank-orders outputs to match a stricter distribution, the alignment with lenient graders could be reduced.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Jointly scoring all criteria in a single prompt ("combined prompting") yields higher agreement and computational efficiency than scoring each criterion separately.
- **Mechanism:** The model can leverage inter-criterion contextual reasoning (e.g., judging methodology details in light of stated research questions) and avoids repeated processing of the same long input text.
- **Core assumption:** Criteria are not fully independent; holistic understanding of the document benefits from considering all rubric dimensions simultaneously.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "combined prompting (scoring all criteria jointly) yields higher agreement and better computational efficiency than single-criterion prompting"
  - [section 6.2] "Table 4 shows main results... combined prompting yields higher agreement"
  - [appendix J, K] Runtime and token tables (24, 25) show 1.3×–5.3× speedup; Figures 9, 10 illustrate token savings.
- **Break condition:** If criteria become so numerous that the combined prompt exceeds the model's context window, or if criteria are truly orthogonal with no cross-dependencies, single-criterion prompts might be necessary.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** **Criterion-based analytic scoring rubrics**.
  - *Why needed here:* The core task is not holistic grading but predicting fine-grained scores on 36 (exposés) or 24 (reviews) pedagogically defined criteria. Understanding how rubrics decompose writing quality into orthogonal dimensions (e.g., Motivation, Approach, Methodology) is essential to interpret model performance per rubric.
  - *Quick check question:* If a model scores high on "Problem relevance" but low on "Teaser (RQ)", what does this suggest about the nature of the student's exposé problem statement?
- **Concept:** **Expertise level annotation**.
  - *Why needed here:* The paper groups criteria into expertise levels (0: formal, 1: academic, 2: expert) to explain performance variance. This taxonomy explains why some criteria are inherently harder for both humans and LLMs.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might "Language quality" (Level 1) show higher human-human agreement than "Teaser (RQ)" (Level 2)?
- **Concept:** **Prompting strategy trade-offs in multi-criteria evaluation**.
  - *Why needed here:* The key architectural decision is between "combined" and "single-criterion" prompting. Understanding the efficiency/accuracy trade-off is crucial for classroom deployment where resources are constrained.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the primary computational disadvantage of single-criterion prompting that becomes critical under deadline-driven grading bursts?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Exposía dataset -> Rubric definitions -> Prompt generator -> LLM inference layer -> JSON parser & validator -> Scoring evaluator -> Agreement metrics
- **Critical path:** Data loading → Rubric loading → For each submission: Prompt generation → Model inference → JSON parsing → Score extraction → Agreement calculation.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Context length vs. number of criteria:** Combined prompts must fit all rubric descriptions + submission; very long exposés with many reviews may exceed limits. Single-criterion avoids this but multiplies latency.
  - **Model size vs. classroom feasibility:** Larger models (Qwen3 80B) give best agreement but require at least 2×A100 80GB; smaller models (GPT OSS 20B) are more deployable but weaker on expert criteria.
  - **Evaluation metric:** QWA is emphasized over α/r because of skewed label distributions (e.g., many criteria are 0/1). Using α could misrepresent model utility.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Parsing failures:** Model inserts non-JSON text; invalid JSON structure. Mitigation: strict decoding, retry with explicit instructions.
  - **OOM on long inputs:** Exposés with many inline comments or very long reviews exceed GPU memory. Mitigation: truncate non-essential sections (e.g., bibliography), or use models with better long-context efficiency.
  - **Poor performance on Level 2 criteria:** Model QWA drops significantly on expert rubrics (Motivation, Approach, Methodology). This is expected and should trigger human-in-the-loop review for those dimensions.
  - **Score distribution mismatch:** If model's mean score is much higher/lower than Group 1 (lenient) baseline, it may indicate a bias shift—check prompting and instruction clarity.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline single-criterion scoring:** Run each model on all criteria individually, compute QWA per criterion and overall. This reproduces the "Ind" column in Tables 22/23.
  2. **Baseline combined scoring:** Run each model with the full rubric in one prompt, compute QWA. This reproduces the "All" column; expect higher agreement and lower latency.
  3. **Expertise-level stratification:** Group criteria by Level 0/1/2 (Appendix D) and compute agreement separately. Verify the monotonic degradation pattern from the paper (Figure 4, 7). If not observed, check rubric mapping and prompt fidelity.

## Limitations
- Single-course dataset (CS/AI) limits domain generalizability.
- Open-weight models may not capture latest capabilities.
- Potential unreported parsing/truncation issues.

## Confidence
- **Expert criteria degradation:** Medium – monotonic trend observed, but human agreement also drops; causality not proven.
- **Alignment with lenient graders:** High – consistent across models and criteria with two distinct human groups.
- **Combined prompting gains:** High – robust agreement and speedup improvements replicated.
