# Beyond Suffixes: Token Position in GCG Adversarial Attacks on Large Language Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03265
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03265
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** Allowing adversarial tokens to be placed as either a prefix or suffix (ASR@k=2) substantially increases jailbreak success rates in white-box evaluations and significantly improves cross-model transferability compared to fixed-position (suffix-only) evaluations.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates a critical oversight in adversarial attack evaluations of large language models (LLMs): the assumption that adversarial tokens are most effective when appended as a suffix. By adapting the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attack to generate prefixes (GCG-Prefix) and comparing both prefix and suffix placements, the authors demonstrate that token position is a significant axis of vulnerability. Across five models, evaluating success at both positions (ASR@k=2) yields higher attack success rates than evaluating at a single position (ASR@k=1). This effect is even more pronounced for cross-model transfer, where positional variation can increase transfer success by up to 49%. The findings reveal that standard robustness assessments, which fix token position, systematically underestimate jailbreak risks and underscore the need for position-aware evaluation protocols.

## Method Summary
The authors extend the standard GCG attack, which greedily optimizes adversarial tokens to minimize a loss function, from its typical suffix-only formulation to a prefix variant. For a given harmful prompt, GCG generates a fixed-length sequence (20 tokens) either to append (suffix) or prepend (prefix). The core experimental insight is the evaluation metric: ASR@k=1 measures success only at the optimized position (suffix for GCG-Suffix, prefix for GCG-Prefix), while ASR@k=2 counts a jailbreak as successful if either placement works. This disentangles the intrinsic quality of the generated tokens from their positional sensitivity. White-box tests generate tokens on the same model they attack, while transfer tests generate tokens on a source model and evaluate them on a different target model. Attack success is judged by a GPT-4 based classifier scoring outputs for harmfulness on a 1–5 scale, with scores ≥5 indicating a successful jailbreak.

## Key Results
- In white-box evaluations, ASR@k=2 is consistently higher than ASR@k=1 across all models. For Vicuna, the success rate rises from 83% to 99%; for Qwen, from 46% to 61% (using GCG-Suffix).
- GCG-Prefix and GCG-Suffix exhibit different effectiveness per model (e.g., GCG-Prefix outperforms GCG-Suffix for Qwen but not for Vicuna), confirming that optimal token position is model-dependent.
- Cross-model transferability improves dramatically with positional variation. The largest gain is a 49% absolute increase when transferring Vicuna-generated suffixes to Deepseek (ASR@k=1: 24% → ASR@k=2: 73%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Adversarial token position influences the model's internal attention distribution, which correlates with jailbreak success. Prefixes and suffixes induce distinct attention patterns across transformer layers, yet both can succeed.
- **Mechanism:** GCG optimizes tokens for a fixed position. The model processes these tokens differently due to positional encoding and autoregressive attention flows. Suffixes tend to attract more attention in later layers, while prefixes shift attention earlier. Jailbreak success occurs despite these differences, indicating multiple attention pathways can bypass alignment.
- **Core assumption:** Attention scores partially reflect the model's focus on adversarial tokens and that position alters these scores. Success depends on whether the adversarial signal reaches critical decision points.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes that for prefix attacks, "attention at most layers remains dominated by the goal tokens, despite successful jailbreaks." Section 4 figures show suffix attacks increase attention in later layers; prefix attacks peak earlier but still succeed.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** GCG optimization is position-specific; tokens optimized for one position (e.g., suffix) are suboptimal for others (e.g., prefix). Evaluating at both positions (ASR@k=2) reveals additional successful jailbreaks missed by fixed-position evaluation (ASR@k=1).
- **Mechanism:** GCG greedily updates tokens based on gradients computed at the training position. Since the loss landscape depends on token context (position), optimized suffixes may not minimize loss when moved to prefix, and vice versa. Allowing both positions acts as a nondifferentiable ensemble.
- **Core assumption:** The model’s output probability distribution is position-dependent for the same token sequence.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract reports Vicuna's ASR rises from 83% to 99% with positional variation. Table 1 shows GCG-Prefix and GCG-Suffix have different ASR@k=1 values, proving position-specific optimization.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Positional variation during evaluation enhances cross-model transferability of adversarial tokens because different models have heterogeneous position biases.
- **Mechanism:** GCG generates tokens on a source model, exploiting that model’s position-sensitive circuitry. When transferred, the same tokens may not align with the target’s position processing. However, if the target processes prefixes and suffixes differently, the token set might fit the alternative position better. Evaluating both positions leverages this heterogeneity.
- **Core assumption:** Position-sensitivity varies across models; a token set’s effectiveness is not uniformly tied to its generation position when generalized.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes transferability improvements up to 49%. Table 2 shows large jumps (e.g., Llama→Vicuna suffix: +49%) when evaluating both positions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attack**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper extends GCG; understanding its suffix-based optimization is prerequisite to grasp the prefix variant and the role of position.
  - *Quick check question:* How does GCG approximate gradients for discrete token optimization, and why is it typically applied as a suffix?
- **Concept: Attack Success Rate (ASR) and ASR@k**
  - *Why needed here:* ASR@k=1 vs. ASR@k=2 is the core metric showing positional impact. Misinterpreting these metrics obscures the paper’s findings.
  - *Quick check question:* What does ASR@k=2 measure that ASR@k=1 does not, and why does it matter for robustness evaluation?
- **Concept: Attention mechanisms in transformers**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper analyzes attention shifts to explain why position matters. Without attention basics, the mechanism in Section 4 is unclear.
  - *Quick check question:* How do query, key, and value interactions in transformer layers lead to position-dependent attention patterns?
- **Concept: Transferability of adversarial examples**
  - *Why needed here:* Cross-model results are a key contribution. Transferability concepts explain why adversarial tokens work across models and how position affects it.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might an adversarial prompt optimized on one LLM succeed or fail on another, and how could token position influence this?
- **Concept: Positional encodings in LLMs**
  - *Why needed here:* Positional encodings (absolute or relative) fundamentally shape how models process token locations, directly affecting the attack surface.
  - *Quick check question:* How do absolute (e.g., sinusoidal) vs. relative (e.g., RoPE) positional encodings differ in their impact on token position sensitivity?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Harmful prompts (AdvBench) → GCG attack (suffix/prefix) → Adversarial tokens (fixed length) → Evaluation: Insert tokens at position(s) → Target LLM → Output generation → LLM Judge (GPT-4) → Harm score (1–5) → Success if ≥5 → Compute ASR@k=1 (single position) and ASR@k=2 (either position). Additional: Attention analysis computes layer-wise attention weights on adversarial/goal tokens vs. output tokens.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. **Token generation:** Run GCG (suffix or prefix) on source model for each harmful prompt until loss converges or max iterations.  
  2. **Positional evaluation:** For each token set, evaluate at its optimized position and the alternative position (prefix↔suffix).  
  3. **Judgment:** Use GPT-4 judge with policy-based scoring to label outputs as harmful (≥5) or not.  
  4. **Aggregation:** Compute ASR@k=1 (only optimized position successful) and ASR@k=2 (at least one position successful) across prompts.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Fixed token length (20) and GCG hyperparameters* may not generalize; longer tokens or different candidate sizes could change results.  
  - *Judge model (GPT-4) bias*: Judge’s own alignment might under- or over-estimate harm.  
  - *Binary position (prefix/suffix only)*: Intermediate or multiple positions unexplored, possibly missing optimal placements.  
  - *No separators*: Whitespace-only separation might not reflect real-world prompt formatting; special tokens could alter dynamics.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *GCG fails to generate*: ASR@k=1 near 0% for a model—GCG hyperparameters may be unsuitable or model robust to suffix/prefix attacks.  
  - *No positional effect*: ASR@k=2 ≈ ASR@k=1 across all models—the model may be position-invariant or adversarial tokens already optimal.  
  - *Attention anomaly*: For prefix attacks, if later layers show high attention to adversarial tokens (contradicting Figure 1), the attack or analysis may be flawed.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **White-box replication:** For Vicuna, run GCG-Suffix and GCG-Prefix on 10 AdvBench prompts. Compute ASR@k=1 and ASR@k=2. Expect ASR@k=2 > ASR@k=1, especially for prefixes.  
  2. **Cross-model transfer:** Use Vicuna-generated suffixes to attack Deepseek. Compare ASR@k=1 vs. ASR@k=2. Expect significant increase (per Table 2).  
  3. **Attention validation:** For a successful prefix attack on Llama, compute attention scores (Equation 1) across layers. Verify earlier layers show higher attention to adversarial tokens than later layers (Figure 1).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: To what extent do other jailbreak attack methods (e.g., PAIR, AutoDAN) exhibit sensitivity to adversarial token position, and does position variation similarly improve their success rates?
- Basis in paper: The authors state in the conclusion: "In future work, we aim to extend this analysis to other attacks..."
- Why unresolved: The study only investigates GCG, leaving unknown whether the observed position-dependent effectiveness generalizes to other attack paradigms like auto-regressive or human-in-the-loop methods.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic replication of the experimental setup (ASR@k=1 vs ASR@k=2) with various attack algorithms across multiple models, measuring positional sensitivity consistently.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does inserting adversarial tokens at intermediate positions (e.g., within the prompt body) affect jailbreak success compared to only prefix or suffix placements?
- Basis in paper: The authors mention: "by systemically varying adversarial token position beyond suffixes or prefixes" as future work.
- Why unresolved: The current work only considers prefix and suffix extremes, so the full positional landscape—including middle, split, or multiple segments—remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extending GCG optimization to generate tokens at arbitrary insertion points and evaluating ASR across a dense set of positions to map the positional effectiveness curve.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What architectural or alignment-related factors cause certain models (e.g., Vicuna) to exhibit greater sensitivity to token position than others (e.g., Mistral)?
- Basis in paper: The results in Table 1 show varying degrees of ASR improvement with position variation across models (e.g., Vicuna's 16% increase vs. Mistral's negligible change), but no analysis of underlying causes is provided.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate why models respond differently to token placement, which is crucial for understanding model-specific vulnerabilities and designing robust alignments.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative analysis correlating positional sensitivity metrics with model architectures (e.g., layer counts, attention heads), training data, and safety tuning methods (e.g., DPO, RLHF) to identify predictive factors.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How do internal safety mechanisms, such as refusal directions, interact with adversarial token position, and can they explain the success of prefix-based attacks despite reduced attention in later layers?
- Basis in paper: The attention analysis shows prefix attacks succeed even with less attention in later layers, and the authors postulate that "attention should be augmented by a view into the internal model mechanisms, such as refusal directions."
- Why unresolved: The paper only provides attention scores, not a mechanistic explanation of how position affects the model's internal refusal behavior or safety circuitry.
- What evidence would resolve it: Applying mechanistic interpretability techniques (e.g., activation patching, directional probing for refusal features) on successful prefix vs. suffix attacks to trace how token position influences the activation of safety-related components.

## Limitations
- The study uses a fixed 20-token length and only binary (prefix/suffix) placements, leaving the impact of intermediate positions and variable token lengths unexplored.
- The GPT-4 judge for evaluating harmfulness may introduce bias, as its own alignment could systematically affect scoring of different prompt formats.
- Cross-model transfer tests are limited to decoder-only LLMs with similar architectures; results may not generalize to models with divergent positional encodings (e.g., encoder-decoders).

## Confidence
- **Claim A (ASR@k=2 > ASR@k=1 in white-box):** High. Consistently observed across 5 models (Table 1), with statistically significant jumps (e.g., Vicuna 83%→99%).
- **Claim B (Positional variation improves cross-model transfer):** Medium. Table 2 shows large gains (up to +49%), but limited to 4 model pairs; transferability is volatile and may not hold for more diverse architectures.
- **Mechanism 1 (Position alters attention patterns):** Medium. Figures 1–5 show correlations, but no ablation proves attention changes *cause* jailbreaks rather than co-occur.
- **Mechanism 2 (GCG optimization is position-specific):** High. ASR@k=1 differences between GCG-Prefix and GCG-Suffix (Table 1) directly support this.
- **Mechanism 3 (Positional heterogeneity aids transfer):** Low-Medium. Inferred from transfer gains; not directly tested by controlling for model-specific position biases.

## Next Checks
1. **Test non-binary positions:** For Vicuna and Llama, evaluate ASR@k=2 using middle-insertion (tokens split around a prompt) alongside prefix/suffix. If gains persist, the effect is not binary-position-specific.
2. **Vary adversarial token length:** Repeat key experiments with 10 and 50 tokens. If the ASR@k=2 advantage diminishes with longer tokens, position sensitivity may be length-dependent.
3. **Judge-model bias audit:** Re-evaluate a sample of attack outputs using both GPT-4 and a fine-tuned, less-aligned judge (e.g., Llama-2-7b-chat). Calculate correlation in harm scores; low correlation would indicate judge bias threatens validity.
