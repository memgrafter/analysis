# RCLRec: Reverse Curriculum Learning for Modeling Sparse Conversions in Generative Recommendation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.28124
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28124
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** On industrial dataset, Recall@5 increases by +11.25% and NDCG@5 by +13.72% over the best baselines; online A/B test shows advertising revenue +2.09% and orders +1.86%.

## Executive Summary
RCLRec tackles the problem of sparse conversion supervision in generative recommendation by introducing reverse curriculum learning. Instead of training directly on the rare conversion event, it selects a short sequence of conversion‑relevant historical items and prepends them to the decoder input, creating denser intermediate token‑level supervision. The approach consists of a Reverse Curriculum Prefix Module (RCPM) that conditionally retrieves a reverse‑ordered prefix and a curriculum quality‑aware loss that ensures the prefix genuinely aids conversion prediction. Evaluated on a large industrial dataset and Tmall, RCLRec yields substantial gains in conversion metrics (e.g., +11.25% Recall@5) and demonstrates positive impact on business metrics in an online A/B test.

## Method Summary
RCLRec follows a two‑stage training pipeline. First, a standard encoder‑decoder generative recommendation model is pre‑trained on all multi‑behavior interactions, learning shared item semantics and sequence modeling. Second, fine‑tuning is performed only on conversion samples. During fine‑tuning, for each user a query is built from user features and the conversion behavior token (e\_pay). This query scores all historical encoder states; the top‑k highest‑scoring items are selected in reverse chronological order and their semantic token sequences form a decoder‑side prefix. The decoder receives `[BOS] + prefix + conversion tokens` and is trained via teacher forcing on every token. A curriculum quality‑aware loss compares the conversion token NLL with and without the prefix, using a frozen pre‑trained model as baseline, and penalizes prefixes that fail to improve the baseline by a margin. This joint optimization densifies supervision while guiding the prefix selection toward truly informative subsequences.

## Key Results
- Recall@5 improves by +11.25% and NDCG@5 by +13.72% on the industrial dataset over the best baselines.
- Online A/B test: advertising revenue increases by 2.09% and orders by 1.86%.
- Significant improvements are also observed on the Tmall dataset.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

1. **Reverse curriculum prefix selection densifies sparse supervision.** For a target conversion, a user‑ and conversion‑conditioned query scores historical encoder states; the top‑k items (selected in reverse order) become a decoder prefix that provides k additional token‑prediction tasks related to the conversion. This creates instance‑specific intermediate targets. Evidence: Ablations removing RCPM or using a naive recent‑k selector show large performance drops, confirming that the selective reverse ordering captures critical decision context.

2. **Curriculum quality‑aware loss steers the prefix toward informativeness.** A hinge loss penalizes prefixes that do not reduce the conversion token NLL relative to a frozen baseline model by at least a margin. This forces the selection distribution to favor prefixes that improve conversion prediction, not just those that are historically relevant. Evidence: Removing this loss (w/o Quality‑Aware Loss) reduces performance, indicating its role in aligning prefix selection with conversion utility.

3. **Two‑stage training enables transfer from abundant multi‑behavior data.** Pre‑training on all behaviors establishes robust item semantics and sequence modeling capabilities. Subsequent fine‑tuning on conversions adapts the decoder and RCPM to the sparse conversion patterns without catastrophic forgetting. Evidence: RCLRec outperforms other multi‑behavior generative methods (e.g., MBGen, GEAR) that also employ pre‑training, demonstrating the effectiveness of this staged transfer.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Generative Recommendation & Semantic Tokenization  
  **Why needed here:** RCLRec is built on the GR paradigm where items are represented by discrete token sequences. Understanding how these tokens are learned (via vector quantization) and shared across behaviors is fundamental to why adding a prefix of tokens is a coherent way to provide supervision.  
  **Quick check:** If an item's semantic token sequence changes during SFT, what potential problem could occur, and why does two‑stage training help prevent it?

- **Concept:** Curriculum Learning  
  **Why needed here:** The core innovation applies a *reverse* curriculum—presenting easier, relevant subproblems (the selected prefix) before the harder target (conversion). Knowing the standard curriculum principle and its use in sparse‑reward RL clarifies the design rationale.  
  **Quick check:** Why is the curriculum constructed in *reverse* chronological order (oldest selected item first) rather than forward, and what would be the risk of a forward ordering?

- **Concept:** Encoder‑Decoder Architecture for Sequence Generation  
  **Why needed here:** RCLRec modifies a standard Transformer encoder‑decoder. The encoder compresses history into context; the decoder performs cross‑attention over the history and self‑attention on its input (prefix + target). The RCPM intervenes on the decoder input side; understanding this flow is critical.  
  **Quick check:** In the decoder's causal self‑attention during prefix generation, why must the prefix tokens appear *before* the target tokens in the input sequence?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Encoder -> Query Builder -> Relevance Scorer -> RCPM Selector -> Prefix Builder -> Curriculum‑Prefixed Decoder

**Critical path:**  
User History → Encoder → H_u & h_t → Query Builder (q) → Relevance Scorer (p) → RCPM Selector (mask m) → Prefix Builder (G_prefix) → Concatenation with [BOS] & z_pay → Decoder → Token Predictions

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Curriculum length (k):** Larger k densifies supervision but risks noise; optimal around 4.
- **Selection temperature (τ):** Controls sharpness of relevance distribution; lower τ makes selection more deterministic but potentially brittle.
- **IBQ vs. Straight‑Through:** Enables end‑to‑end training but introduces gradient mismatch; performance depends on stable selection distribution.
- **Two‑stage training:** Pre‑training cost high but necessary; SFT only on conversions is efficient but risks overfitting if prefix selection is poorly initialized.

**Failure signatures:**
1. RCPM selects irrelevant prefixes (e.g., random items, non‑conversion‑indicative behaviors): lack of improvement over recent‑k baseline; L_qual remains high.
2. Decoder ignores prefix: performance similar to w/o RCPM; may indicate weak conditioning or poor initialization of e_pay/q.
3. Quality‑aware loss misguides selection: L_qual stays near zero or negative; suggests baseline misalignment or margin issue.
4. Online degradation despite offline gains: offline evaluation uses full history while online serving may have truncated history or rapidly shifting user intent.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Ablation study (Table 2):** Remove RCPM, use naive recent‑k selector, and remove quality‑aware loss to isolate contributions of core modules.
2. **Scalability/Sensitivity analysis (Fig 3):** Vary curriculum length k (1‑6) to find the trade‑off between denser supervision and noise.
3. **Online A/B Test:** Deploy against a strong baseline (TIGER) on a live e‑commerce platform; measure advertising revenue and orders.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can RCLRec be extended to other sparse target behaviors (e.g., add‑to‑cart) or multi‑objective scenarios? (Conclusion mentions future work.)
- Does fixing the baseline parameters (θ₀) in the quality‑aware loss limit the model's ability to learn optimal curricula, and would a dynamic or jointly optimized baseline improve performance?
- How sensitive is performance to the design of the pay‑conditioned query (q = MLP([e_u; e_pay]))? Could alternative query constructions (e.g., richer user context, temporal patterns) yield more informative curricula?
- What is the impact of the margin hyperparameter (m) in L_qual, and is the chosen value (m ≥ 0 small) optimal across datasets?

## Limitations
- The assumption that conversions are preceded by a coherent cluster of relevant history may fail for impulse purchases, leading to noisy prefixes.
- The quality‑aware loss uses a frozen baseline that may become misaligned during fine‑tuning, potentially guiding selection suboptimally.
- Offline evaluation uses full user history, while online serving may face truncated histories or rapidly shifting intent, causing distribution shift.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (reverse curriculum prefix selection): Medium
- Mechanism 2 (curriculum quality‑aware loss): Low
- Mechanism 3 (two‑stage training): High

## Next Checks
1. **Prefix relevance test:** Generate prefixes from the immediate prior item and random items; compare conversion NLL to RCLRec prefixes to assess whether selected prefixes are truly informative.
2. **Calibration analysis:** Correlate the quality‑loss signal (L_base_pay − L_curr_pay) with actual conversion metrics (e.g., Recall@5); a weak correlation would suggest redesigning L_qual.
3. **Truncation robustness:** Evaluate RCLRec with limited user history (e.g., last 10 interactions) during prefix selection; measure performance drop and investigate dynamic k adjustment.
