# AI in Insurance: Adaptive Questionnaires for Improved Risk Profiling

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.02034
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02034
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: Adaptive dynamic questionnaires reduce the number of questions by about 50% compared to static forms, with a slight decrease in risk assessment accuracy and 70% real-user preference for the adaptive approach.

## Executive Summary
Traditional insurance underwriting relies on static, lengthy questionnaires that are prone to fraud and user fatigue. This paper introduces ARQuest, an LLM-driven system that generates adaptive, personalized questioning flows using both user responses and alternative data (e.g., social media images, geographic indicators). Evaluated on a life insurance prototype with 85 synthetic and 10 real users, ARQuest cut the number of required questions by roughly half. While static questionnaires achieved marginally better risk assessment accuracy (lower mean average error), two-thirds of real users favored the adaptive method for its engagement and contextual relevance. The system also flags large discrepancies between predicted and corrected answers as a potential fraud indicator.

## Method Summary
ARQuest leverages large language models to dynamically select subsequent questions based on a combination of prior answers and external data sources. The process starts with baseline user inputs, which are enriched by automatically retrieved alternative data. The LLM processes this integrated context to produce the next most informative question, aiming to minimize redundancy while maximizing predictive power. This iterative loop continues until the system deems sufficient information has been collected to compute a risk score. A final fraud detection step compares initial predictions against corrected responses, highlighting significant mismatches for human review. The prototype was tested in a life insurance scenario, comparing the adaptive flow against a conventional static questionnaire.

## Key Results
- Adaptive questionnaires required approximately 50% fewer questions than static ones.
- Static questionnaires achieved slightly lower mean average error (MAE) against ground-truth risk scores, indicating a minor accuracy trade-off for the adaptive method.
- 70% of real users expressed preference for the adaptive approach due to increased engagement and contextual relevance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
By continuously tailoring the question set to the individual’s context, ARQuest eliminates irrelevant or redundant queries, reducing completion time and user burden. The integration of alternative data (social media, geography) allows the system to infer information that users might omit or misrepresent, thereby enriching the risk profile without direct questioning. The LLM’s natural language understanding ensures that generated questions remain coherent and contextually appropriate, maintaining user engagement. Moreover, the discrepancy flagging mechanism provides a simple yet effective heuristic for identifying potential fraud: large deviations between predicted and corrected answers may signal intentional misreporting.

## Foundational Learning
- **Adaptive Questioning**: Dynamically selects follow-up questions based on prior answers; needed to minimize total questions while preserving predictive power; quick check: average question count reduction vs static baseline.
- **Large Language Models (LLMs)**: Generates and interprets natural language; needed to understand diverse inputs and produce context-aware questions; quick check: human evaluation of question relevance.
- **Alternative Data Integration**: Incorporates external signals (social media images, geolocation); needed to supplement self-reported data and improve accuracy; quick check: correlation between alternative features and ground-truth risk.
- **Risk Scoring**: Maps collected information to a quantitative risk metric; needed for underwriting decisions; quick check: compare model MAE to expert underwriter assessments.
- **Fraud Detection via Discrepancy Flagging**: Highlights inconsistencies between predictions and corrected answers; needed to flag suspicious applications; quick check: precision/recall on a labeled fraud dataset.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: User Input -> Data Collector (alternative data retrieval) -> LLM Processor -> Question Selector -> Presentation Layer -> Response Recorder -> Risk Scorer -> Discrepancy Analyzer -> Output

**Critical path**: The primary latency bottleneck lies in the LLM inference step, which must generate a contextually appropriate question in real time after each user response. The system sequentially processes: (1) gather current context (including alternative data), (2) invoke LLM to suggest next question, (3) present to user, (4) record response, (5) update risk model, and repeat until termination condition. Final scoring and fraud analysis occur after questionnaire completion.

**Design tradeoffs**: 
- *Accuracy vs. Brevity*: Fewer questions improve user experience but may sacrifice some predictive detail; the system balances by using alternative data to compensate.
- *Data richness vs. Privacy*: Leveraging social media and geolocation enhances modeling but raises privacy and bias concerns; the prototype does not address these.
- *Static simplicity vs. Adaptive complexity*: Adaptive systems are harder to develop, test, and explain to regulators but offer efficiency gains.

**Failure signatures**:
- LLM generates off-topic, repetitive, or confusing questions, leading to user dropout.
- Alternative data sources become unavailable (e.g., privacy restrictions), causing the system to fall back on less informative questioning.
- Discrepancy flags fire too frequently (low precision) or miss obvious fraud (low recall), undermining trust.
- High variance in question counts across users, indicating poorly tuned termination criteria.

**First experiments**:
1. Compare the average number of questions required by ARQuest versus a static questionnaire across both synthetic and real user cohorts.
2. Evaluate risk assessment accuracy by computing MAE between predicted and ground-truth risk scores for both approaches.
3. Conduct a user preference survey after the real-user study to measure acceptance and perceived engagement.

## Limitations
- Synthetic data reliance: 85 out of 95 test users were synthetic; real-world behavioral validity remains uncertain.
- Small real-user sample: Only 10 real users participated, limiting generalizability of preference and engagement results.
- Lack of rigorous statistical validation: No significance testing for the observed MAE differences, and the fraud detection component remains unvalidated against actual fraud cases.

## Confidence
- 50% fewer questions: Medium (reduction is plausible but synthetic scenario may understate real-world complexity).
- Slightly lower accuracy: Low (no statistical testing; MAE difference may be negligible).
- 70% user preference: Medium-High (real user data, but tiny sample risks overfitting).
- Fraud flagging utility: Low (theoretical concept without empirical evaluation).

## Next Checks
1. Run paired statistical tests (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank) on MAE differences between static and adaptive flows across a larger, diverse real-user cohort.
2. Evaluate cross-demographic robustness: Test adaptive flows on underrepresented groups and non-life insurance products (e.g., auto, property) to assess generalizability.
3. Benchmark fraud detection: Validate discrepancy flags against a labeled dataset of known fraudulent applications (e.g., historical underwriting fraud cases).
