# Extending Puzzle for Mixture-of-Experts Reasoning Models with Application to GPT-OSS Acceleration

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11937
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11937
- **Reference count:** 35
- **Primary result:** Extending Puzzle to gpt-oss-120B yields gpt-oss-puzzle-88B, achieving 1.63× (64K/64K) and 1.22× (4K/4K) throughput speedups on an 8×H100 node and up to 2.82× and 2.44× on a single H100, while matching or exceeding parent model accuracy across reasoning efforts (100.8–108.2% retention).

## Executive Summary
Reasoning large language models incur high serving costs due to long generation traces. This work extends Puzzle, a post‑training neural architecture search framework, to **gpt‑oss‑120B**, a mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) reasoning model, producing **gpt‑oss‑puzzle‑88B**. The approach combines heterogeneous MoE expert pruning (varying experts per layer), selective window attention (guided by long‑context scoring), FP8 KV‑cache quantization with calibrated scales, and reinforcement‑learning fine‑tuning. On an 8×H100 node, speedups reach 1.63× for 64K/64K and 1.22× for 4K/4K scenarios; on a single H100, speedups increase to 2.82× and 2.44× respectively. Accounting for tokens generated, request‑level efficiency improves by up to 1.29×. Accuracy across diverse reasoning benchmarks is preserved or enhanced, demonstrating that post‑training architecture search can substantially reduce inference cost without quality loss.

## Method Summary
The reproduction requires: (1) preparing the `LNPT-gpt-oss` dataset by generating high/medium reasoning‑effort responses from the parent model; (2) constructing a block library per layer with MoE variants (8–128 experts) and window‑attention variants; (3) scoring blocks via replace‑1‑block MSE for MoE/FFN and AA‑LCR benchmark for attention; (4) solving a mixed‑integer program (MIP) to select one block per layer under throughput constraints (≥1.6× for 64K/64K, ≥1.2× for 4K/4K on 8×H100); (5) training the student model with knowledge distillation (84B tokens, frozen MoE router/experts) and reinforcement learning (two policies merged); (6) applying FP8 KV‑cache quantization with per‑layer max‑calibrated scales. The critical path flows from block library creation through scoring, MIP optimization, distillation, RL, and finally quantization.

## Key Results
- Throughput speedups: 1.63× (64K/64K) and 1.22× (4K/4K) on 8×H100; up to 2.82× and 2.44× on single H100.
- Request‑level efficiency (throughput normalized by tokens) improves by up to 1.29×.
- Accuracy retention across reasoning benchmarks averages 100.8%–108.2% relative to the parent model.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Heterogeneous MoE expert pruning (per‑layer expert counts) preserves accuracy while reducing compute and memory because the contribution of MoE layers to final output is not uniform across network depth.
- **Mechanism:** Experts are ranked by their contribution to layer output (via removal MSE on a validation set). Puzzle’s replace‑1‑block scoring estimates full‑architecture quality as the sum of per‑layer scores for chosen variants. A mixed‑integer program selects a pruned expert count for each layer under throughput constraints, producing a non‑uniform architecture that avoids over‑pruning highly influential early layers.
- **Core assumption:** The sum of per‑layer, single‑block replacement scores is a good predictor of the full‑architecture’s quality loss when that block is swapped.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “heterogeneous MoE expert pruning (per‑layer expert counts)”; [section 2] “our approach combines heterogeneous MoE expert pruning… For each MoE layer, we first rank the experts by their contribution…”; [section 2, Figure 8] shows layerwise agreement between different scoring methods.
- **Break condition:** If replace‑1 scoring fails to correlate with full‑architecture performance for heavily pruned MoE blocks (e.g., due to router sensitivity), the MIP may select dangerously low expert counts for critical layers, causing drops in specific capabilities (e.g., mathematical reasoning).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Selective replacement of full‑context attention with window attention (guided by a long‑context reasoning benchmark score) preserves long‑range dependencies while drastically reducing KV‑cache memory and bandwidth, because only a subset of layers are critical for global context.
- **Mechanism:** A candidate block library includes window‑attention variants (e.g., 8192‑token window) for each original global‑attention layer. Each candidate is scored by its impact on the Artificial Analysis Long‑Context Reasoning (AA‑LCR) benchmark. The MIP then selects which global layers to convert to window based on this long‑context‑aware score, under a constraint on total full‑attention layers.
- **Core assumption:** The AA‑LCR benchmark score serves as a reliable proxy for the model’s long‑context reasoning ability, and degradation from a single‑layer window replacement predicts full‑model long‑context capability loss.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “selective window attention (guided by long‑context scoring)”; [section 2] “To determine which global attention layers are the most suitable to be transformed into window attention… we instead measured accuracy scores on the Artificial Analysis Long‑Context Reasoning benchmark (AA‑LCR) for each block replacement.”; [section 2, Figure 8] shows activation‑based scores have a locality bias.
- **Break condition:** If AA‑LCR does not fully capture the long‑context dependencies used in deployment (e.g., retrieval‑heavy vs. reasoning‑heavy tasks), critical global layers may be incorrectly pruned, harming performance on specific long‑context benchmarks.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** FP8 KV‑cache quantization with per‑layer max‑calibrated scales reduces KV‑cache memory footprint and bandwidth by ~2×, accelerating attention in long contexts without significant accuracy loss, because the scales adapt to the dynamic range of cached activations.
- **Mechanism:** During a calibration pass over the training dataset (`LNPT-gpt-oss`), the maximum absolute value of K and V tensors per layer is recorded to compute a per‑layer FP8 scaling factor. These scales are applied during inference to quantize KV cache to FP8 and dequantize for matmul. Rounding scales to powers of 2 mitigates underflow.
- **Core assumption:** The maximum activation value observed during calibration is a safe upper bound for the dynamic range during inference, preventing overflow/underflow that would degrade attention outputs.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “FP8 KV‑cache quantization with calibrated scales”; [section 3] “We first tried the common practice of not using KV scales. However, this resulted in subpar accuracy. Therefore, we computed KV scales based on our LNPT‑gpt‑oss dataset using max calibration, resulting in better accuracy.”; [section 4.2.1/Table 4] shows KV scales improve accuracy vs. no scales.
- **Break condition:** If the calibration data (`LNPT-gpt-oss`) is not representative of deployment data, certain layers may experience activation values exceeding the calibrated max, causing overflow and severe localized degradation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Replace‑1‑Block Scoring**
  - *Why needed here:* This is the core quality estimation primitive in Puzzle, allowing scoring thousands of layer variants in isolation and making NAS tractable without full‑model retraining for each candidate. Understanding its limitations (e.g., locality bias for attention) is key to extending Puzzle.
  - *Quick check question:* Why would a replace‑1 activation MSE score fail to predict the quality impact of switching a layer to window attention for a long‑context QA task?
- **Concept: Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) Routing and Expert Parallelism**
  - *Why needed here:* Pruning MoE layers must respect the router’s learned expert selection and the expert‑parallel deployment schema. Heterogeneous pruning requires understanding how router load and expert utilization scale with expert count.
  - *Quick check question:* If you halve the number of experts in an MoE layer without adjusting the router, what happens to the load balance and the effective capacity of that layer?
- **Concept: KV‑Cache Dynamics in Long‑Context Autoregressive Decoding**
  - *Why needed here:* The primary cost driver for 64K/64K scenarios is KV‑cache memory bandwidth and capacity. Window attention and quantization directly attack this. One must understand the trade‑off: bounded cache vs. lost long‑range dependencies.
  - *Quick check question:* In a 64K context, what are the two dominant hardware costs for a standard transformer decoder layer during the *decode* phase (generating one token)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** 
  Parent Model (gpt-oss-120B) → Block Library Generator → Scorer (replace‑1 MSE for MoE/FFN, AA‑LCR for attention) → MIP Solver → Knowledge Distillation (84B tokens, frozen router/experts) → Reinforcement Learning (two policies merged) → Quantization (FP8 KV‑cache) → Final Model (gpt-oss-puzzle-88B)
- **Critical path:** Block library creation → Layerwise scoring → MIP solve → Distillation → RL → Quantization. The MIP selection is the irreversible architectural decision; subsequent training (KD, RL) attempts to heal quality loss from the chosen compressed blocks.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Heterogeneous vs. uniform pruning: Heterogeneous preserves more accuracy but complicates MIP and deployment mapping.
  - Scoring signal: Activation‑MSE is general but locally biased; AA‑LCR is task‑specific but better for long‑context attention selection.
  - RL data mixture: High‑effort RL maximizes accuracy but inflates token count; balanced RL controls length but may underfit; merging checkpoints is a heuristic compromise.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. Catastrophic drop on a specific benchmark (e.g., GPQA‑Diamond, AIME25) → Likely over‑pruned early MoE layers or incorrectly converted a critical global attention layer.
  2. Generation length runs away (effort length ratio >> parent) → RL stage trained too long on high‑effort data without proper length regularization.
  3. Single‑GPU max batch does not increase → Memory savings from pruning/window attention insufficient; MoE pruning too shallow or window attention not applied enough.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replace‑1 Scoring Sanity Check:** For a random layer, compare parent vs. replace‑1 variant on a small validation set. MSE should be low; for attention, compute AA‑LCR drop for window replacement.
  2. **MIP Solution Inspection:** After solving, plot the selected architecture. Verify earlier layers have higher expert counts and only a subset of global layers become window.
  3. **Post‑Distillation Recovery:** After KD, re‑evaluate on a held‑out set. Accuracy should improve vs. pre‑KD model.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can reinforcement learning for reasoning models simultaneously improve accuracy and preserve generation length controllability across reasoning efforts?
  - *Basis in paper:* The authors identify a central tension: RL on high‑effort data improves accuracy but increases generation length and breaks effort controllability, while balanced mixing regularizes length but underperforms in accuracy. They resort to checkpoint averaging, which restores controllability only approximately.
  - *Why unresolved:* No principled method exists to directly optimize for both objectives during RL; the trade‑off remains poorly understood.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Experiments with constrained RL objectives or length regularization penalties, measuring effort length ratio and accuracy across multiple runs.

- **Open Question 2:** What scoring function best predicts the impact of converting attention layers to window attention on long‑context reasoning performance?
  - *Basis in paper:* The authors state that standard replace‑1‑block scores have a locality bias and therefore use AA‑LCR to score window‑attention candidates. They do not claim AA‑LCR is optimal.
  - *Why unresolved:* It is unclear whether AA‑LCR is the most reliable or generalizable proxy; other long‑context signals might correlate better with final accuracy.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Compare multiple scoring metrics (e.g., synthetic retrieval tasks, other benchmarks) against actual performance after layer replacement, measuring correlation.

- **Open Question 3:** To what extent are Puzzle’s architectural decisions (expert counts per layer, window attention selection) transferable across different hardware platforms and context length distributions?
  - *Basis in paper:* The optimization targets specific deployment scenarios (8×H100 node, 4K/4K and 64K/64K). Results on B200 and single GPU are reported, but the selected architecture is fixed after one Puzzle run.
  - *Why unresolved:* It is unknown whether the same architecture remains optimal under different memory/bandwidth constraints or predominant context lengths; re‑running Puzzle for each scenario is costly.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Run Puzzle with varied hardware profiles and context‑length distributions, then compare the selected architectures and their efficiency/accuracy trade‑offs.

- **Open Question 4:** Does the linear “replace‑1‑block” additivity assumption hold when combining heterogeneous modifications (MoE pruning, window attention, quantization) in a single model?
  - *Basis in paper:* Puzzle estimates full‑architecture quality as the sum of per‑block replace‑1 scores. This assumption is untested for the combined modifications studied here.
  - *Why unresolved:* Interactions between different types of blocks (e.g., pruning an MoE layer might change the impact of window attention elsewhere) could violate additivity, leading to suboptimal selections.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* For a sample of architectures chosen by Puzzle, directly evaluate their accuracy and compare with the predicted sum; analyze residuals for systematic interaction effects.

## Limitations
- **Scoring assumptions:** Replace‑1‑block additivity may fail for heavily pruned MoE layers due to complex expert interactions or router sensitivity, risking over‑pruning of critical layers.
- **Benchmark reliance:** AA‑LCR‑guided window attention selection assumes the benchmark fully represents deployment long‑context patterns; mismatch may harm specific tasks (e.g., RULER 128K).
- **Calibration coverage:** FP8 KV‑cache scales derived from `LNPT-gpt-oss` may not cover all deployment data distributions, risking overflow/underflow in out‑of‑distribution contexts.
