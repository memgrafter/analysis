# Learning When to Act or Refuse: Guarding Agentic Reasoning Models for Safe Multi-Step Tool Use
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03205
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03205
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** MOSAIC reduces harmful behavior by up to 50% (Qwen2.5), increases harmful-task refusal by over 20% on injection attacks, cuts privacy leakage by up to 23%, and preserves or improves benign task performance (e.g., Qwen3 completion nearly doubles to 85%).

## Executive Summary
Agentic language models that orchestrate tools across multiple steps can cause irreversible harm when misused, yet existing alignment strategies overlook sequential decision‑making and adversarial feedback. This paper presents MOSAIC, a post‑training framework that imposes a structured “plan, check, then act or refuse” loop, elevating safety reasoning and explicit refusal to first‑class actions. Training employs preference‑based reinforcement learning with pairwise trajectory comparisons, capturing fine‑grained temporal distinctions (e.g., early refusal vs. late abort). Evaluated on Qwen2.5‑7B, Qwen3‑4B‑Thinking, and Phi‑4 across harmful, injection, and privacy benchmarks, MOSAIC achieves substantial safety gains—up to 50% reduction in harm, >20% higher refusal under injection, up to 23% less privacy leakage—while maintaining or improving benign utility (e.g., Qwen3 completion approaches 85%). Ablations confirm the necessity of explicit safety checks and pairwise rewards; token overhead remains low (<20%). Open‑weight models trained with MOSAIC even surpass unscaffolded GPT‑4o and GPT‑5 on agentic safety, underscoring the power of explicit safety scaffolding over scale alone.

## Method Summary
MOSAIC post‑trains a base LLM to follow a predetermined protocol: first output a plan, then optionally emit a structured safety assessment within `<safety_thoughts>`, and finally select an action—either a tool call, the terminal `refuse_unsafe_task` action with justification, or a final `<answer>`. The decision to invoke a safety check is a discrete, learned gate `g_t`. Training uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) where, for each prompt, a group of trajectories is generated and an LLM judge provides pairwise preferences. These preferences are aggregated into a group‑relative alignment reward `r_align`. The total reward combines `r_align`, a format reward `r_fmt` (ensuring syntactically valid outputs), and a token length penalty `p_len` to encourage efficiency. This objective encourages the policy to perform safety reasoning only when necessary, keeping overhead low while learning nuanced temporal safety trade‑offs. The method was applied to Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct, Qwen3‑4B‑Thinking‑2507, and Phi‑4 using the Agent‑SafetyBench training suite.

## Key Results
- Harmful behavior reduced by up to 50% (Qwen2.5) on AgentHarm; harmful‑task refusal increased by >20% under prompt injection (ASB).
- Privacy leakage cut by up to 23% (PrivacyLens).
- Benign task completion improved or preserved; Qwen3 completion on BFCL nearly doubles to 85%.
- MOSAIC‑trained open models outperform unscaffolded GPT‑4o and GPT‑5 on agentic safety benchmarks.
- Explicit safety reasoning adds less than 20% token overhead; Qwen3‑4B‑Thinking achieves a 4× reduction in total tokens compared to a naïve always‑on check baseline.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Explicit Plan‑Check‑Act/Refuse Loop**  
MOSAIC structures inference into three steps: (1) generate a high‑level plan, (2) optionally produce a structured safety assessment (`<safety_thoughts>`), and (3) choose an action (tool call, `refuse_unsafe_task`, or answer). Refusal is a terminal action with a justification field, making it a learnable policy choice. By separating safety deliberation from general reasoning, the model can allocate computation selectively and learns a discrete gate `g_t` that controls whether a safety check is performed at a given turn. Ablation (removing the explicit safety block) degrades safety and injection robustness, confirming that explicit safety scaffolding is essential.

**Mechanism 2: Pairwise Preference Learning**  
Instead of a scalar reward that collapses a trajectory’s temporal dynamics into a single score, MOSAIC uses pairwise comparisons among a group of rollouts. An LLM judge orders each pair, preserving distinctions such as “early refusal” versus “late abort.” These preferences are converted into a group‑relative reward via GRPO. The pairwise signal provides a stable learning gradient that captures the timing of unsafe actions, which pointwise scalar rewards fail to encode. Ablations show that replacing pairwise with pointwise rewards raises harm scores and lowers refusal rates.

**Mechanism 3: Learned Token‑Efficient Gating**  
Because emitting `<safety_thoughts>` adds tokens, the policy is incentivized to use it only on high‑risk turns. The composite reward includes a length penalty `p_len`, driving the model to discover a gating policy that activates safety reasoning selectively. Empirically, MOSAIC keeps safety‑related tokens below 20% of the total, and for Qwen3 it reduces total tokens by more than 4× compared to a constant‑check baseline. This shows that token‑efficient safety is achievable when the model learns *when* to reason about safety rather than always doing so.

## Foundational Learning
- **Sequential Decision‑Making in Agentic Systems**  
  *Why needed here:* Multi‑step tool use creates cascading risks; a single early mistake can lead to irreversible harm. Single‑turn safety filters cannot intervene between steps.  
  *Quick check question:* Why can't a safety filter applied only to the final output prevent harm from a misstep in the middle of a tool sequence?

- **Preference Learning vs. Scalar Reward Modeling**  
  *Why needed here:* MOSAIC relies on pairwise trajectory preferences to preserve temporal information about when unsafe actions occur. Scalar rewards would merge such distinctions.  
  *Quick check question:* How might a scalar reward model treat two trajectories—one that refuses immediately on a harmful task, and another that executes one unsafe tool call then aborts—if both end with “refusal”?

- **Structured Outputs and Tool‑Use Interfaces**  
  *Why needed here:* The agent must emit well‑formed tags (e.g., `<think>`, `<safety_thoughts>`, `<tool call>`) to interact with tools. Format errors break execution.  
  *Quick check question:* What happens if the model places a `<tool call>` outside of the designated tag block or fails to close a `<think>` tag?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Base LLM → GRPO Trainer → LLM Judge → Tool Environment Simulator → Reward Aggregator → Gate Controller (implicit)

**Critical path:**  
1. Prompt is fed to the policy (Base LLM).  
2. Policy generates a plan, optionally a `<safety_thoughts>` block based on the learned gate `g_t`, and then an action.  
3. The Tool Environment Simulator executes the action (tool call) or observes a terminal refusal and returns an observation.  
4. Steps 2‑3 repeat until termination (refusal, answer, or maximum steps).  
5. For each prompt, a group of trajectories (size n=4) is collected.  
6. The LLM Judge compares all trajectory pairs, producing a preference matrix.  
7. The Reward Aggregator computes group‑relative `r_align`, adds `r_fmt` (syntax correctness) and subtracts `p_len` (token count penalty).  
8. GRPO updates the policy using the total reward.  
9. Iterate from step 1.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Explicit safety tokens vs. token efficiency:* Adding `<safety_thoughts>` increases per‑turn cost but improves safety; the length penalty and learned gating balance this trade‑off.  
- *Pairwise vs. pointwise rewards:* Pairwise captures temporal nuances but requires O(n²) judge calls; pointwise is cheaper but leads to inferior safety calibration.  
- *Frontier vs. open‑weight models:* Smaller open models gain larger relative safety improvements; already‑conservative models (e.g., Phi‑4 base) may see small regressions or reduced over‑refusal.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Over‑refusal:** `refuse_unsafe_task` invoked on benign tasks → high benign refusal rate, low completion on BFCL.  
- **Under‑refusal / unsafe execution:** Model proceeds with harmful actions, especially after prompt injection → high attack success rate (ASR) on ASB and high Harm Score on AgentHarm.  
- **Verbose loops:** Excessive generation of `<think>` or `<safety_thoughts>` without acting → high average response length and slow benign completion, particularly on reason‑heavy models (Qwen3 base).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Zero‑shot safety evaluation:** Run base and MOSAIC‑trained models on AgentHarm, ASB, PrivacyLens, and BFCL to establish the main performance table, verifying harm reduction, refusal gains, privacy improvement, and benign utility preservation.  
2. **Ablation of explicit safety checks:** Train a variant without `<safety_thoughts>` (only `<think>`) and compare to the full MOSAIC model. Expect degraded safety and injection robustness, confirming the contribution of the explicit safety step.  
3. **Token efficiency analysis:** Measure average per‑turn token counts for base vs. MOSAIC models, breaking down safety vs. reasoning tokens. Validate that safety overhead stays below 20% and total token usage often decreases (as seen with Qwen3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalizability to other agent architectures**  
  *Basis:* MOSAIC is built around a specific plan‑check‑act/refuse loop and evaluated only on models that adopt it.  
  *Why unresolved:* Agent frameworks vary (hierarchical, reactive); it is unclear if gains depend on this exact structure.  
  *Needed evidence:* Implement MOSAIC components in alternative agent frameworks and measure safety‑utility across diverse tasks.

- **Impact of LLM‑judge biases**  
  *Basis:* Training relies on an LLM judge; the paper reports improving agreement but does not analyze how judge mistakes affect the policy.  
  *Why unresolved:* LLM judges exhibit positional and other biases; if these propagate, MOSAIC may learn misaligned preferences.  
  *Needed evidence:* Inject controlled errors into judge comparisons or use multiple heterogeneous judges to assess policy robustness and consistency.

- **Reliability of learned safety gating under distribution shift**  
  *Basis:* Gating is learned from reward feedback on training data; the paper does not test OOD tools or environments.  
  *Why unresolved:* Gating decisions may fail to trigger checks for unseen risks, leading to unsafe actions or over‑refusal.  
  *Needed evidence:* Evaluate gating accuracy on agents with previously unseen tool categories or in environments with altered risk profiles, correlating with safety outcomes.

- **Transfer to high‑stakes domains**  
  *Basis:* Evaluations cover general agentic benchmarks but not regulated domains like healthcare or finance.  
  *Why unresolved:* Domain‑specific safety norms and tool risks may require different refusal thresholds; MOSAIC could under‑ or over‑refuse.  
  *Needed evidence:* Deploy MOSAIC‑trained agents in simulated high‑stakes environments (e.g., medical device control, trading systems) with domain‑tailored metrics.

- **Earlier integration into model development**  
  *Basis:* MOSAIC is applied as a post‑training stage; earlier integration was not compared.  
  *Why unresolved:* Post‑training may not fully internalize safety reasoning; earlier exposure could shape priors and reduce fine‑tuning reliance.  
  *Needed evidence:* Ablations comparing pre‑training with safety curricula, SFT on synthetic safety trajectories, and post‑RL, using identical architectures and measuring learning curves and OOD generalization.

## Limitations
- **Unknown judge configuration:** Exact prompt templates, few‑shot examples, and ordering randomization used in the LLM judge are not fully specified, risking reproducibility of the reward signal.
- **Opaque gating mechanism:** The precise implementation of the discrete gate `g_t` (e.g., special token prediction, logit head, gradient path) is unclear, hindering verification and analysis.
- **Missing tool‑response masking details:** How tool‑output tokens are excluded from policy gradients in `verl` is not described, which may affect training stability.
- **Data provenance ambiguity:** The exact version and train/eval splits of *Agent‑SafetyBench* used are not provided, raising potential leakage concerns.

## Confidence
- **Explicit safety loop enables controllable tool use:** Medium (ablation supports benefit, but gate mechanism is opaque).  
- **Pairwise preferences capture temporal safety distinctions:** Medium‑High (pointwise ablation degrades performance, though reliance on a single judge introduces bias).  
- **Learned gating yields token efficiency:** Medium‑High (empirical token reduction is strong, but sensitivity to length‑penalty hyperparameters remains under‑explored).

## Next Checks
1. **Judge signal fidelity:** Collect pairwise preferences from multiple diverse judges, compute inter‑judge agreement, and correlate noise with final attack success rates to assess robustness of the reward oracle.
2. **Gate introspection:** Log per‑turn `g_t` activations on a risk‑stratified sample to verify selective safety reasoning and diagnose patterns of over‑ or under‑refusal.
3. **Data provenance audit:** Acquire the exact *Agent‑SafetyBench* tool specifications and splits, re‑run data preparation, and ensure no train/eval leakage for reproducibility.
