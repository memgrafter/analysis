# How Do Lexical Senses Correspond Between Spoken German and German Sign Language?
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13790
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13790
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: Semantic Similarity (SS) with SBERT embeddings achieves 88.52% accuracy in identifying sense correspondences between German and DGS, substantially outperforming Exact Match (71.31%), especially for one-to-many mappings.

## Executive Summary
This study addresses the underrepresentation of polysemous and homonymous mappings in sign language bilingual dictionaries by analyzing sense correspondence between spoken German and German Sign Language (DGS). Researchers manually annotated 1,404 word use-to-sign mappings from 32 German words and 49 DGS signs, identifying three correspondence types: one-to-many (Type 1), many-to-one (Type 2), and one-to-one (Type 3). Computational evaluation compared Exact Match (EM) and Semantic Similarity (SS) using SBERT embeddings. SS outperformed EM overall (88.52% vs. 71.31% accuracy) and showed a dramatic improvement for one-to-many correspondences (+52.1 percentage points). However, both methods struggled with many-to-one cases, and SS performance collapsed without vocabulary overlap, indicating reliance on lexical memorization rather than cross-modal semantics.

## Method Summary
The study uses a manually annotated dataset of 1,404 mappings linking German word uses (from D-WUG) to DGS sign IDs (from DW-DGS), split into training (723), validation (100), test_overlap (150), and test_no_overlap (431). Two retrieval methods are evaluated: Exact Match (EM) searches for lexical overlap between the German usage text and the sign's German translation/definition; Semantic Similarity (SS) computes cosine similarity of SBERT embeddings for the same texts. For a given word, a set of candidate signs is retrieved using top-k (k∈{3,5,7}) and a similarity threshold τ (τ∈{0.65,0.70,0.75,0.80}). The correspondence type is inferred from the number of candidates and their scores: multiple candidates → Type 1; one candidate with similarity < τ → Type 2; one candidate with similarity ≥ τ → Type 3; no candidates → No Match. Hyperparameters are tuned on validation; best SS uses model `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` with τ=0.70 and k=3.

## Key Results
- Semantic Similarity (SS) achieves 88.52% overall accuracy on the overlap test set, versus 71.31% for Exact Match (EM).
- For one-to-many correspondences (Type 1), SS reaches 93.8% accuracy, a 52.1 percentage point gain over EM (41.7%).
- Both methods fail at many-to-one correspondences (Type 2), with 0% agreement in the confusion matrix; EM performs perfectly on one-to-one cases (Type 3, 100.0%).
- SS performance drops catastrophically to 17.59% overall accuracy on the no-overlap test set, revealing heavy dependence on lexical overlap.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: SS for One-to-Many Mappings
Claim: SBERT-based Semantic Similarity successfully identifies one-to-many correspondences by clustering semantically similar word usages with appropriate sign candidates. Mechanism: SBERT generates contextual embeddings for each word use and sign entry (translation + definition); cosine similarity captures semantic relatedness, allowing disambiguation of polysemous/homonymous words. Evidence: SS accuracy for Type 1 is 93.8% vs. EM's 41.7% (Table 5). Break condition: Without vocabulary overlap, SS collapses (Table 10), indicating reliance on memorized lexical items.

### Mechanism 2: EM for One-to-One Mappings
Claim: Exact Match excels at one-to-one correspondences where the sign's conventional translation contains the exact word form. Mechanism: EM retrieves signs by lexical string overlap; for unambiguous senses, the correct sign's translation field typically includes the headword, making EM highly precise. Evidence: EM achieves 100.0% accuracy on Type 3 (Table 5) and better ranking quality (P@3: 91.8% vs. SS's 88.5%, Table 7). Break condition: Fails for ambiguous words (Type 1) or signs (Type 2) where lexical match is absent or multiple.

### Mechanism 3: Failure on Many-to-One Mappings
Claim: Both EM and SS cannot correctly identify many-to-one correspondences because the dataset lacks fine-grained sign sense annotations. Mechanism: DW-DGS entries provide a single sign ID with multiple German translations, but no partitioning of the sign's own contextual usages. Without this, methods cannot determine that distinct word senses map to distinct senses of the same sign. Evidence: Confusion matrix shows 0% agreement for all 5 Type 2 test items (Table 14); both methods achieve only 66.7% accuracy on Type 2 (Table 5). Break condition: Inherent to the task formulation; any Type 2 case will be misclassified as No Match or Type 3.

## Foundational Learning
- **Lexical Ambiguity (Polysemy & Homonymy):** Understanding that a single word form can have multiple related or unrelated senses is core to defining the correspondence types. Quick check: If "Bank" (bench vs. bank) maps to two unrelated DGS signs, that is Type 1 (one-to-many).
- **Cross-Modal Semantics:** Measuring semantic equivalence between spoken language text and sign language descriptions is non-trivial due to differences in modality (e.g., spatial grammar in DGS). Quick check: Why might a direct translation of "Mauer" (wall) miss the iconic "barrier" aspect of a DGS sign? Because the sign's meaning may be broader or shaped by visual-gestural features.
- **SBERT Embeddings for Semantic Similarity:** The method relies on the hypothesis that semantically similar texts have similar vector representations. Quick check: What is a risk of using multilingual SBERT for German-DGS? The model sees only German text descriptions of DGS signs, with no knowledge of sign language semantics or iconicity.

## Architecture Onboarding

Critical path: Data preparation (matching words to signs) -> Human annotation -> Candidate generation (EM/SS) -> Hyperparameter tuning on validation -> Evaluation on test splits (overlap/no-overlap) -> Ablation and error analysis.

Design tradeoffs: EM is precise for Type 3 but fails for Type 1; SS handles Type 1 but overgeneralizes for Type 3 and fails for Type 2; both suffer from vocabulary dependency; ablation shows "Word Only" outperforms "Full Context" for SS.

## Limitations
- Small dataset: Only 32 headwords and 1,404 mappings limit generalizability.
- Inherent task limitation: Type 2 cannot be solved without finer-grained sign sense annotations in DW-DGS.
- Memorization over semantics: SS's collapse on no-overlap suggests reliance on surface lexical overlap rather than cross-modal understanding.
- Modality blindness: SBERT processes only German text, ignoring visual-gestural aspects of DGS.

## Confidence
- SS outperforms EM for Type 1: High (clear result, sufficient n=48)
- EM achieves perfect accuracy for Type 3: High (absolute 100.0%, n=27)
- Both fail at Type 2: High (0% agreement, systematic)
- SS collapses without vocabulary overlap: High (drastic drop to 17.59%)
- SBERT captures general cross-modal semantics: Low (contradicted by no-overlap collapse)
- Findings generalize to other sign languages: Low (specific to DGS and German)
