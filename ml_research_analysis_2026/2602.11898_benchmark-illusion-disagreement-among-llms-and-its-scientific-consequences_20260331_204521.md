# Benchmark Illusion: Disagreement among LLMs and Its Scientific Consequences
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11898
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11898
- Reference count: 8
- Primary result: LLM benchmark accuracy masks substantial item-level disagreement, creating a "benchmark illusion" that can drastically alter scientific inferences.

## Executive Summary
The paper reveals that high accuracy on popular LLM benchmarks like MMLU-Pro and GPQA does not guarantee that top-performing models will agree on individual item responses. Even models with >60% accuracy disagree on 16–38% of items, a phenomenon termed the "benchmark illusion." This hidden disagreement becomes critical when LLMs are used as annotators in scientific research. Re-analyses of two published studies—one in education and one in political science—show that substituting different LLMs for human annotation changes estimated treatment effects by up to 84% and can even reverse effect signs. A simulation further demonstrates that systematic errors from high-accuracy annotators induce larger biases than random errors from less accurate ones. Consequently, the choice of LLM for annotation is a hidden but consequential variable that threatens reproducibility in science.

## Method Summary
The authors first measured pairwise disagreement among eight state-of-the-art LLMs on two challenging multiple-choice benchmarks: MMLU-Pro (12,032 questions) and GPQA (448 questions). Using model predictions from the official MMLU-Pro repository and the HELM benchmark suite, they computed the proportion of items on which any two models with >60% accuracy differed. To assess downstream impact, they re-analyzed two prior studies that relied on human annotations. For Kim et al. (2021), which evaluated an educational intervention on student essay scores, they replaced the original human ratings with scores generated by eight LLMs using the study's rubric prompt. They then re-ran the hierarchical linear model to estimate the treatment effect. For Rozenas and Stukal (2019), which examined news media attribution, they used LLMs to classify news fragments and recomputed the relative attribution measure. Finally, they conducted a simulation with synthetic annotators having controlled accuracy and error profiles (systematic vs. random) to compare biases in estimated treatment effects. All LLM inference used fixed prompts and greedy decoding (temperature=0) via vLLM.

## Key Results
- Top-performing models (accuracy >60%) still disagree on 16–38% of MMLU-Pro items and 17–32% of GPQA items.
- In an education trial, LLM annotation choice changed treatment effect estimates by 84%, ranging from 0.19–0.35 compared to the original human-coded 0.44.
- In a political science study, some LLMs reversed the sign of the key effect for “Russian officials” attribution (some positive, some negative).
- Simulation: Systematic errors from high-accuracy annotators cause larger biases than random errors from less accurate ones.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Benchmark accuracy aggregates item-level correctness but fails to capture whether models err on the same instances. Two models can both exceed 60% accuracy yet diverge on a substantial fraction of items because they succeed on different subsets and make distinct types of errors. This discrepancy arises from systematic differences in how models internalize knowledge, perform reasoning, and handle ambiguous or under-specified questions. When such models are deployed as annotators, the choice of model directly influences the outcome variable because each model's error pattern is unique. Consequently, a study's findings become contingent on which LLM was used, violating the principle that scientific measurements should be independent of the specific measuring instrument. The "benchmark illusion" is the mistaken belief that a high aggregate score implies reliable and interchangeable annotations.

## Foundational Learning
- **LLM Benchmark Accuracy**
  - *Why needed*: Standard metric for evaluating model performance, but aggregates over items and masks error distribution.
  - *Quick check*: Compute the proportion of correct answers on a held-out test set.
- **Pairwise Disagreement**
  - *Why needed*: Directly measures whether models produce the same responses on individual items; central to identifying the benchmark illusion.
  - *Quick check*: For two models, divide the number of items with differing predictions by the total number of items.
- **Measurement Error**
  - *Why needed*: LLM annotations deviate from true human labels, introducing error that can bias statistical estimates.
  - *Quick check*: Calculate the correlation (e.g., Pearson’s r) between LLM scores and human scores on a validation sample.
- **Systematic vs. Random Error**
  - *Why needed*: The paper’s simulation indicates systematic errors from high-accuracy annotators are more damaging; distinguishing error types is key to understanding bias mechanisms.
  - *Quick check*: Inspect a confusion matrix for consistent patterns (e.g., a model always over-scoring one group) versus evenly dispersed mistakes.
- **Benchmark Illusion**
  - *Why needed*: The core concept that high accuracy erroneously suggests annotation reliability; it connects the empirical findings to broader implications for scientific practice.
  - *Quick check*: Plot a model’s accuracy against its average disagreement with other high-accuracy models; a positive relationship or high disagreement despite high accuracy signals the illusion.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
The architecture comprises two parallel pipelines for the empirical analyses and one for simulation.  
Pipeline 1 (Benchmark analysis): MMLU-Pro or GPQA dataset → LLM inference (using specific model checkpoints) → Prediction files → Pairwise disagreement computation module → Disagreement statistics.  
Pipeline 2 (Case studies): Original study datasets (essays or news fragments) → LLM annotation with fixed rubric prompt → Generated outcome variable → Original statistical model (e.g., hierarchical linear model) → Estimated treatment effect.  
Simulation pipeline: Synthetic data generation (true scores + treatment) → Synthetic annotator module (simulated LLM with specified accuracy and error type) → Noisy outcome → Statistical model → Bias measurement.

**Critical Path**  
The main claim hinges on successfully reproducing (a) the pairwise disagreement rates from the benchmark analysis, and (b) the variation in treatment effects across LLMs in the case studies. Therefore, critical path includes: obtaining the exact model predictions for the benchmarks; computing and verifying disagreement ranges; accessing the original study datasets; generating LLM annotations that align with the described rubric; re-running the original statistical models correctly. The simulation can be run independently but must implement the same error structures to compare bias magnitudes.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Using precomputed predictions vs. re-running inference ensures comparability but limits control over model versions and decoding parameters.  
- Fixed greedy decoding ensures deterministic outputs, aiding reproducibility, but may not reflect real-world usage where sampling is common; could underestimate variance due to stochasticity.  
- Selecting eight diverse models for case studies captures error profile variation but increases computational cost and complexity.  
- Adhering to original rubrics without modification is necessary for fair comparison, yet any deviation from human interpretation (e.g., due to prompt phrasing) could affect results.  
- Focus on text-based tasks limits generalizability to other modalities, an acknowledged open question.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Accuracy mismatch: Model accuracy in downloaded predictions differs significantly from values reported in source papers.  
- Disagreement out of range: Pairwise disagreement rates fall outside the reported 16–38% (MMLU-Pro) or 17–32% (GPQA) intervals.  
- Low annotation-human correlation: LLM scores correlate poorly (e.g., r < 0.5) with original human labels.  
- Inability to recover original estimates: Replicating the original analysis with human data does not yield the published effect size.  
- Simulation contradiction: Results show random errors cause larger bias than systematic ones at equal accuracy, opposing the paper’s conclusion.

**First Experiments**  
1. **Benchmark prediction verification**: Download official MMLU-Pro prediction files and HELM GPQA results; compute accuracy for each model and compare to source tables; compute pairwise disagreement among models with >60% accuracy; verify disagreement ranges (MMLU-Pro: 16–38%, GPQA: 17–32%).  
2. **Annotation fidelity check**: For Kim et al., run the eight LLMs with the exact rubric prompt on a sample of essays; compute correlation of each LLM’s scores with human scores; ensure values are comparable to reported correlations (e.g., ~0.73 for GPT-oss-120B).  
3. **Partial treatment effect replication**: Using the LLM annotations from step 2 (or a larger set), re-run the hierarchical linear model for the social studies writing outcome; confirm the range of treatment effects across models (0.19–0.35) and that the human-coded estimate (0.44) is notably higher.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Evolution of disagreement over time**  
   - *Question*: How does benchmark disagreement and its downstream consequences on scientific inference evolve as LLMs improve over time and across model families?  
   - *Basis*: "Future work should investigate how benchmark disagreement and its downstream consequences evolve as models change."  
   - *Why unresolved*: The study captures a single snapshot of model capabilities; it is unknown whether newer or more advanced models exhibit reduced or shifted disagreement patterns.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: Longitudinal re-analyses of the same benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA) and case studies using successive generations of frontier models (e.g., GPT-4 to GPT-4o, Claude 3 to 3.5) to track changes in pairwise disagreement rates and variability of estimated treatment effects.

2. **Generalization beyond text annotation**  
   - *Question*: Do the patterns of substantial model disagreement and severe downstream bias generalize beyond text annotation tasks to other modalities (e.g., image, audio, video) or structured data prediction in scientific contexts?  
   - *Basis*: "Whether similar patterns arise in domains beyond those we consider here, including tasks that go beyond text annotation."  
   - *Why unresolved*: The paper focuses exclusively on multiple-choice QA benchmarks and text annotation applications; it is unclear if similar dynamics occur for perceptual tasks or other common scientific AI applications.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: Parallel analyses using established benchmarks in vision (e.g., ImageNet variants), audio (e.g., speech command recognition), or tabular data (e.g., UCI datasets), measuring pairwise disagreement among top-performing models, followed by re-analyses of published studies using LLMs or vision models for annotation in those domains.

3. **When to suppress, correct, or harness disagreement**  
   - *Question*: Under what specific research conditions should LLM disagreement be deliberately suppressed (e.g., via ensemble consensus), statistically corrected (e.g., via measurement error models), or strategically harnessed (e.g., to quantify uncertainty or surface alternative hypotheses) in scientific workflows?  
   - *Basis*: "Exploring when disagreement should be suppressed, corrected, or harnessed remains an open question."  
   - *Why unresolved*: The paper demonstrates disagreement is consequential but provides no framework for when each approach is appropriate; the optimal strategy likely depends on task type, error structure, cost constraints, and research goals.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: Pre-registered, multi-lab studies applying different disagreement-handling strategies (e.g., majority vote, model-based error adjustment, preserving disagreement as a variance measure) across a diverse set of annotation tasks, measuring bias in estimated effects, coverage of confidence intervals, and ability to detect genuine heterogeneity compared against human gold standards.

4. **Sources of distinct error profiles**  
   - *Question*: What underlying architectural, training, or alignment factors (e.g., pre-training data composition, fine-tuning objectives, decoding strategies) systematically explain the distinct error profiles—and thus the pairwise disagreement—among LLMs with similar benchmark accuracy?  
   - *Basis*: (inferred) Authors attribute disagreement to "systematic differences in how models represent and reason about information" but do not investigate causal drivers.  
   - *Why unresolved*: Without understanding the root causes of divergent errors, it is difficult to predict which models will disagree on which items or how to select a "correct" model for a given annotation task.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: Correlational studies linking model characteristics (size, training data dates, RLHF specifics) with fine-grained error patterns on benchmark subsets (e.g., by knowledge domain, reasoning type); causal experiments via controlled fine-tuning to test if specific alignment interventions reduce disagreement on targeted item subsets.

5. **Benchmark designs for annotation suitability**  
   - *Question*: Can new benchmark designs and evaluation metrics—beyond aggregate accuracy—effectively predict an LLM's suitability for scientific annotation by measuring its agreement with other high-performing models, stability across prompts, and error correlation with relevant covariates?  
   - *Basis*: (inferred) Authors argue current evaluations are "misaligned with scientific needs" and call for "agreement, stability, and inferential robustness as first-class targets," but no concrete metric or benchmark is proposed.  
   - *Why unresolved*: Researchers currently select annotation models based on leaderboard rankings (e.g., MMLU-Pro scores), which the paper shows are insufficient. A validated "science-readiness" benchmark or suite of metrics is needed.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: Development and validation of new benchmark tasks that include: (1) pairwise agreement rates among top models, (2) sensitivity to prompt variations, (3) measurement of error asymmetry across demographic or treatment-related subgroups. Validation would involve correlating these metrics with the magnitude of bias in downstream re-analyses of real studies.

## Limitations
- Model version ambiguity: The paper lists model names but not precise Hugging Face IDs or checkpoints, risking mismatched predictions during replication.
- Inaccessible original datasets: The student essays from Kim et al. (2021) and news fragments from Rozenas & Stukal (2019) are not publicly available, preventing faithful reanalysis.
- Undisclosed annotation prompts: The exact prompt text used for LLM scoring is omitted; prompt variations could alter disagreement rates and downstream effects.

## Confidence
- Pairwise disagreement on benchmarks (16–38% on MMLU-Pro, 17–32% on GPQA): Medium
- Case study impact (treatment effect variability and sign reversal): Low
- Simulation conclusion (systematic errors from high-accuracy annotators cause larger biases): Medium
