# Symbol-Aware Reasoning with Masked Discrete Diffusion for Handwritten Mathematical Expression Recognition

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03370
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03370
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** Achieves 5.56% Character Error Rate (CER) and 60.42% Exact Match (EM) on MathWriting, outperforming strong Transformer and commercial baselines.

## Executive Summary
Handwritten Mathematical Expression Recognition (HMER) suffers from exposure bias and structural inconsistency in autoregressive models. This work reformulates HMER as an iterative symbolic refinement task using a discrete diffusion framework, enabling parallel token prediction without causal dependencies. A symbol-aware tokenization decomposes expressions into symbols and structural modifiers, preserving syntactic integrity. Random-Masking Mutual Learning further improves robustness. On the MathWriting benchmark, the method achieves state-of-the-art 5.56% CER and 60.42% EM, with consistent gains on CROHME 2014–2023 (up to +4.2 EM points), demonstrating superior structural stability and recognition accuracy.

## Method Summary
The approach combines three key innovations. First, a discrete diffusion process starts with a fully masked token sequence and, over T steps, iteratively predicts and unmasks tokens while sometimes remasking incorrect ones. This reverse process refines both symbol identities and structural relations simultaneously, correcting early errors without causal locks. Second, Symbol-Aware Tokenization (SAT) converts raw LaTeX into a sequence where each token is a complete symbolic unit: visible symbols are kept, while structural syntax tokens (e.g., `{`, `^`, `_`) become modifier embeddings attached to the governed symbol. This shortens sequences, improves attention efficiency, and ensures local edits respect global grammar. Third, Random-Masking Mutual Learning (RMML) during training creates two independently masked views of the same expression, processes them with the diffusion decoder, and minimizes symmetric KL divergence between their output distributions. This consistency regularization forces invariance to masking patterns, enhancing robustness. The architecture uses a Vision Transformer image encoder and a Transformer decoder attending to visual features; at inference, the diffusion decoder runs for T steps, and the final sequence is postprocessed back to LaTeX.

## Key Results
- On MathWriting: **5.56% CER** and **60.42% EM**.
- Outperforms strong Transformer and commercial baselines.
- On CROHME 2014–2023: consistent EM gains, up to **+4.2 points** improvement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Discrete Diffusion Eliminates Exposure Bias
- **Claim:** Reformulating HMER as an iterative discrete diffusion process removes exposure bias and improves structural consistency by enabling parallel, multi-step token refinement without causal dependencies.
- **Mechanism:** The model starts with a fully masked token sequence and, over T diffusion steps, iteratively predicts and "unmasks" tokens while sometimes "remasking" incorrect ones. This reverse process (Alg. 2) refines both symbol identities and their structural relations simultaneously, allowing later steps to correct errors made in earlier steps—unlike autoregressive decoding where an early mistake is locked in.
- **Core assumption:** Structural inconsistencies in handwritten math expressions can be resolved through gradual, global refinement of a token sequence, where each step's prediction is informed by the entire partially-reconstructed context.
- **Evidence:** The abstract and Section 2.1 state that this formulation eliminates causal dependencies and maintains global structural consistency. Neighboring work (e.g., NAMER) identifies exposure bias as a core problem; this diffusion approach directly addresses it.
- **Break condition:** If T is too small (e.g., T=2), refinement iterations are insufficient, leading to higher error rates and output instability (Table 4, Fig. 6).

### Mechanism 2: Symbol-Aware Tokenization Preserves Syntax
- **Claim:** Symbol-aware tokenization (SAT) preserves syntactic integrity and simplifies learning by aligning structural modifiers (e.g., braces, superscript markers) with their corresponding visible symbols in a one-to-one representation.
- **Mechanism:** Raw LaTeX tokens are decomposed: visible symbols (digits, variables) are kept, while structural syntax tokens (e.g., `{`, `^`, `_`) are extracted and assigned as "modifier embeddings" to the symbol they govern. The final token representation is the sum of symbol and modifier embeddings, creating a sequence where each position is a complete, self-contained symbolic unit (e.g., `x` with a superscript modifier for `2` in `x^2`).
- **Core assumption:** By integrating syntax into symbol-level tokens, local edits (unmasking a token) automatically respect global grammar, preventing discontinuous edits that break brace parity or spatial layout.
- **Evidence:** The abstract and Section 2.2 note that SAT shortens sequence length, improves attention efficiency, and allows local structural edits without breaking global consistency; Fig. 3 illustrates the problem with raw LaTeX. Section 4.4 ablation shows SAT consistently reduces Syntax Error Rate (SER) across all T.
- **Break condition:** For extremely complex or nested expressions (e.g., a fraction bar spanning multiple numerator terms), the one-to-one alignment may become ambiguous, potentially requiring multi-symbol modifier assignments not handled by the current SAT scheme.

### Mechanism 3: Random-Masking Mutual Learning Improves Robustness
- **Claim:** Random-Masking Mutual Learning (RMML) improves robustness to handwriting diversity and structural ambiguity by enforcing consistency between predictions from different masked views of the same expression during training.
- **Mechanism:** For a given ground-truth expression, two independently masked sequences (`F1`, `F2`) are created. Both are processed by the same diffusion decoder, producing token probability distributions `P_F1` and `P_F2`. The model is trained to minimize symmetric KL divergence between these distributions, forcing representations invariant to which tokens are masked and reducing sensitivity to ambiguous or poorly written symbols.
- **Core assumption:** Handwriting variation and syntactic ambiguity cause stochasticity in the input-masking space; consistency regularization across diverse masks forces the model to capture the underlying, unambiguous symbolic structure.
- **Evidence:** The abstract and Section 2.3 describe RMML and its KL consistency loss. Section 4.4 shows adding RMML further decreases CER and SER, with largest gains at smaller T. Section 4.5 (Fig. 6) shows RMML reduces output diversity, indicating more stable predictions.
- **Break condition:** If the two masked views are too dissimilar (e.g., one masks mostly symbols, the other mostly modifiers), the KL penalty could over-regularize, suppressing valid alternative parses for genuinely ambiguous handwriting (though Fig. 7 shows some valid alternatives are still produced).

## Foundational Learning

### Concept 1: Discrete Diffusion for Discrete Data
- **Why needed:** The core innovation applies a diffusion process (typically for continuous data) to discrete token sequences. An engineer must understand the forward noising (masking) and learned reverse denoising (unmasking/remasking) steps to implement or modify the training/inference loop.
- **Quick check:** How does the reverse process in Alg. 2 differ from standard autoregressive decoding, and why does this difference mitigate exposure bias?

### Concept 2: Symbol-Aware Representation for Structured Outputs
- **Why needed:** SAT redefines the output token vocabulary and sequence structure. An engineer needs to grasp the LaTeX-to-SAT conversion algorithm, how modifier embeddings are computed and assigned, and how this impacts loss calculation and sequence length.
- **Quick check:** Why does decomposing a LaTeX token like `{x}^{2}` into a symbol `x` with a superscript modifier lead to a shorter, more learnable sequence than the raw 5-token LaTeX sequence?

### Concept 3: Consistency Regularization via Mutual Learning
- **Why needed:** RMML is a specific application of deep mutual learning to the diffusion context. An engineer must understand how to generate multiple masked views of a single sample, compute KL divergence between their predictive distributions, and integrate this with the primary cross-entropy loss.
- **Quick check:** What is the intended effect of minimizing `D_KL(P_F1 || P_F2) + D_KL(P_F2 || P_F1)` on the model's internal representations, and how might it differ from simple data augmentation?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
InputImage -> ViT Encoder -> Visual Features -> Diffusion Decoder (with Masked Tokens) -> Predicted Token Distributions -> Sampling/Remasking -> Next Step Tokens -> (repeat for T steps) -> Final Unmasked Sequence -> Postprocess (merge symbol-modifier) -> LaTeX Output

**Critical path:**  
The image is processed by a Vision Transformer to extract visual features. A fully masked token sequence (after SAT preprocessing) is fed into a Transformer decoder, which attends to the visual features. At each diffusion step t, the decoder predicts token distributions for all masked positions. These predictions are sampled and combined with a remasking function to produce the token sequence for the next step. After T steps, the unmasked sequence is postprocessed to merge symbol and modifier embeddings, yielding the final LaTeX output.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Diffusion Steps T:** Higher T (e.g., 50) yields better accuracy but lower inference speed; lower T (e.g., 10) is faster but relies more on SAT and RMML for stability.  
- **SAT vs. Raw LaTeX:** SAT shortens sequences and enforces syntax but loses absolute positional information; the model must learn structure from modifier embeddings.  
- **RMML Cost:** Adds ~2x forward passes per training sample, increasing memory and compute; the KL loss weight requires tuning.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **High Syntax Error Rate (SER):** Indicates SAT is failing to enforce structural parity; check modifier alignment logic.  
- **High Output Diversity:** Model predictions vary across diffusion runs for the same input; suggests insufficient training, too few steps T, or missing RMML.  
- **Poor Cross-Dataset Generalization:** Performance drop on CROHME vs. MathWriting may indicate overfitting to MathWriting's rendering style or vocabulary; check the 224x224 normalization.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Reproduction:** Train without SAT and without RMML for small T (e.g., 10). Verify it runs; expect high CER and SER.  
2. **Ablation - SAT:** Add only SAT; retrain. Measure SER and EM improvement.  
3. **Ablation - RMML:** Train with SAT and RMML; compare CER and output diversity to step 2.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can HMER evaluation metrics account for mathematically equivalent but syntactically different outputs when multiple valid parses exist?**  
   Section 4.5 and Fig. 7 show ambiguous handwriting often yields multiple valid LaTeX outputs; current CER/EM penalize all deviations. No standard metric fairly scores this; LgEval checks structural but not full mathematical equivalence, leading to underestimation of capability. A benchmark with multiple valid references or an equivalence oracle normalizing expressions to canonical forms would resolve.

2. **What is the minimal diffusion step count T that maintains accuracy while enabling real-time HMER on resource-constrained devices?**  
   Ablations (Table 4, Sec. 4.4) show T=50 yields best accuracy but T=10 is 2× faster with moderate drop; the paper adopts T=50 without exploring intermediate trade-offs. The accuracy-speed curve is unquantified; acceptable latency varies by application, and breaking points where EM degrades sharply are unknown. Systematic measurement of EM vs. FPS across T=5–50 on edge devices is needed.

3. **Which proposed component (symbol-aware tokenization or Random-Masking Mutual Learning) primarily drives cross-dataset robustness from MathWriting to CROHME?**  
   Tables 2 and 3 demonstrate consistent EM gains on CROHME when trained on MathWriting, but ablations in Sec. 4.4 are only on MathWriting; thus, the contribution of each component to out-of-distribution performance is unclear. Without isolating SAT and RMML in cross-dataset settings, it is unknown whether structural bias (SAT) or consistency regularization (RMML) is key for generalization. Training ablations (full model, SAT-only, RMML-only) on MathWriting and evaluating on CROHME would identify the critical component.

## Limitations
- **Generalization gaps:** Performance may drop on datasets with significantly different handwriting styles, paper quality, or symbol vocabularies, indicating potential overfitting to MathWriting's distribution.
- **Computational overhead:** The diffusion process with T steps and RMML's dual forward passes substantially increase training time and memory; the accuracy-inference speed trade-off needs optimization for real-time applications.
- **SAT boundary cases:** Symbol-aware tokenization assumes one-to-one symbol-modifier alignment, which may fail for complex structures like multi-symbol modifiers (e.g., a fraction bar spanning multiple numerator terms), risking syntactic errors not captured in current SER metrics.

3. **Remasking policy ablation:** Ablate the stochastic remasking in Alg. 2 by setting remask probability to zero (only unmask). Compare final CER/EM for T=10, 25, 50 to isolate the contribution of iterative error correction versus simple multi-step unmasking.
