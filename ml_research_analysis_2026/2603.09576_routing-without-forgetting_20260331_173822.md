# Routing without Forgetting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09576
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09576
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art final average accuracies on Split-ImageNet-R (74.09%) and Split-ImageNet-S (61.37%) with only a 2.1% parameter increase, using a buffer-free, task-agnostic approach.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Routing without Forgetting (RwF), a method for online continual learning (OCL) with vision transformers. RwF reframes continual learning as an input-conditioned routing problem. It augments transformer layers with energy-based associative retrieval inspired by Modern Hopfield Networks. This retrieval is computed in closed form within each forward pass by minimizing a strictly convex free-energy functional, enabling immediate adaptation to new data without gradient-based specialization. The method places these routing modules in early transformer blocks to intercept interference before it propagates. Under strict single-pass protocols on Split-ImageNet-R and Split-ImageNet-S, RwF achieves state-of-the-art final average accuracies of 74.09% and 61.37%, respectively, while being robust to task fragmentation and few-shot regimes. It requires no task identifiers and adds only about 2.1% extra parameters.

## Method Summary
RwF modifies a pre-trained Vision Transformer (ViT) by inserting HopfieldPooling layers into the first \(k\) transformer blocks. Each HopfieldPooling layer contains learnable query vectors and fixed key/value projections. Given input token embeddings \(Z^\ell\) at layer \(\ell\), it computes routing prompts \(P^\ell = A^\ell V^\ell\) where \(A^\ell = \text{softmax}(\beta \tilde{Q}^\ell (K^\ell)^\top)\) with \(\tilde{Q}^\ell = Q^\ell W^Q\), \(K^\ell = Z^\ell W^K\), \(V^\ell = Z^\ell W^V\). The prompts are concatenated with the original tokens and processed by the block's self-attention. Only the updated backbone tokens \(\tilde{Z}^\ell\) are forwarded to subsequent layers; the routing prompts are discarded. This design decouples routing from iterative optimization, allowing input-conditioned reconfiguration in a single forward pass. Training uses the Adam optimizer, with a single epoch per task and no replay buffer. The method is buffer-free and does not require task identifiers at inference.

## Key Results
- Final average accuracy of **74.09%** on Split-ImageNet-R, significantly outperforming prior prompt- and LoRA-based methods.
- Final average accuracy of **61.37%** on Split-ImageNet-S, demonstrating strong performance on a more challenging, fine-grained split.
- Maintains robustness in few-shot regimes and under increasing task fragmentation, with only a **2.1%** parameter increase.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Closed‑Form Associative Routing Enables Single‑Pass Adaptation
RwF provides immediate adaptation to new data without waiting for gradient updates because routing decisions are computed analytically via energy minimization in each forward pass. Each modified transformer block inserts a HopfieldPooling operator. Given input tokens \(Z^\ell\), learnable queries \(Q^\ell\), and fixed projections \(W^K, W^V\), it computes routing prompts \(P^\ell = A^\ell V^\ell\), where \(A^\ell = \text{softmax}(\beta \tilde{Q}^\ell (K^\ell)^\top)\) with \(\tilde{Q}^\ell = Q^\ell W^Q\). This softmax assignment solves the minimization of the strictly convex free‑energy \(F(p;q) = -\sum_i p_i\langle\tilde{q},k_i\rangle + \beta^{-1} H(p)\), yielding a unique equilibrium in closed form. The prompts are then concatenated with the original tokens and processed by attention; the transformed prompts are discarded, while only the backbone tokens propagate forward. In Online Continual Learning each sample is seen exactly once, so gradient‑based specialization of task‑specific modules is too slow; a representation‑reconfiguration mechanism that works within a single forward pass is necessary. If the energy functional were modified to be non‑convex or if numerical instability caused the softmax to saturate, the closed‑form solution might fail.

### Mechanism 2: Entropy‑Regularized Smoothness Prevents Abrupt Representational Jumps
The entropy term in the free‑energy functional ensures that routing weights change continuously with input features, buffering against sudden shifts that cause catastrophic forgetting. The negative entropy \(\beta^{-1} H(p)\) is strictly concave, so \(-\beta^{-1}H(p)\) is convex; together with the linear alignment term the overall functional is strictly convex. The resulting softmax is a continuous, differentiable function of the pre‑softmax similarities. Moreover, LayerNorm keeps activations bounded, making the softmax Lipschitz: \(\|A^\ell(Z^\ell+\Delta Z^\ell)-A^\ell(Z^\ell)\| \leq L_\beta \|\Delta Z^\ell\|\). Thus small feature perturbations induce small routing changes. Forgetting is exacerbated by discrete or large‑magnitude changes in internal representations when adapting to new tasks; smooth routing mitigates this by gradually reallocating subspaces. If the inverse temperature \(\beta\) is too large, the softmax approaches uniform (over‑smooth, losing plasticity); if too small, it approaches one‑hot (discontinuous). Additionally, if LayerNorm is omitted or feature magnitudes diverge, the Lipschitz guarantee may fail.

### Mechanism 3: Early‑Layer Routing Intercepts Interference Before Propagation
Placing HopfieldPooling layers in the early transformer blocks is crucial because it mitigates task interference at the level of shared, low‑level representations before they contaminate deeper, task‑specific pathways. In ViTs, early layers capture generic patterns common across tasks, while deeper layers encode high‑level semantics specific to the current distribution. By applying associative routing early, the model dynamically reallocates these shared features to align with the incoming task, preventing conflicting updates from reaching deeper layers. Ablations consistently show that early placement outperforms deep placement. If routing layers are placed only after many self‑attention blocks, interference may have already propagated, reducing effectiveness. Conversely, if placed before patch embedding, the pre‑trained feature hierarchy might be overly disrupted.

## Foundational Learning
- **Modern Hopfield Networks (Energy‑Based Associative Memory):** RwF’s routing operator is inspired by Modern Hopfield Networks, where retrieval is framed as minimizing a free‑energy functional. The softmax update rule is the exact solution, providing stability and closed‑form computation. *Why needed:* It enables stable, input-conditioned routing without iterative optimization. *Quick check:* Given \(F(p) = -\sum_i p_i\langle q, k_i\rangle + \beta^{-1}H(p)\), derive \(p^*\) and explain \(\beta\)'s role.
- **Vision Transformer (ViT) Architecture:** The method modifies a pre‑trained ViT by inserting HopfieldPooling layers into its blocks. Understanding the flow of tokens (patch embeddings, class token, layer structure) is essential. *Why needed:* To see where and how RwF operates. *Quick check:* Describe the data flow in a ViT block. Where would you insert an associative retrieval step?
- **Online Class‑Incremental Learning (Class‑IL):** The problem setting (single‑pass, non‑stationary stream, no task IDs) defines constraints RwF addresses. It distinguishes this work from multi‑epoch or task‑aware CL. *Why needed:* To understand why gradient‑driven prompt specialization fails. *Quick check:* What are the three main constraints of the Online Continual Learning protocol used? How do they differ from standard Class‑Incremental with multiple epochs?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Frozen pre‑trained ViT backbone → (for selected blocks) HopfieldPooling layer (learnable \(Q^\ell\), fixed \(W^K, W^V\)) → self‑attention → discard routing prompts → next block. After all blocks, classification head.
- **Critical path:** Input image → patch embedding → tokens \(Z^1\). For each modified block \(\ell\): compute keys \(K^\ell = Z^\ell W^K\), values \(V^\ell = Z^\ell W^V\); compute queries \(\tilde{Q}^\ell = Q^\ell W^Q\); compute \(A^\ell = \text{softmax}(\beta \tilde{Q}^\ell (K^\ell)^\top)\); retrieve \(P^\ell = A^\ell V^\ell\); concatenate \([P^\ell; Z^\ell]\); apply block self‑attention → \(\tilde{R}^\ell = [\tilde{P}^\ell; \tilde{Z}^\ell]\); discard \(\tilde{P}^\ell\); forward \(\tilde{Z}^\ell\) to next block (or MLP after attention within same block). After final block, use class token → classification head.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - Number of routing layers \(k\): more increases capacity but adds parameters; \(k=3\) optimal.
  - Placement: early layers (first \(k\)) outperform deeper; interference intercepted earlier.
  - Query count \(m\): larger \(m\) increases routing richness but linearly increases parameters; typically \(m=30\).
  - Temperature \(\beta\): controls sharpness; set to \(1/\sqrt{d}\); tuning needed.
  - Fixed \(W^K, W^V\): prevents drift; training them causes instability.
  - Residuals: no residual around HopfieldPooling; adding not explored.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High forgetting: monitor entropy of \(A^\ell\); very low entropy → brittle routing; increase \(\beta\).
  - Low accuracy on new tasks: insufficient capacity; increase \(m\) or \(k\).
  - Training instability: check activation norms; ensure LayerNorm and proper \(\beta\).
  - No improvement: verify insertion points and discard mechanism.
  - Overfitting to current task: routing too sensitive; increase \(\beta\) or add entropy regularization.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Closed‑form verification:** Implement HopfieldPooling; for a random batch compute \(P^\ell\). Separately minimize \(F(p)\) via gradient descent. Results should match exactly.
  2. **Routing smoothness test:** Feed two nearly identical images (tiny Gaussian noise). Compute routing matrices \(A^\ell\); measure Frobenius norm difference; should scale with perturbation. Also compute average entropy of \(A^\ell\) (should be 0.5–2.0 bits per row for typical \(\beta\)).
  3. **Synthetic online CL test:** Use two‑domain stream (e.g., MNIST digits 0‑4 then 5‑9) with shallow ViT. After training on second domain, accuracy on first domain should exceed 80%; collapse indicates broken routing or interference.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can RwF be adapted to handle fine‑grained classification tasks where subtle local features are critical? (The paper notes poor performance on CUB‑200 and hypothesizes that similarity‑weighted aggregation smooths details and attractors overlap.)
2. Does the convex free‑energy functional of RwF provide any theoretical guarantee against catastrophic forgetting? (No formal link established; stability claim empirical.)
3. How do the routing hyperparameters (number of prompts \(m\), temperature \(\beta\)) affect performance across diverse OCL scenarios, and could adaptive schedules be beneficial? (Paper fixes \(m=30\) and \(\beta=1/\sqrt{d}\) without sensitivity analysis.)
4. Can combining RwF with a small experience replay buffer further improve OCL performance without losing its buffer‑free nature? (Synergy between input‑conditioned routing and rehearsal untested.)

## Limitations
- No official implementation; critical details (layer insertion, prompt discarding, fixed projection initialization) are inferred, risking reproduction errors.
- Hyperparameters (learning rate schedule, weight decay) are missing; tuning bias may affect reported numbers.
- The method relies on a frozen pre‑trained ViT; generalizability to other architectures is unclear.
- Performance on fine‑grained tasks is poor; the approach may smooth important details.

## Confidence
- Closed‑form associative routing: **High** (mathematical derivation, easily verifiable).
- Entropy‑regularized smoothness: **Medium** (theoretical basis but impact on forgetting empirical).
- Early‑layer routing superiority: **High** (consistent strong ablations).
- Final SOTA accuracies: **Low** (no code, potential overfitting, single‑protocol evaluation).

## Next Checks

2. **Smoothness test:** Feed two nearly identical images; compute Frobenius norm difference of their routing matrices; it should scale linearly with perturbation. Also compute average entropy of routing weights to confirm they remain reasonable (0.5–2.0 bits per row).
3. **Synthetic online CL test:** Use a two‑domain stream (e.g., MNIST 0‑4 then 5‑9) with a small ViT. After training on the second domain, accuracy on the first domain should exceed 80%; collapse indicates broken routing.
