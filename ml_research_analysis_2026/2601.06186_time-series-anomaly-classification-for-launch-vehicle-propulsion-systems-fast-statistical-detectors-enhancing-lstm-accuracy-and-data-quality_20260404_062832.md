# Time-Series Anomaly Classification for Launch Vehicle Propulsion Systems: Fast Statistical Detectors Enhancing LSTM Accuracy and Data Quality

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.06186
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06186
- **Reference count**: 37
- **Primary result**: Relabeling simulation-derived anomaly intervals via a Mahalanobis-distance detector improved LSTM multiclass F1 from 0.710 to 0.831; additional manual correction raised it to 0.876.

## Executive Summary
Supervised LSTM anomaly classification for launch vehicle propulsion systems is hampered by suboptimal training labels: simulated anomalies may not match true detectability due to variable strength and timing. This paper introduces a fast statistical detector that combines Mahalanobis distance with forward‑backward detection fractions to relabel anomaly intervals in a massive digital twin dataset (O(10^8) timesteps). The relabeling yields a 7% precision gain and 22% recall gain, boosting test F1 from 0.710 to 0.831. Manual post‑processing to resolve class confusions further increases F1 to 0.876. The approach demonstrates that improving label quality can substantially enhance deep anomaly classifiers in high‑stakes engineering domains.

## Method Summary
The study uses a digital twin (GiNA solver) to generate telemetry from a ground‑stage propulsion system. Each trial lasts 1250 s and records 7 sensor parameters (pressure, temperature). A nominal ensemble (300+ trials) defines normal operating statistics. Twenty‑five anomaly classes (valve failures, leaks, etc.) are injected with variable start times, durations, and strengths, totaling ~15 000 trials (~400 M timesteps).

The core innovation is a statistical detector that relabels anomaly intervals. For each trial, a sliding‑window Mahalanobis distance is computed using the nominal mean and covariance; the maximum per window is normalized by the 99th percentile of nominal distances. Forward‑ and backward‑looking detection fractions are evaluated at three window scales (1, 20, 100 timesteps) and the minimum of the two directions is taken. The scale‑wise fractions are combined as `F_ovr(t)=max(F_FB(1), F_FB(20), F_FB(100))`. An anomaly is declared when `F_ovr>1`, and the detected intervals replace the original simulation‑based labels.

A custom 3‑layer LSTM (100, 75, 50 units) is trained on 100‑timestep input windows (the last 50 timesteps used for prediction) with categorical cross‑entropy, using an 80/10/10 split and 750 epochs (learning rate 3e‑4). After training, manual correction rules are applied to fix confusion among classes with identical post‑failure signatures (e.g., fail open vs. internal leakage). All experiments use Monte Carlo generation with 101 random settings to ensure diversity.

## Key Results
- Relabeling increased precision by 7% and recall by 22%, raising test F1 from 0.710 to 0.831.
- Additional manual adjustments further improved F1 to 0.876.
- The benefit grew with dataset size: 300 trials per anomaly gave modest gains, while 600 trials per anomaly produced the reported figures (~15000 total trials).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The simulation labels are often inaccurate because the exact moment an anomaly becomes detectable depends on its strength and the evolving system state. The Mahalanobis‑based detector measures multivariate deviation while accounting for sensor correlations; its adaptive normalization (99th percentile of nominal data) ensures robustness to non‑Gaussian residuals. The forward‑backward fractions across multiple window scales capture both transient spikes and persistent shifts, reducing spurious detections. By aligning training labels with the detector’s assessment, the LSTM learns a decision boundary that better matches the true distinguishability of anomalies. The manual step resolves residual confusions where physical signatures are genuinely identical in the available sensors, indicating that some classes are inherently ambiguous without additional information.

## Foundational Learning
- **Mahalanobis distance**: A metric that measures the distance of a point from a distribution, accounting for covariance; needed to detect anomalies in correlated multivariate data. *Quick check*: Compute the Mahalanobis distance of a sample from a Gaussian with known mean and covariance.
- **Sliding window analysis**: Extracts local temporal contexts; needed because anomalies may appear over different time scales. *Quick check*: Apply a moving average or rolling standard deviation to a time series.
- **Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM)**: A recurrent neural network that handles long‑range dependencies; needed to classify sequential telemetry windows. *Quick check*: Train a simple LSTM to predict the next value in a sine wave.
- **Adaptive thresholding**: Sets decision thresholds from data (e.g., 99th percentile) rather than theoretical distributions; needed because residuals are non‑Gaussian. *Quick check*: Determine the 95th percentile of a metric on a held‑out normal dataset.
- **Anomaly relabeling**: Improves training data quality by correcting simulation inconsistencies; needed to close the reality gap. *Quick check*: Compare classifier performance on a toy dataset before and after label correction.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Simulated Telemetry → Mahalanobis Distance Detector → Anomaly Fraction (F_ovr) → Thresholding → Relabeled Training Data → 3‑Layer LSTM → Predictions (Manual Adjustment) → Final Labels

**Critical Path**  
The pipeline’s backbone is the statistical detector: it converts raw sensor streams into binary anomaly labels that replace unreliable simulation labels. These labels directly feed the LSTM, making detector accuracy paramount.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Window sizes*: Small (1) captures fast transients but is noisy; large (100) smooths but may miss brief events. A max‑over‑scales strategy balances coverage.
- *Threshold*: Fixed `F_ovr>1` is simple; the adaptive 99th‑percentile normalization provides robustness but dependency on nominal data quality.
- *Manual vs. automatic*: Manual correction boosts final F1 but introduces subjectivity; a fully automatic system would be preferable for operational use.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Detector false positives if nominal covariance is poorly estimated or if operation drifts into rarely seen regimes.
- Persistent confusion between classes with identical post‑failure behaviour (e.g., fail open vs. internal leakage) despite relabeling.
- Overfitting of the LSTM when training windows are insufficient or when relabeling introduces noise; monitor train‑test F1 gap.

**First Experiments**  
1. Verify detector false positive rate ≤1% on a held‑out nominal ensemble at the prescribed 99th‑percentile cutoff.
2. Replicate LSTM training with original and detector‑relabeled data; confirm F1 improvement from ~0.71 to ~0.83.
3. Ablate manual corrections: train and evaluate without post‑processing to isolate the pure contribution of detector‑based relabeling (expect F1≈0.831).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Dataset size vs. relabeling benefit** – The paper observed gains with 300–600 trials per anomaly but did not systematically vary size to identify saturation points.
- **Principled threshold adaptation** – The empirical 99th‑percentile cutoff may fail if nominal statistics shift; a statistical method (e.g., extreme value theory) could provide automatic adaptation.
- **Resolving ambiguous classes** – Post‑failure signatures of some anomalies are identical in the current sensor suite; it remains unclear whether richer models or additional sensors (e.g., valve commands) can separate them.
- **Generalization to other subsystems and real data** – The method is validated only on a digital twin of a ground‑stage propulsion system; transferability to engines, turbopumps, or actual launch telemetry is untested.

## Limitations
- GiNA solver details and full anomaly injection parameters are undocumented, preventing exact dataset replication.
- Manual correction rules for confusable classes are unspecified, limiting reproducibility of the final 0.876 F1.
- No evaluation on real‑world telemetry; simulation fidelity and broader applicability remain uncertain.

## Confidence
- Detector label correction: **Medium**
- LSTM F1 gain from relabeling: **Medium**
- Manual adjustment benefit: **Low**
- Simulation generalizability: **Low**
