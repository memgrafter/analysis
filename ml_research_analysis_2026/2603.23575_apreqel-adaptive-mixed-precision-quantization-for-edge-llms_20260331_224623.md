# APreQEL: Adaptive Mixed Precision Quantization For Edge LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.23575
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23575
- **Reference count:** 18
- **Primary result:** APreQEL achieves an average hypervolume indicator gain of ~9% over uniform quantization baselines, demonstrating a broader Pareto front for memory-latency-accuracy trade-offs on edge devices.

## Executive Summary
APreQEL addresses the challenge of deploying large language models on resource-constrained edge devices by replacing uniform quantization with an adaptive mixed-precision approach. Instead of applying a single bit-width to all layers, it quantizes each layer individually (3–8 bits) based on its contribution to model performance and user-defined priorities (memory, latency, accuracy). The method first scores layers by the cosine similarity between consecutive hidden states, then uses TOPSIS multi-criteria analysis to determine the optimal distribution of bit-widths that best satisfies weighted QoS metrics. Evaluated on Llama3.1, Phi3.5, and Qwen3-4B, APreQEL consistently outperforms uniform quantization, yielding models tailored to specific edge constraints while maintaining higher overall solution quality.

## Method Summary
APreQEL consists of three main stages. First, it computes a **layer contribution score**: for each layer (attention and FFN separately), it measures cosine similarity between its output and the next layer’s input across calibration prompts. A reward-penalty mechanism with threshold γ=0.9 boosts the score when similarity is low (indicating a more transformative layer) and reduces it when similarity is high. Layers are then ranked by score. Second, it measures baseline QoS metrics (memory, latency, perplexity) for each uniform quantization type (q3_K through q8_0) on the target hardware. It enumerates all feasible distributions of the L layers across the M quantization types (solutions Zₖ). For each solution, the combined metrics are estimated as weighted averages of the uniform baselines, assuming linearity. Third, TOPSIS ranks these solutions: metrics are normalized (Euclidean norm across solutions), multiplied by user-provided weights, and each solution’s score is computed based on its distance to the ideal (best) and anti-ideal (worst) points. The top-ranked distribution Z_best dictates how many layers receive each bit-width. Finally, layers are allocated in order of contribution score to the quantization types from least aggressive (highest bit) to most aggressive, producing the mixed-precision model.

## Key Results
- **Pareto front improvement:** Average hypervolume indicator gain of ~9% over uniform quantization baselines.
- **Priority-aware adaptation:** Reliable generation of models aligned with user weights: APreQEL-Memory (minimal footprint), APreQEL-Latency (fastest inference), APreQEL-Accuracy (lowest perplexity).
- **Cross-architecture effectiveness:** Validated on Llama3.1-8B, Phi3.5, and Qwen3-4B.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
APreQEL works because it explicitly respects two key factors: (1) layers differ in their sensitivity to quantization, captured by the contribution score (low cosine similarity means the layer significantly transforms the representation, so it needs higher precision), and (2) hardware-specific costs (memory, latency) and accuracy loss vary non-uniformly across bit-widths. By letting users weigh these objectives and using TOPSIS to select the best distribution, the method navigates the trade-off space efficiently. The linear combination assumption allows rapid exploration without exhaustive measurement, while the enumeration of all feasible distributions guarantees optimality under that assumption. This combination yields a model that concentrates higher precision on the most important layers while aggressively quantizing less impactful ones, thereby maximizing the Pareto front for the given priorities.

- **Mixed-Precision Quantization**  
  *Why needed:* Uniform quantization applies the same bit-width to all layers, ignoring that some layers are more sensitive to quantization errors.

- **Layer-wise Importance Scoring**

  *Quick check:* How does the reward-penalty mechanism with threshold γ=0.9 differentiate important layers?

- **TOPSIS (Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution)**  
  *Why needed:* Combines multiple user-weighted objectives (memory, latency, perplexity) into a single ranking of candidate distributions.  
  *Quick check:* What are the two reference solutions in TOPSIS, and how are they used?

- **Hypervolume Indicator**  
  *Why needed:* Measures the quality of a Pareto front by the volume it dominates in objective space; higher values indicate a better set of trade-offs.  
  *Quick check:* Does a larger hypervolume always imply a better Pareto front? (Yes, it covers more of the desirable region.)

- **Cosine Similarity of Hidden States**  
  *Why needed:* Captures how much a layer changes the representation; low similarity indicates a more critical layer that should be less quantized.  
  *Quick check:* What does a cosine similarity close to 0 between two layer outputs suggest?

- **Linearity Assumption for QoS Estimation**  
  *Why needed:* Avoids the combinatorial cost of measuring every possible mixed-precision configuration; treats metrics as weighted averages of uniform baselines.  
  *Quick check:* Is this assumption always valid? (No—interactions between quantization types can cause non-linear effects.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Calibration Prompts → Layer Scorer → Baseline QoS Measurement → TOPSIS Distributor → Allocation Engine → Quantized Model Builder  

**Critical path:**  
1. Run calibration data through full-precision model to extract hidden states.  
2. Compute layer contribution scores and rank layers.  
3. Measure memory, latency, perplexity for each uniform quantization type on target hardware.  
4. Enumerate all feasible layer distributions across bit-widths; estimate combined QoS via linear combination.  
5. Apply TOPSIS (normalize, weight, compute distance to ideal/anti-ideal) to select best distribution Z_best.  
6. Allocate layers to bit-widths according to Z_best (highest-scored layers → least aggressive bits).  
7. Construct mixed-precision model (e.g., via llama.cpp) and validate.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Linearity vs. accuracy:** The linear combination assumption reduces search cost but may misestimate true metrics if non-linear hardware interactions exist.  
- **Exhaustive enumeration vs. scalability:** For typical LLMs (L ≈ 64 layers, M = 5 bit-widths), the number of compositions (~10⁶) is tractable; but scaling to more types or very deep models could become expensive.  
- **Scoring simplicity vs. robustness:** Cosine similarity on hidden states is cheap and interpretable, but may not capture all aspects of layer importance (e.g., gradient-based sensitivity).

**Failure signatures:**  
- Mixed-precision model’s perplexity degrades more than expected from linear estimates → violation of linearity assumption.  
- TOPSIS-selected distribution yields actual latency far from estimated → inaccurate baseline measurement or non-linear latency effects.  
- Layer ranking changes drastically with different calibration data → instability in contribution scoring, leading to unreliable allocation.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Uniform Benchmark:** On the target edge hardware (e.g., Jetson Orin-AGX), measure memory footprint, latency (ms/token via llama-bench), and perplexity (wiki-text-2) for each uniform quantization (q3_K, q4_K, q5_K, q6_K, q8_0).  
2. **Layer Scoring Stability Test:** Compute contribution scores using two different calibration sets (e.g., the paper’s 70 WebInstructSub prompts vs. 70 random Wikipedia passages). Calculate Spearman’s rank correlation; a low correlation indicates high sensitivity and may require more robust scoring.  
3. **Linearity Validation:** For a chosen user weight vector, obtain Z_best from TOPSIS, build the corresponding mixed-precision model, and measure its true QoS metrics. Compare against the linearly estimated values; compute percentage errors to quantify the linearity violation.

## Limitations
- **TOPSIS implementation ambiguity:** The paper’s description of normalization (column-wise Euclidean norm) and ideal/anti-ideal computation is unclear, risking irreproducible distribution selection.  
- **Linearity assumption risk:** Mixed-precision metrics are approximated as weighted averages of uniform baselines; non-linear interactions between quantization types could degrade actual performance.  
- **Enumeration space undefined:** The claimed combinatorial count for feasible solutions does not match standard integer compositions; hidden constraints (e.g., minimum layers per type) may exist but are not specified.

## Confidence
- **High:** APreQEL achieves a broader Pareto front with ~9% hypervolume gain over uniform quantization.  
- **Medium:** The method reliably tailors models to user priorities (Memory/Latency/Accuracy variants) across different architectures.  
- **Low:** The sensitivity of layer contribution scoring to calibration data and hidden state extraction is unstudied; small changes could alter allocation quality.

## Next Checks

2. **Linearity Assumption Test:** Build a mixed-precision model from a TOPSIS-recommended distribution and compare its measured memory/latency/perplexity against linearly estimated values; quantify deviation.  
3. **Layer Ranking Stability:** Recompute scores with a different calibration dataset (e.g., 70 random Wikipedia passages) and compute Spearman correlation with the original ranking; low correlation signals instability.
