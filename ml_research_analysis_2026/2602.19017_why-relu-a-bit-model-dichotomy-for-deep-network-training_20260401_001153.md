# Why ReLU? A Bit-Model Dichotomy for Deep Network Training

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19017  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19017  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: The paper proves a sharp complexity dichotomy: training deep networks with polynomial activations (degree ≥2) is #P-hard, while with ReLU (piecewise-linear) it is in NP (NP-complete) and backpropagation is polynomial-time, explaining ReLU's empirical prevalence.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates the bit-level computational complexity of exact empirical risk minimization (ERM_bit) for deep neural networks, where parameters and data are polynomially bounded rational numbers. It establishes a dichotomy based on the activation function: non-linear polynomial activations of degree at least two render the training problem #P-hard, and even deciding the sign of a single gradient bit is #P-hard under standard complexity assumptions. In contrast, for piecewise-linear activations such as ReLU, ERM_bit lies in NP (and is NP-complete) and backpropagation can be performed in polynomial time. These results provide a complexity-theoretic foundation for the widespread empirical use of ReLU and highlight fundamental limits of training with other activations.

## Method Summary
The authors define a precise bit-model ERM problem where all inputs, parameters, and computations are rational with polynomially bounded representation. They analyze the complexity of deciding whether there exists a set of parameters achieving loss below a given threshold. For polynomial activations, they reduce from counting problems (#SAT, counting solutions to polynomial equations) to show #P-hardness and further prove that determining gradient signs or individual gradient bits is also #P-hard. For ReLU, they exploit its piecewise linearity to encode the training problem as a mixed-integer linear program, placing it in NP and proving NP-completeness via a reduction from a known NP-complete problem. They also demonstrate that backpropagation under the bit-model runs in polynomial time for ReLU, contrasting with the #P-hardness for polynomial activations.

## Key Results
- For any non-linear polynomial activation with rational coefficients and degree ≥2, the exact training problem (ERM_bit) is #P-hard.
- Under standard conjectures (P≠NP, #P⊄BPP), deciding a gradient sign or a single gradient bit for such activations is #P-hard.
- For piecewise-linear activations like ReLU, ERM_bit is in NP (indeed NP-complete) and backpropagation is polynomial-time computable.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The dichotomy stems from the algebraic structure of the activation. Polynomial activations of degree ≥2 introduce nonlinearities that make the network's output a polynomial function of the parameters; minimizing a loss (often a sum of squares) leads to solving systems of polynomial equations. Counting exact solutions or determining properties of gradients then becomes equivalent to evaluating sums over exponentially many assignments, which is #P-hard. ReLU, being piecewise linear, partitions the parameter space into polyhedral regions where the network is linear. Training can be expressed as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) by introducing binary variables to select the active linear piece. MILPs are in NP, and backpropagation corresponds to evaluating a piecewise linear function, which is computationally feasible. This mechanistic difference explains the complexity gap.

## Architecture Onboarding
We outline the computational model and analysis pipeline:

**Component map**
Input Data -> Network Layers (Linear Transform + Activation) -> Loss -> Gradient Computation -> Decision Problem (ERM feasibility or gradient bit)

**Critical path**
The hardness analysis follows two critical paths:
1. For polynomial activations: Show that the loss minimization or gradient bit decision can encode counting of polynomial equation solutions, leading to #P-hardness.
2. For ReLU: Represent the piecewise linear network as an MILP; verify feasibility in NP; reduce from a known NP-complete problem to prove NP-completeness; show backpropagation reduces to evaluating linear functions on polyhedra.

**Design tradeoffs**
The bit-model trades realism (finite-precision, stochastic optimization) for theoretical clarity. The analysis assumes exact rational arithmetic, which is stronger than typical floating-point training but reveals intrinsic complexity barriers. Hardness for polynomial activations suggests that even with exact arithmetic, training is infeasible unless #P ⊆ BPP, whereas ReLU remains in NP.

**Failure signatures**
- Polynomial activation: Any exact solver would require super-polynomial time in the worst case (unless #P ⊆ BPP). Empirically, one expects exponential blow-up in network size/depth.
- ReLU: Although NP-complete, many instances are tractable; but worst-case instances may cause exponential growth in MILP solving time, especially with many ReLU units leading to many binary variables.

**First experiments**
1. Implement exact ERM_bit solvers (e.g., using MILP solvers) for minimal networks (2 layers, ≤10 neurons) with ReLU and quadratic activation; measure runtime scaling with network width/depth.
2. Construct specific instances where gradient sign decision reduces to counting solutions; verify computational difficulty empirically by attempting to solve small cases.
3. Compare the bit-complexity of backpropagation for ReLU vs polynomial activations on synthetic data; observe whether polynomial activations lead to explosion in bit-length of intermediate values.

## Limitations
- The bit-model assumes exact rational arithmetic with polynomially bounded parameters; real-world finite-precision training (e.g., floating-point SGD) may have different complexity behavior.
- Hardness results for polynomial activations rely on standard conjectures (P≠NP, #P⊄BPP); if these fail, the dichotomy could collapse.
- The analysis does not cover non-polynomial activations like sigmoid or tanh; their bit-complexity remains unknown.
- NP-completeness for ReLU indicates worst-case hardness, but heuristic optimizers may solve many practical instances efficiently; the practical gap remains to be quantified.
- The work is recent with low citation count, so independent validation of the framework and proofs is limited.
