# Procedural Fairness via Group Counterfactual Explanation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11140
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11140
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** Introduces Group Counterfactual Integrated Gradients (GCIG), an in‑processing method that reduces explanation disparity across protected groups while maintaining predictive performance and outcome fairness; outcome and procedural fairness show only weak correlation (Pearson r=0.244).

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles procedural fairness by ensuring that machine learning models produce consistent feature attributions across protected groups, conditional on the true label. The authors propose Group Counterfactual Integrated Gradients (GCIG), which computes Integrated Gradients with group‑conditional baselines and adds a regularization term to minimize cross‑group variance in normalized attributions.

Evaluated on four binary classification datasets, GCIG markedly lowers the GCIG disparity metric (e.g., from 0.190 to 0.066 on German Credit) without harming classification accuracy (F1) or outcome fairness (Equalized Odds). A correlation analysis across models reveals a weak relationship between outcome fairness and procedural fairness (Pearson r=0.244), suggesting that GCIG addresses a distinct fairness dimension that traditional metrics miss.

## Method Summary
The core method, FairX, jointly optimizes three losses during training: the standard predictive loss (binary cross‑entropy), a differentiable Equalized Odds penalty, and the GCIG loss. GCIG is computed by, for each instance, integrating gradients from the instance to two baselines: the expected feature values for its own true label within each protected group. These Integrated Gradient vectors are normalized (ℓ₂) and their pairwise distance across groups yields an instance‑level disparity, which is averaged over the batch to form the GCIG regularization term. Group‑conditional baselines are maintained via exponential moving averages to ensure stability. Hyperparameters λ_ig and λ_fair control the trade‑offs. Experimental results show that FairX achieves substantial improvements in procedural fairness (lower GCIG) while preserving utility and outcome fairness, and that the procedural fairness objective is only weakly correlated with outcome fairness, underscoring its novelty.

## Key Results
- **Reduced explanation disparity:** GCIG score decreases dramatically (e.g., German Credit: 0.190 → 0.066) compared to conventional fairness methods.
- **Preserved utility and outcome fairness:** F1 scores remain competitive and Equalized Odds gaps stay low, demonstrating that procedural fairness can be enforced without sacrificing other objectives.
- **Weak EO–GCIG alignment:** Across datasets, the correlation between Equalized Odds gap and GCIG is low (Pearson r=0.244), indicating that outcome and procedural fairness capture different aspects of model behavior.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** GCIG reduces group‑dependent explanation disparity by penalizing variance in group counterfactual Integrated Gradient attributions during training.  
**Mechanism:** For each input, IG is computed relative to group‑conditional baselines (mean features for same label/group). Normalized attributions are compared across groups, and their element‑wise variance is aggregated into a scalar disparity loss `LGCIG(θ)`. This loss is added to the training objective, encouraging model parameters to yield similar feature‑importance patterns across protected groups for identical outcomes.  
**Core assumption:** Minimizing cross‑group variance in normalized IG attributions leads to procedurally fairer models where reasoning patterns are consistent across groups conditional on the true label.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "GCIG computes Integrated Gradients relative to group conditional baselines and penalizes cross‑group variation during training."; [section 3.4] Formal definition of `V(x;y)` as aggregated variance of normalized attributions across groups; [corpus] Neighbor papers on procedural fairness and explanations confirm growing interest, but GCIG’s specific in‑processing regularization is novel.  
**Break condition:** If the model learns to minimize disparity by collapsing feature attributions (e.g., near‑zero gradients), completeness property may be violated or utility degraded.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Group conditional baselines provide contextually meaningful reference points that enable fair counterfactual comparisons across groups.  
**Mechanism:** Baselines `b_y,g` are moving averages of feature means for each (label, group) pair. IG is computed as `IG(x, b_y,g)`, attributing prediction change relative to a "typical" group member with the same outcome. Fixed during gradient computation, preventing degenerate baseline adaptation.  
**Core assumption:** Using group‑specific, label‑conditioned baselines reveals whether the model applies different reasoning standards to the same individual when evaluated against different group contexts.  
**Evidence anchors:** [section 3.3] Definition: `b_y,g = E[X|Y=y, A=g]` and EMA stabilization; [section 3.4] "IG(g)(x;y) represents the contribution of features measured relative to the typical feature profile of group g..."  
**Break condition:** If group conditional distributions overlap significantly, baselines may not provide sufficiently distinct contexts, reducing sensitivity to group‑dependent reasoning.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Joint optimization of predictive loss, outcome fairness (EO), and GCIG loss yields models with balanced utility, outcome parity, and explanation consistency.  
**Mechanism:** Total loss `L_total = L_pred + λ_ig LGCIG + λ_fair L_fair`. `L_fair` can be a differentiable EO penalty (e.g., Lagrangian method). Gradients from all terms update model parameters simultaneously.  
**Core assumption:** Procedural fairness (GCIG) and outcome fairness (EO) are complementary objectives that can be optimized together without fundamental trade‑offs in the evaluated settings.  
**Evidence anchors:** [section 3.5] Equation defining `FairX` objective; [table 4.2] `FairX` achieves low GCIG and competitive EO/F1 across datasets; [section 4.4] Ablation shows combining both components yields best procedural fairness (+24% vs prediction‑only).  
**Break condition:** Excessive λ_ig may force explanation invariance at the cost of predictive performance or EO; λ values require tuning.

## Foundational Learning
### Integrated Gradients
**Why needed:** GCIG builds on IG to generate feature attributions. Understanding IG’s path integral, baseline sensitivity, and completeness is essential to grasp how GCIG modifies its application.  
**Quick check:** If IG completeness holds, what does the sum of all feature attributions equal? (Answer: the difference between the model output at the input and at the baseline.)

### Group Conditional Distribution
**Why needed:** GCIG’s baselines are defined as `P(X|Y=y, A=g)`. This conditional modeling is core to constructing counterfactual group contexts.  
**Quick check:** Why condition on the true label `Y=y` when defining baselines for procedural fairness? (Answer: To ensure attributions reflect reasoning conditional on the actual outcome, isolating group‑dependent effects.)

### Outcome vs. Procedural Fairness Distinction
**Why needed:** GCIG explicitly targets procedural fairness, a dimension not captured by EO. Recognizing this distinction is key to understanding the paper’s motivation.  
**Quick check:** Can two models have identical Equalized Odds gaps but different explanation disparities? What does this imply? (Answer: Yes; it implies that outcome fairness alone does not guarantee consistent reasoning across groups, so procedural fairness is a separate concern.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data stream → Baseline manager → Model forward → Loss calculator → Optimizer  
- **Data stream:** Supplies mini‑batches `(x_i, y_i, a_i)`.  
- **Baseline manager:** Maintains EMA estimates `b_y,g` updated per‑batch using instances in `B_y,g`.  
- **Model forward:** Computes logits `f_θ(x_i)`.  
- **Loss calculator:** Computes `L_pred` (binary cross‑entropy), `L_GCIG` (disparity from group‑conditional IG), and optionally `L_fair` (EO penalty).  
- **Optimizer:** Updates `θ` using gradient of `L_total`.

**Critical path:** Batch → EMA baseline update → forward pass → IG computation for both group baselines → normalization → disparity aggregation → loss summation → backward pass → parameter update.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- `T` (IG steps): Lower `T` reduces overhead but increases approximation error; paper uses `T∈{8,16}`.
- Normalization norm: ℓ₂ used by default; ℓ₁ may emphasize sparsity.
- λ_ig vs. λ_fair: Higher λ_ig increases procedural fairness pressure but may harm utility/EO; requires grid search.
- Baseline stability: EMA rate γ trades responsiveness vs. variance; too low → noisy baselines; too high → slow adaptation.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Utility drop:** GCIG loss dominating (`λ_ig` too high) forces model to flatten gradients, harming predictive accuracy.
- **Unstable baselines:** Small batch sizes or severe class/group imbalance cause high‑variance `hat{b}_y,g`, leading to erratic `L_GCIG`.
- **No improvement:** λ_ig=0 or poorly chosen; or model already near explanation invariance.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline replication:** Run unconstrained ERM and standard outcome fairness methods (DIR, Hardt, Agarwal) to establish F1/EO/GCIG benchmarks.
2. **GCIG ablation:** Train with `(Prediction+EO)`, `(Prediction+GCIG)`, and `(Full)` to verify both fairness components contribute (as in Table 4.4).
3. **Sweep λ_ig:** Fix λ_fair at tuned value, vary λ_ig across `[0.1, 10]` to generate Figure 4.1, confirming robustness and trade‑off curves.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to multi‑valued/intersectional attributes and non‑tabular data:** How can GCIG be generalized beyond binary protected attributes and tabular data? (Conclusion mentions future work; requires adapting baselines and explanations.)
2. **Synergy/conflict between outcome and procedural fairness:** Under what conditions does optimizing EO synergize or conflict with GCIG during joint optimization? (Acknowledgement that complementarity may depend on dataset, model, weighting.)
3. **Why EO alone sometimes degrades procedural fairness:** Why does enforcing EO regularization alone increase GCIG in some ablations? (Not explained; likely due to optimization dynamics or representational changes.)
4. **Dataset properties driving EO–GCIG correlation:** What factors determine the strength of correlation between outcome fairness and procedural fairness? (Observed variability across datasets not investigated.)
5. **Alternative explanation methods:** Can SHAP, LRP, or other attribution methods replace Integrated Gradients to reduce computational overhead? (Conclusion notes as future work; needs verification of compatibility with GCIG’s axioms.)

## Limitations
- **Missing architectural and hyperparameter details:** Exact MLP architecture and key hyperparameters (λ_ig, λ_fair, EMA smoothing rate γ) are not reported, hindering exact replication.
- **Limited correlation analysis:** The weak EO‑GCIG correlation rests on only four datasets, reducing confidence in the generalizability of the finding.
- **Risk of degenerate solutions:** Excessive GCIG regularization may cause Integrated Gradients to collapse, violating completeness and harming interpretability; this issue is not thoroughly investigated.

## Confidence
- **Effectiveness claim** (GCIG reduces explanation disparity while maintaining predictive performance and outcome fairness): **Medium** – Empirical results are promising but full reproducibility is hindered by missing details.
- **Distinctness claim** (procedural fairness is weakly correlated with outcome fairness): **Low** – Correlation from four datasets is underpowered and may not generalize.
- **Mechanism claim** (penalizing cross‑group variance of group‑conditional IG yields consistent reasoning): **Medium** – Theoretically sound but requires validation that baselines remain meaningful and solutions do not degenerate.

## Next Checks
1. Obtain missing implementation specifics: MLP architecture, EMA smoothing factor γ, and final λ_ig/λ_fair values per dataset; if unavailable, perform a systematic sensitivity sweep to establish robust operating ranges.
2. Extend the correlation analysis to additional datasets (e.g., more recidivism or credit datasets) or controlled synthetic setups, to assess whether the weak outcome‑procedural fairness relationship holds more generally.
3. Monitor attribution integrity during training: check that the sum of IG values approximately equals the output difference (completeness) and that normalized attributions do not collapse to near‑zero; this verifies that GCIG loss does not induce degenerate feature‑importance patterns.
