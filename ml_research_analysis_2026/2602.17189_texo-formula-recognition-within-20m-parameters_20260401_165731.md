# Texo: Formula Recognition within 20M Parameters
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17189
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17189
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: 20M-parameter model achieving 80% reduction vs. UniMERNet-T while maintaining comparable performance on simple printed expressions (CDM 0.958) and 7× faster inference (311ms/sample).

## Executive Summary
Texo addresses the prohibitive size of state-of-the-art mathematical expression recognition models (often exceeding 50M parameters), which hinders deployment on consumer hardware. It achieves extreme parameter efficiency through three core innovations: distilling a domain-specific LaTeX tokenizer from KaTeX that reduces the vocabulary from ~50,000 to 687 tokens; transferring knowledge from a pretrained large-vocabulary model (PPFormulaNet-S) into this compact token space via mean embedding initialization; and employing an efficient CNN encoder (HGNetV2-B4) with a shallow Transformer decoder (2-layer MBart). On the UniMER-Test benchmark, Texo attains a Character Detection Matching score of 0.958 on Simple Printed Expressions—comparable to much larger models—while halving output token length and running 7× faster than UniMERNet-T. This enables real-time in-browser inference on standard consumer GPUs.

## Method Summary
Texo’s method centers on minimizing the dominant parameter overhead in encoder-decoder models: the embedding layers (vocabulary size × hidden dimension). First, it replaces a general BPE tokenizer with a rule-based tokenizer built from KaTeX’s LaTeX macro set, ensuring each token corresponds to a complete semantic command (e.g., `\leftarrow`) rather than subword fragments. This reduces the vocabulary to 687 tokens and shortens sequences. Second, to preserve representational knowledge from a pretrained model (PPFormulaNet-S), it initializes the new model’s input and output embeddings by averaging the embeddings of the base tokenizer’s sub-tokens that compose each new token. Third, it selects a parameter-efficient architecture: an HGNetV2-B4 CNN encoder and a 2-layer MBart Transformer decoder with a smaller hidden dimension (384). The model is trained on UniMER-1M with standard augmentations for 100k steps. Finally, it is exported to ONNX for deployment via Transformers.js in the browser.

## Key Results
- **Parameter Efficiency:** 20M total parameters—an 80% reduction from UniMERNet-T (104M) and 65% from PPFormulaNet-S (58M).
- **Recognition Performance:** Character Detection Matching (CDM) of 0.958 on Simple Printed Expressions (SPE), comparable to UniMERNet-T (0.991) and PPFormulaNet-S (0.949). Performance drops on more complex subsets (CPE: 0.825 vs. 0.949 for PPFormulaNet-S).
- **Speed:** 311ms per sample on an A40 GPU (batch size 1)—7× faster than UniMERNet-T (2266ms). Output token length is halved relative to prior models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Vocabulary Distillation
- **Claim:** Drastically reducing the vocabulary size from ~50K to 687 tokens minimizes the dominant embedding layer overhead while preserving semantic coverage for LaTeX formulas.
- **Mechanism:** Replace a general-purpose BPE tokenizer with a domain-specific, rule-based tokenizer built from the KaTeX LaTeX parser’s macro set. Each token corresponds to a complete LaTeX command (e.g., `\leftarrow`), reducing sequence length and embedding matrix size.
- **Core assumption:** The full semantic space of mathematical expressions in the UniMER dataset can be covered by KaTeX’s common macros; splitting these macros into subwords is unnecessary and harmful for efficiency.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section II.B describe distilling from KaTeX; no direct OOV rate is reported.
- **Break condition:** If the KaTeX-derived tokenizer fails to tokenize a significant portion of the dataset (high OOV rate), forcing fallback to subword splitting and negating the size benefit.

### Mechanism 2: Mean Embedding Knowledge Transfer
- **Claim:** Knowledge transfer from a pretrained large-vocabulary model (PPFormulaNet-S) to the new small-vocabulary model via mean embedding initialization preserves representational knowledge without full retraining, maintaining performance.
- **Mechanism:** For each new token in the small vocabulary, tokenize it with the old BPE tokenizer to get sub-token IDs. Initialize the new token’s embedding as the mean of the old embeddings for those sub-tokens. This maps the old model’s semantic space onto the new token space.
- **Core assumption:** The mean of embeddings for a fragmented token (e.g., `["left", "arrow"]`) is a sufficiently good approximation of the embedding for the entire token (`\leftarrow`).
- **Evidence anchors:** Algorithm 1 and Section II.B detail the mean computation; cited works [33,34] propose similar heuristics.
- **Break condition:** If mean initialization leads to poor gradient dynamics or loses critical semantic information for tokens previously split into multiple, varied subwords.

### Mechanism 3: Efficient Encoder-Decoder Architecture
- **Claim:** Selecting a lightweight CNN encoder (HGNetV2-B4) and a shallow Transformer decoder (2-layer MBart) is sufficient for formula recognition when combined with the distilled tokenizer, enabling high parameter efficiency and speed.
- **Mechanism:** Remove architectural redundancy by using an efficient modern CNN for visual feature extraction and minimizing decoder depth. The reduced vocabulary and sequence length lessen the computational load on the decoder, allowing it to be shallower without performance loss.
- **Core assumption:** The primary complexity lies in visual symbol detection and spatial layout (handled by encoder); syntactic sequence generation (handled by decoder) is relatively straightforward given constrained LaTeX grammar.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section II.A and Table I highlight embedding layers as the dominant overhead; Section III.C shows comparable SPE CDM with far fewer parameters; Table II reports 311ms vs. 217.60ms for PPFormulaNet-S (using a speed-accuracy trade-off).
- **Break condition:** If complex, nested formulas (CPE/HWE) require deeper hierarchical reasoning in the decoder, causing performance collapse on those subsets.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Tokenization for Structured Domains**
  - Why needed: Standard NLP tokenizers (BPE) break semantic units like LaTeX macros; a formula tokenizer must preserve complete symbolic commands to maintain semantic integrity and minimize sequence length.
  - Quick check: Why might splitting `\frac{a}{b}` into `["\frac", "{", "a", "}", "{", "b", "}"]` be less efficient than keeping `\frac` as a single token?
- **Concept: Vision-Language Encoder-Decoder Architectures**
  - Why needed: Texo encodes an image into features and decodes to a token sequence; understanding cross-attention between visual features and text tokens is key.
  - Quick check: In a cross-attention decoder, what is the role of the `key` and `value` matrices from the image encoder versus the `query` from the text tokens?
- **Concept: Parameter Budgeting and Dominant Terms**
  - Why needed: The paper’s insight is that embedding layers dominate parameters (`Vocab Size × Hidden Dim`); reducing vocab from 50K to 687 saves ~18.8M parameters.
  - Quick check: For a decoder with hidden dimension 384, how many parameters does the output embedding layer consume with a vocabulary of 50,000 tokens versus 687 tokens?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input image -> HGNetV2-B4 encoder -> flattened feature map -> MBart decoder (2 layers) -> output linear layer -> token IDs.
Critical path: Image encoding, then sequential autoregressive decoding in the MBart decoder.
Design tradeoffs:
- Vocabulary size vs. coverage: Smaller vocab saves parameters/sequence length but risks OOV on rare macros.
- Encoder/decoder depth vs. speed: Shallow decoder and efficient encoder maximize speed but may limit modeling of extremely complex layouts.
- Tokenization simplicity vs. robustness: Rule-based tokenizer is deterministic and compact but may fail on non-standard LaTeX.
Failure signatures:
- High output token length (>115 on SPE) indicates tokenizer fragmentation or model uncertainty.
- Sharp CDM drop on CPE/HWE (e.g., below 0.8) suggests architectural inability to handle layout complexity or dataset noise.
- Inference slower than claimed (~311ms) could indicate inefficient ONNX export or hardware mismatch.
First 3 experiments:
1. Ablate vocabulary size impact: Train models with varying distilled vocab sizes (687, 1500, 5000); measure CDM on all UniMER-Test subsets and average output token length.
2. Ablate transfer initialization vs. random: Initialize with mean embeddings vs. random; compare convergence speed and final CDM to isolate knowledge transfer benefit.
3. Inference profiling & deployment test: Benchmark ONNX model on consumer hardware (e.g., RTX 3060); integrate into Transformers.js/Web Worker pipeline; measure browser-based end-to-end latency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Impact on Rare Macros:** What is the impact of the distilled 687-token vocabulary on recognizing expressions with rare or custom LaTeX macros?
- **Whitespace Sensitivity:** Does removing whitespace from the tokenizer cause semantic errors in expressions where whitespace is meaningful?
- **Generalization to OCR:** Can Texo’s parameter-efficient design be adapted to general document OCR tasks without significant performance loss?
- **Parallel Prediction:** How would multi-token parallel prediction (used in PPFormulaNet-S) affect Texo’s inference speed and accuracy trade-off?

## Limitations
- **Tokenizer Coverage:** No out-of-vocabulary (OOV) rate is reported for the KaTeX-derived tokenizer on the actual training corpus; the assumption of complete semantic coverage remains untested.
- **Transfer Efficacy:** No ablation compares mean embedding initialization to random initialization; the contribution of knowledge transfer to performance is inferred but not isolated.
- **Architectural Limits:** The performance drop on Complex Printed Expressions (CPE) and Handwritten Expressions (HWE) is attributed to dataset noise, but an architectural insufficiency (shallow decoder) remains a plausible, unproven alternative explanation.
- **Reproducibility:** Critical details are missing: exact KaTeX version and macro list, normalizer implementation from [9], augmentation magnitudes, and base BPE tokenizer specifics.

## Confidence
- **Parameter reduction claim (20M params, 80%/65% reduction):** High (direct architectural count).
- **Tokenizer distillation efficacy (687 tokens):** Medium (OOV rate unknown).
- **Knowledge transfer via mean embedding:** Medium (no ablation proving necessity).
- **Performance parity on Simple Printed Expressions:** High (empirical result).
- **Performance on Complex/Handwritten subsets:** Low-to-Medium (attribution to noise unproven).
- **Real-time inference claim (311ms/sample, 7× speedup):** Medium (baseline comparison uses speed-accuracy trade-off technique).

## Next Checks
1. **Quantify Tokenizer Coverage:** Compute and report the OOV rate of the distilled 687-token KaTeX tokenizer on the full UniMER-1M training set and UniMER-Test. A rate >1–2% challenges the semantic completeness assumption.
2. **Ablate Knowledge Transfer Mechanism:** Train two variants of Texo: one with mean embedding initialization and one with random embeddings for the new tokenizer. Compare convergence speed and final CDM across SPE/CPE/HWE to confirm transfer’s necessity.
3. **Stress-Test Architectural Limits on Complex Formulas:** Curate a high-quality, noise-filtered subset of CPE formulas (e.g., using the normalizer) and evaluate Texo, UniMERNet-T, and PPFormulaNet-S. If Texo’s gap persists, it suggests architectural insufficiency rather than dataset noise.
