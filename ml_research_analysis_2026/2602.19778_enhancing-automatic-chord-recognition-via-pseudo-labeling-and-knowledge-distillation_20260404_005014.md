# Enhancing Automatic Chord Recognition via Pseudo-Labeling and Knowledge Distillation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19778
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19778
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Two-stage pseudo-labeling and knowledge distillation enables student models to exceed teacher performance by 1.1-3.2% and supervised baselines by up to 2.67%, with rare chord accuracy (Dim7) rising from 0% to 45.6%.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the scarcity of labeled data in automatic chord recognition (ACR) by introducing a two-stage training pipeline that leverages a pre-trained BTC model as a teacher. In the first stage, the teacher generates pseudo-labels for over 1,000 hours of unlabeled audio, which are used to train a student model. In the second stage, the student is fine-tuned on a limited set of ground-truth chord annotations while applying selective knowledge distillation from the teacher to avoid catastrophic forgetting. Experimental results show that after stage 1, the BTC student already achieves over 99% of the teacher’s performance across seven mir_eval metrics. After stage 2, the student surpasses the teacher by 1.1-3.2% and a traditional supervised baseline by 2.5%, with particularly large gains on rare chord qualities such as Dim7 (0% → 45.6% accuracy). The approach is validated on two student architectures (BTC and 2E1D), demonstrating broad applicability.

## Method Summary
The proposed method consists of two sequential training phases. Stage 1: A pre-trained bi-directional transformer chord (BTC) model is used as a teacher to infer frame-wise chord labels (via argmax) from constant-Q transform (CQT) features of a large corpus of unlabeled audio (∼1,072 hours from FMA, DALI, MAESTRO). These pseudo-labels train a student model (either a BTC or a dual-encoder 2E1D network) from scratch, with an optional knowledge distillation (KD) loss that accelerates convergence but is not necessary for high accuracy. Stage 2: The stage-1 student is further trained on a small labeled dataset (∼600 songs) using standard cross-entropy on ground-truth chords, while simultaneously applying a selective KD regularizer from the original teacher. The selective KD down-weights teacher predictions that are either too low-confidence (<0.1) or too high-confidence (>0.9) to reduce the influence of noisy pseudo-labels. This two-stage decoupling allows the student to benefit from both the broad coverage of unlabeled data and the precision of limited labeled data, without overwriting the representations learned in stage 1. The entire pipeline avoids data augmentation (unlike supervised baselines that use pitch-shifting) and uses a temperature τ=3.0 and a KD weight α (0.3 for BTC, 0.5 for 2E1D) determined by ablation.

## Key Results
- After Stage 1 pseudo-label pretraining, the BTC student attains >99% of the teacher’s performance on seven mir_eval metrics.
- After Stage 2 fine-tuning with selective KD, the BTC student exceeds the teacher by 1.1-3.2% and a supervised baseline by 2.5%; the 2E1D student outperforms the baseline by 2.67% on average.
- Rare chord qualities see dramatic improvements: Dim7 accuracy increases from 0% in supervised models to 45.6% with the proposed method.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Uniform chord distribution from heterogeneous unlabeled audio
The teacher model's pseudo-labels across a diverse unlabeled corpus (FMA, DALI, MAESTRO) naturally yield a nearly uniform distribution of chord roots (98.4% uniformity). This uniformity mitigates the key bias commonly observed in manually labeled datasets, which often over‑represent certain keys. The uniform coverage is achieved without artificial pitch‑shifting augmentation, avoiding its spectral artifacts. The mechanism relies on the assumption that the teacher’s predictions are sufficiently accurate across genres. Evidence is provided in Section 3.2.1 and the abstract, which cite the 1,072 hours of unlabeled audio. A break condition would be if the teacher systematically errs on specific keys or genres, thereby transferring its bias to the student.

### Mechanism 2: Selective knowledge distillation as a regularizer
During Stage 2 fine‑tuning, a weighted KD loss is applied: teacher predictions with confidence scores outside the interval [0.1, 0.9] are either discarded (below 0.1) or down‑weighted (above 0.9), while mid‑confidence predictions retain full weight. This selective weighting reduces gradient variance under noisy or misaligned ground‑truth labels, effectively anchoring the student to the teacher’s generalized harmonic patterns and preventing catastrophic forgetting. The core assumption is that the teacher’s softmax distribution encodes more robust knowledge than potentially erroneous hard labels. Evidence includes the definition in Section 3.2.4 and ablation studies showing optimal α values (0.3 for BTC, 0.5 for 2E1D) and severe performance degradation without KD (Figure 5). A break condition occurs if teacher confidence correlates with error, causing the down‑weighting to discard useful signals, or if the ground‑truth is clean and the teacher is weaker, where KD might hinder adaptation.

### Mechanism 3: Decoupled two‑stage training yields rare chord gains
Pseudo‑label pretraining on massive unlabeled data exposes the student to a wide variety of chord qualities, including rare ones that are underrepresented in the small labeled set. The subsequent fine‑tuning stage uses selective KD to preserve these rare chord representations while adapting decision boundaries to the limited labeled examples. This combination allows the student to surpass both the teacher and supervised baselines, with particularly large improvements on rare chords (e.g., Dim7 accuracy from 0% to 45.6%). Evidence is in the abstract and Section 5.3, which report ACQA improvements of 10.5% for BTC. The break condition is that pseudo‑label errors for rare chords could become entrenched, or insufficient labeled data might fail to correct them.

## Foundational Learning
### Knowledge Distillation
*Why needed here*: Soft targets from a teacher model provide a smoothed loss landscape that regularizes the student during fine‑tuning, helping retain broad harmonic patterns and stabilize training under noisy labels.  
*Quick check*: Why does temperature‑scaled softmax (τ>1) produce richer supervision than hard labels, and how does the gradient τ(p_s - p_t) pull the student toward the teacher?

### Catastrophic Forgetting
*Why needed here*: Fine‑tuning on a small labeled dataset risks overwriting the robust representations learned from vast pseudo‑labeled data; selective KD mitigates this by anchoring the student to the teacher’s distribution.  
*Quick check*: In this paper, what evidence shows that fine‑tuning without KD causes performance degradation, especially for rare chords?

### Pseudo‑labeling
*Why needed here*: Stage 1 relies entirely on teacher‑generated pseudo‑labels to leverage unlabeled audio, addressing label scarcity without manual annotation.  
*Quick check*: What are two risks of training on pseudo‑labels, and how does the use of a high‑quality teacher and a large diverse unlabeled set address them?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Pre‑trained BTC teacher → Pseudo‑label generator (teacher inference on unlabeled CQT) → Student (BTC or 2E1D) → Selective KD module (confidence‑weighted KL divergence) → Two‑stage training pipeline → Evaluation (mir_eval, per‑chord‑quality metrics).

### Critical Path
1. Load pre‑trained BTC teacher weights.  
2. Preprocess unlabeled audio (FMA, DALI, MAESTRO) to CQT features (F=144, hop=2048, z‑score normalized).  
3. Run teacher inference to produce frame‑wise pseudo‑labels (argmax).  
4. Train student on pseudo‑labeled data (Stage 1) with AdamW, LR=1e‑4, batch=256, sequence length=108 frames; optional KD may accelerate convergence.  
5. Save Stage 1 checkpoint; load ground‑truth labeled dataset (∼600 songs).  
6. Continue training the student on labeled data with LR=1e‑5, while applying selective KD from the original teacher (α=0.3 for BTC, α=0.5 for 2E1D, τ=3.0, θ_min=0.1, θ_max=0.9, K=0.8).  
7. Evaluate on held‑out test set using mir_eval and other metrics; compare against supervised baseline trained with pitch‑shifting augmentation.

### Design Tradeoffs
- BTC student (deeper, sequential attention) is inherently more robust to noisy pseudo‑labels than the wider, parallel 2E1D architecture, thus requiring less aggressive KD (α=0.3 vs. 0.5).  
- The proposed pipeline avoids any data augmentation, sidestepping artifacts from pitch‑shifting; in contrast, the supervised baseline relies on such augmentation for performance.  
- 2E1D is computationally faster at inference but needs stronger KD regularization and may be more prone to collapse under misaligned labels.  
- Selective KD adds memory overhead for storing teacher softmax probabilities and introduces hyperparameters for confidence thresholds.

### Failure Signatures
- Stage 1: Student mir_eval metrics fall below 95% of teacher performance, indicating poor pseudo‑label quality or domain mismatch.  
- Stage 2: Validation loss rises without KD (Figure 5); rare chord accuracy (e.g., Dim7) stays near 0% despite overall gains; 2E1D degrades more sharply than BTC under label noise.  
- Overfitting to systematic pseudo‑label errors (e.g., teacher bias on a particular key) may manifest as poor performance on those keys.

### First Experiments
1. **FMA‑only Stage 1**: Train BTC student on pseudo‑labels derived solely from FMA (∼100k tracks). Expected mir_eval Root ~78‑79% (Table 1), illustrating the impact of dataset diversity.  
2. **KD weight sweep**: Vary α ∈ {0,0.1,0.3,0.5} for BTC and 2E1D under both clean and synthetically noisy labels. Confirm optimal α (0.3 for BTC, 0.5 for 2E1D) and that any α>0 improves over supervised baseline even with clean labels (Tables 2‑3).  
3. **Architecture sensitivity**: Compare BTC and 2E1D with identical Stage 1 (All+D) and Stage 2 (α=0.3) on clean labeled data. Verify that 2E1D starts at ~97% of teacher performance (vs. 99% for BTC) and requires higher α for comparable gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper identifies several directions for future research:
1. How do biases in the teacher model’s representations affect the student’s performance, and what strategies can mitigate such biases? The authors note reliance on teacher quality as a limitation.
2. Can ensembles of multiple teacher models improve robustness and final performance, especially for rare chord qualities?
3. What are the scaling laws or saturation points when increasing the quantity and diversity of unlabeled data in Stage 1? Which factor (quantity vs. diversity) matters more?
4. Does the two‑stage pipeline generalize to other MIR tasks with different label structures (e.g., beat tracking, key estimation), and how must the method be adapted?
5. How sensitive are the results to the hyperparameters of selective KD (θ_min, θ_max, K) and the KD weight α, and can these be automatically optimized per student architecture?

## Limitations
- Exact train/val/test splits for the 600‑song labeled dataset are not provided, making precise replication of the evaluation difficult.
- The 2E1D model’s full hyperparameters (number of layers, attention heads, dropout rates, Gaussian kernel width, overlap ratio) are unspecified.
- It is unclear whether any data augmentation (e.g., mild time‑stretch) was applied during the proposed two‑stage training, while the supervised baseline uses pitch‑shifting.

## Next Checks
1. **Pseudo‑label quality**: Measure frame‑wise agreement between teacher pseudo‑labels and a held‑out clean validation set (e.g., 20 songs). Agreement below 90% would threaten the uniformity assumption.
2. **Uniformity break**: Train the pipeline on a deliberately biased unlabeled subset (e.g., only FMA) and evaluate on out‑of‑distribution keys/qualities. A significant performance drop would confirm the importance of natural uniformity.
3. **Selective KD under noise**: Inject synthetic noise (random chord substitutions) into the labeled training data and compare selective KD against uniform KD. Robustness of the weighted approach would validate its design.
