# Attention Head Entropy of LLMs Predicts Answer Correctness

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.13699  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13699  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Head Entropy, a white-box method using attention entropy, predicts answer correctness with out-of-domain AUROC improvement of +8.5% over the strongest baseline and +17.7% when using only question/context tokens before answer generation.

## Executive Summary
Large language models often produce plausible but incorrect answers, especially in safety-critical domains like medicine. Existing error detection methods are either costly (human evaluation) or unreliable (LLM-as-judge). This paper introduces Head Entropy, a white-box approach that predicts answer correctness by measuring the 2‑Rényi entropy of attention distributions across transformer heads. The entropy is computed from the self‑attention weights and aggregated over tokens in a semantic section (e.g., the question). These per‑head features are fed into a sparse logistic regression classifier. Across five instruction‑tuned LLMs and three QA datasets (general knowledge, multi‑hop, medical), Head Entropy matches or exceeds in‑distribution baselines and substantially outperforms them out‑of‑domain. It also yields a predictive signal even before answer generation and adds negligible inference overhead while maintaining well‑calibrated probabilities.

## Method Summary
Head Entropy operates in two phases: feature extraction and linear classification. For a given input and generated answer, the full attention tensor is captured during a single forward pass (with `output_attentions=True`). For each attention head *k* and token position *t*, the 2‑Rényi entropy is computed as \(H_k(t) = -\log\left(\sum_j A_{k,t,j}^2\right)\), where \(A_k\) is the head’s softmax attention matrix. These per‑token entropies are averaged over all tokens belonging to a semantic section (e.g., the question or context) to produce a single value \(\bar{H}_k\) per head. The vector \([\bar{H}_1, \dots, \bar{H}_m]\) (with *m* total heads) becomes the feature vector. An ℓ1‑regularized logistic regression (scikit‑learn’s `LogisticRegression` with `penalty='l1'` and `C=0.1`) is trained on labeled examples to predict binary correctness (exact match or F1 threshold). At inference, the trained model outputs a probability score. The method adds less than 1 % overhead beyond the standard forward pass and achieves strong calibration.

## Key Results
- Matches or exceeds in‑distribution baselines; out‑of‑domain AUROC improves by **+8.5 %** on average over the strongest baseline (Lookback Lens ratio).  
- Using only question/context tokens yields a pre‑generation signal with average **+17.7 %** AUROC improvement over the hidden‑state baseline.  
- Well‑calibrated probabilities (low Expected Calibration Error) and minimal inference overhead (< 1 %).  

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Jacobian norm equals 2‑Rényi entropy
The attention weights are produced by a softmax over query‑key logits. The Jacobian of the softmax, \(J = \text{diag}(p) - pp^\top\), has a Schatten‑2 (Frobenius) norm that is mathematically equivalent to the negative logarithm of the squared L₂ norm of the probability vector \(p\), i.e., \(H_2(p) = -\log(\sum_i p_i^2)\). Thus, measuring 2‑Rényi entropy is a direct proxy for the sensitivity of the attention pattern to parameter updates. The core assumption is that the Jacobian norm accurately quantifies how much a gradient step will alter an attention distribution.

### Mechanism 2: Gradient descent suppresses high‑entropy patterns
During training, cross‑entropy loss with gradient descent exerts downward pressure on attention pattern entropy because high‑entropy patterns have large Jacobian norms and are highly sensitive to small logit changes. Optimisation therefore regularises the model toward low‑entropy (stable) attention distributions. After training, any generation that triggers a high‑entropy attention pattern suggests the model encountered a context not well‑represented in the training data, leading to unstable representations and a higher chance of error.

### Mechanism 3: Sparse logistic regression on head‑wise entropy provides a robust, generalizable signal
Aggregating per‑head 2‑Rényi entropy over question/context tokens creates an order‑invariant feature vector. An ℓ1‑regularised linear classifier automatically selects the most predictive heads and yields well‑calibrated probabilities. The entropy‑based features are less tied to specific positional cues than location‑based approaches (e.g., Lookback Lens), which explains their superior out‑of‑domain generalisation. Using only the question tokens already gives a strong pre‑generation signal, confirming that the entropy pattern is established before the answer is produced.

## Foundational Learning
- **Transformer Self‑Attention**: Each token computes a weighted sum over all previous tokens using attention scores. *Why needed*: Head Entropy uses the resulting attention weight distribution. *Quick check*: What is the shape of the attention matrix for a sequence of length *T* in a causal LM? (Answer: \(T \times T\).)
- **Softmax Function**: Converts raw logits into a probability distribution. Its Jacobian governs sensitivity of outputs to input changes. *Why needed*: The theoretical link between entropy and Jacobian norm arises from softmax’s derivative. *Quick check*: Write entries of the softmax Jacobian: \(J_{ij} = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)\).)
- **α‑Rényi Entropy**: A family of entropy measures; the 2‑Rényi entropy is \(H_2(p) = -\log(\sum_i p_i^2)\). *Why needed*: Head Entropy uses \(H_2\) as a measure of attention spread. *Quick check*: For a uniform distribution over *K* categories, Shannon entropy \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) are equal and both equal \(\log K\).)
- **Sparse Logistic Regression**: Logistic regression with an ℓ1 penalty to shrink some coefficients to zero. *Why needed*: It selects which attention heads matter and provides interpretable, well‑calibrated predictions. *Quick check*: As you increase the regularisation strength λ, the number of non‑zero coefficients decreases.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input → Tokenization → LLM forward pass (with attention capture) → Per‑token entropy computation → Section averaging → Feature vector construction → Sparse logistic regression → Correctness score.
- **Critical path**: The pipeline must preserve correct token indices for semantic sections; entropy is computed from the raw softmax outputs of each head; the feature vector is a simple concatenation of average entropies across all heads.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Entropy vs. location: Entropy is position‑invariant, improving robustness to context length and style changes; location features may overfit to positional patterns.
  - Linear vs. non‑linear: Logistic regression is data‑efficient, interpretable and well‑calibrated; a non‑linear model might capture interactions but risks overfitting and loss of calibration.
  - ℓ1 regularisation: Enforces sparsity, automatically selecting predictive heads and reducing overfitting, especially for high‑dimensional feature vectors (1000+ heads).
  - Section aggregation: Using only question/context tokens reduces noise, memory and avoids answer‑stage artifacts, enabling pre‑generation prediction.
- **Failure signatures**:
  1. **Domain gap** – AUROC drops sharply on a new dataset (e.g., medical) despite good in‑domain results, indicating feature distribution shift.
  2. **Poor calibration** – Expected Calibration Error (ECE) is high (> 0.2), meaning probability outputs are unreliable.
  3. **Incoherent head selection** – ℓ1 regularisation leaves most heads non‑zero or SHAP values show mixed signs, suggesting the signal is weak or noisy.
  4. **No separation** – Entropy distributions for correct and incorrect examples overlap heavily, causing classifier performance near random.
- **First three experiments**:
  1. **Uniform entropy sanity check** – Feed a uniform attention distribution (all \(p_i = 1/T\)) to the entropy code; verify \(H_2 = \log(T)\) (e.g., ~4.61 for \(T=100\)).
  2. **Baseline replication** – On 500 TriviaQA examples, implement both Lookback Lens ratio and Head Entropy; confirm Head Entropy’s AUROC exceeds baseline by > 0.05.
  3. **Pre‑generation test** – Train logistic regression using only question/context token entropies; evaluate on held‑out questions; expect AUROC ≥ 0.70, matching the paper’s +17.7% relative gain.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Which head combinations yield the most transferable signal?** The current method uses all heads, but domain‑specific weighting limits cross‑domain performance; identifying robust head subsets remains future work.
- **Why do different attention heads have positive or negative contributions?** Shapley value analysis shows mixed signs, and the theoretical framework does not explain the functional roles behind these opposing effects.
- **Can the supervised data requirement be eliminated?** Reliance on labeled correctness data is a practical barrier; developing a zero‑shot criterion that matches supervised performance is an open challenge.

## Limitations
- **Token boundary ambiguity** – The exact delimiting of semantic sections (question, context, answer) is unspecified, especially for models without an explicit “thinking” phase; this critically affects pre‑generation feature extraction.
- **Layer aggregation uncertainty** – It is unclear whether all layers’ heads are used or a subset; the feature‑vector dimension impacts sparsity and out‑of‑domain generalisation.
- **Generalisation scope** – OOD gains are demonstrated only on QA datasets; it is unknown whether entropy signals hold for other tasks (e.g., code generation, creative writing) or different model architectures.

## Confidence
- **Core predictive claim** (Head Entropy predicts correctness): **High** – Robust cross‑dataset AUROC improvements and calibration are reported.
- **Mechanistic link** (Jacobian norm → training stability): **Medium** – Theoretical derivation is sound, but empirical validation across training checkpoints is limited to one model.
- **OOD generalisation advantage** over location‑based methods: **High** – Consistent +8.5 % AUROC boost suggests entropy features are less dataset‑specific.
