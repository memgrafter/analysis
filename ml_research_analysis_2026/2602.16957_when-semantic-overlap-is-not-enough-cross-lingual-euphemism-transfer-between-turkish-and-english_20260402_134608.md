# When Semantic Overlap Is Not Enough: Cross-Lingual Euphemism Transfer Between Turkish and English

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16957
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16957
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** Zero-shot EN→TR transfer yields high F1 (up to 0.90), while TR→EN yields low (as low as 0.18); semantic overlap insufficient, fine-tuning degrades cross-lingual performance, label distribution alignment affects NOPET results.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates cross-lingual transfer in euphemism detection between English (high-resource) and Turkish (low-resource) using XLM-R. The authors expand the Turkish dataset and categorize Potentially Euphemistic Terms (PETs) into Overlapping (OPETs) and Non-Overlapping (NOPETs) subsets based on functional and semantic equivalence. Zero-shot transfer experiments reveal a pronounced asymmetry: English-to-Turkish transfer remains strong (e.g., Employment F1=0.90), while Turkish-to-English transfer degrades significantly (e.g., Employment F1=0.36, Politics F1=0.18), even for OPETs. Counterintuitively, training on English NOPETs sometimes improves performance on Turkish NOPETs. A comparison with zero-shot GPT-4o shows its apparent NOPET advantage stems from prediction bias, whereas fine-tuned XLM-R’s patterns reflect genuine constraints from semantic overlap and resource imbalance. The findings demonstrate that semantic overlap is insufficient for positive transfer, especially from low- to high-resource languages.

## Method Summary
The study uses XLM-RoBERTa (base) for binary classification of euphemistic vs. literal uses of Potentially Euphemistic Terms (PETs). The English dataset (Gavidia et al., 2022) contains 71 PETs; the Turkish dataset is expanded to 70 PETs using SketchEngine’s Turkish Web 2020 Corpus. PETs are annotated as OPETs (semantically/pragmatically equivalent across languages) or NOPETs (non-equivalent) with high inter-annotator agreement (κ=0.96). Instances are split at the sentence level (not PET‑grouped) to avoid lexical leakage. Models are fine‑tuned via 10‑fold cross‑validation using AdamW (learning rate 1e‑5), batch size 4, up to 30 epochs with early stopping (patience=5). The primary evaluation metric is F1‑score. Zero‑shot transfer is assessed by fine‑tuning on a source language subset (EN/TR × OPET/NOPET) and evaluating on all target subsets without further adaptation. A frozen XLM‑R baseline with logistic regression is also reported.

## Key Results
- **Asymmetric zero‑shot transfer:** EN→TR yields high F1 (e.g., Employment 0.90), while TR→EN yields low F1 (e.g., Employment 0.36, Politics 0.18), even for OPETs.
- **Fine‑tuning can degrade cross‑lingual performance:** Training on English OPETs reduces F1 on Turkish OPETs compared to the frozen baseline (0.68 vs. 0.72).
- **Label distribution alignment influences NOPET transfer:** EN→TR transfer to Turkish NOPETs (F1 0.70) exceeds the frozen baseline (0.68) and surpasses OPET transfer (0.68), aligning with the English‑trained model’s bias toward predicting euphemism.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Resource Imbalance and Baseline Proficiency Drives Asymmetry
- **Claim:** Transfer asymmetry arises primarily from resource imbalance and baseline language proficiency in the pre‑trained model, not semantic overlap alone.
- **Mechanism:** XLM‑R’s pre‑training data is heavily skewed toward English (high‑resource). Models fine‑tuned on English develop robust euphemism detection representations that generalize better to Turkish (low‑resource) due to the model’s stronger foundational understanding of English. Conversely, Turkish‑trained models lack a sufficiently rich pre‑trained foundation to generalize to English, even for overlapping concepts.
- **Core assumption:** Cross‑lingual transfer efficacy is bounded by the relative strength of the source language’s representation in the multilingual model’s pre‑training, overridden by fine‑tuning.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]; [section 5.2]; corpus (weak).
- **Break condition:** If transfer were symmetric, the Δ between EN→TR and TR→EN F1 scores would be minimal. The observed large asymmetry (>0.5 F1 in some categories) supports the mechanism.

### Mechanism 2: Label Distribution Alignment Inflates NOPET Transfer
- **Claim:** Label distribution alignment between source training data and target test data can artificially inflate or deflate transfer performance, especially for NOPETs.
- **Mechanism:** A model fine‑tuned on a source subset learns a prediction bias (e.g., higher euphemism probability) that may coincidentally match the label distribution of a target subset, leading to spuriously high performance. This effect is pronounced for NOPETs, which have more variable distributions.
- **Core assumption:** Model predictions are influenced by the prior (label distribution) learned during fine‑tuning, not just semantic alignment.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 5.1] & [Table 5]; [section 5.2]; [section 5.4] (GPT‑4o).
- **Break condition:** If performance were purely based on semantic signal, OPET/NOPET performance differences should be consistent regardless of source language. The observed reversal (NOPET advantage in one direction, not the other) indicates distributional effects.

### Mechanism 3: Fine‑Tuning Overwrites Cross‑Lingual Representations
- **Claim:** Task‑specific fine‑tuning can degrade cross‑lingual generalization by overwriting language‑agnostic representations with language‑specific features.
- **Mechanism:** Fine‑tuning optimizes the model for classification on the source language’s specific lexical and contextual patterns. This specialization reduces the model’s reliance on shared cross‑lingual representations present in the frozen model, harming zero‑shot transfer to a different language.
- **Core assumption:** The frozen XLM‑R contains more robust, language‑neutral features for the euphemism concept than a model fine‑tuned on a single language’s data.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 5.2] (EN‑OPET to TR‑OPET drop vs. baseline); [section 5.2]; [section 6.3] (successful transfer cases align with conceptually similar framing).
- **Break condition:** If fine‑tuning always improved cross‑lingual transfer, all EN→TR and TR→EN scores would be ≥ frozen baseline. The systematic degradation in most transfer conditions confirms the mechanism.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Potentially Euphemistic Terms (PETs)**
  - **Why needed here:** The entire analysis depends on identifying and categorizing PETs as the unit of study. Understanding that a PET is a context‑sensitive term (euphemistic in some contexts, literal in others) is fundamental to the task formulation.
  - **Quick check:** Given the sentence "The company will downsize its workforce," is "downsize" a PET? What contextual feature determines its classification?
- **Concept: Cross‑lingual Semantic/Pragmatic Equivalence (OPET vs. NOPET)**
  - **Why needed here:** This is the paper’s core explanatory variable. Distinguishing between terms that share a euphemistic function (OPET) and those that don’t (NOPET) is necessary to test the "semantic overlap" hypothesis.
  - **Quick check:** For the PET "passed away" (English), is "vefat etmek" (Turkish) an OPET or NOPET? Justify based on the annotation guidelines’ criteria of functional equivalence.
- **Concept: Label Distribution Alignment / Prediction Bias**
  - **Why needed here:** It explains counterintuitive results (e.g., better transfer to NOPETs). A learner must understand that performance is a function of both semantic matching *and* statistical alignment of training/test priors.
  - **Quick check:** If an English‑trained model has an 80% euphemism prediction bias, on which Turkish subset (OPET or NOPET) would you expect higher F1, assuming equal semantic alignment? Why?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** English/Turkish PETs datasets (instance‑level, OPET/NOPET) -> XLM‑RoBERTa (base) -> Fine‑tuning (10‑fold CV, AdamW, lr=1e‑5, early stopping) -> Zero‑shot evaluation (F1) + Frozen baseline (Logistic Regression on frozen embeddings).

**Critical path:**
1. Obtain & preprocess PETs datasets, ensuring balanced euphemistic/literal instances per PET.
2. Annotate PETs as OPET/NOPET using guidelines (Appendix B).
3. For each source subset (EN-OPET, EN-NOPET, TR-OPET, TR-NOPET): run 10‑fold fine‑tuning.
4. For each fine‑tuned model: evaluate zero‑shot on all 4 target subsets; record F1.
5. Analyze: compare (a) EN→TR vs. TR→EN, (b) OPET‑target vs. NOPET‑target under same source, (c) vs. frozen baseline.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Instance‑level vs. PET‑level splits:** Instance‑level splits (same PET can appear in train/test) risk lexical memorization; PET‑grouped splits would be stricter but harder with limited data.
- **Binary classification vs. graded intent:** Binary label ignores euphemistic strength or speaker intent; multi‑label/regression might capture nuance but complicates transfer analysis.
- **Single model (XLM‑R) focus:** Results may be XLM‑R‑specific (e.g., vocabulary, pretraining corpus); generalizability requires testing other architectures (mBERT, mT5).

**Failure signatures:**
- **Lexical memorization:** Model predicts “euphemistic” for a term like “dört kollu” in any context, indicating reliance on surface form over contextual disambiguation.
- **Cultural gap:** Systematic false negatives on culturally specific PETs (e.g., “between jobs”, “second base”) when source language lacks the concept.
- **High variance on NOPET training:** Large standard deviations across CV folds for NOPET‑trained models, reflecting heterogeneous patterns.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replicate asymmetry:** Train on EN‑OPET/EN‑NOPET, test on TR‑OPET/TR‑NOPET. Confirm EN→TR performs better than TR→EN (especially on OPETs).
2. **Test label distribution hypothesis:** For fixed target (e.g., TR‑NOPET), compare performance from EN‑OPET vs. EN‑NOPET source models. Expect EN‑OPET (closer distribution to TR‑NOPET?) to perform better if distribution alignment is key.
3. **Validate fine‑tuning degradation:** For a specific OPET target (e.g., TR‑OPET), compare F1 from (a) frozen baseline, (b) EN‑OPET‑trained model, (c) TR‑OPET‑trained model. Expect (b) < (a) and (c) >> (a), demonstrating fine‑tuning harms cross‑lingual but helps in‑domain.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: Resource Imbalance vs. Typological Distance
- **Question:** What is the primary driver of the pronounced transfer asymmetry (EN→TR strong, TR→EN weak): resource imbalance, typological/morphological distance, or a combination?
- **Basis in paper:** Authors attribute asymmetry to resource imbalance but note typological differences cannot be disentangled due to single language pair.
- **Why unresolved:** Only one language pair studied; confound between resource and structure remains.
- **What evidence would resolve:** Evaluate multiple language pairs varying both resource levels and typological features (e.g., English‑Spanish, Turkish‑Azerbaijani, Chinese‑Japanese) using same OPET/NOPET framework.

### Open Question 2: Generalizability of Label Distribution Alignment
- **Question:** Is label distribution alignment a generalizable mechanism that can artificially boost zero‑shot transfer for low‑resource languages in other tasks with imbalanced labels?
- **Basis in paper:** EN→TR NOPET transfer exceeds baseline and is linked to alignment of English‑trained model’s euphemism bias with higher euphemism rate in Turkish NOPETs.
- **Why unresolved:** Observed as explanatory pattern in this dataset; not tested as a general hypothesis.
- **What evidence would resolve:** Replicate on other classification tasks (e.g., sentiment, toxicity) with controlled label distributions; test if transfer gains correlate with distribution alignment independent of semantic content.

### Open Question 3: Conceptual vs. Superficial Transfer in NOPETs
- **Question:** Does the OPET/NOPET framework reveal meaningful constraints on *conceptual* transfer for figurative language, or does it primarily capture superficial lexical/distributional effects? Can EN‑NOPET training ever learn a generalizable euphemism concept that applies to TR‑NOPETs?
- **Basis in paper:** EN‑NOPET training yields poor TR‑OPET performance but decent TR‑NOPET performance; the success on TR‑NOPET is not deeply explained.
- **Why unresolved:** No diagnostic test isolates conceptual learning from memorization or distribution effects; positive TR‑NOPET result lacks clear interpretation.
- **What evidence would resolve:** Probe with novel held‑out NOPETs sharing no lexical features with training but requiring same pragmatic function; or use layer‑wise analysis to see if successful transfer aligns representations in semantic/pragmatic space rather than lexical space.

### Open Question 4: Model Architecture vs. Fine‑Tuning Paradigm
- **Question:** To what extent do observed transfer dynamics stem from XLM‑R’s specific pretraining regime versus a fundamental limitation of standard fine‑tuning for cross‑lingual pragmatic transfer?
- **Basis in paper:** Comparison with zero‑shot GPT‑4o shows different patterns: GPT‑4o’s NOPET advantage is bias‑driven, while XLM‑R’s reflect semantic overlap and resource constraints. However, only one encoder and one generative model are compared.
- **Why unresolved:** Unknown if asymmetry and NOPET effects persist across other multilingual architectures (e.g., mBERT, mT5) under fine‑tuning.
- **What evidence would resolve:** Repeat the OPET/NOPET zero‑shot transfer protocol with a suite of multilingual encoders (mBERT, mT5, XLM‑V) and possibly instruction‑tuned variants, controlling for size. If TR→EN OPET failure persists, it suggests a deeper limitation.

### Open Question 5: Domain Sparsity and Cultural Specificity
- **Question:** Can category‑level transfer performance be improved by explicitly aligning source and target domains (e.g., via in‑domain data selection or auxiliary tasks), or is the sparsity and cultural specificity of euphemism domains an insurmountable barrier?
- **Basis in paper:** Category‑level analysis (Table 6) shows extreme variance and failure in low‑resource categories (e.g., TR→EN Politics F1=0.18 with only 11 TR Politics instances). Authors suggest domain‑specific alignment but evidence limited by sparsity.
- **Why unresolved:** Unclear whether poor Politics transfer is due to cultural non‑overlap (missing Turkish euphemisms for “regime change”), data scarcity, or both.
- **What evidence would resolve:** Targeted study on 2‑3 challenging, sparse categories (Politics, Illness) across multiple language pairs with similar sparsity, using data augmentation, category‑specific prompts/adapters, and evaluation on unseen terms. If transfer remains poor despite sufficient data, it supports cultural specificity.

## Limitations
- Exact CV fold assignments and random seeds are unreleased, risking replication variance, especially for heterogeneous NOPET subsets.
- Findings may be XLM‑R‑specific due to its pretraining skew; generalizability to other multilingual architectures is unknown.
- Resource imbalance and typological distance are confounded; the study cannot isolate which factor drives the asymmetric transfer.
- NOPET category heterogeneity (varying euphemism rates across domains) may drive label distribution effects, making it hard to isolate pure semantic mismatch.

## Confidence
- **Resource‑driven transfer asymmetry:** High
- **Label distribution alignment influencing NOPET transfer:** Medium
- **Fine‑tuning degradation of cross‑lingual generalization:** Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate with PET‑grouped splits:** Rerun key experiments using PET‑level (not instance‑level) splits to confirm results are not inflated by lexical leakage across train/test.
2. **Distribution‑matching ablation:** For EN→TR transfer, deliberately resample EN training data to match the euphemism prevalence of the target TR subset (OPET/NOPET), then measure F1 change. A significant improvement would confirm distribution alignment effects.
3. **Architecture stress‑test:** Repeat the full transfer pipeline with mBERT‑base and mT5‑base to determine if the observed asymmetry and NOPET patterns persist, or are artifacts of XLM‑R’s pretraining regime.
