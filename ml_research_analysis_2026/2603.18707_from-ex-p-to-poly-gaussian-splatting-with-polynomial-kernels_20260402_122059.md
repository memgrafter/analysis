# From ex(p) to poly: Gaussian Splatting with Polynomial Kernels

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18707
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18707
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A ReLU-activated polynomial approximation replaces the exponential kernel in Gaussian Splatting, enabling 4–15% speedups with negligible quality loss (<0.5 PSNR drop) while remaining fully compatible with existing datasets.

## Executive Summary
The high computational cost of evaluating the exponential kernel in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is mitigated by approximating it with a low-order polynomial followed by a ReLU activation. This modification introduces a finite support, allowing a tighter, opacity-independent culling radius that aggressively reduces the number of tiles and splats processed during rasterization. Crucially, the polynomial kernel can be applied as a drop-in replacement to models optimized with the original exponential kernel, avoiding the need for re-training. First-order polynomials yield consistent speedups of 4–15% across multiple 3DGS implementations with only minimal perceptual quality degradation; higher-order polynomials can match or exceed the original quality.

## Method Summary
The core method involves fitting polynomial coefficients to the exponential kernel over a uniform distribution of squared quadric values (ρ²), which matches the area-sampled distribution encountered during 2D rasterization. A first-order polynomial `f₁(x) = ReLU(c₀ + c₁·x)` is derived, where the ReLU introduces a zero-crossing at `x = -c₀/c₁`. This zero enables a universal culling radius `t' = √(-c₀/c₁) ≈ 2.1σ`, significantly tighter than the original 3σ exponential cutoff. For per-splat culling, an opacity-aware radius `t = √((ϵ - o·c₀)/(o·c₁))` (with ϵ=1/255) is used. The polynomial kernel is applied at inference time without altering the underlying Gaussian parameters, ensuring backward compatibility with pre-optimized datasets.

## Key Results
- **Performance:** First-order polynomial (`f₁`) kernels yield 4–15% speedups across various 3DGS implementations (vanilla, Mip-Splatting, etc.) due primarily to increased culling efficiency.
- **Quality:** Drop-in use of `f₁` on models trained with the exponential kernel causes negligible quality degradation (<0.5 PSNR drop). Higher-order polynomials (`f₂`, `f₃`) can match or exceed original quality.
- **Compatibility:** The approach requires no re-optimization of existing datasets and integrates seamlessly with standard 3DGS pipelines, including anti-aliasing techniques like Mip-Splatting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### 1. Reduced Per‑Splat Computation
Replacing `exp(-0.5·x)` with a multiply‑add (`c₀ + c₁·x`) and a ReLU comparison drastically lowers the arithmetic cost per quadric evaluation, especially on hardware with efficient ReLU support (e.g., NPUs). The approximation remains accurate over the relevant range `[0, -2·ln(1/255)]`.

### 2. Aggressive Culling via Finite Support
The ReLU causes the polynomial to reach zero at a finite quadric value (≈4.386 for `f₁`), corresponding to a radius of ~2.1σ. This is much tighter than the exponential’s effective 3.3σ cutoff. The resulting universal bound `t'` can be pre‑computed and used to cull entire tiles before per‑pixel work, substantially reducing rasterization workload. An opacity‑aware variant `t` further tightens culling per splat.

### 3. Backward Compatibility
Because the polynomial approximates the same exponential kernel used during original dataset optimization, the pre‑trained Gaussian parameters (means, covariances, opacities, colors) remain valid. No retraining is needed, and the blending order and quadric representation are unchanged.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Quadric Projection**  
*Why needed:* The kernel operates on a 2D quadric `Qᵢ(v)` derived from projecting a 3D Gaussian’s covariance and mean. Understanding this projection is essential because the polynomial is fitted to the distribution of `Q` values encountered during rendering.  
*Quick check:* How does a 3D Gaussian’s covariance Σ and center µ project to the 2D quadric matrix used in splat evaluation?

**Concept: Area‑Sampled Kernel Fitting**  
*Why needed:* Fitting coefficients requires sampling the quadric distribution that matches the *area‑sampled* distribution during rasterization (uniform in squared radius ρ² over the unit disk), not a volumetric sample. This ensures the polynomial accurately approximates the kernel where it matters on the 2D pixel grid.  
*Quick check:* Why is uniform sampling in ρ²∼U(0,1) over a disk the correct strategy for fitting the splat kernel?

**Concept: Tile‑Based Culling Radii**  
*Why needed:* Most speedup comes from culling entire tiles before per‑pixel work. This requires an analytically computable radius `t` (in pixels) that bounds where the kernel’s contribution falls below a threshold (ϵ=1/255). The polynomial’s finite support enables a tighter, universal `t'`.  
*Quick check:* For a first‑order polynomial, what is the universal culling radius `t'`, and how is it derived from the coefficients?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Quadric Computation → Kernel Evaluation → Culling → Anti‑Aliasing → Backward Pass

**Critical path:**  
Quadric (`Q`) → Culling Decision (using `t'` or `t` and `Q` at tile corners) → Kernel Evaluation (`f₁(Q)`) → Alpha Blending

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Polynomial order:* `f₁` is fastest and simplest; `f₂`/`f₃` improve quality fit but increase instruction count and may require more complex root‑finding for culling.
- *Culling strategy:* Universal `t'` is simplest but fails in HDR scenes; opacity‑aware `t` is tighter but requires per‑splat computation.
- *Optimization:* Drop‑in use on `g`‑trained models guarantees compatibility; training from scratch with `f` may improve quality but breaks `g`‑based pipelines.

**Failure signatures:**
- Dark spots in overexposed/bright regions (e.g., `treehill`) when using universal `t'` with color clamping.
- Blocky/stepped edges on large, low‑opacity splats if only tighter `g` culling is used without the polynomial’s zero‑crossing.
- Potential performance regression with higher‑order polynomials on highly optimized CUDA backends due to increased arithmetic.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline integration:** Integrate `f₁` and `t'` into a vanilla 3DGS implementation; measure frame time on `bicycle` and verify no corruption.
2. **Culling isolation:** Compare three configs on the same model/scene: (a) `g` + 3σ, (b) `g` + `t'`, (c) `f₁` + `t'`. Isolate polynomial vs. culling contributions.
3. **Quality stress test:** Render high‑frequency scenes (e.g., `bonsai`) with `f₁` and `f₃` vs. `g`; compute PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS and perform A/B visual inspection.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does polynomial kernel efficiency compare across NPU architectures (Tensor Cores, Neural Engine, Ascend) versus conventional GPUs? The paper only discusses theoretical NPU compatibility without actual implementation or benchmarking.
2. Does enforcing monotonicity in quadratic kernels (constraining `c₂ < 0`) improve quality or stability? The authors propose this but provide no experimental validation.
3. What are the impacts of end‑to‑end training with polynomial kernels on splat statistics (count, size, opacity), training dynamics, and final quality? Only inference‑time drop‑in use is thoroughly evaluated.
4. Can direct optimization with polynomial kernels mitigate the color‑clamping artifacts seen in HDR scenes (e.g., `treehill`) by altering splat properties? This is speculated but not tested.

## Limitations
- Fitting assumes an area‑sampled quadric distribution; actual rendering distributions may vary, potentially causing localized quality drops not captured by aggregate metrics.
- The universal culling radius `t'` fails in high‑dynamic‑range scenes with color clamping, requiring per‑splat opacity‑aware culling (`t`) which adds overhead.
- Higher‑order polynomials (`f₂`, `f₃`) may negate culling gains on state‑of‑the‑art CUDA backends due to increased instruction count.
- The approach lacks independent validation from related work; the polynomial‑ReLU combination remains novel but unconfirmed by other studies.

## Confidence
- **Computational efficiency (polynomial reduces per‑splat cost):** High
- **Aggressive culling (finite support enables tighter bounds):** Medium
- **Backward compatibility (drop‑in use on existing datasets):** High
- **General performance gains (4–15% improvement):** Medium
