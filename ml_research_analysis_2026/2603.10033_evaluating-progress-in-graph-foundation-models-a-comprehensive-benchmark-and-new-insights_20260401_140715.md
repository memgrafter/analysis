# Evaluating Progress in Graph Foundation Models: A Comprehensive Benchmark and New Insights
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.10033
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10033
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: The paper proposes a benchmark that jointly assesses topic and format domain shifts for graph foundation models, revealing that multi-domain pre-training yields mixed improvements, with topic diversity generally helping but dataset-level invariances outweighing topic proximity; format diversification benefits heterophilic and textual graphs but harms transfer to heterogeneous and dynamic ones; and text-assisted GFMs degrade severely without text during pre-training.

## Executive Summary
Graph foundation models (GFMs) have emerged as powerful tools for transfer learning across graph datasets, yet evaluation has largely focused on single-axis domain shifts (e.g., only topic). This paper introduces a comprehensive benchmark that evaluates GFMs along two orthogonal domain axes: *topic* (semantic domain of nodes/edges) and *format* (structural schema such as heterogeneity, temporality). Using 33 datasets spanning 7 topics and 6 formats, the authors assess eight GFMs under four carefully designed settings that separate semantic from representational generalization in few-shot node, edge, and graph classification. Results show that pre-training on multiple topics often helps but benefits do not correlate with semantic proximity; format mixing aids some formats (heterophilic, textual) but harms others (heterogeneous, dynamic); and text-assisted GFMs suffer catastrophic failure when text is absent during pre-training. The findings underscore the need for robust multi-domain knowledge integration and format-aware architectural designs, and the benchmark is released as a standard for future GFM development.

## Method Summary
The benchmark operationalizes two graph domain axes: *topic* (semantic category like citation, molecular) and *format* (schema traits like homogeneous/heterogeneous, static/dynamic, presence of text). Four evaluation settings are defined: (I) pre-train on diverse topics/formats, adapt to unseen datasets; (II) same pre-training but adapt to seen datasets; (III) pre-train on a single topic (citation), adapt to other topics; (IV) pre-train on a base format (homogeneous, homophilic, static, text‐free), adapt to other formats. Pre-training uses self-supervised objectives on unlabeled graphs; downstream tasks are 1-shot and 5-shot classification, where a frozen encoder is paired with a small task adapter. Reproduction involves installing dependencies (PyTorch Geometric, etc.), preprocessing 33 datasets into a unified canonical format (handling heterogeneity, dynamic snapshots, textual attributes), running model-specific pre-training for each setting, then evaluating via few-shot adaptation. Missing details include exact pre-training dataset subsets per model and the nondeterministic text summarization process for text-assisted GFMs. Known failure modes are OOM on large graphs, canonicalization mismatches, and reproducibility issues due to LLM API calls.

## Key Results
- Multi-domain pre-training yields mixed improvements across downstream tasks.
- Broader topic diversity generally helps, but dataset-level invariances outweigh coarse topic proximity.
- Format diversification benefits heterophilic and textual graphs but harms transfer to heterogeneous and dynamic graphs.
- Text-assisted GFMs (e.g., GraphCLIP, G2P2) suffer severe performance degradation when text is omitted during pre-training.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Topic Domain Diversity in Pre-training
- **Claim:** Broader topic diversity in pre-training generally improves downstream adaptation across various topics, but the benefit does not strongly correlate with semantic proximity between pre-training and downstream topics.
- **Mechanism:** Pre-training on multiple topic domains (e.g., citation, social, molecular) exposes the model to a wider variety of node/edge semantics and feature distributions. This encourages learning representations that capture more universal graph structural patterns rather than topic-specific shortcuts. Adaptation then requires aligning these universal patterns to the specific node/edge features and label semantics of a new topic.
- **Core assumption:** Some graph structural knowledge (e.g., community structure, connectivity patterns) is invariant across topic domains and can be transferred.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "broader topic diversity generally helps but dataset-level invariances outweigh topic proximity."
  - [section 5.3] "expanding pre-training from a single citation topic to multi-topic domains is generally beneficial... the benefit of multi-topic pre-training does not appear to correlate with topic proximity."
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; the corpus shows related work on domain diversity but this specific mechanism is a new insight from the paper's experiments.
- **Break condition:** If the downstream topic's data characteristics (feature scales, label definitions, graph statistics) differ too drastically from all pre-training topics, even broad topic diversity may fail. The paper finds dataset-level invariances matter more than coarse topic labels.

### Mechanism 2: Format Domain Mixing Effects
- **Claim:** Expanding format diversity during pre-training benefits adaptation to target formats with smaller representational gaps from a canonical base format (homogeneous, homophilic, static, text-free), but can harm adaptation to formats with larger gaps (heterogeneous, dynamic).
- **Mechanism:** Pre-training on a diverse set of format domains forces the model to develop representations robust to variations in topology (hetero/homo), connection patterns (homo/heterophilic), temporal dynamics, and attribute schemas. For formats that are "closer" to the base format (e.g., heterophilic, relational, text-attributed graphs where text is present during pre-training), this exposure builds useful invariances. For formats that require fundamentally different inductive biases (e.g., heterogeneous graphs needing type-aware message passing, dynamic graphs needing temporal memory), mixing can introduce interference, as a single unified objective may obscure format-specific signals.
- **Core assumption:** There is a hierarchy of representational gap between graph formats; some shifts are more disruptive to a shared encoder than others.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Format diversification benefits heterophilic and textual graphs but harms transfer to heterogeneous and dynamic ones."
  - [section 5.4] "multi-format pre-training is not uniformly helpful... for heterophilic, relational, and text-attributed graphs, Setting I [multi-format] typically outperforms Setting IV [base-only]... for heterogeneous and dynamic graphs, the pattern flips."
  - [corpus] No direct corpus anchor; this is a primary experimental finding.
- **Break condition:** When the target format's inductive biases are fundamentally incompatible with those of the base format and the pre-training objective does not have explicit mechanisms to preserve/activate format-specific computation (e.g., separate type embeddings, temporal modules), mixing in other formats will likely degrade performance.

### Mechanism 3: Text Modality Dependency
- **Claim:** Graph foundation models that leverage textual signals (e.g., node attributes, summaries) during pre-training experience severe performance degradation when adapting to text-attributed graphs if text was absent during pre-training.
- **Mechanism:** Text-assisted GFMs (e.g., GraphCLIP, G2P2) align graph representations with a text encoder's output space via contrastive or other cross-modal objectives. If no text is seen during pre-training, the text encoder remains uninitiated or randomly initialized. When text becomes available downstream, the model must jointly learn to process text and align it to graph structure *and* solve the downstream task, which is a much more complex, under-specified problem with limited few-shot data. The textual modality then acts as noise or an uninformative signal rather than a bridge.
- **Core assumption:** Cross-modal alignment objectives require co-training on both modalities; a frozen or poorly initialized text encoder cannot be effectively aligned from scratch in a few-shot downstream setting.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Text-assisted GFMs degrade severely without text during pre-training."
  - [section 5.4] "When adapting to text-attributed graphs, GFMs with text encoders... pre-trained on the base format (Setting IV) generally tend to underperform... the additional modality behaves more like noise."
  - [section C] Table 1 shows which models use PLM/LLM and textual inputs.
- **Break condition:** The presence of any textual signal during pre-training, even if weak or synthetic, might mitigate this. The break condition is the complete absence of any text-modality training signal in the pre-training corpus for text-reliant architectures.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Two-Dimensional Graph Domain Shift**
  - Why needed here: The paper's entire premise is that graph domains are defined by both **topic** (semantics of nodes/edges) and **format** (schema/structure). Understanding this dichotomy is prerequisite to designing their benchmark and interpreting results. One-dimensional evaluation (varying only topic) is insufficient.
  - Quick check question: Can two graphs from the same topic domain (e.g., citation networks) belong to different format domains? (Yes: Cora is homogeneous, DBLP is heterogeneous).
- **Concept: Self-Supervised Multi-Domain Pre-training**
  - Why needed here: This is the defining pre-training paradigm for GFMs in this paper. It's distinct from single-domain pre-training or supervised pre-training. The benchmark specifically evaluates models pre-trained on *unlabeled* graphs from *multiple* topic and format domains.
  - Quick check question: What distinguishes a GFM (per this paper's scope) from a standard self-supervised graph model? (Pre-training on a broad, multi-domain corpus of *unlabeled* graphs).
- **Concept: Few-Shot Cross-Domain Adaptation**
  - Why needed here: The downstream evaluation protocol is K-shot (K=1,5) learning on *unseen* or *seen* datasets. This tests the transferability of pre-trained representations with minimal task-specific labels. It's a core part of the GFM pipeline being benchmarked.
  - Quick check question: In Setting I, are the downstream datasets seen during pre-training? (No, they are specifically held-out unseen datasets).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1.  Data Ingestion & Canonicalization: Raw datasets → unified `Data` objects with standardized fields (`x`, `edge_index`, `node_type`, `edge_type`, `raw_texts`). Handles heterogeneity flattening, dynamic snapshot creation, directionality.
  2.  Pre-training Engine: Batches of canonical graphs from `D_pre` (multi-domain). Applies model-specific sampling (whole-graph, ego-graphs). Optimizes self-supervised objective (contrastive, reconstruction, prediction) on the GFM encoder.
  3.  Downstream Adapter: For each `D_down` task, constructs K-shot episodes. Encoder frozen; trains a lightweight task head `f_φ` (or prompts) on few labeled instances.
  4.  Evaluation Controller: Orchestrates the four Settings (I–IV). Manages dataset splits for pre-training and downstream, enforcing topic/format shifts.
- **Critical path:** Raw Datasets → Canonicalization → Unified Graph Corpus → Multi-Domain Pre-training → Pre-trained Encoder → Setting-specific Downstream Split → K-shot Adaptation → Performance Metrics.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Sampling Strategy: Whole-graph vs ego-graphs (structural context vs memory/batch diversity).
  - Format Handling: Homogenizing heterogeneous graphs (lose type info) vs requiring all models to support heterogeneity (limits coverage); paper chooses former for fairness.
  - Seen vs Unseen: Setting II (seen) measures interpolation; Setting I (unseen) measures extrapolation; both needed.
  - Task Head: Freezing encoder + small head vs prompting vs full fine-tuning; paper standardizes frozen encoder + trained head for fair few-shot comparison.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Severe drop in Setting I vs Setting II: poor extrapolation, model memorizes pre-training distribution.
  - Performance inversion between Setting IV (base pre-train) and Setting I (multi-format) on heterogeneous/dynamic targets: format mixing introduces harmful interference.
  - Text-assisted models (G2P2, GraphCLIP) failing in Setting IV on textual graphs: text encoder not properly initialized due to absent text during pre-training.
  - Consistently low performance on a specific format (e.g., heterogeneous): fundamental architectural limitation.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  Sanity Check - Seen vs Random: Run GCN in Setting II (seen). Expect strong performance, confirming pipeline.
  2.  Baseline - Single-Topic Pre-train (Setting III): Pre-train MDGPT only on citation networks, evaluate on molecular graph (BZR). Expect poor performance, showing need for topic diversity.
  3.  Core Test - Format Shift (Setting IV vs I): Pre-train SAMGPT on base-format only (IV) and multi-format (I). Evaluate both on heterophilic graph (Wisconsin); expect I > IV. Repeat on heterogeneous graph (ogbn-mag); possible inversion (IV > I).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do current GFMs fail to integrate complementary knowledge from diverse topic and format domains, and what architectural or objective modifications can enable effective cross-domain knowledge consolidation without interference?
2. What specific dataset-level invariances (beyond coarse topic categories) predict successful cross-dataset transfer, and how can pre-training objectives be designed to learn such invariances explicitly?
3. Why does format diversification during pre-training harm transfer to heterogeneous and dynamic graphs despite benefiting other formats, and what format-aware mechanisms can prevent this negative transfer?
4. Can text-assisted GFMs be made robust to the absence of textual content during pre-training, or must they always be pre-trained with text? What architectural changes would allow flexible use of text when available without degradation when absent?
5. How can GFMs better exploit cross-format alignment mechanisms to handle large format shifts, particularly for heterogeneous and dynamic graphs, and what are the most effective ways to incorporate format-specific inductive biases without sacrificing universality?

## Limitations
- Pre-training data composition per model for Settings I/IV is unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Text-assisted GFMs rely on large LLMs for summary generation; API access and nondeterminism threaten reproducibility.
- Graph homogenization for heterogeneous datasets may discard type information, potentially biasing format-shift conclusions; additionally, OOM failures on large graphs limit coverage and may skew comparative results.

## Confidence
- Topic Domain Diversity: Medium
- Format Domain Mixing Effects: Medium
- Text Modality Dependency: High
