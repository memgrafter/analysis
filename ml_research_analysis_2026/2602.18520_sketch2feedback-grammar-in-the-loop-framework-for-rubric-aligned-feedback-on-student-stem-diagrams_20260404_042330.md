# Sketch2Feedback: Grammar-in-the-Loop Framework for Rubric-Aligned Feedback on Student STEM Diagrams

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.18520  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18520  
- **Reference count**: 16  
- **Primary result**: Grammar-in-the-loop reduces VLM hallucination; end-to-end LMM leads on free-body diagrams (F1 0.471), grammar pipeline excels on circuit schematics (F1 0.329) with perfect actionability.

## Executive Summary
Sketch2Feedback tackles the problem of generating rubric-aligned feedback for student-drawn STEM diagrams, a task where large multimodal models often hallucinate. The framework employs a grammar-in-the-loop pipeline that decomposes the task into four stages: hybrid computer vision perception, symbolic graph construction, domain-specific constraint checking, and a constrained VLM that verbalizes only verified violations. Evaluated on two benchmarks—FBD-10 (free-body diagrams) and Circuit-10 (circuit schematics)—the results show no single architecture dominates: an end-to-end LMM (LLaVA-1.5-7B) achieves higher detection performance on free-body diagrams (micro-F1 0.471) and better feedback quality, while the grammar pipeline (using Qwen2-VL-2B) substantially outperforms on circuit schematics (micro-F1 0.329) and attains perfect actionability (5.0/5). The modular architecture also enables precise failure diagnosis; the high circuit hallucination rate (0.925) is traced to false positives from the classical CV perception module, not VLM confabulation, demonstrating the value of modularity for targeted improvement.

## Method Summary
Sketch2Feedback is implemented as a four-stage grammar-in-the-loop pipeline. Stage 1 uses hybrid classical computer vision (CLAHE, adaptive thresholding, HoughLinesP, contour analysis) to detect diagram primitives (arrows, wires, components, junctions) with bounding boxes. Stage 2 builds a typed symbolic graph from these detections: nodes represent elements (with type and confidence), edges are added based on a spatial proximity threshold (80px). Stage 3 performs domain-specific constraint checking by evaluating predicate functions over the graph against a scenario key, producing a list of verified violation descriptors (e.g., "missing_ground", "wrong_direction_arrow"). Stage 4 employs a constrained VLM (Qwen2-VL-2B) that receives only the input image and the verified violation list to generate natural language feedback; if the list is empty, it outputs "Diagram looks correct." This design confines the VLM to symbolically grounded evidence, limiting hallucination. Baselines include an end‑to‑end LMM (LLaVA-1.5-7B) that directly processes the image and a vision‑only system using Stage 1 with template‑based feedback.

## Key Results
- On FBD-10, end‑to‑end LMM (LLaVA-1.5-7B) achieves higher detection (micro‑F1 0.471) and feedback quality than grammar pipeline (micro‑F1 0.263).
- On Circuit-10, grammar pipeline (Qwen2-VL-2B) substantially outperforms end‑to‑end LMM (micro‑F1 0.329 vs. 0.038) and achieves perfect actionability (5.0/5).
- Grammar pipeline’s high circuit hallucination rate (0.925) is attributed to false positives from its classical CV perception module, not VLM confabulation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of Sketch2Feedback arises from three key mechanisms.

### Separation of perception and reasoning via a constraint‑checking gate
The pipeline restricts the VLM’s input to a list of violations verified by an upstream rule engine. This ensures the VLM can only verbalize errors that have been symbolically validated, preventing it from inventing issues not present in the constraint list. However, if the constraint checker yields false positives, the VLM will amplify these spurious violations. Evidence: The authors localize the high circuit hallucination to Stage 1 false positives, not VLM confabulation, confirming the gate’s role in localizing errors.

### Symbolic graph intermediate representation
Detected primitives are structured into a typed graph (nodes=components/forces, edges=connections). This abstraction enables precise domain‑specific constraints (e.g., force balance, circuit ground) as predicate functions over the graph, which are difficult for end‑to‑end LMMs to learn implicitly. Evidence: The grammar pipeline excels at structural errors (wrong direction, missing ground) while the end‑to‑end LMM performs better on omission errors (missing force), showing the complementary strengths.

### Modular architecture for precise failure diagnosis
Each stage has a delimited responsibility, allowing metrics and errors to be attributed to specific components. This transparency aids debugging and trust in educational deployments. Evidence: The modular design allowed the authors to trace circuit hallucinations to the perception stage, demonstrating the diagnostic value of the architecture.

## Foundational Learning
Key concepts underpinning the framework:

- **Symbolic Graph Representation**  
  *Why needed*: Converts detected image primitives into a structured graph (nodes=elements, edges=spatial connections) to apply domain constraints like “all forces must converge on the body”.  
  *Quick check*: For a circuit with a battery, resistor, and wires connecting them, what would the node types and edge criteria be in the graph G?

- **Classical Computer Vision for Primitive Detection**  
  *Why needed*: Stage 1 uses specific CV techniques (CLAHE, adaptive thresholding, HoughLinesP, contour analysis with solidity/aspect‑ratio filters) to detect arrows, wires, and components without learned models.  
  *Quick check*: Why might HoughLinesP be suitable for detecting wires but not for detecting a diode symbol? What shape‑based feature would you use for a diode?

- **Constraint Satisfaction over Graphs**  
  *Why needed*: The core reasoning uses predicates \(c_i(G,s)\) to check for violations; this symbolic reasoning over the graph state enables precise, discrete constraint checking.  
  *Quick check*: Design a “local constraint” predicate for the error “wrong polarity” in a diode. What nodes/edges in G would it examine, and what condition would it check?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
1. Hybrid Perception (Stage 1): Input raw image; output detected primitives (arrows, wires, components, junctions) with bounding boxes; uses classical CV.  
2. Graph Builder (Stage 2): Input detected primitives; output typed graph \(G=(V,E)\); nodes carry type/confidence; edges added via spatial proximity threshold (80px).  
3. Constraint Checker (Stage 3): Input graph G and scenario key s; output set C of verified violation descriptors; applies domain‑specific predicate functions.  
4. Constrained VLM (Stage 4): Input image x, violation list C, rubric prompt; output natural language feedback y; if C empty, returns “Diagram looks correct.”; uses Qwen2‑VL‑2B.

**Critical path:** Image → Stage 1 detections → Stage 2 graph → Stage 3 constraints → (if non‑empty) Stage 4 VLM → Feedback. The constraint list is the sole gateway to the VLM.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Modularity vs. Recall*: Modular design improves debuggability and precision but sacrifices recall if upstream stages miss errors; end‑to‑end LMMs may have higher recall by inferring errors holistically but hallucinate more.  
- *Classical CV vs. Learned Perception*: Classical CV is lightweight and interpretable but brittle to drawing variations; a learned detector (e.g., YOLO) could improve recall but adds data needs and opacity.  
- *Model size asymmetry*: Grammar pipeline uses a 2B VLM; end‑to‑end uses a 7B model, making performance differences possibly due to architecture, not just scale.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Grammar pipeline*: High hallucination (e.g., circuits: 0.925) indicates Stage 1 false positives or over‑sensitive constraint rules; low detection recall (e.g., FBD missing forces) indicates Stage 1 failure to detect that primitive or Stage 3 rules too strict.  
- *End‑to‑end LMM*: Near‑zero detection on a domain (circuits) suggests the model’s instruction‑tuning distribution lacks that diagram type; high hallucination rate (0.78 on FBDs) indicates confabulation.  
- *Shared bottleneck*: Zero detection on missing component or wrong polarity for both models indicates a fundamental perception failure for those primitives.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. *Isolate Stage 1 Performance*: Evaluate the hybrid CV detector alone against ground‑truth primitive bounding boxes to compute per‑primitive recall/precision.  
2. *Ablate the Constraint Gate*: Run the VLM (Qwen2‑VL‑2B) with the raw image and a generic prompt (no constraint list); compare detection F1, hallucination, and feedback scores to the constrained version.  
3. *Domain Transfer Test*: Apply the grammar pipeline trained/evaluated on FBDs to Circuit‑10 and vice versa without rule changes; measure F1 drop and hallucination spike to test rule portability and perception robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can perception for STEM diagram understanding be improved, particularly for detecting missing components and polarity errors?**  
   *Basis*: Both the grammar pipeline and end‑to‑end LMM fail to detect missing components and wrong polarity.  
   *Why unresolved*: These error types are critical but currently undetected, limiting system coverage.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Train a learned detector (e.g., fine‑tuned YOLO or DETR) on diverse diagram annotations; evaluate per‑type F1 on benchmarks and real student data.

2. **How can an ensemble approach effectively combine the grammar pipeline and end‑to‑end LMMs to exploit their complementary strengths?**  
   *Basis*: An ensemble oracle (using ground truth) achieves higher F1 (0.556) with moderate hallucination (0.320) on FBDs, indicating complementarity.  
   *Why unresolved*: No practical, implementable ensemble method is proposed or tested.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Design a decision‑level fusion mechanism (e.g., confidence‑based selection, meta‑classifier) that merges predictions from both architectures; compare its performance against individual models and the oracle.

3. **What is the pedagogical impact of Sketch2Feedback in real classroom settings?**  
   *Basis*: The study lacks classroom validation; pedagogical impact remains unmeasured.  
   *Why unresolved*: Real‑world effectiveness for student learning, instructor acceptance, and usability is unknown.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Conduct a controlled classroom study where students use Sketch2Feedback for diagram practice; measure learning gains, feedback actionability from student surveys, and instructor feedback; compare to standard methods.

4. **How can the constraint checker’s over‑firing be mitigated to reduce hallucinations while preserving detection recall?**  
   *Basis*: The grammar pipeline’s high circuit hallucination (0.925) is caused by classical CV false positives that cascade through the constraint checker and VLM.  
   *Why unresolved*: Amplification of small perception errors undermines trust; simply replacing Stage 1 may not fully solve the cascade.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Integrate uncertainty propagation (e.g., confidence thresholds on detections before constraint checking) or probabilistic constraint logic; evaluate the trade‑off between hallucination reduction and detection F1.

## Limitations
- Small test sets (40 samples per dataset) limit statistical reliability and generalizability of reported metrics.
- Hyperparameters for the classical CV pipeline (e.g., HoughLinesP thresholds, contour solidity) are not fully disclosed, hindering exact reproduction.
- The classical CV perception module is brittle to natural sketch variability, and the study provides no evaluation on diverse, real‑world student drawings.

## Confidence
- **Constraint gate reduces VLM hallucination**: Medium  
- **Symbolic graph enables precise constraint checking**: High  
- **Modularity permits failure diagnosis**: High
