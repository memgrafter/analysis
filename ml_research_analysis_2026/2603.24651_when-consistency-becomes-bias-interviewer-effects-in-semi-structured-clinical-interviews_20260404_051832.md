# When Consistency Becomes Bias: Interviewer Effects in Semi-Structured Clinical Interviews

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24651
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24651
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Models trained solely on interviewer turns match or outperform those using patient turns by exploiting fixed, scripted interview prompts rather than patient language.

## Executive Summary
This study reveals that high-performing depression detection models from semi-structured clinical interviews often rely on artifacts of the interview script itself, not patient speech. By training separate models on interviewer-only (I) and participant-only (P) turns across three datasets, the authors found I-models consistently achieved comparable or superior performance. Interpretability analyses showed I-models concentrated decision-making on a small set of recurring, standardized prompts, while P-models distributed evidence broadly. This "prompt-induced bias" is architecture-agnostic and persists across datasets, indicating that reported model performance can be inflated by exploiting predictable protocol structure rather than genuine linguistic markers of depression.

## Method Summary
The authors trained and compared two model variants per architecture (Longformer, GCN): one using only interviewer turns (I-model) and one using only participant turns (P-model). Models were evaluated on macro-F1 for depression detection across three datasets: ANDROIDS, DAIC-WOZ, and E-DAIC. For interpretability, GCN models provided per-word weights used to generate heatmaps over the normalized interview timeline, visualizing where models concentrated decision evidence. This setup isolates the contribution of each speaker's text and reveals the source of model predictions.

## Key Results
- I-models matched or outperformed P-models on all datasets (e.g., I-Longformer F1=0.98 vs P=0.79 on ANDROIDS dev).
- GCN heatmaps showed I-models focused on narrow, high-contrast bands aligned with specific interviewer prompts.
- P-model heatmaps displayed low-contrast activity spread broadly across the timeline.
- The bias persisted across both transformer (Longformer) and graph-based (GCN) architectures.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** I-models exploit systematic shortcuts tied to the interview script, focusing on recurring prompts and question positions.
- **Mechanism:** Semi-structured interviews use a finite set of standardized prompts. Models learn to associate specific prompts (e.g., "How do you cope with that?") or their positions with diagnostic labels.
- **Break condition:** If interview protocols are fully dynamic with no fixed prompts, the I-model shortcut vanishes.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The prompt-induced bias is architecture-agnostic and persistent across datasets.
- **Mechanism:** Agreement between Longformer (contextual semantics) and GCN (keyword frequencies) indicates the bias is embedded in the lexical/positional patterns of the prompts themselves.
- **Break condition:** A highly contextual, dialogue-state-tracking architecture that eliminates the I-model advantage would suggest the bias is an artifact of simpler models.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** P-models require broader, more diffuse linguistic evidence aligned with expected clinical markers.
- **Mechanism:** Genuine depression signals are subtle and variable, forcing P-models to distribute attention across many patient utterances rather than cluster on fixed points.
- **Break condition:** If P-models also clustered on specific non-prompt moments (e.g., a common narrative structure), it would challenge the link between dispersion and genuine patient-derived signals.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Semi-Structured Clinical Interviews**
  - **Why needed:** The bias stems from the protocol's trade-off: fixed prompts ensure consistency but create deterministic, non-clinical cues for models.
  - **Quick check:** Why might a clinician prefer a semi-structured over a fully unstructured interview, and what unintended consequence does this have for ML models?
- **Concept: Model Interpretability via Evidence Localization**
  - **Why needed:** The paper’s core evidence comes from visualizing where models "look" (GCN word weights, heatmaps). Without this, the bias is hidden in aggregate scores.
  - **Quick check:** How does a GCN with word and document nodes allow one to identify which parts of an interview are most influential for a prediction?
- **Concept: Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) Pipeline Implications**
  - **Why needed:** Two datasets (ANDROIDS, E-DAIC) lack full ground-truth transcripts, forcing ASR use. This introduces noise that could interact with the bias.
  - **Quick check:** If ASR errors are random, would they likely increase or decrease the observed I-model advantage, and why?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Preprocessing -> Model Variants (I/P Longformer, I/P GCN) -> Interpretability Layer (GCN weights) -> Evaluation (F1 comparison, heatmap generation)
- **Critical path:**
  1. Replicate P/I GCN training on DAIC-WOZ dev; expect I > P.
  2. Generate heatmaps; expect I-show bands on prompts, P-show diffuse.
  3. Test generalization: train I-model on one dataset, evaluate on another's I-turns.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Longformer vs. GCN:** Longformer for performance benchmarking (full context); GCN for diagnosis (direct word weights).
  - **Transcript source:** Gold transcripts isolate linguistic bias; ASR transcripts test real-world robustness but add noise.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - I-model test << dev performance → overfitting to train-specific prompt positions.
  - P-model heatmap shows concentration → model ignoring speaker role or data contamination.
  - No P/I performance gap on a dataset → either no fixed prompts or prompts not discriminative for that condition.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Baseline replication: Train P and I GCNs on DAIC-WOZ dev. Confirm I > P and contrasting heatmap patterns.
  2. Prompt ablation: Remove the top 3 most-attended interviewer prompts (from heatmaps) for I-models; performance should drop significantly.
  3. Cross-dataset transfer: Train I-GCN on ANDROIDS, evaluate on E-DAIC I-turns. Sustained high performance confirms prompt bias generalizes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- To what extent can a model classify depression using only interviewer prompts, and what specific properties of the semi-structured interview script make certain prompts more exploitable as bias indicators?
- How does the use of automatically generated transcripts (ASR) for datasets lacking ground-truth interviewer turns (ANDROIDS, E-DAIC) impact the measurement and generalizability of the reported prompt-induced bias?
- Does the prompt-induced bias persist, weaken, or strengthen when multimodal cues (acoustic prosody, facial expressions) are incorporated into the model, and does the bias manifest differently across modalities?
- What specific linguistic and semantic characteristics distinguish the "bias-carrying" prompts from other clinically relevant but less exploited prompts, and can these characteristics be used to automatically flag high-risk prompts in new interview protocols?

## Limitations
- ASR noise in ANDROIDS and E-DAIC may affect prompt detection and patient language.
- GCN heatmaps provide indirect evidence; alternative interpretability methods could yield different patterns.
- The assumption that prompts are independent of diagnosis is untested; clinicians may alter prompts based on observed symptoms.
- P-model dispersion is taken as evidence of genuine cues without external validation (e.g., clinical rating).
- Only two architectures examined; other models might behave differently.

## Confidence
- I-models exploit fixed prompts: **High**
- Architecture-agnostic bias: **Medium**
- P-models capture genuine linguistic cues: **Low**

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt ablation:** Remove top N most-attended interviewer prompts from I-model training; performance should drop if bias is prompt-driven.
2. **Cross-protocol transfer:** Train I-models on one semi-structured dataset and test on another with different prompt sets; sustained high performance confirms generalizable bias.
3. **Human clinician evaluation:** Present only the I-model-attended prompts to clinical experts for diagnosis; low accuracy would confirm the exploited cues lack clinical meaningfulness.
