# Guidelines for the Annotation and Visualization of Legal Argumentation Structures in Chinese Judicial Decisions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05171  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05171  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Proposes a comprehensive guideline (v1.0) for annotating and visualizing legal argumentation structures in Chinese judicial decisions, enabling reproducible computational analysis and downstream AI tasks.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the lack of fine‑grained representations of legal argumentation in judicial documents for computational analysis and artificial intelligence. It introduces a four‑type proposition taxonomy (General Normative, Particular Normative, General Factual, Particular Factual) with GM subtypes, and five relation types (support, attack, joint, match, identity) to model argumentative links. Formal representation rules, visualization standards (rectangles for propositions, circles with distinct fills for relations), and a staged annotation workflow with dual independent annotation and consistency control are provided. This creates a standardized data foundation for argument mining, reasoning modeling, and explainable legal AI, though no quantitative annotation metrics are reported.

## Method Summary
The methodology comprises a systematic framework for capturing the reasoning structure within Chinese judicial decisions. First, propositions are categorized using the four‑type taxonomy, with GM subtypes indicating generic references. Relations between propositions are labeled using the five‑type scheme. Formal representation rules define a syntax for encoding the structure, while visualization standards prescribe diagramming conventions (rectangles for propositions, symbol‑coded circles for relations). The annotation workflow involves two independent annotators per document, followed by a consistency check and reconciliation to produce final annotations. The guideline (v1.0) is fully illustrated but lacks empirical evaluation of its reliability or scalability.

## Key Results
- A four‑type proposition taxonomy (with GM subtypes) that distinguishes normative vs. factual and general vs. particular statements.
- Five relation types (support, attack, joint, match, identity) to model argumentative interactions.
- A complete annotation workflow integrating dual independent annotation and consistency control, accompanied by clear visualization standards.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The structured representation captures both the hierarchical and relational aspects of legal reasoning, making it amenable to computational parsing and machine interpretation. The dual independent annotation followed by consistency control reduces individual annotator bias and improves reliability. Standardized visual symbols ensure that diagrams are interpretable across different cases and by both humans and algorithms.

## Foundational Learning
- **Proposition Types** – Why needed: Disentangle normative statements from factual ones and general from particular contexts. Quick check: Classify a sample legal claim into one of the four types.  
- **Relation Types** – Why needed: Encode how propositions interact (support, attack, etc.). Quick check: Given two propositions, identify the relation present.  
- **GM Subtypes** – Why needed: Mark propositions that refer generically to norms or facts (e.g., “the law” or “the evidence”). Quick check: Flag every GM occurrence in a short excerpt.  
- **Formal Representation Rules** – Why needed: Provide an unambiguous, machine‑readable encoding. Quick check: Translate a simple two‑proposition argument into the formal syntax.  
- **Visualization Standards** – Why needed: Ensure consistent diagram creation for intuitive analysis. Quick check: Draw a valid diagram using the prescribed shapes and fills.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Raw Document → Initial Annotation (Annotator A) → Initial Annotation (Annotator B) → Consistency Check → Reconciliation → Final Annotated Structure → Visualization  
Critical path: The dual annotation and reconciliation steps form the longest sequence, as they cannot be fully parallelized.  
Design tradeoffs: Dual annotation increases reliability but doubles effort; strict rules reduce ambiguity but may reduce flexibility; manual consistency checks guarantee quality but add time.  
Failure signatures: Low inter‑annotator agreement; missing consistency checks; systematic misclassifications due to inadequate training.  
First experiments:  
1. Pilot annotation on 10 Chinese judgments to refine guidelines.  
2. Compute inter‑annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ) on 50 judgments.  
3. Assess scalability on 200+ diverse cases, measuring time‑per‑document and error rates.
