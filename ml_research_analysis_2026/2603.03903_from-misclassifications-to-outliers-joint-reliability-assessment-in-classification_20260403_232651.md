# From Misclassifications to Outliers: Joint Reliability Assessment in Classification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03903
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03903
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** SURE+ pipeline achieves state-of-the-art DS-F1 (70.67 on CIFAR-100) and lower DS-AURC (173.45), while boosting ID accuracy (81.66% CIFAR-100, 88.49% ImageNet), with larger gains on Far-OOD shifts.

## Executive Summary
Classifiers deployed in the real world must reliably handle both in-distribution (ID) misclassifications and out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs. Traditional evaluation separates failure prediction (detecting wrong ID predictions) and OOD detection, but this can be misleading because a model that excels at one may fail at the other. This paper introduces a unified double-scoring framework that uses two thresholds on independent scores (sOOD for OOD detection, sID for failure prediction) to define a joint acceptance set. The corresponding metrics, DS‑F1 (point optimal) and DS‑AURC (area under risk‑coverage), extend conventional F1 and AURC to the two‑score setting. Extensive experiments on the OpenOOD benchmark show that double scoring consistently outperforms single‑score approaches across Near‑ and Far‑OOD shifts. Moreover, the proposed SURE+ training pipeline—integrating RegMixup, RegPixMix, F‑SAM, EMA, and ReBN—pushes the state of the art, achieving higher DS‑F1 and lower DS‑AURC while improving ID accuracy. The gains are particularly pronounced for Far‑OOD, underscoring the practical value of joint reliability assessment and integrated training.

## Method Summary
The paper’s method consists of two parts: (1) a double‑scoring evaluation framework and (2) the SURE+ training pipeline. For evaluation, given a trained classifier and a test set containing both ID and OOD samples, two scores are computed per sample: sID (a confidence measure for correct ID classification, e.g., maximum softmax probability) and sOOD (an OOD detection score, e.g., Energy or ReAct). A prediction is accepted only if sOOD ≥ τ_OOD and sID ≥ τ_ID, where thresholds are chosen to optimize DS‑F1 (maximum F1 over threshold pairs) or DS‑AURC (minimum selective risk integrated over coverage). This joint approach avoids the pitfalls of optimizing one metric at the expense of the other.

SURE+ is a training recipe designed to improve the underlying model for both tasks. It combines several techniques: (a) RegMixup, which augments the training batch by interpolating inputs and labels to regularize decision boundaries; (b) RegPixMix, which further mixes images with auxiliary textures (from DTD) via random pixel‑level transformations to simulate OOD‑like variations; (c) F‑SAM, a variant of Sharpness‑Aware Minimization that perturbs parameters to seek flat minima; (d) Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of weights to stabilize training; and (e) ReBN, which renormalizes batch‑norm statistics on the full training set after training. The loss is the standard cross‑entropy plus the mixup‑induced regularization terms. The pipeline is implemented with SGD, cosine learning rate decay, and weight decay. Through ablations, the authors demonstrate that each component contributes to the final DS‑F1/DS‑AURC improvements.

## Key Results
- Double scoring (using any combination of post‑hoc OOD scores with MSP as sID) yields strictly higher DS‑F1 and lower DS‑AURC than the best single‑score approach across multiple datasets and OOD shifts.
- SURE+ achieves state‑of‑the‑art joint reliability: on CIFAR‑100, DS‑F1 = 70.67 (vs 67.42 baseline) and DS‑AURC = 173.45 (vs 202.38), while ID accuracy reaches 81.66%; on ImageNet, ID accuracy = 88.49%.
- The improvements are larger for Far‑OOD distributions than for Near‑OOD, indicating that the pipeline enhances robustness to more severe distribution shifts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Double scoring leverages complementary signals
The core claim is that two independent scoring functions (sOOD and sID) provide non‑redundant information about OOD presence and ID classification confidence. By accepting a prediction only when both exceed their thresholds, the model can simultaneously filter OOD samples and low‑confidence ID errors. The DS‑F1 and DS‑AURC metrics evaluate the entire acceptance set and, under mild assumptions, are guaranteed not to underperform their single‑score counterparts (DS‑F1 ≥ standard F1, DS‑AURC ≤ standard AURC). The mechanism breaks down when the scores are highly correlated or when both struggle to discriminate (e.g., Near‑OOD with subtle shifts).

### SURE+ synergistically improves representation and calibration
Each component of SURE+ targets a different aspect of joint reliability. RegMixup smooths decision boundaries via input‑label interpolation, improving generalization and OOD detection. RegPixMix introduces texture‑level perturbations that mimic OOD characteristics, forcing the model to ignore spurious pixel patterns. F‑SAM optimizes for flat minima, which are linked to better robustness under distribution shift. EMA averages weights over time, and ReBN renormalizes batch statistics after training; together they stabilize predictions and improve calibration for both ID and OOD samples. Ablations show that removing any component degrades DS‑F1/DS‑AURC, confirming their complementary roles. Over‑regularization (e.g., too many RegPixMix iterations) can harm ID accuracy, highlighting the need for careful hyperparameter tuning.

### Joint metrics reflect real‑world deployment
Traditional single‑score metrics either focus on OOD detection (AUROC) or failure prediction (AURC) in isolation. A model that excels at one but fails the other is unreliable in practice. DS‑F1 and DS‑AURC evaluate the simultaneous handling of accepted OOD samples and accepted misclassified ID samples. DS‑F1 finds the best precision‑recall trade‑off across the two‑threshold space; DS‑AURC integrates the minimal selective risk at each coverage level. This captures the full operating range and avoids misleading conclusions from isolated metrics. The paper shows cases where rankings based on single scores conflict, while DS metrics provide a clear, consistent ordering.

## Foundational Learning
- **Selective classification / rejection option**
  - *Why needed*: The paper extends selective classification (where a classifier can abstain) to a double‑scoring setting. Understanding the basic framework (score, threshold, coverage, risk) is essential.
  - *Quick check*: How does a selective classifier decide to accept or reject a prediction? What is the trade‑off between coverage and selective risk?
- **Out‑of‑distribution (OOD) detection**
  - *Why needed*: sOOD is an OOD detection score. The paper uses many post‑hoc OOD methods (MSP, Energy, KLM, etc.), so familiarity with their principles (e.g., softmax confidence, logit magnitude) is important.
  - *Quick check*: What is the difference between OOD detection and failure prediction? Can the same score be used for both? Why might they conflict?
- **Failure prediction (in‑distribution error detection)**
  - *Why needed*: sID estimates confidence in the ID classification. This is about predicting whether the predicted label is correct, distinct from OOD detection.
  - *Quick check*: How does failure prediction differ from OOD detection? What are common scoring functions for failure prediction (e.g., MSP, entropy)?
- **Evaluation metrics: AURC and F1‑score**
  - *Why needed*: DS‑F1 and DS‑AURC generalize these. Understanding AURC (area under risk‑coverage curve) and F1 (harmonic mean of precision and recall) is prerequisite.
  - *Quick check*: How is selective risk defined? What does AURC measure? How is the F1 score computed in selective classification?
- **Data augmentation for robustness (Mixup, PixMix)**
  - *Why needed*: SURE+ relies on RegMixup and RegPixMix as core regularization. Mixup interpolates between samples; PixMix mixes with auxiliary textures. Both aim to improve model robustness to distribution shift.
  - *Quick check*: What is Mixup? How does it regularize a classifier? What is PixMix and how does it differ from Mixup?
- **Sharpness‑aware minimization (SAM, F‑SAM)**
  - *Why needed*: F‑SAM is the optimizer in SURE+. SAM seeks flat minima, which generalize better. Understanding the perturbation step and the bi‑level optimization is key.
  - *Quick check*: What is sharpness‑aware minimization? Why do flat minima correlate with robustness? How does F‑SAM improve upon SAM?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Input (image) -> Trained classifier (ResNet‑18 / ViT) -> Compute sID (MSP) and sOOD (e.g., Energy) -> Apply thresholds τ_ID and τ_OOD -> Accept if both exceed thresholds else reject. Training pipeline: Loss = CE + RegMixup + RegPixMix -> Optimized with F‑SAM -> EMA applied during training -> After training, ReBN applied -> Final model used for inference.

### Critical path
1. Prepare data splits (ID training/validation/test, OOD validation/test) following OpenOOD protocol.
2. Train the classifier using the SURE+ recipe (hyperparameters: α=10 for RegMixup, 1‑4 RegPixMix iterations, F‑SAM radius ρ_f and γ, EMA momentum τ, learning rate schedule).
3. For all evaluation samples, compute sID and sOOD with chosen post‑hoc methods.
4. Perform threshold grid search: generate quantiles for each score (e.g., 100 thresholds), iterate over pairs, compute acceptance set, and record DS‑F1 (maximum F1) and DS‑AURC (minimum area under risk‑coverage).
5. Report metrics on test sets (separate Near‑OOD and Far‑OOD if desired).
6. Also log ID accuracy for completeness.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Choice of sID/sOOD:** MSP is stable for sID; OOD‑specific methods (Energy, ReAct, VIM) usually better for sOOD. Using the same method for both is suboptimal.
- **Computational cost:** Double scoring requires storing two score arrays and a 2D threshold search (O(T²)). Single scoring uses 1D. Grid resolution trades accuracy for time/memory.
- **Training vs post‑hoc:** SURE+ improves the base model; post‑hoc OOD scores can still be applied on top. The pipeline is designed to work well with various post‑hoc methods.
- **Near vs Far OOD:** Double scoring gains are larger on Far‑OOD because OOD scores are more discriminative; on Near‑OOD both scores may have limited signal, so improvements are smaller.

### Failure signatures
- **sID and sOOD highly correlated** (e.g., both derived from softmax) leads to minimal DS improvement. Check correlation on validation set.
- **Over‑regularization** (too strong RegPixMix) degrades ID accuracy. Monitor ID accuracy during ablation.
- **Threshold misalignment** when validation OOD distribution differs from test (e.g., tune on Far‑OOD but test on Near‑OOD) leads to poor generalization. Use representative validation sets.
- **Implementation bugs** especially in DS‑AURC aggregation (must take min risk per coverage bin, not last value) can overestimate risk.
- **Model collapse** with F‑SAM if hyperparameters (ρ_f, γ) are inappropriate. Start with recommended defaults and monitor stability.

For each, compute single‑score metrics (F1, AURC) using various OOD scores (MSP, Energy, etc.) and double‑score metrics (MSP as sID, other as sOOD). Confirm double scoring improves both metrics on both Near‑OOD (CIFAR‑10) and Far‑OOD (MNIST).
2. **SURE+ ablation on CIFAR‑100:** Starting from a basic CE model, incrementally add RegMixup, RegPixMix, switch from SAM to F‑SAM, and replace SWA with EMA+ReBN. For each variant, compute DS‑F1/DS‑AURC (using MSP and Energy) and ID accuracy. The full SURE+ configuration should yield the best joint metrics.

The maximum should occur at interior values (both thresholds non‑trivial) and the surface should lie above the best single‑score F1 obtained by fixing one threshold to its optimal 1D value.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Integrating auxiliary outlier data with double scoring:** The paper suggests exploring how outlier exposure (using external OOD data during training) can be incorporated into the double‑scoring framework to further boost reliability. Current experiments focus on post‑hoc methods and augmentation without external outliers.
- **Improving Near‑OOD discrimination:** Despite overall gains, performance on Near‑OOD shifts remains limited. Future work should develop specialized objectives or architectures that capture fine‑grained semantic differences needed for Near‑OOD detection.
- **Automating threshold selection under distribution shift:** Manual tuning of τ_ID and τ_OOD on a held‑out set may not generalize. The paper proposes using generative models to synthesize diverse validation distributions for robust calibration; this direction is unexplored.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameters for SURE+ components (F‑SAM radius, EMA momentum, RegPixMix iterations) are not fully disclosed, leading to uncertainty in reproducibility and potential performance variance.
- Double‑scoring evaluation requires a 2D threshold search that is computationally intensive; the optimal grid resolution is unspecified and may trade off accuracy for efficiency.
- The study is restricted to standard OpenOOD benchmarks and ResNet/ViT architectures on CIFAR‑100/ImageNet; generalizability to other architectures, datasets, or more realistic OOD distributions is not established.
- The assumption that sID and sOOD are non‑redundant is not always verified; high correlation could nullify the reported gains.
- SURE+ combines several regularizers that may interact sensitively; over‑regularization could reduce ID accuracy, especially on smaller datasets.
