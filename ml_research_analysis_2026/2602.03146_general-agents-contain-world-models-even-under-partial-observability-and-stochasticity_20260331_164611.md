# General Agents Contain World Models, even under Partial Observability and Stochasticity

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.03146
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03146
- **Reference count**: 14
- **Primary result**: Capable goal-conditioned agents implicitly learn an approximate model of transition dynamics, even when stochastic or operating under partial observability, and this holds even for agents optimal only on a narrow set of goals.

## Executive Summary
This paper extends a foundational theoretical result that capable agents must contain an implicit world model. The prior theorem required deterministic agents and fully observable environments. This work removes both assumptions, proving that stochastic agents (with δ < 1/2 optimality) operating in partially observable environments still encode transition dynamics when optimized for state-based goals. Furthermore, the requirement for the agent to be "general" is weakened: policies optimal only on goals of width at most 2 (exponentially simpler) suffice to recover a world model with improved error scaling. The core method involves querying a goal-conditioned policy with specially constructed "dichotomic" goals to deduce transition probabilities.

## Method Summary
The recovery algorithm treats the agent as a black-box goal-conditioned policy. To estimate a specific transition probability \(P(s'|s,a)\), it constructs goals that encode repeated attempts to visit \((s,a)\) and then reach \(s'\) (success) or a different state (failure). For a target depth \(n\), it generates goals where the success probability is \(p^r\) and the alternative is \((1-p)^s\), with \(p = P(s'|s,a)\). By creating disjunctions (\(\phi_{a,r} \vee \psi_{b,s}\)), the optimal first action between two strategic choices reveals a threshold comparing these probabilities. This threshold is used to bound \(p\). Two main goal families are used: depth-\((2n+1)\) dichotomic goals for stochastic agents (yielding \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) error) and width-2 goals (yielding \(O(\log n / n)\) error for deterministic agents). The method assumes a finite communicating cMDP and that goals are expressed in terms of underlying states, not observations.

## Key Results
*   For stochastic, \(\delta\)-optimal (\(\delta < 1/2\)) agents, an estimator with error \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) can be constructed using goals of depth \(2n+1\).
*   The result holds for partially observable environments when goals are phrased in terms of true states; using observation-based goals may fail.
*   Policies optimal only on goals with width at most 2 already contain enough information to recover a model, with a tighter error bound of \(O(\log n / n)\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim**: For stochastic \(\delta\)-optimal goal-conditioned policies with \(\delta < 1/2\), an estimator of transition probabilities with error \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) can be constructed from queries on depth-\((2n+1)\) goals.
*   **Mechanism**: The proof constructs dichotomic goals \(\xi_k = \psi_{a,k} \vee \chi_{b,k}\) that force the policy to choose between two actions \(a\) and \(b\) based on which of \(\Pr(X \le k)\) or \(\Pr(X > k)\) is larger, where \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(p, n)\) and \(p = P(s'|s,a)\). The policy's choice at the first step reveals a threshold \(x\) such that the policy prefers action \(a\) when \(p^r \ge (1-p)^s\) for some \(r,s\). By analyzing \(x\) via Bernstein-Freedman concentration inequalities, we bound \(|x/n - p|\), giving an estimate of \(p\).
*   **Core assumption**: \(\delta < 1/2\) ensures that when the two goal probabilities are not exactly equal, the optimal policy must assign strictly more probability mass to the easier goal, making the switch detectable. Also assumes finite communicating cMDP.
*   **Break condition**: If \(\delta \ge 1/2\), the policy can set \(p_{a,k} = p_{b,k} = 1/2\) for all \(k\), providing no information about \(p\).

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim**: The result holds for partially observable environments when goals are phrased in terms of real states (not observations), though optimal policies may not "know" the world if goals are observation-based.
*   **Mechanism**: For partially observable cMDPs, the proof shows that a stochastic uniform policy (random actions) achieves goal probabilities that depend solely on the underlying transition probability \(p\) and not on the observation kernel \(\Omega\). An optimal policy can mimic this random behavior after an initial strategic action. By querying the same dichotomic goals, the policy's initial action choice still reveals \(p\) because the goal success probabilities are analytically identical to the fully observable case.
*   **Core assumption**: The environment is finite and communicating, and goals are expressed in terms of underlying states (not observations). The policy may be stochastic.
*   **Break condition**: If goals are observation-based and observations are completely uninformative (all states map to same observation), optimal policies may be independent of \(p\), preventing recovery.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim**: Policies optimal only on goals of width at most 2 (exponentially fewer) already contain enough information to recover transition probabilities, with error \(O(\log n / n)\).
*   **Mechanism**: Instead of using exponentially many goals, use simple goals \(\phi_{b,r}\) (repeat \(r\) times) and \(\psi_{b,r}\) (repeat \(r\) times with opposite outcome). Querying \(\xi_{r,s} = \phi_{a,r} \vee \psi_{b,s}\) yields constraints like \(p^r \ge (1-p)^s\) or vice versa. By systematically varying \(r\) and \(s\), we identify a boundary that estimates \(\alpha = \log(1-p)/\log(p)\). Using bounds on the Lambert W function and derivative analysis, we convert the confidence interval on \(\alpha\) into one on \(p\) with error \(O(\log n / n)\). For \(\delta\)-optimal policies, similar reasoning with an additional \(\delta\) factor.
*   **Core assumption**: Deterministic policy for the exact \(O(\log n/n)\) bound; for stochastic, \(\delta < 1/2\). Finite communicating cMDP.
*   **Break condition**: If the policy is not sufficiently optimal (large \(\delta\)), the switch thresholds may be smeared, increasing error beyond \(O(\log n/n)\). Also if the environment is not communicating, reaching \(s\) may not be guaranteed.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Controlled Markov Decision Process (cMDP)**: The formal model of the environment. It defines states, actions, and transition probabilities \(P(s'|s,a)\). It is needed because the world model being recovered is precisely this transition kernel.
    *   **Quick check**: Given a cMDP, what does it mean for it to be *communicating*?
*   **Goal-conditioned policy**: The agent is treated as a function \(\pi(\text{goal})\) that outputs actions. It is needed because the recovery algorithm queries this policy with specific goals to infer \(P\).
    *   **Quick check**: How does a goal-conditioned policy differ from a standard policy?
*   **\(\delta\)-optimality**: A policy is \((1-\delta)\)-optimal for a goal if its success probability is within \(\delta\) of the optimum. It is needed to characterize the quality of the agent; the theorems assume a certain level of competence.
    *   **Quick check**: If a policy is \(\delta\)-optimal for a goal, how does its success probability compare to the optimum?
*   **Binomial distribution**: The number of successes in \(n\) independent trials with probability \(p\). It is needed because the success probability for certain compound goals (e.g., \(\phi_{a,r}\)) is exactly \(p^r\), which behaves like a binomial event's tail probability.
    *   **Quick check**: If \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(p, n)\), what is \(E[X]\) and \(\text{Var}[X]\)?
*   **Bernstein-Freedman inequalities**: A family of concentration inequalities for sums of independent random variables. It is needed to bound how far the observed switch threshold \(x\) can deviate from its expected value \(np\), providing confidence intervals for \(p\).
    *   **Quick check**: What do Bernstein-Freedman inequalities require about the random variables they are applied to?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map**:
    Goal Generator -> Policy Oracle -> Switch Detector -> Transition Estimator
*   **Critical path**:
    1.  Select a transition \((s, a, s')\) to estimate.
    2.  For a chosen \(n\) (depth/width parameter), generate a sequence of goals \(\xi_k\) or \(\xi_{r,s}\) and query the policy.
    3.  Record the first action taken by the policy for each goal.
    4.  Identify the switch point \(x\): the largest \(k\) (or \(r\)) where the policy chooses the "less likely" action according to the goal structure.
    5.  Compute \(p_{\text{est}}\) from \(x/n\) (or by solving \(\log(1-p)/\log(p) = x/n\) via Lambert W/binary search).
    6.  Apply the theoretical error bound (e.g., \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) or \(O(\log n/n)\)).
*   **Design tradeoffs**:
    *   Deterministic vs. stochastic policies: Stochastic policies require multiple rollouts per goal to estimate the action distribution accurately, increasing sample complexity per goal.
    *   Depth \(n\) vs. query budget: Larger \(n\) reduces error but requires \(O(n)\) queries per transition; both main approaches have linear query complexity in \(n\).
    *   \(\delta\)-optimality: A larger \(\delta\) degrades the error bounds and may require larger \(n\) to achieve the same confidence.
    *   Goal phrasing: State-based goals are essential for recovery under partial observability; observation-based goals can fail completely if observations are not sufficiently distinguishing.
*   **Failure signatures**:
    *   Policy not \(\delta\)-optimal: The switch threshold becomes noisy; empirical \(x\) may violate the theoretical inequalities (e.g., the policy sometimes chooses the action that is provably suboptimal for any \(p\)).
    *   Non-communicating MDP: The agent cannot guarantee reaching the target state \(s\) from the start, breaking the binomial-like success probability assumptions.
    *   High variance in stochastic policies: Insufficient samples per goal lead to misidentifying the switch point due to noise in the estimated action probabilities.
    *   Incorrect goal construction: Deviating from the prescribed goal structure (e.g., wrong subgoal order) invalidates the analytical link to the binomial probability and thus to \(p\).
*   **First 3 experiments**:
    1.  **Synthetic deterministic MDP**: Implement a simple 5-state line domain. Fix \((s,a,s')\) and true \(p\). Vary \(n\) and run the width-2 estimator (Theorem 4). Plot error vs. \(n\) and verify the \(O(\log n / n)\) decay rate.
    2.  **Stochastic policy test**: Implement a \(\delta\)-optimal stochastic policy (e.g., with probability \(1-\delta\) choose optimal first action, else the other). Use the depth-\((2n+1)\) dichotomic goals (Theorem 2). Measure recovery error for different \(\delta < 0.5\) and compare to the \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) bound.
    3.  **Partial observability test**: In a POMDP, add observation noise (e.g., state \(s\) misobserved with probability \(p_F\)). Use state-based goals to recover \(p\); success confirms Theorem 3. Then, use observation-based goals where all states map to the same observation; failure (inability to recover) is the expected outcome.

## Limitations
*   The results assume a **finite, communicating cMDP**; it is unknown if they extend to infinite or non-communicating environments.
*   The method requires the agent to be **goal-conditioned and sufficiently optimal** on a large (or specially structured) set of goals, which may be unrealistic for many practical agents.
*   For **partial observability**, recovery only works if goals are specified in terms of **true states**, not observations. The case for observation-based goals is largely unresolved.
*   Error bounds are **asymptotic**; for very small \(n\) or transition probabilities \(p\) near 0 or 1, empirical error may exceed theoretical predictions.
*   For **stochastic policies**, the analysis requires multiple samples per goal to estimate action probabilities, increasing practical sample complexity.

## Confidence
*   **Stochastic agents (\(\delta<1/2\)) O(1/√n)**: **Medium**. The mechanism using dichotomic goals and concentration inequalities is plausible and internally consistent, but it rests on a specific construction whose practical robustness is untested. Supporting evidence in the immediate academic corpus is weak.
*   **Partial observability with state-based goals**: **Medium**. The proof leverages a uniform random policy to preserve the binomial structure. The assumption that goals encode true states is critical and limits applicability. Direct external validation is scarce.
*   **Width-2 goals O(log n/n)**: **Medium**. The use of the Lambert W function to improve the rate is mathematically clever but introduces complexity and assumes deterministic policies for the exact bound. The extension to stochastic policies is less clear. The paper has low citation impact so far.

## Next Checks
1.  **Empirical validation of error rates**: Implement the deterministic width-2 estimator on synthetic MDPs for a range of \(n\) (e.g., 10 to 500) and transition probabilities \(p\). Fit the decay of \(|p_{\text{est}} - p|\) vs. \(n\) to \(c/n\) and \(c \log n / n\) to verify the \(O(\log n / n)\) rate.
2.  **Stochastic policy threshold test**: For a fixed \(p\) and \(\delta\) (e.g., 0.1, 0.3, 0.45), simulate the depth-\((2n+1)\) dichotomic querying process. Check if the observed switch threshold \(x\) satisfies the theoretical confidence interval derived from the Bernstein-Freedman bound as \(n\) increases.
3.  **POMDP state-based vs. observation-based contrast**: In a small POMDP (e.g., a 3-state tiger problem variant), run the recovery algorithm using goals defined by true states (should recover transition probabilities) and then using goals defined only by observations (should fail or recover only coarse-grained information).
