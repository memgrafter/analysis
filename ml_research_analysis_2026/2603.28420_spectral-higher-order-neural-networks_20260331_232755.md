# Spectral Higher-Order Neural Networks

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2603.28420
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28420
- **Reference count:** 24
- **Primary result:** Higher-order neural networks are made efficient and stable via spectral decomposition, reducing parameters to O(N²) while matching or exceeding MLP performance on image classification benchmarks.

## Executive Summary

Higher-order neural networks (HONNs) capture interactions beyond pairwise, but they suffer from cubic parameter scaling and training instability, limiting their adoption. This paper introduces Spectral Higher-Order Neural Networks (SHONNs), which reparameterize the weight tensors for triadic interactions using spectral decomposition: higher-order eigenvectors are fixed after initialization, while only the associated eigenvalues and linear eigenvectors are learned. This reduces the number of trainable parameters from O(N³) to O(N²), matching the complexity of a standard MLP. SHONNs are proven to be universal approximators for continuous functions on compact sets. Empirically, spectral triadic models outperform standard MLPs on MNIST, Fashion‑MNIST, and CIFAR‑10, and they remain stable even without pointwise nonlinearities. The approach thus enables efficient, stable higher‑order computations in general‑purpose feedforward architectures.

## Method Summary

A SHONN layer consists of two components: a linear part and a triadic (quadratic) part. The linear part is expressed in a spectral form with trainable eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The triadic part reparameterizes the third‑order weight tensor as differences of trainable eigenvalues multiplied by fixed higher‑order eigenvectors that are randomly initialized and kept constant. For each output neuron, the computation is:  
`y_k = Σ_i (λ_in_i - λ_out_k) φ_ki x_i + Σ_{i≤j} (λ̃_in,ij - λ̃_out_k) φ̃_kij x_i x_j`.  
Stacking multiple such layers yields a network that can represent polynomials of increasing degree (up to 2ᴸ−1 for L layers), providing high expressivity. Only O(N²) parameters per layer are required, comparable to a standard MLP. Training uses standard backpropagation with optimizers like Adam; the fixed eigenvectors stabilize optimization while the trainable spectral parameters are updated.

## Key Results

- **Parameter efficiency:** Triadic interactions are reduced from O(N³) to O(N²) trainable parameters.
- **Theoretical guarantee:** SHONNs are universal approximators for continuous functions on compact sets (Theorem 4.1).
- **Empirical performance:** Spectral triadic models consistently achieve higher test accuracy than baseline MLPs on MNIST, Fashion‑MNIST, and CIFAR‑10, while avoiding the instability observed in direct‑space higher‑order models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1 (Parameter reduction).** SHONNs reparameterize the triadic weight tensor so that only eigenvalues are trained while higher‑order eigenvectors are fixed. This changes the parameter count from O(N²K) to O(N² + K), yielding an overall O(N²) scaling per layer and matching the complexity of a standard MLP. The derivation (Eq. 7, Section 4.2) shows the parameter count as 1.5N² + 2.5N. The fixed eigenvectors, if randomly initialized to nonzero values, preserve expressivity (proven in Section 4.3). Risks arise if the fixed eigenvectors become linearly dependent or zero, which is avoided by proper initialization.

**Mechanism 2 (Training stability).** Direct‑space HONNs exhibit instability and confidence saturation due to the ill‑conditioned high‑dimensional weight tensor. In SHONNs, the fixed eigenvectors provide a stable basis, and the eigenvalues adjust smoothly during training, leading to a better‑conditioned optimization landscape. Empirically, Section 2.2 shows that spectral triadic models train stably than direct‑space triadic models, and Section 2.3 demonstrates successful training on CIFAR‑10 without any explicit nonlinearities. Possible break conditions include poorly conditioned fixed eigenvectors or unbounded eigenvalue growth.

**Mechanism 3 (Expressivity and universal approximation).** Stacking L triadic layers generates polynomials of degree up to 2ᴸ−1. Because the spectral parametrization can construct any monomial basis (the trainable linear eigenvectors and eigenvalues span the necessary space), the resulting network can approximate any continuous function on a compact domain by the Stone‑Weierstrass theorem. Theorem 4.1 formalizes this universal approximation property, building on Lemmas 4.2 and 4.3. The proof requires each layer to have enough width and the fixed higher‑order eigenvectors to be nonzero.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Input vector x ∈ ℝⁿ → For each layer: Linear term (λ_in, λ_out, φ) + Triadic term (λ̃_in, λ̃_out, fixed φ̃) → Output y ∈ ℝᴷ → (stacked) next layer. All trainable parameters: λ_in (N), λ_out (K), φ (K×N), λ̃_in (N(N+1)/2), λ̃_out (K). Fixed parameters: φ̃ (K × N(N+1)/2) initialized to random nonzero values.

**Critical path**  
1. Initialize trainable parameters randomly; fix φ̃ (nonzero random).  
2. For each layer, compute the linear sum and quadratic sum as above.  
3. Backpropagate loss to update only trainable parameters.  
4. Use standard optimizers (e.g., Adam); include warm‑up and learning‑rate scheduling as in the experiments.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Fixed higher‑order eigenvectors reduce parameters but limit flexibility; universal approximation still holds via the trainable linear part.  
- Omitting pointwise activations relies on polynomial expressivity, which can act as an implicit regularizer.  
- Quadratic operations scale as O(N²) per output neuron; for large N the hidden dimension must be chosen carefully to avoid excessive cost.

**Failure signatures**  
- Diverging eigenvalues indicate gradient explosion; monitor magnitudes and consider regularization or a lower learning rate.  
- Underperformance compared to an MLP may mean the task does not benefit from higher‑order terms; try different random seeds for the fixed φ̃.  
- Memory issues arise from the O(N²) quadratic term; reduce hidden size or use memory‑efficient tensor operations.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Single‑layer spectral triadic perceptron on MNIST: compare accuracy and stability to a standard perceptron and a direct‑space triadic perceptron.  
2. 4‑layer spectral triadic MLP on CIFAR‑10 without activations versus a 4‑layer ReLU‑MLP.  
3. Replace MLP blocks in MLP‑Mixer with spectral triadic layers and evaluate on CIFAR‑10 to measure performance gains and overfitting reduction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Application to physics‑informed neural networks (PINNs)** – The paper explicitly identifies using SHONNs in physical contexts (e.g., via PINNs) as a key future direction. It remains unresolved because no PINN experiments are reported. Resolving this would require benchmarking SHONN‑enhanced PINNs against standard MLP‑based PINNs on common PDE datasets (e.g., Burgers’, Navier‑Stokes) and measuring accuracy, sample efficiency, and stability.  
2. **Training the higher‑order eigenvectors** – The current design freezes φ̃ to reduce parameters, but no justification or ablation is provided. It is unknown whether learning φ̃ could improve performance or optimization dynamics. Evidence would come from training SHONNs with and without trainable φ̃ under matched parameter budgets and comparing accuracy, convergence speed, and gradient norms.  
3. **Sample complexity and depth/width requirements** – While universal approximation is proven, the theorem is non‑constructive and gives no quantitative bounds on network size or required training data. Practical SHONNs might need vastly different sizing than MLPs. Resolution would involve deriving theoretical bounds on approximation error versus depth/width/samples, complemented by systematic empirical studies on various function families.  
4. **Why spectral parametrization mitigates instability** – The paper claims improved stability but provides no analysis of the optimization landscape or gradient dynamics. Understanding this mechanism is important for trust and further improvements. Evidence would compare gradient norm distributions, Hessian spectra, or loss curvature during training of spectral versus direct‑space HONNs on controlled tasks, and develop theory linking spectral reparameterization to better conditioning.

## Limitations

- Fixed higher‑order eigenvectors depend on random initialization; suboptimal choices could limit expressivity or stability.
- Quadratic operations scale as O(N²), which becomes prohibitive for high‑dimensional inputs (e.g., large image patches).
- Empirical validation is restricted to three vision datasets; generalisation to other domains or distribution shifts is unknown.
- Stability guarantee is empirical; no theoretical bound on gradient norms or eigenvalue conditioning exists.

## Confidence

- **Parameter reduction (O(N³) to O(N²))**: High – derivation is explicit and matches the count.
- **Training stability**: Medium – supported by experiments but lacking formal proof; depends on initialization.
- **Universal approximation**: High – rigorous proof with polynomial basis construction.
- **Empirical superiority over MLPs**: Medium – limited task diversity; may be dataset‑specific.

## Next Checks

1. **Cross‑domain evaluation** – Apply SHONNs to at least two non‑vision tasks (e.g., molecular property prediction, text classification) and compare against strong baselines.
2. **Eigenvector sensitivity analysis** – Systematically vary the fixed higher‑order eigenvectors (random seeds, orthogonal bases, structured bases) and measure impact on accuracy, stability, and convergence speed.
3. **Theoretical stability analysis** – Derive bounds on the condition number of the effective weight tensor under spectral reparameterization, relating it to eigenvalue magnitudes and eigenvector orthogonality.
