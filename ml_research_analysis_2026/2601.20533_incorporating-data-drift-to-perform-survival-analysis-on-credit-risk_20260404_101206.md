# Incorporating data drift to perform survival analysis on credit risk

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.20533
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20533
- Reference count: 11
- Primary result: The landmark-based LMISO model consistently outperforms classical Cox regression, XGBoost, and drift-adaptive online learners under simulated drift scenarios, achieving AUC 0.812, Brier score 0.102, and F1 0.924 on 2020 data under sudden drift.

## Executive Summary
Credit risk models based on survival analysis often degrade under data drift caused by changing borrower behavior and economic conditions. This paper introduces a dynamic joint modeling framework that integrates a longitudinal behavioral marker derived from loan balance mechanics with discrete-time hazard modeling. The framework enhances robustness through landmark one-hot encoding to adjust for temporal heterogeneity and isotonic calibration to maintain probability accuracy under distribution shift. Evaluated on Freddie Mac mortgage datasets with simulated sudden, incremental, and recurring drift, the proposed LMISO model surpasses baseline methods including Cox, XGBoost, and online adaptive learners across key metrics. Ablation studies attribute performance gains primarily to the longitudinal marker, with landmark encoding and calibration providing additional stability.

## Method Summary
Using the Freddie Mac Single Family Loan-Level Dataset, the study samples 50,000 loans per year from 2000, 2010, and 2020–2021. The core innovation is a longitudinal marker based on the percentage deviation of the actual unpaid balance from the scheduled amortization balance (BD_pct). For each loan, a regularized linear regression of BD_pct on normalized age yields an intercept (early deviation) and slope (trend), summarizing repayment dynamics.

The modeling pipeline converts continuous survival into discrete landmark tasks (quarterly starting at month 12 with a 12‑month horizon). For each landmark, loans active at that age are represented by static origination features, current time‑varying covariates, the two longitudinal coefficients, and a one‑hot landmark indicator. A logistic regression predicts binary default within the horizon. Variants include ablations without the longitudinal marker, without landmark encoding, and with additional techniques (time‑decay weighting, importance weighting). Final predictions are calibrated via isotonic regression. Models are compared against Cox proportional hazards, XGBoost, and drift‑adaptive online learners (HAT, ARF) under three simulated drift regimes applied to interest rates, loan‑to‑value, and balances. Performance is assessed using AUC, Brier score, and F1 with grouped five‑fold cross‑validation.

## Key Results
- Under sudden drift on 2020 data, LMISO achieves AUC 0.812, Brier score 0.102, and F1 0.924, exceeding XGBoost (AUC 0.794, Brier 0.126, F1 0.890) and other baselines.
- The longitudinal behavioral marker contributes the largest gains in discrimination; landmark one‑hot encoding adjusts for temporal heterogeneity; isotonic calibration ensures probability robustness.
- LMISO consistently outperforms across all drift types (sudden, incremental, recurring) and evaluation metrics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Longitudinal Behavioral Marker via Balance Mechanics
The balance‑deviation marker BD_pct(t) captures the relative difference between actual unpaid balance and the scheduled amortization path. Per‑loan linear regression on normalized age produces an intercept (early deviation) and slope (trend), which summarize repayment behavior in a form that remains informative under drift because it measures percentage departure from a theoretically derived schedule. Core assumption: the linear trajectory adequately summarizes dynamics and residuals are mean‑zero, and the relationship with default risk is stable across drift regimes. Evidence: [abstract], [section 3.1]. Break conditions include alterations to the amortization schedule (e.g., modifications, forbearance) or extreme policy interventions that decouple balance behavior from default risk.

### Mechanism 2: Landmark One‑Hot Encoding for Temporal Heterogeneity
Landmark‑specific intercepts (one‑hot encoding) allow the baseline log‑odds to shift across loan ages while keeping covariate effects constant, thereby adjusting for systematic risk variations (e.g., seasoning effects, survivorship) that may change under drift. This separates time‑specific risk levels from borrower‑specific predictors. Core assumption: temporal heterogeneity primarily acts on the baseline hazard, not on coefficients; landmark intervals are chosen to balance granularity and sample size. Evidence: [abstract], [section 3.4]. Break conditions include time‑varying covariate effects (non‑proportional hazards) or an excessive number of landmarks leading to overfitting.

### Mechanism 3: Isotonic Calibration for Probability Robustness
Isotonic regression is applied post‑hoc to raw hazard model predictions, enforcing monotonicity to align predicted probabilities with observed frequencies without altering the ranking. This corrects miscalibration induced by shifts in class prevalence or feature distributions. Core assumption: the true calibration curve is monotonic; higher raw scores correspond to higher true default probabilities even under drift. Evidence: [abstract], [section 3.5]. Break conditions: raw scores are not monotonically related to true probabilities (severe misspecification) or the calibration set is unrepresentative of the target distribution.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Data flows from raw monthly performance through balance deviation calculation (BD_pct) to per‑loan linear regression (ridge), producing longitudinal summaries (b0, b1). These, combined with static features and current time‑varying covariates at a chosen landmark age, are fed into a landmark sampler that creates training instances with a one‑hot landmark indicator. A logistic regression model (discrete‑time hazard) generates raw predictions, which are then passed to an isotonic calibrator before final probability output. Key computational components include the amortization engine (scheduled balance), per‑loan trajectory estimator, landmark constructor, hazard model, and calibrator.

**Critical path:**  
1. Preprocess: Merge origination and monthly data; compute BD_pct for each loan‑month.  
2. Fit per‑loan ridge regression of BD_pct on normalized age to obtain b0 and b1.  
3. For each landmark month L (e.g., quarterly starting at 12) and horizon H (12 months), construct training samples from loans alive at L, using features (static, time‑varying at L, b0, b1·(L/N_term), and one‑hot for L); target = 1 if default occurs in next H months.  
4. Train logistic regression (L2‑regularized) on these samples; optionally include time‑decay or importance weighting.  
5. Fit isotonic regressor on held‑out or out‑of‑fold predictions to map raw scores to calibrated probabilities.  
6. At prediction time, compute BD_pct history up to current age, derive b0/b1, assemble feature vector with landmark indicator, apply model and calibration.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Landmark frequency*: More landmarks give finer temporal resolution but reduce samples per landmark and increase model size.  
- *Longitudinal summary*: Linear fits are lightweight but may miss nonlinear repayment patterns; more flexible per‑loan models risk overfitting with short histories.  
- *Calibration*: Isotonic regression is non‑parametric and monotonic but can overfit with few calibration points; parametric alternatives (e.g., temperature scaling) trade flexibility for stability.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Poor discrimination* (AUC decreases): Longitudinal marker not predictive or misspecified; check per‑loan R² of trajectory fits.  
- *Poor calibration* (Brier increases): Drift causes baseline shift not captured by landmark encoding; isotonic regressor may be underfitting or calibration set unrepresentative.  
- *High variance across CV folds:* Model instability; could indicate sensitivity to landmark selection or insufficient per‑loan data.  
- *Very low F1:* Class imbalance not addressed; inspect prevalence in training vs test and adjust class weights.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline hazard without longitudinal marker:** Train M1 (no b0,b1) and compare AUC/Brier to M1‑Joint; expect substantial AUC gain from adding the marker.  
2. **Landmark ablation:** Compare M1‑Joint to M1‑LM (with landmark one‑hot); expect moderate AUC improvement and check if landmark coefficients vary across ages.  
3. **Calibration check:** Plot reliability diagrams for M1‑LM vs M1‑LMISO under sudden drift; isotonic calibration should align predicted probabilities with observed frequencies, reducing Brier while maintaining AUC.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** How does integrating multiple longitudinal behavioral markers (e.g., payment timing, credit utilization) alongside the balance‑based deviation affect model robustness and accuracy under data drift?  
*Basis:* The authors note extending to a multi‑trajectory joint model as natural future work.  
*Why unresolved:* Only a single balance‑based marker is evaluated; the added value of complementary signals remains unknown.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic ablations adding markers one by one (or in combinations) under various drift scenarios, measuring changes in AUC, Brier, and F1.

**Open Question 2:** To what extent do real macroeconomic factors (e.g., House Price Index, unemployment rate) improve model robustness compared to the parametric drift simulations?  
*Basis:* Future work acknowledges that additional realism could be achieved by incorporating macroeconomic factors, criticizing current simulations for lacking them.  
*Why unresolved:* Drift simulations rely on simple parametric shifts without capturing co‑movements with actual economic cycles, limiting external validity.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Retrospective validation on historical periods with documented shocks (e.g., 2008 crisis, COVID‑19), comparing models with and without macro variables; or embedding macro time series into the simulation framework.

**Open Question 3:** Does a non‑linear representation of the longitudinal trajectory (e.g., splines, Gaussian processes, RNNs) yield better discrimination and calibration under drift than the linear per‑loan OLS regression?  
*Basis:* The methodology uses a lightweight linear fit per loan, justified by efficiency, but may oversimplify complex repayment dynamics.  
*Why unresolved:* No alternative trajectory models are compared; the linear assumption could miss non‑linear behaviors that remain predictive under drift.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Replace linear per‑loan fits with flexible alternatives, evaluate stability of estimates and predictive performance under sudden vs. recurring drift, and check if non‑linearities improve calibration.

**Open Question 4:** Are isotonic regression calibrations optimal for all drift types, or do alternative post‑hoc calibrators (e.g., temperature scaling, Bayesian methods) maintain probability calibration better under certain regimes?  
*Basis:* Isotonic calibration is applied exclusively without comparison, assuming its monotonic property is universally beneficial.  
*Why unresolved:* Isotonic can overfit in small samples or under severe label drift; other calibrators may handle different error structures more robustly.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Apply multiple calibrators to the same raw LM predictions across all drift settings, assess Brier score breakdown by drift severity, and examine reliability diagrams to identify the best calibrator for each drift type.

## Limitations
- Major unknowns hinder faithful replication: exact hyperparameters (e.g., regularization strengths, landmark frequency), drift simulation details (label‑flipping mechanics), handling of missing values, and per‑loan regression window (leakage risk).
- External validity is limited to the Freddie Mac dataset and a subset of simulated drift patterns; real‑world macroeconomic factors are absent.
- Ablation analyses may conflate effects of longitudinal marker, landmark encoding, and calibration, making it difficult to isolate individual contributions.

## Confidence
- Longitudinal marker efficacy: High
- Landmark encoding benefit: Medium
- Isotonic calibration robustness: Medium
