# Are Large Language Models Truly Smarter Than Humans?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.16197
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16197
- Reference count: 12
- Primary result: Benchmark contamination on MMLU is pervasive, inflating LLM performance and undermining claims that LLMs surpass human expertise.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates benchmark contamination—the presence of public test data in LLM training corpora—in the MMLU benchmark, a widely used measure of knowledge and reasoning. The authors apply three independent methods to six frontier models, revealing substantial contamination across all subjects, with particularly high rates in STEM, Professional, and Humanities fields. The findings demonstrate that current evaluation practices overestimate LLM capabilities and challenge the narrative that these models have achieved human-level or superhuman intelligence.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates six large language models (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-V3, Llama-3.3-70B, Qwen3-235B) on a stratified sample of 513 MMLU questions. Experiment 1 uses lexical web detection: queries a search API for each question, flags contamination if ≥30% of the question’s 8‑grams appear in the returned snippets and the correct answer is verbatim present, and computes an Estimated Performance Gain (EPG). Experiment 2 generates paraphrase and indirect‑reference variants of a 100‑question subset using GPT-4o, then measures accuracy drops between original and variant forms. Experiment 3 employs TS‑Guessing behavioral probes: it masks either a wrong answer option (Option Mask) or a content word in the stem (Word Mask) and flags a question if the model reconstructs the original text above threshold (partial match ≥50% for Option Mask, exact match for Word Mask). The three approaches converge on a contamination ranking: STEM > Professional > Social Sciences > Humanities.

## Key Results
- Overall lexical contamination rate 13.8% (STEM 18.1%, Philosophy 66.7%); estimated performance gains of +0.030 to +0.054 accuracy points across categories.
- Surface‑form changes cause an average 7.0‑percentage‑point accuracy drop, with Law and Ethics each dropping 19.8 pp.
- TS‑Guessing signals appear on 72.5% of questions; DeepSeek‑R1 shows 76.6% partial reconstruction and 0% verbatim recall, indicating a distributed memorization pattern.
- All three methods independently rank contamination levels: STEM > Professional > Social Sciences > Humanities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Lexical contamination detection based on web overlap can identify benchmark questions likely present in training corpora, providing an external exposure estimate.
- **Mechanism**: For each sampled MMLU question, a web search query (first 150 chars) retrieves top snippets; the question is flagged if (a) ≥30% of its 8‑grams appear in the snippets and (b) the correct answer text appears verbatim. Contamination rate then informs estimated performance gain (EPG).
- **Core assumption**: Web presence of a question (or its exact answer) implies potential inclusion in training data; n‑gram overlap captures paraphrased exposure.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - Abstract: "Experiment 1 applies a lexical contamination detection pipeline to 513 MMLU questions across all 57 subjects, finding an overall contamination rate of 13.8% (18.1% in STEM, up to 66.7% in Philosophy) and estimated performance gains of +0.030–+0.054 accuracy points by category."
  - Section 4: "flagged contaminated if: (a) the fraction of the question’s 8‑grams ... exceeded 0.30; and (b) the correct answer text appeared verbatim in the snippets."
  - CorTAM/EPG metric validated in neighbor paper arXiv:2411.03923.
- **Break condition**: If the model’s training data includes non-indexed sources or heavily paraphrased versions not captured by the query substring, this method yields a lower bound; also, high lexical overlap might occur for common factual statements without indicating true benchmark exposure.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Accuracy degradation when questions are rephrased exposes dependence on surface‑form memorization rather than robust knowledge.
- **Mechanism**: Sample MMLU questions; generate paraphrased wording and indirect‑reference variants (replacing key entities with descriptive properties). Evaluate multiple models on all three forms; compute drop in accuracy from original to indirect. Larger drops indicate stronger reliance on surface patterns.
- **Core assumption**: A model that truly understands the underlying concept should be invariant to superficial wording changes; sensitivity suggests memorization of specific phrasing from training data.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "Experiment 2 applies a paraphrase and indirect‑reference diagnostic to 100 MMLU questions... finding that model accuracy drops by an average of 7.0 percentage points when surface wording changes to indirect reference—rising to 19.8 pp in both Law and Ethics."
  - Section 5: "For each question, GPT‑4o generated two variants: (a) a paraphrased version ... and (b) an indirect‑reference version... All six models were evaluated on all three forms at temperature 0."
  - Supporting paper arXiv:2509.00072 addresses memorization vs. reasoning distinction.
- **Break condition**: If variant generation inadvertently alters the difficulty or semantic load (e.g., making the question easier/harder), accuracy changes may not reflect memorization; careful validation of equivalent knowledge demand is needed.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: TS‑Guessing (Testset Slot Guessing) provides an internal, model‑agnostic probe of memorization by masking parts of the benchmark question and measuring the model’s ability to reconstruct the original content.
- **Mechanism**: Two tasks: (A) Option Mask – replace one wrong answer choice with [MASK], ask the model to output the original text of that choice; (B) Word Mask – blank a content word in the stem and ask for the missing word. A question is flagged if reconstruction rate exceeds thresholds (OM partial ≥50% or WM exact = 1). High flagging indicates memorized content.
- **Core assumption**: A model that has stored the exact benchmark can regenerate masked segments; a model that has not seen the item will rely on logical inference or guessing, yielding near‑chance performance.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "Experiment 3 applies TS‑Guessing behavioral probes to all 513 sampled questions... finding that 72.5% trigger memorization signals far above chance, with DeepSeek‑R1 displaying a distributed memorization signature (76.6% partial reconstruction, 0% verbatim recall)."
  - Section 6: "Task A – Option Mask... Task B – Word Mask... A question is flagged contaminated if OM partial≥0.50 or WM exact = 1."
  - Original TS‑Guessing paper arXiv:2311.09783.
- **Break condition**: The probe may yield false positives if the masked content can be logically deduced from the remaining question stem and options; using a wrong‑answer mask (Option Mask) and setting partial‑match thresholds mitigates this, but residual inference risk remains.

## Foundational Learning
- **Benchmark Contamination**  
  *Why needed*: The entire paper hinges on the idea that public benchmarks may be present in training data, invalidating performance comparisons.  
  *Quick check*: What are two ways benchmark questions can infiltrate LLM training corpora? (Answer: direct inclusion from scraped repositories; indirect inclusion via online tutorials, solution blogs, or textbook excerpts.)

- **Surface‑Form Robustness**  
  *Why needed*: Experiment 2’s diagnostic relies on the principle that true knowledge should survive wording changes; this concept distinguishes memorization from reasoning.  
  *Quick check*: If a model’s accuracy drops by 20 pp when the question is rephrased without changing the answer, what is the likely explanation? (Answer: The model was relying on memorized surface patterns rather than robust conceptual understanding.)

- **Behavioral Memorization Probes (TS‑Guessing)**  
  *Why needed*: Experiment 3 demonstrates a method to directly test whether the model internally stores specific benchmark content, complementing external lexical checks.  
  *Quick check*: What is the key difference between Option Mask and Word Mask in TS‑Guessing? (Answer: Option Mask hides a wrong answer choice and asks for its original text; Word Mask hides a content word in the question stem and asks to fill it.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Data Sampler -> Lexical Detector -> Variant Generator -> Model Evaluator -> TS‑Guessing Engine -> Aggregator Analyzer

**Critical path**:  
1. Obtain MMLU test split and fix random seed (42).  
2. Run Lexical Detector on all sampled questions to get contamination labels and EPG.  
3. For the 100‑question subset, generate variant questions (paraphrase/indirect).  
4. Evaluate all six models on original + variants; compute subject‑level drops.  
5. Run TS‑Guessing on the full 513‑question set for all models; compute flagging rates.  
6. Cross‑check consistency of category rankings across the three results.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Lexical vs. Behavioral: Lexical detection is external and low‑cost but incomplete; behavioral probes are internal and comprehensive but expensive. The paper combines both to balance coverage and depth.  
- Variant fidelity vs. diversity: Paraphrase/indirect generation aims for semantic equivalence, but too much lexical change could alter difficulty; a moderate transformation is chosen.  
- Sample size vs. cost: 513 questions cover all subjects; 100 for Exp2 balances statistical power with API costs; larger samples would strengthen but increase expense.  
- Threshold selection: 8‑gram overlap≥0.30 and answer presence; OM partial≥0.50; these cutoffs control false positives but may miss subtle contamination.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Inconsistent category rankings across the three experiments: would suggest method‑specific biases. The paper shows convergence, validating the approach.  
- Extremely high verbatim recall in Exp3 plus low Exp2 drops: could indicate overfitting to specific wording; not observed.  
- Variant generation producing unsound questions: would be flagged by human inspection or by unusually large accuracy increases (as seen in GPT‑4o’s paraphrased scores).  
- TS‑Guessing flag rates near random: would mean no memorization detected; actual rates are far above baseline.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Lexical contamination detection (Exp1) – establishes exposure rates and EPG.  
2. Paraphrase/indirect‑reference diagnostic (Exp2) – quantifies surface‑form sensitivity and domain‑specific impact.  
3. TS‑Guessing behavioral probe (Exp3) – directly measures internal memorization and explains model‑specific anomalies.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: What benchmark design principles and evaluation protocols can reliably separate genuine LLM generalization from memorization‑driven performance on contaminated evaluations? The paper notes that without decontaminated, withheld benchmarks and surface‑form robustness testing, claims of human‑level performance cannot be taken at face value.
- **Open Question 2**: What causes the distinct "distributed memorization" signature observed in DeepSeek‑R1—high partial reconstruction of wrong options with zero verbatim recall—and is it architecturally determined? The paper suggests investigating training data curation, architecture, or optimization choices.
- **Open Question 3**: How can contamination detection methods be improved to capture non‑lexical memorization (e.g., paraphrased, conceptual exposure) that evades current web‑based lexical scans? The gap between Exp1 (web) and Exp3 (behavioral) rates suggests significant "externally invisible" memorization.
- **Open Question 4**: Which specific properties of online textual presence (e.g., source type, volume of duplicates, solution depth) are strongest predictors of behavioral memorization signals in LLMs? The paper proposes a systematic study of web‑presence features.
- **Open Question 5**: What is the causal relationship between benchmark contamination exposure and increased hallucination rates on out‑of‑distribution, real‑world tasks? The paper posits that contamination may interact with hallucination but a direct causal study is lacking.

## Limitations
- Prompt templates for TS‑Guessing and variant generation are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.
- The EPG formula assumes a fixed 40 % contamination impact per flagged question, which is not empirically derived for all models or subjects.
- Indirect‑reference variants may alter question difficulty, confounding the interpretation of accuracy drops; TS‑Guessing partial‑match thresholds risk false positives from logical inference.

## Confidence
- **High**: Convergent ranking of contamination across all three methods supports the presence of pervasive, non‑uniform contamination.
- **Medium**: Quantitative magnitudes (EPG +0.030–0.054, 7 pp average drop, 72.5 % memorization signal) are method‑dependent and sensitive to threshold choices.
- **Low**: The claim that observed performance gains are entirely attributable to contamination cannot be fully separated from other factors; some individual subject‑level results (e.g., 66.7 % Philosophy rate) are fragile due to small samples.

## Next Checks

2. Conduct a human evaluation of variant equivalence: sample original/variant triples and have domain experts judge whether knowledge demand and semantic difficulty are invariant; compute inter‑rater reliability and correlate with accuracy drop.
