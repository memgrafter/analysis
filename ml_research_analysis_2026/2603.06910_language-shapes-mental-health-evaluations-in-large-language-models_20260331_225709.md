# Language Shapes Mental Health Evaluations in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06910
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06910
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Chinese prompts systematically increase stigma scores and decrease sensitivity in mental health evaluations compared to English prompts.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether the language of a prompt systematically influences mental health evaluations in large language models (LLMs). Using GPT-4o and Qwen3, the authors compared Chinese and English prompts across validated stigma scales and two downstream tasks: mental health stigma detection and depression severity classification. Results show that Chinese prompts consistently led to higher stigma-related scores and reduced sensitivity in detection tasks, alongside more under-estimation errors in severity prediction. The findings demonstrate that prompt language shifts both evaluative orientation and decision thresholds in LLM outputs, highlighting the cultural embedding of LLM behavior.

## Method Summary
The study employed a zero-shot prompting design to compare bilingual (Chinese vs. English) performance on mental health assessments. Validated stigma instruments (e.g., DDS, MISS, SSOSH) were administered with 100 iterations per item, and two downstream tasks—stigma detection and depression severity classification—were evaluated with 30 iterations per instance. Responses were aggregated (majority vote for classification, mean for scales) and analyzed using Welch's t-tests for scale differences, McNemar's test for paired classification outcomes, and bootstrap methods for precision/F1. Models were fixed at temperature 0.0, and translation equivalence was pursued via back-translation and verification.

## Key Results
- Chinese prompts produced significantly higher stigma scores across social, self, and professional stigma measures for both models.
- In stigma detection, Chinese prompts reduced recall: GPT-4o (0.428 vs. 0.466) and Qwen3 (0.441 vs. 0.547).
- In depression severity prediction, Chinese prompts led to more under-estimation errors, indicating a systematic downward shift in predicted severity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** Prompt language activates different cultural priors in the model's latent space, shifting normative evaluative orientation. Chinese-language prompts may more strongly activate cultural narratives linking mental illness to social reputation loss or family shame, leading to higher agreement with stigmatizing items. This is supported by the consistent directional effect across scales and the use of original (not translated) Chinese prompts.

**Mechanism 2:** The elevated stigma scores under Chinese prompts reflect a more conservative, negative baseline stance, which shifts the effective decision threshold in downstream tasks. This manifests as reduced sensitivity (higher threshold for classifying content as stigmatizing) and a tendency to downgrade severity estimates. The correlated pattern between scale scores and task errors across models supports a shared latent disposition.

**Mechanism 3:** Model provenance and alignment may moderate sensitivity to prompt language. Qwen3, a Chinese-origin model, showed larger performance gaps than GPT-4o, possibly due to more intensive alignment on Chinese-language safety or value objectives. This suggests that alignment recipes influence the degree of language-specific behavioral plasticity.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Bilingual prompt generation → Model inference (frozen weights, temperature 0.0) → Response aggregation (majority vote/mean) → Response parsing and scoring → Statistical comparison module.

**Critical path:** Construct zero-shot prompts → Run inference (30–100 iterations) → Extract structured outputs → Compute metrics → Apply paired statistical tests.

**Design tradeoffs:** 
- Determinism vs. robustness: Temperature 0 ensures reproducibility but may mask inherent variability; robustness checks with temperature 1 confirmed pattern stability.
- Translation fidelity: Back-translation and GPT-4.1 verification were used, but subtle pragmatic differences may remain.
- Task framing: Zero-shot avoids few-shot confounding but may not elicit optimal performance; it ensures fair cross-lingual comparison of base knowledge.

**Failure signatures:**
- Scale inversion: Reversed-scored items not properly inverted during parsing.
- Translation drift: Chinese prompts inadvertently becoming more/less severe in connotation.
- Model version drift: API updates altering behavior; requires version pinning.
- Parsing failures: LLM output format violations breaking extraction.
- Low statistical power: Inadequate iterations leading to unstable estimates.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Replicate stigma scale differences with a new random seed (temperature 0) to verify directional hypotheses.
2. Probe threshold sensitivity by varying prompt wording (e.g., "consider ANY hint of stigma" vs. "only CERTAIN stigma") to test if language gap narrows/widens.
3. Deep-dive error patterns: manually inspect pairs where Chinese prompt under-estimated and English over-estimated depression severity, coding for linguistic features (e.g., somatic vs. psychological language).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Do cross-linguistic differences extend to other widely spoken languages beyond Chinese and English (e.g., Spanish, Arabic, Hindi)?
- Do other LLMs, particularly open-source or differently aligned ones, exhibit similar cross-linguistic shifts?
- Beyond stigma, what other culturally embedded mental health constructs (e.g., emotional expression norms, somatic vs. psychological symptom interpretation) show cross-linguistic variation?
- How stable are these patterns over time as models are updated? Do alignment interventions reduce or amplify differences?
- Can language-contingent shifts create feedback loops that alter human discourse and stigma attitudes within linguistic communities?

## Limitations
- Translation equivalence concerns: subtle pragmatic or connotative differences in Chinese prompts may confound results despite back-translation.
- Dataset and prompt opacity: exact benchmark datasets [45, 46] and full prompt templates are unavailable, blocking exact replication.
- Generalizability limited to two models (GPT-4o, Qwen3) and two languages; effects may not hold for other architectures or language pairs.

## Confidence
- **Core finding (language effects):** High confidence. Consistent directional differences across models, scales, and tasks with statistical testing.
- **Mechanism 1 (cultural prior activation):** Medium confidence. Aligns with known cross-cultural stigma patterns but direct evidence for latent activation is inferential.
- **Mechanism 2 (threshold shift linkage):** Medium confidence. Correlated directional shifts support linkage, but the theoretical model of a shared latent disposition is not directly validated.
- **Mechanism 3 (model-specific sensitivity):** Low confidence. Magnitude difference between models is suggestive but based on only two data points; alignment recipe speculation lacks internal access.

## Next Checks
1. **Independent translation audit:** Recruit bilingual mental health experts to rate semantic/pragmatic equivalence of a stratified sample of prompts/disclosures on severity, emotional tone, and stigma-likelihood.
2. **Third-model replication:** Re-run core comparisons using a distinct model (e.g., Claude 3.5 Sonnet) with identical protocols to test persistence of Chinese>English effect.
3. **Causal ablation via "translate-back" prompt sets:** Create English prompts that are first translated to Chinese and then back-translated; if the effect attenuates/reverses, it supports a language-structure mechanism over pure semantics.
