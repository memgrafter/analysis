# Making AI Evaluation Deployment Relevant Through Context Specification

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.06811
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06811
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Proposes context specification to convert stakeholder priorities into explicit, measurable constructs via a Context Brief, enabling AI evaluations that directly inform real-world deployment decisions.

## Executive Summary
Current AI evaluations frequently rely on generic benchmarks that disregard operational context, yielding metrics disconnected from actual deployment outcomes. This paper introduces "context specification" as a structured process to elicit and formalize what matters—such as productivity, fairness, or over-reliance—within a specific organizational setting. The core output is a Context Brief, which maps stakeholder concerns to named, evaluable constructs and distinguishes evidence needs for simulated (`in silico`) versus real-world (`in situ`) observation. This framework shifts evaluation design from model-centric testing to impact-centric assessment, providing organizations with a actionable roadmap to make go/no-go, scaling, and governance decisions based on criteria that truly reflect the AI's probable effects in their unique workflows.

## Method Summary
The paper presents a conceptual framework development. It synthesizes literature on AI evaluation limitations and stakeholder theory to define a new process. No empirical validation or case studies are reported; the contribution is the principled design of the context specification workflow and the Context Brief artifact. The method is normative, outlining how organizations *should* translate qualitative priorities into evaluation plans, rather than describing how a prior evaluation was conducted.

## Key Results
- Identifies a critical gap: operational context is absent from standard AI evaluations, leading to metrics that do not predict real-world utility or harm.
- Defines "context specification" as the process of making stakeholder values explicit, naming measurable constructs, and planning evidence collection.
- Produces the Context Brief template, which operationalizes this process by linking concerns (e.g., fairness) to specific constructs (e.g., disparate impact by demographic group) and specifying whether observation requires sandboxed testing or live deployment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism works by forcing a translation between the abstract, often tacit, priorities of stakeholders (e.g., "we can't trust this AI") and the concrete, operationalized constructs that an evaluation can measure (e.g., "rate of human override in high-stakes decisions"). This translation bridges the valueladen, contextual world of deployment with the formal, metric-driven world of evaluation. By explicitly naming constructs and their evidence requirements (in silico vs. in situ), the process prevents the accidental substitution of convenient but irrelevant benchmarks for truly impactful assessment criteria. It makes the implicit trade-offs in AI deployment explicit and debatable, aligning evaluation design with organizational goals.

## Foundational Learning
- **Operational Context:** The specific workflow, constraints, and human-AI interaction patterns of a deployment site. *Why needed:* Generic benchmarks ignore these, leading to poor predictive validity. *Quick check:* Can you list the three most common failure modes in the target workflow?
- **Construct Mapping:** The act of defining a latent concept (e.g., "fairness") as a specific, observable, and measurable attribute. *Why needed:* Enables evaluation instead of vague discussion. *Quick check:* For a stated concern, can you write a single sentence describing exactly what data would indicate it is present or absent?
- **In Silico vs. In Situ Evidence:** Distinguishing between observations made in controlled, offline settings (e.g., test datasets) versus live, operational environments. *Why needed:* Determines feasibility and risk of evidence gathering. *Quick check:* For your key construct, must it be measured with real users and consequences, or can a proxy suffice?
- **Stakeholder Prioritization:** Eliciting and ranking what "matters" from diverse parties (users, managers, compliance, etc.). *Why needed:* Evaluation scope must reflect the most critical organizational risks and goals. *Quick check:* Have you obtained written agreement on the top three evaluation priorities from each stakeholder group?
- **Construct Validity Argument:** The rationale linking a proposed metric to the underlying construct of interest. *Why needed:* Prevents metric hijacking where a easily-measured proxy diverges from the true goal. *Quick check:* For each metric, can you articulate two ways it could be gamed without improving the real-world outcome?

## Architecture Onboarding
The Context Brief process can be viewed as a sequential workflow:
Stakeholder Elicitation -> Construct Definition -> Metric Specification -> Evidence Planning -> Decision Rule Formulation

**Critical Path:** The most dependent sequence is from **Stakeholder Elicitation** through **Construct Definition**. Ambiguous or misaligned stakeholder concerns will propagate into poorly defined constructs, undermining all subsequent steps. The earliest validation point is achieving consensus on a prioritized list of concerns.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Breadth vs. Depth:** Capturing many minor concerns yields an unmanageable evaluation plan versus focusing on a few critical constructs.
- **Stakeholder Diversity vs. Consensus:** Including all voices increases buy-in but makes prioritization harder; excluding voices risks blind spots.
- **Generality vs. Specificity:** More generic constructs (e.g., "trust") are easier to discuss but harder to measure validly than highly specific ones (e.g., "time to first override").

**Failure Signatures:**
- Constructs remain vague or operationalized with trivial, already-collected metrics (e.g., "user satisfaction" measured by a single 5-star rating).
- The evidence plan defaults entirely to `in silico` testing for all constructs, avoiding the cost/risk of `in situ` observation where many critical outcomes (e.g., workflow integration) manifest.
- No clear connection between a construct and a specific decision (e.g., "proceed to scale," "pause for mitigation").
- Key stakeholder groups (e.g., frontline workers) are not consulted, leading to post-deployment surprises.

**First Three Experiments:**
1. **Construct Clarity Stress Test:** Present the draft Context Brief's construct definitions to 3-5 subject matter experts *not* involved in its creation. Ask them to propose specific data collection methods for each. Inconsistencies indicate unclear constructs.
2. **Metric Hijacking Probe:** For each proposed metric, brainstorm 2-3 ways a team could "game" the metric (improve the number) while causing real-world harm. If gaming is trivial, the metric is invalid.
3. **Decision Threshold Mock Run:** Using the planned evidence, simulate a "report card" where all metrics are at the 95th percentile vs. the 50th percentile. Does the resulting recommendation (go/no-go) change in a way that aligns with stakeholder priorities? If not, the scoring/weighting is likely misconfigured.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
None explicitly raised by the authors; the paper is a framework proposal without an empirical study generating new research questions.

## Limitations
- The framework's adoption feasibility in resource-constrained organizations is unknown; the process requires significant cross-functional time and expertise.
- Generalizability is untested; construct mapping may break down in highly regulated domains (e.g., medical devices) or for certain AI capabilities (e.g., generative content).
- Operationalizing constructs introduces risk of new biases, as the choice of metrics and evidence-gathering methods can themselves shape and distort the very outcomes they intend to measure.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** Current evaluations fail due to ignoring operational context. **Confidence:** High (well-supported in existing literature on AI alignment and deployment).
- **Claim:** Context specification yields deployment-relevant evaluation criteria. **Confidence:** Medium (conceptually sound and addresses a clear gap, but lacks real-world implementation evidence).
- **Claim:** Organizations can readily adopt this process for go/no-go decisions. **Confidence:** Low (no adoption studies; process complexity, required skills, and organizational politics present major unknown barriers).

## Next Checks
1. **Pilot Implementation Study:** Deploy the full Context Brief process in 3–5 organizations across different sectors (e.g., healthcare diagnostic aid, manufacturing defect detection, customer service chatbot). Document process fidelity, time/resource costs, stakeholder engagement challenges, and the usability of the final Brief for actual launch decisions.
2. **Longitudinal Impact Tracking:** For pilot sites, track actual deployment outcomes (e.g., productivity changes, fairness incident rates, user burnout) over 6–12 months. Compare these outcomes against predictions made using Context Brief-derived metrics versus standard model benchmark scores to assess predictive validity.
3. **Cross-Sector Replicability Analysis:** Apply the process in two starkly different sectors (e.g., a high-stakes, low-volume domain like radiology vs. a low-stakes, high-volume domain like recommendation systems). Analyze the transferability of the construct mapping step, identifying which constructs and evidence plans are context-universal versus context-specific.
