# Large Language Model Reasoning Failures
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.06176
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06176
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Introduces a two-axis taxonomy (reasoning type vs. failure scope) to systematically categorize LLM reasoning failures and releases a curated GitHub repository of relevant research.

## Executive Summary
This survey paper addresses the persistent issue of reasoning failures in large language models (LLMs), even in seemingly simple tasks. The authors propose a novel two-axis taxonomy to classify these failures along reasoning type (non-embodied, subdivided into informal and formal; and embodied) and failure scope (fundamental, application-specific, and robustness). Through systematic analysis of existing literature, they identify root causes—such as the next-token prediction objective, architectural constraints, and lack of embodiment—and summarize mitigation strategies. As a practical outcome, they release a comprehensive GitHub repository (https://github.com/Peiyang-Song/Awesome-LLM-Reasoning-Failures) curating research on LLM reasoning failures, providing a unified perspective on systemic weaknesses and guidance for building more reliable reasoning capabilities.

## Method Summary
The paper employs a literature survey methodology. It defines a two-dimensional taxonomy (reasoning type × failure scope) to organize existing research on LLM reasoning failures. The authors analyze each failure category, explore underlying root causes (e.g., training objectives, architecture, grounding), and compile mitigation strategies. No new experiments or model training are conducted; instead, the work synthesizes and structures a fragmented research landscape. The primary artifact is a public GitHub repository that curates and categorizes relevant papers according to the taxonomy, serving as a living resource for the community.

## Key Results
- Proposes a two-axis taxonomy to classify LLM reasoning failures by reasoning type (embodied, informal/formal non-embodied) and failure scope (fundamental, application-specific, robustness).
- Identifies three primary root causes: (1) superficial pattern matching from next-token prediction, (2) architectural constraints (causal masking, attention dispersion, limited context), and (3) absence of embodied grounding.
- Releases a curated GitHub repository (Awesome-LLM-Reasoning-Failures) providing a collection of research works mapped to the taxonomy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Superficial Pattern Matching due to Next Token Prediction Training Objective
LLMs fail at many reasoning tasks because the standard next-token prediction objective optimizes for statistical pattern completion rather than deliberate, multi-step reasoning. The training objective encourages the model to capture shallow correlations and frequent surface patterns in the training data. When faced with a problem requiring logical deduction or composition, the model often retrieves or interpolates similar token sequences instead of constructing an explicit reasoning chain. This leads to failures on tasks that deviate from training distribution or demand integration of multiple pieces of information. The break condition would be training with objectives that explicitly reward correct intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., process supervision, reinforcement learning on step-wise accuracy).

### Mechanism 2: Architectural Constraints on Context and Information Flow
Fundamental limitations of the Transformer architecture—including causal masking, attention dispersion, and finite context windows—cause systematic breakdowns in tasks requiring long-range dependencies or bidirectional inference. Decoder-only Transformers process tokens autoregressively, enforcing a left-to-right order that prevents learning symmetric relations (e.g., “A is B” vs. “B is A”). Self-attention, though theoretically global, often distributes focus across many tokens, reducing effective working memory. These constraints lead to failures like the reversal curse, poor compositional reasoning, and order-sensitivity. The break condition would be replacing or augmenting Transformers with architectures with recurrence, bidirectional context, or explicit working memory mechanisms.

### Mechanism 3: Absence of Embodied Grounding and Real-World Experience
LLMs lack embodied interaction with the physical and social world, resulting in flawed representations of physical laws, object properties, and mental states, which manifest as failures in embodied and social reasoning. Human reasoning develops through sensorimotor experience and social interaction, providing direct feedback for calibrating internal models. LLMs trained solely on text derive their knowledge from linguistic co-occurrence, which may encode superficial correlations without causal or physical validity. Consequently, they struggle with physical commonsense (e.g., object affordances), theory of mind, and tasks requiring dynamic spatial reasoning. The break condition would be integrating LLMs with embodied agents that learn from active interaction (simulation or robotics).

## Foundational Learning
### Two-Axis Taxonomy (Reasoning Type × Failure Scope)
- **Why needed**: The entire survey organizes reasoning failures using this two-dimensional framework; understanding it is essential to navigate the field and map individual failures to broader categories.
- **Quick check**: What are the three failure-scope categories (fundamental, application-specific, robustness) and how do they differ? (Fundamental: intrinsic to LLM architecture/training; Application-specific: confined to a domain/task; Robustness: performance drops under minor input variations.)

### Embodied vs. Non-Embodied Reasoning
- **Why needed**: This distinction separates reasoning that requires physical interaction (embodied) from purely cognitive tasks (non-embodied), clarifying why certain failures (e.g., spatial misestimation) arise only in embodied contexts.
- **Quick check**: Give one example of a task that involves embodied reasoning and one that involves informal non-embodied reasoning. (Embodied: predicting the outcome of pouring water into a tilted cup; Informal non-embodied: resolving ambiguous pronoun references in a narrative.)

### Fundamental, Application-Specific, and Robustness Failures
- **Why needed**: These scopes indicate the breadth of a failure—whether it stems from core architecture, is confined to a domain, or manifests as inconsistent performance under minor variations—guiding mitigation strategies.
- **Quick check**: What is the key difference between a fundamental failure and a robustness failure? (Fundamental failures are inherent to model design and persist across tasks; robustness failures are brittle responses to perturbations, even if base performance is high.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input text → Tokenizer → Token embeddings → Transformer stack (multiple layers, each with self-attention and feed-forward networks) → Output token distribution. Key components: (1) Training objective (next-token prediction), (2) Attention mechanism (causal masking, head diversity), (3) Context window size, (4) Training data (biases, heuristics).

**Critical path**: During inference, the model processes tokens sequentially. Embeddings are added with positional encodings, then each layer updates representations via self-attention (computing pairwise token interactions with causal masking) and a feed-forward network. The final layer produces logits over the vocabulary. Failures may occur at any stage: tokenization can split concepts, attention may fail to capture long-range dependencies, and the objective biases toward short-term pattern matching.

**Design tradeoffs**: Scale (parameters, data) vs. efficiency; autoregressive decoding vs. bidirectional pre-training; next-token prediction vs. alternative objectives (e.g., process supervision). Each tradeoff influences reasoning—for example, larger models improve fact recall but may still fail at composition.

**Failure signatures**: Strong sensitivity to option order in MCQs → causal masking bias; performance drop on paraphrased prompts → brittleness/overfitting; inability to handle multi-hop questions → working memory limits; inconsistent moral judgments → data-embedded biases.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Perturbation robustness test**: Take a standard reasoning benchmark (e.g., GSM8K or a ToM dataset) and generate semantically equivalent variants by reordering premises, renaming entities, or changing phrasing. Compare performance to quantify fragility.
2. **Working memory probe**: Use an N-back or A-not-B task to directly measure the model’s ability to maintain and manipulate information over short intervals.
3. **Reversal test**: Assess bidirectional understanding by asking the model to answer both “A is B” and “B is A” given a factual premise; a large performance gap indicates a reversal curse.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What are the precise internal mechanisms (e.g., attention coordination, representational limitations) that cause LLMs to fail at compositional reasoning, higher-order theory of mind, 2D/3D physical commonsense, and brittle multi-agent planning?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "First, root cause analyses remain incomplete for some failures, including compositional reasoning breakdowns (Section 4.1), higher-order ToM failures (Section 3.2), physical commonsense gaps in 2D and 3D environments (Sections 5.2, 5.3), and brittle multi-agent planning (Section 3.3). Bridging these requires connecting behavioral errors to specific internal mechanisms, e.g., faulty attention head coordination or insufficient intermediate representation alignment."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper identifies these as key gaps where we have observed systematic failures but lack deep understanding of the underlying neural or architectural causes, preventing targeted architectural improvements.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Detailed mechanistic analyses (e.g., probing attention patterns, activation analysis, or controlled ablations) that correlate specific failure patterns with definable internal model behaviors across multiple architectures.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How should a unified, persistent LLM reasoning failure benchmark be designed to comprehensively cover all failure types in the proposed taxonomy, remain contamination-resistant over time, and track longitudinal model progress?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "Second, the field would benefit from unified, persistent failure benchmarks that span all failure types, akin to the very recent effort Malek et al. (2025), updated regularly to test the latest general-purpose and reasoning-specialized models. Such benchmarks should preserve historically challenging cases while incorporating newly discovered ones, enabling longitudinal tracking of failure persistence."
- **Why unresolved**: Current evaluation is fragmented across domain-specific benchmarks; no single resource systematically covers the full taxonomy of failures, making it difficult to assess whether improvements on one benchmark generalize or merely reflect overfitting.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A publicly maintained, versioned benchmark suite with items mapped to the two-axis taxonomy, including both canonical failure cases and adaptive perturbations, with documented model performance trajectories over multiple releases.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Which failure-injection and perturbation strategies most effectively and efficiently expose robustness vulnerabilities across diverse reasoning domains while preserving task validity?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "Third, failure-injection principles could be applied not only to dedicated robustness benchmarks but also to general reasoning benchmarks—by adding adversarial sections, multi-level task difficulty, or cross-domain compositions designed to trigger known weaknesses."
- **Why unresolved**: Various perturbation techniques exist (option reordering, irrelevant context insertion, symbolic template variants), but their relative effectiveness, efficiency, and domain transferability are not well compared; some may introduce trivial changes, others may alter task semantics.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic comparative studies measuring: (a) sensitivity rates across reasoning categories, (b) correlation with real-world deployment failures, and (c) computational cost of generating valid perturbed instances across domains.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Do current reasoning-enhancement approaches (inference-time scaling, analogical reasoning, neuro-symbolic integration) genuinely mitigate established reasoning failure modes, or do they merely shift failures to new contexts?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "we advocate future research to examine if these emerging frameworks effectively mitigate established reasoning failures. Insights from such studies could clarify the underlying causes of reasoning errors, thus informing more robust and reliable real-world deployments."
- **Why unresolved**: Many techniques improve scores on specific benchmarks, but it is unclear whether they address fundamental failure mechanisms (e.g., working memory limits, lack of grounding) or provide only superficial pattern-matching improvements that fail under distribution shift.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Rigorous evaluations of enhanced models using the proposed failure taxonomy and perturbation-based robustness tests, comparing failure rates and patterns against baseline models across multiple reasoning categories, not just aggregate accuracy gains.

## Limitations
- **Taxonomy Scope & Completeness**: The two-axis taxonomy is a conceptual framework from a single survey; its exhaustiveness and objectivity in capturing all failure modes are uncertain. It may miss emergent failure types or over-split categories.
- **Mechanism Evidence**: The three root causes are synthesized from cited literature, not experimentally validated by this paper. Causal attribution is inferential; competing explanations (e.g., data quality, tuning artifacts) are not fully disentangled.
- **Repository Curation**: The GitHub curation is valuable but not a controlled study; selection bias in included papers and potential staleness are unknowns. Success is qualitative organization, not quantitative measurement of coverage.

## Confidence
- **High**: The training objective (next-token prediction) as a primary cause of superficial pattern matching is widely accepted in the literature.
- **Medium**: Architectural constraints (causal masking, attention limits) and absence of embodiment as fundamental causes are well-supported but not fully proven; alternative factors may contribute.
- **Low**: The completeness and operationalizability of the proposed taxonomy; the GitHub repository's coverage and maintenance over time.

## Next Checks
1. **Replication of Reversal Curse**: Test a modern LLM (e.g., GPT-4, Llama 3.1) on the original Berglund et al. (2023) reversal dataset to confirm if the failure persists at scale and under varied prompts.
2. **Taxonomy Application Audit**: Independently classify 50 recent "LLM reasoning failure" papers using the paper’s two-axis taxonomy. Measure inter-rater agreement with the authors’ implied classifications (from the GitHub repo) to assess clarity and operationalizability.
3. **Robustness Perturbation Benchmark**: Take a standard reasoning benchmark (e.g., GSM8K), create a suite of semantic perturbations (reorder conditions, rename entities, change phrasing), and evaluate model performance delta. Compare results against the paper’s robustness failure scope to quantify brittleness.
