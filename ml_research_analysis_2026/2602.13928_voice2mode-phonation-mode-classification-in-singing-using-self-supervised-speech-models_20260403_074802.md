# voice2mode: Phonation Mode Classification in Singing using Self-Supervised Speech Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13928
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13928
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Early HuBERT layers + linear SVM classify four singing phonation modes with 95.7% accuracy, beating conventional features by 12–15 percentage points.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents voice2mode, a method for automatically classifying four phonation modes (breathy, neutral, flow, pressed) in singing sustained vowels. It leverages hidden-state embeddings from self-supervised speech models (HuBERT, wav2vec2) and lightweight classifiers. On a public soprano dataset (763 recordings), embeddings from early HuBERT layers paired with a linear SVM achieve 95.7% accuracy—substantially outperforming spectrogram-based baselines (79.9%). Lower transformer layers, which retain richer acoustic detail, are optimal. Linear SVM consistently surpasses XGBoost, likely due to the small dataset size.

## Method Summary
The study uses a publicly available dataset of 763 sustained vowel recordings from a single professional soprano singer, sampled at 44.1 kHz (downsampled to 16 kHz) and normalized. The task is 4‑way classification of phonation modes. Hidden states from pre‑trained HuBERT‑Large, wav2vec2‑Base, and wav2vec2‑Large are extracted for each audio clip with `output_hidden_states=True`. Each layer’s output is globally pooled over time (mean) to produce a fixed‑length feature vector. These features train two classifiers: a linear SVM and XGBoost. Hyperparameters are tuned via grid search inside a nested 5‑fold cross‑validation scheme. Baseline features include time‑averaged spectrogram, mel‑spectrogram, and MFCCs processed identically.

## Key Results
- HuBERT layer 5 embeddings with linear SVM yield 95.7% mean accuracy (± std).
- This represents an absolute 12–15 pp gain over the best conventional baseline (spectrogram + SVM at 79.9%).
- SVM outperforms XGBoost across all tested feature sets, including baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Lower transformer layers in self‑supervised models preserve fine‑grained acoustic and phonetic details—such as harmonic structure and noise components—that are critical for distinguishing subtle singing phonation modes. Higher layers are optimized for abstract ASR targets (e.g., phoneme recognition) and discard this information. The linear SVM’s simplicity and strong regularization generalize well to the small, imbalanced dataset, while XGBoost’s greater capacity leads to overfitting despite hyperparameter tuning.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Audio (16 kHz) → SSL Model (HuBERT/wav2vec2) → Hidden States (all layers) → Global Mean Pooling (per layer) → Feature Vector → Classifier (SVM/XGBoost) → Predicted Mode

**Critical path:** Feature extraction from SSL models is the foundational step; classification depends entirely on the quality and dimensionality of these embeddings. Proper stratification in cross‑validation is crucial due to class imbalance.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Layer choice: Lower layers preserve acoustic detail but may contain more redundancy; higher layers are more compact but lose task‑relevant information.  
- Classifier choice: Linear SVM offers simplicity and regularization, while XGBoost models non‑linearities but risks overfitting on small data.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Incorrect pooling (e.g., including padding tokens) yields inflated feature dimensions and noise.
- Non‑nested hyperparameter tuning causes data leakage and overly optimistic accuracy.
- Dataset version mismatch (e.g., different recordings or labels) breaks comparability.
- Sampling rate not enforced at 16 kHz leads to model input errors or degraded features.
- XGBoost shows large gap between training and validation accuracy, indicating overfit.
