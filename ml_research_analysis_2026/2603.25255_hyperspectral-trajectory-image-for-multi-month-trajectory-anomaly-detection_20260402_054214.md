# Hyperspectral Trajectory Image for Multi-Month Trajectory Anomaly Detection
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.25255
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25255
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Achieves state-of-the-art AUC‑PR on dense (temporal 0.84, agent 0.98) and sparse (temporal 0.54, agent 0.63) benchmarks; Cyclic Factorized Transformer is 11–75× faster than a standard Transformer at comparable memory usage, enabling the first feasible multi‑month dense trajectory anomaly detection.

## Executive Summary
Trajectory anomaly detection traditionally faces a trade‑off: dense GPS preserves fine‑grained evidence but incurs prohibitive quadratic cost for multi‑month analysis, while sparse stay‑point methods discard such details, leading to separate architectures with no knowledge transfer. This paper unifies both modalities by reformulating anomaly detection as a vision problem through Hyperspectral Trajectory Images (HTI)—a day × time‑of‑day grid where each pixel encodes spatial, semantic, temporal, and kinematic information from either dense or sparse data. To efficiently model HTI, the Cyclic Factorized Transformer (CFT) factorizes attention along the within‑day and across‑day axes, encoding cyclic human routines and reducing attention complexity from quadratic to near‑linear scaling. TITAnD achieves state‑of‑the‑art AUC‑PR across benchmarks: on dense Tokyo data, it attains 0.84 temporal and 0.98 agent AUC; on sparse NumoSim‑LA, it reaches 0.54 temporal and 0.63 agent AUC, surpassing UNet and sparse‑specific methods. Compared to a standard Transformer, CFT is 11–75× faster at comparable memory, enabling the first feasible multi‑month dense trajectory anomaly detection.

## Method Summary
The method constructs an HTI tensor of shape (D, S, C) with D days, S = 288 five‑minute slots per day, and C = 256 channels. Spatial semantics are derived from a QuadTree‑based cell embedding that pools POI category information via attention. Temporal features embed calendar attributes (hour, day‑of‑week, day‑of‑month, part‑of‑day, week‑of‑month, month). Kinematic features differ for dense GPS (per‑slot velocity, acceleration statistics) and sparse stay‑points (event duration, distance, bearing). These three feature streams are concatenated and fused per slot via an MLP. The backbone is the Cyclic Factorized Transformer (CFT) with L = 4 blocks, each applying intra‑day attention over slots and inter‑day attention over days, using Rotary Position Embeddings on each axis. A per‑slot MLP classifier produces anomaly logits; the agent‑level score is the maximum over all slots. Training uses binary cross‑entropy plus Dice loss (positive class weight = 50), AdamW optimizer, cosine learning rate decay, mixed precision, and SpecAugment (frequency‑mask and time‑mask) for regularization.

## Key Results
- **Dense Tokyo**: temporal AUC‑PR 0.84, agent AUC‑PR 0.98.
- **Sparse NumoSim‑LA**: temporal AUC‑PR 0.54, agent AUC‑PR 0.63, surpassing UNet and sparse‑specific baselines.
- **Efficiency**: CFT is 11–75× faster than a standard Transformer while using comparable memory, making multi‑month dense analysis tractable.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Unifying representation via Hyperspectral Trajectory Images (HTI)**  
- **Claim**: Representing trajectories as an HTI unifies dense and sparse modalities under a single vision formulation, enabling knowledge transfer and reducing architectural specialization.  
- **Mechanism**: The HTI is a day × time‑of‑day grid (D × S) where each pixel aggregates spatio‑semantic, temporal, and kinematic features from raw GPS points or stay‑points via modality‑specific encoders. Agent‑level detection becomes image classification (max over pixels), and temporal localization becomes semantic segmentation (per‑pixel prediction).  
- **Core assumption**: Human mobility exhibits a natural two‑dimensional cyclic structure (within‑day episodes and across‑day routines) that can be mapped onto a fixed grid regardless of input density.  
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "reformulating anomaly detection as a vision problem through Hyperspectral Trajectory Images (HTI)..."; [section 3.1] "Under this formulation, agent-level detection reduces to image classification and temporal localization to semantic segmentation."  
- **Break condition**: If the cyclic grid fails to preserve modality‑specific nuances (e.g., short‑duration events in dense data) or if anomaly patterns do not align with the fixed 5‑minute slot granularity, the unified representation may lose discriminative signal.

**Mechanism 2: Efficient cyclic modeling via factorized attention**  
- **Claim**: Factorizing attention along within‑day (slot) and across‑day (day) axes reduces computational complexity from quadratic to near‑linear scaling while encoding human routine periodicity.  
- **Mechanism**: CFT interleaves intra‑day self‑attention (over S slots per day) and inter‑day self‑attention (over D days per slot). Intra‑day layers model episodes like commutes or detours; inter‑day layers model routine consistency across days (e.g., Monday vs. Tuesday morning commute). Complexity drops from O(D²S²C) to O((DS² + SD²)C).  
- **Core assumption**: Within‑day and across‑day patterns are conditionally independent given the HTI structure, and modeling them separately is sufficient for anomaly detection.  
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "factorizes attention along the two temporal axes, encoding the cyclic inductive bias of human routines, while reducing attention cost by orders of magnitude."; [section 3.3] describes the factorization; [section 4.3] ablation shows intra‑day alone 62% AUC, inter‑day alone 35%, full 84% on Dense Tokyo.  
- **Break condition**: If anomalies are predominantly cross‑day without strong within‑day structure (e.g., a rare multi‑day trip), inter‑day attention alone might suffice but factorization still incurs overhead; conversely, if routines are highly irregular, the cyclic bias may force misalignment.

**Mechanism 3: Joint necessity of HTI structure and factorized backbone**  
- **Claim**: The HTI representation and CFT architecture are jointly necessary; HTI alone does not guarantee gains, the backbone must explicitly exploit the cyclic structure.  
- **Mechanism**: HTI provides a structured grid, but without an architecture that respects its axes, a model must rediscover periodicity from a flat sequence (inefficient). CFT’s axial attention embeds the inductive bias directly.  
- **Core assumption**: Performance differences between HTI backbones are attributable to how well they capture the two cyclic axes, not merely representational capacity.  
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 4.2] shows Transformer (flat) performs worst among HTI backbones; U‑Net approaches CFT on agent detection but lags on temporal localization; [section 4.3] on Sparse NumoSim‑LA, intra‑day alone (0.09) and inter‑day alone (0.07) both fail, but combined CFT achieves 0.54, showing factorization is necessary; [abstract] "gains arise when the backbone explicitly exploits their cyclic structure."  
- **Break condition**: If anomaly detection primarily relies on local spatial patterns rather than temporal cycles, factorization may not confer advantage over local‑convolution backbones (U‑Net), as suggested by the agent‑detection results.

## Foundational Learning
- **Trajectory Discretization and Slotting**: Converting raw GPS streams or stay‑points into a fixed day × time‑of‑day grid (5‑minute slots) defines the HTI’s axes and temporal resolution.  
  *Quick check*: What are the trade‑offs in choosing a smaller (e.g., 1‑minute) vs. larger (e.g., 15‑minute) slot size for anomaly detection, considering data sparsity and computational cost?

- **Axial/ Factorized Attention**: CFT’s efficiency and inductive bias come from applying self‑attention separately along the two temporal axes (within‑day and across‑day) instead of flattening the HTI, enabling near‑linear scaling for long contexts.  
  *Quick check*: How does the computational complexity of a standard Transformer on a flattened HTI compare to CFT’s factorized approach for a 120‑day trajectory with 288 slots per day?

- **Hyperspectral Representation (Multi‑channel Grid)**: The HTI is “hyperspectral” because each pixel holds a feature vector (spatial, temporal, kinematic) rather than a scalar, allowing rich semantics (e.g., POI types, velocity statistics) to be encoded within the grid structure.  
  *Quick check*: Why is it advantageous to encode kinematic features (speed, acceleration) as part of the pixel embedding rather than as separate temporal sequences?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Input (dense or sparse trajectory) → modality‑specific encoder (DenseTrajEmbed or SparseTrajEmbed) → spatial cell embedding + temporal embedding + kinematic embedding → slot‑fusion MLP → HTI (tensor D × S × C) → Cyclic Factorized Transformer (L = 4 blocks: intra‑day attention → FFN → inter‑day attention → FFN) → per‑slot MLP classifier → per‑slot logits → agent score (max over slots).

**Critical path**  
Raw trajectory → Encoder → HTI → CFT → Per‑slot logits → Agent score. The CFT is the core innovation enabling efficient long‑context modeling; without it, the HTI would be too large for standard attention.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *HTI grid resolution*: Finer slots (5‑min) preserve short anomalies but increase D × S and memory; coarser slots reduce cost but may merge anomalous events with normal ones.  
- *Factorized vs. full attention*: CFT reduces cost and embeds cyclic bias but restricts direct cross‑talk between non‑aligned slots (e.g., 9 am Monday to 10 am Tuesday requires two hops). Full attention could capture arbitrary dependencies but is infeasible for multi‑month data.  
- *Encoder complexity*: DenseTrajEmbed resamples each slot to P = 30 points; this handles variable GPS density but discards fine timing within the slot. SparseTrajEmbed’s boundary blending (stop fraction f) smooths transitions but adds implementation complexity.

**Failure signatures**  
- Poor temporal localization (low mIoU): CFT missing one attention axis (ablation), slot size too coarse, or kinematic features omitted.  
- High agent‑level AUC but low temporal AUC: Model detects anomalous agents but cannot pinpoint when—likely because backbone lacks cross‑slot structure (e.g., U‑Net) or intra‑day attention disabled.  
- Memory blow‑up at scale: Using a standard Transformer instead of CFT leads to quadratic memory growth; check DS product (e.g., 120 × 288 = 34 560 tokens) and ensure CFT is used.  
- Encoder mismatch: Using the wrong encoder for the input modality (e.g., DenseTrajEmbed on sparse data) produces invalid HTI; verify input type.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. *Sanity check HTI construction*: For a single agent, visualize the HTI for one channel (e.g., velocity) to ensure expected patterns (high velocities on commute slots, low at home). Confirm sparse HTI shows discrete events.  
2. *Ablate CFT axes*: Train with only intra‑day attention, only inter‑day attention, and full CFT on a small subset. Expect full CFT to outperform on temporal AUC; if not, check positional encoding (RoPE) implementation per axis.  
3. *Scaling test*: Run inference with CFT and standard Transformer on increasing horizons (2, 4, 6, 8 months). Expect Transformer latency to grow quadratically; CFT should grow roughly linearly. Monitor GPU memory—both should be similar if using flash attention.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How can researchers construct large‑scale, real‑world trajectory datasets with verified anomaly labels to enable reliable evaluation of anomaly detection methods?  
  *Basis*: The paper acknowledges reliance on synthetically injected anomalies because large‑scale datasets with verified real‑world mobility anomaly labels are unavailable.  

- **Open Question 2**: Does TITAnD generalize to different geographic regions and demographic populations beyond the private Tokyo dataset?  
  *Basis*: Evaluation relies on benchmarks sharing the same private Tokyo data source, limiting geographic and demographic diversity.  

- **Open Question 3**: How do self‑supervised anomaly detection methods (e.g., USTAD, ICAD) compare to TITAnD when evaluated in their native self‑supervised settings, and can TITAnD benefit from self‑supervised pre‑training?  
  *Basis*: Baselines were adapted to supervised training; original self‑supervised objectives remain uncompared, and TITAnD’s potential for self‑supervised pre‑training is unexplored.  

- **Open Question 4**: Why does cyclic factorized attention provide a larger relative gain on sparse trajectories compared to dense ones, and what does this imply about the complementary roles of within‑day and across‑day patterns in sparse mobility data?  
  *Basis*: Ablation shows intra‑day alone yields 0.09 AUC (17% of full 0.54) and inter‑day alone 0.07 (13% of full) on sparse data, whereas on dense data intra‑day alone achieves 0.62 (74% of full 0.84). The paper does not investigate this discrepancy.

## Limitations
- **Spatial discretization details unspecified**: The QuadTree construction (initial bounding box, recursion order, boundary handling) is not fully described, risking shifts in spatial semantics and reproducibility.  
- **Sparse event‑to‑grid blending ambiguity**: The method for distributing multi‑slot events across the HTI (e.g., “stop fraction” blending) is ambiguous; it is unclear whether blending applies only at boundaries or across all overlapping slots.  
- **Evaluation limited to private/synthetic data**: Two benchmarks originate from a private Tokyo dataset, and all benchmarks use synthetically injected anomalies, limiting generalizability to real‑world anomalies and diverse geographic settings.

## Confidence
- **HTI unification**: Medium – The formulation is clear but no direct experiment demonstrates cross‑modality knowledge transfer; modality‑specific encoders may still diverge.  
- **CFT efficiency & cyclic bias**: High – Ablations isolate intra‑ vs. inter‑day contributions; complexity reduction is mathematically defined and empirically validated.  
- **Joint necessity (HTI+CFT)**: Medium‑Low – CFT outperforms other HTI backbones, but U‑Net matches on agent‑level detection; the advantage may be task‑dependent (temporal localization is harder).

## Next Checks
1. Modality transfer test: Pre‑train CFT on a large sparse dataset (NumoSim‑LA), freeze the HTI encoder, and fine‑tune the prediction head on a small dense subset (simulated via dense2sparse conversion). Expect transfer gain versus random initialization.  
2. Reconstruct sparse mapping: Implement both interpretations (“repeat features across all occupied slots” vs. “blend only at boundaries”) and compare resulting HTI sparsity patterns against any available statistics or visualizations to determine the correct implementation.  
3. QuadTree sensitivity sweep: Vary the QuadTree split threshold (25–100 POIs) and minimum cell size (10–50 m) on a fixed sparse dataset. Measure agent‑level AUC to identify a stable regime; report unique cell count to compare with the paper’s reported ~78 k.
