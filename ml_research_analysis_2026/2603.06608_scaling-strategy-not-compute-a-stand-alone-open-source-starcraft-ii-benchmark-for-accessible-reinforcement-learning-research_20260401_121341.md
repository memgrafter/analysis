# Scaling Strategy, Not Compute: A Stand-Alone, Open-Source StarCraft II Benchmark for Accessible Reinforcement Learning Research

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.06608
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06608
- Reference count: 27
- Primary result: The Two-Bridge Map Suite provides a lightweight, Gym-compatible StarCraft II benchmark that isolates tactical navigation and combat, enabling progress with modest compute budgets.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the gap between full, compute-intensive StarCraft II games and oversimplified mini-games by introducing the Two-Bridge Map Suite, a custom open-source map that disables economy mechanics. This isolates two core tactical skills—long-range navigation and micro-combat—within a controlled environment. Using standard PPO agents trained for 5 million timesteps on consumer-grade hardware, the benchmark yields coherent maneuvering and engagement behaviors across nine difficulty variants. Results demonstrate that agents learn to arbitrate between mutually exclusive navigation and combat objectives, with performance varying strategically based on unit count and objective proximity. The suite offers an accessible testbed that exposes clear headroom for algorithmic advancement without requiring massive computational resources.

## Method Summary
The Two-Bridge Map Suite is built on the PySC2 interface to StarCraft II. The custom map disables resource collection, base building, fog-of-war, and unit upgrades, leaving only basic movement and ranged attacks. The agent controls all friendly units centrally, receiving combined vector observations (unit metadata, beacon info) and spatial observations (screen and minimap). Actions are structured discrete commands (no-op, move, attack) with unit selection and direction/target choices; invalid actions are masked to reduce wasted exploration. Rewards are densely shaped: navigation reward depends on average distance change to a beacon; combat reward incorporates distance to enemy centroid, health changes, and kill bonuses; terminal bonuses reflect success or failure. The environment is wrapped as a Gym-compatible interface. The authors trained Maskable PPO from Stable-Baselines3 for 5M timesteps on a single RTX A4500 (5-6 days). Nine variants are generated by crossing unit counts (5v3, 5v5, 5v8) and proximity layouts (Base, Combat, Navigate), systematically probing strategic trade-offs. Evaluation measures terminal outcome distributions (navigation win, combat win, combat loss, timeout loss) and qualitative behaviors.

## Key Results
- Introduced an open-source, lightweight SC2 benchmark that isolates tactical navigation and combat without economy or fog-of-war.
- Standard PPO agents trained on consumer hardware exhibit coherent maneuvering and strategic arbitration between mutually exclusive objectives across all nine variants.
- Performance varies meaningfully with unit count and proximity, demonstrating the suite's diagnostic power and exposing headroom for algorithmic improvements.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark’s effectiveness stems from three design mechanisms:

1. **Economy removal isolates tactical decision-making.** By disabling resource collection, base building, fog-of-war, and upgrades, the environment eliminates long-horizon planning and information-gathering subproblems. The state is reduced to immediate unit positions, health, and a static beacon, making reward correlations dense and directly tied to movement and attack decisions. This isolates the core tactical loop, assuming sparse rewards in full SC2 arise mainly from strategic subcomponents.

2. **Mutually exclusive objectives force explicit arbitration.** The two tasks—capturing a beacon (navigation) and eliminating enemies (combat)—cannot both be achieved in a single episode. Agents must commit to one path based on initial conditions (spawn positions, unit counts), creating a clear behavioral stratification across variants. This binary choice serves as a sufficient proxy for strategic depth within the constrained domain.

3. **Variant cross-product samples the state space systematically.** The Cartesian product of unit count (three levels) and proximity layout (three levels) yields nine configurations that impose distinct tensions (e.g., numerical disadvantage vs. proximal combat). Randomized spawning within each variant requires generalization. This “choose‑your‑own‑adventure” suite reveals specific methodological gaps where agents fail, providing a gradient of difficulty for progressive research.

## Foundational Learning
- **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**  
  *Why needed:* PPO is used as a stable on-policy algorithm to validate that the environment itself presents a learning challenge, not that a specialized algorithm is required. Its sample efficiency under moderate compute is essential.  
  *Quick check:* Why might PPO struggle with the “camera-bound” failure mode despite dense rewards?

- **Action Masking**  
  *Why needed:* The structured action space includes many invalid combinations (e.g., attacking with no enemies). Masking prevents wasted exploration and speeds up learning.  
  *Quick check:* In the V2_Combat variant, which branches of the action space would be masked at the initial state?

- **Dense Reward Shaping**  
  *Why needed:* Sparse win/loss signals are insufficient within 5‑minute episodes. Shaping provides frequent feedback via bidirectional distance changes for navigation and distance/health/kill bonuses for combat.  
  *Quick check:* Why is the navigation reward averaged over all friendly units rather than per‑unit?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
StarCraft II (via PySC2) → custom Two‑Bridge maps → Gym wrapper → reward function → Stable‑Baselines3 (Maskable PPO) training

**Critical path:** Map design (Editor) → Wrapper implementation (Gym API) → Reward shaping integration → Training loop (SB3) → Evaluation logging.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Full observability vs. partial: No fog-of-war removes scouting but limits hidden‑information generalization.  
- Centralized vs. decentralized control: Single policy for all units; multi‑agent credit assignment left for future work.  
- Camera lock: Centering screen on friendly units avoids camera‑control confound but may induce myopic “camera‑bound” behavior.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Camera‑bound: Agent repeatedly selects no‑op after clearing local enemies, ignoring distant objectives.  
- Degenerate navigation: Single‑unit chases a fixed direction regardless of beacon.  
- Timeout dominance: >50% timeout losses in V3 variants indicate failure to commit to any objective.  
- Combat fragmentation: Units fail to focus fire, leading to inefficient engagements.  
- Map boundary exploitation: Agent retreats to edges to avoid combat.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Pilot: Train PPO/A2C on V2_Base with standard/streamlined observations for 2M steps to observe baseline behavior and identify single‑unit control patterns.  
2. Action Masking: Implement full masking across verb/unit/direction/target branches. Train on all nine variants for 5M steps to verify masking improves learning efficiency.  
3. Camera Lock: Enforce camera centering on friendly units. Retrain on all variants to assess whether richer screen observations mitigate or exacerbate camera‑bound failures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does introducing fog‑of‑war (partial observability) affect learning dynamics and strategic reasoning in the Two‑Bridge benchmark?
2. Does incorporating heterogeneous unit types (e.g., Marines with different attributes) increase tactical complexity and how does it impact learning?
3. Can decentralized multi‑agent control architectures achieve comparable or better performance than centralized control, and what coordination patterns emerge?
4. What causes the camera‑bound failure mode where agents ignore enemies outside the locked camera view, and how can it be mitigated?
5. Why do agents adopt a deterministic retreat path after partial unit loss, and is this behavior robust?

## Limitations
- Reproducibility details are missing: exact SB3 hyperparameters, evaluation protocol, and dynamic action masking implementation are unspecified.
- Camera‑lock design may induce myopic behavior (camera‑bound no‑ops) that limits scalability.
- Single‑policy, full‑observability assumptions restrict applicability to multi‑agent or partial‑information settings.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (economy removal isolates tactics): Medium – plausible but untested whether core tactical loop is fundamentally Markovian.
- Mechanism 2 (mutual exclusivity enables arbitration): High – clear behavioral divergence across variants supports the claim.
- Mechanism 3 (variant matrix exposes strategic pressure): Medium – some variants plateau, but others show headroom; broader difficulty gradient validation needed.

## Next Checks
1. Ablate action masking: train variants with/without masking to measure sample efficiency and final performance impact.
2. Diagnose camera‑bound failure: log unit positions vs. screen center; re‑train with enlarged screen to see if myopia persists.
3. Reproduce core results: fix random seed and retrain 5M steps; compare outcome distributions against paper’s curves; test sensitivity to missing reward scaling coefficients.
