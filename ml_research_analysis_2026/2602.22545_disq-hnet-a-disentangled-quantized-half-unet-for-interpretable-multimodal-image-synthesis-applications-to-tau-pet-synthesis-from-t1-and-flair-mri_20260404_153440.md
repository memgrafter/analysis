# DisQ-HNet: A Disentangled Quantized Half-UNet for Interpretable Multimodal Image Synthesis Applications to Tau-PET Synthesis from T1 and FLAIR MRI

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.22545
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22545
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: DisQ-HNet achieves best Braak staging accuracy (hard 0.482, soft 0.779) and reduces SUVR bias to -3.3% via PID-guided disentanglement and edge-conditioned decoding.

## Executive Summary
Tau-PET imaging is a key biomarker for Alzheimer's disease but suffers from high cost and limited availability. This paper introduces DisQ-HNet, a framework for synthesizing tau-PET from routinely acquired T1 and FLAIR MRI. The method combines a PID-guided vector-quantized encoder that separates latent information into redundant, unique, and complementary components with a Half-UNet decoder that uses pseudo-skip connections derived from a multi-scale T1 edge pyramid. On a validation cohort, the DQ2H-MSE-Inf variant of DisQ-HNet achieves state-of-the-art Braak staging accuracy (hard 0.482, soft 0.779) and reduces SUVR bias to -3.3% compared to UNet baselines (-18.4%), while preserving reconstruction fidelity. PID-Shapley analysis confirms that complementary cross-modal information contributes the most, demonstrating the model’s interpretability.

## Method Summary
DisQ-HNet processes T1 and FLAIR MRI through two redundant-unique (RU) encoders and a complementary (C) encoder. The RU encoders produce continuous latents that are discretized via vector quantization with a shared codebook; each quantized RU latent is split into a redundant part (r) and a unique part (u). The C encoder produces a complementary code (c). A Partial Information Decomposition (PID) loss enforces high redundancy between the two r codes, low mutual information between the two u codes, and low dependence between u and r or c. The decoder is a Half-UNet that starts from the concatenated latent [r1, u1, u2, c] at the coarsest scale and upsamples through four stages. At each stage, it fuses features with a pseudo-skip connection formed by a learned projection of a multi-scale Sobel edge pyramid extracted from the T1 image. Auxiliary heads predict the edge pyramid to enforce consistency. Training combines reconstruction losses (MSE, volumetric contrast, adversarial), vector quantization commitment, PID loss, variance floor to prevent latent collapse, and skip consistency loss. The model is trained with AdamW, learning rate warm-up and cosine decay, and uses stabilizations like random modality swapping and stochastic mixing of redundant codes.

## Key Results
- Achieves best Braak staging accuracy (hard 0.482, soft 0.779) on validation.
- Reduces SUVR bias to -3.3% compared to UNet baseline (-18.4%).
- PID-Shapley attributes largest marginal gains to complementary cross-modal information, confirming interpretable design.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: DisQ‑HNet’s PID‑guided vector‑quantized encoder is designed to enable interpretable modality attribution by explicitly partitioning latent information into redundant, unique, and complementary components.
- **Mechanism**: Two encoder streams process the inputs: (i) a redundant‑unique (RU) encoder applied independently to each modality produces pairs \((r_i, u_i)\); (ii) a complementary (C) encoder processes the concatenated modalities to produce \(c\). Each partition is quantized using separate codebooks. A differentiable soft‑assignment approximates discrete mutual information; the loss \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{info}} \) (Eqs. 9a–9e) enforces high dependence between \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) (and value consistency), low dependence between \(u_1\) and \(u_2\), and low cross‑factor dependence among \((u_i,r_1)\) and \((c,u_i/r_i)\). A variance‑floor penalty (Eq. 10) prevents collapse.
- **Core assumption**: The PID structure correctly characterizes multimodal contributions; i.e., the target tau‑PET signal can be decomposed into shared anatomy (redundant), modality‑specific contrasts (unique), and cross‑modal synergies (complementary) that map onto these latent groups.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “PID‑guided, vector‑quantized encoder that partitions latent information into redundant, unique, and complementary components.”
  - [section] §2.4 (Eqs. 9a–9e) defines \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{info}} \); Eq. 10–11 describe variance floor; swapping/mixing described in same section.
  - [corpus] Weak: no direct precedent for PID + VQ in medical synthesis; neighboring papers on disentangled representations (e.g., hierarchical diffusion autoencoders) and multimodal fusion (e.g., flexible multimodal neuroimaging) support the broader goal of interpretable latent factors.
- **Break condition**: After training, if \( \text{NMI}(u_1,u_2) \) remains high or \( \text{NMI}(r_1,r_2) \) is low, the enforced structure failed. Shapley analysis showing negligible complementary contribution on downstream tasks would also contradict the mechanism.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The Half‑UNet decoder with edge‑conditioned pseudo‑skip connections preserves anatomical detail while maintaining a bottleneck that remains interpretable, avoiding the shortcut effect of standard UNet skips.
- **Mechanism**: A multi‑scale Sobel‑based edge pyramid is built from T1 MRI. The decoder is initialized with the fused quantized latent factors at the coarsest scale. At each finer scale, the upsampled decoder features are concatenated with a learned projection of the corresponding edge map (pseudo‑skip). Auxiliary heads predict the edge pyramid to enforce \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{skip}} \) (Eq. 16).
- **Core assumption**: Structural edges extracted from a single MRI are sufficient to guide high‑frequency detail; direct reuse of encoder features is unnecessary and obscures latent interpretation.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “Half‑UNet decoder that preserves anatomical detail using pseudo‑skip connections conditioned on structural edge cues rather than direct encoder feature reuse.”
  - [section] §2.5 (Eqs. 12–16) details edge extraction, pyramid construction, and pseudo‑skip fusion; Figure 3 illustrates.
  - [corpus] Classical pyramid decompositions (Burt & Adelson) are well‑established; however, the corpus presents few direct medical examples of edge‑conditioned decoding.
- **Break condition**: If the edge reconstruction loss \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{skip}} \) does not decrease, the pseudo‑skip signal is weak, likely causing blurring. Poor MS‑SSIM despite decent PSNR could also indicate failure to preserve anatomy.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Anti‑collapse variance floor and redundant pathway stabilization prevent latent degeneration and ensure all PID partitions retain informative capacity, leading to stable training and meaningful attributions.
- **Mechanism**: For each partition \( z \in \{u_1,u_2,c\} \), a penalty \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{vf}}(z) \) (Eq. 10) discourages channel‑wise standard deviations below threshold \( \sigma_0 \). Additionally, during training, the two RU branches are symmetrized via (i) random modality swapping (probability 0.5) and (ii) stochastic mixing \( r_{\text{mix}} = \alpha r_1 + (1-\alpha)r_2 \), \( \alpha \sim U(0,1) \).
- **Core assumption**: Without explicit regularization, the optimizer may collapse unnecessary partitions to near‑zero variance or cause one redundant branch to dominate, breaking the intended disentanglement.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] Implied by “PID‑guided factorization.”
  - [section] §2.4 (subsection “Anti collapse variance floor” and “Redundant training stabilization”) describes these terms in detail.
  - [corpus] Neighbors on β‑VAE and disentanglement use variance‑related regularizers; this work adapts those to discrete PID, which is novel but conceptually aligned.
- **Break condition**: Per‑channel standard deviations of \( u \) or \( c \) near zero indicate collapse. A performance jump when swapping is enabled reveals pre‑existing asymmetry. Training instability (loss spikes) may also signal insufficient stabilization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Vector Quantization (VQ)**: Enables discrete codebook representations that lend themselves to information‑theoretic constraints via soft assignments and produce structured latents for PID factoring. Quick check: How does the straight‑through estimator allow gradients to flow through the non‑differentiable quantization step?
- **Partial Information Decomposition (PID)**: Provides the theoretical framework to separate information from two sources into redundant, unique, and synergistic parts, directly matching the multimodal attribution goal. Quick check: In tau‑PET synthesis, what kinds of signal would be captured by the unique components versus the complementary component?
- **Information Bottleneck Principle**: Underlies the motivation to compress information through a latent bottleneck; the principle guides the trade‑off between retaining task‑relevant signal and discarding irrelevant variation, which the PID losses further shape. Quick check: How does maximizing mutual information between redundant partitions differ from simply adding another reconstruction loss?
- **Sobel Edge Detection**: Used to extract structural edges from T1 MRI to guide the decoder without direct skip connections. Quick check: Why are edges more robust to intensity variations than raw pixel values for preserving anatomy?
- **Half-UNet Architecture**: Decoder that lacks encoder-to-decoder skip connections, relying instead on alternative guidance (edge pyramid) to preserve details while maintaining a strict latent bottleneck. Quick check: What are the risks of removing skip connections in high-resolution medical image synthesis?
- **Shapley Value Attribution**: Adapted to quantify each PID factor’s marginal contribution to the final synthesis, enabling interpretable multimodal analysis. Quick check: How does PID-Shapley differ from standard feature attribution methods like Integrated Gradients?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
- Modality encoders: two independent RU encoders (shared weights) for T1 and FLAIR, and one C encoder on concatenated modalities.
- Quantizers: separate codebooks \( E_{\text{RU}} \) (split into redundant \( E_R \) and unique \( E_U \)) and \( E_C \).
- PID factor: merges the two redundant streams \( r_1, r_2 \) into a shared \( z_R \) and retains unique \( z_{U1}, z_{U2} \); combines with complementary \( z_C \).
- Edge extractor: 3D Sobel filters → multi‑scale pyramid.
- Decoder: Half‑UNet with initial latent injection and pseudo‑skip fusion at each upsampling stage; auxiliary edge prediction heads.
- Loss modules: reconstruction (MSE/VCC/AG), VQ commitment, \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{info}} \), \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{skip}} \).

### Critical path
1. Encode T1 and FLAIR through RU encoders → continuous latents → quantize → split into \((r_i, u_i)\).
2. Encode concatenated inputs through C encoder → quantize → \(c\).
3. PID factorization: combine \(r_1, r_2\) into \(z_R\); keep \(u_1, u_2\) separate.
4. Concatenate \([z_R, u_1, u_2, c]\) → initial decoder feature \(d_L\).
5. For \(\ell = L-1,\dots,0\): upsample \(d_{\ell+1}\), fuse with projected edge map \(\psi_\ell(\hat{M}_\ell)\) → \(d_\ell\).
6. Final \(1\times1\times1\) projection yields predicted PET.

### Design tradeoffs
- PID factorization adds codebooks and loss terms → improved interpretability at cost of memory and hyperparameter tuning.
- Half‑UNet loses direct skip connections → may sacrifice some high‑frequency fidelity but avoids latent bypass.
- Edge pyramid uses simple Sobel filters → computationally cheap but sensitive to registration errors; a learned edge detector could be more robust.
- Codebook size \(K\) and embedding dimensions affect capacity: small \(K\) risks information loss; large \(K\) hinders disentanglement.

### Failure signatures
- **Latent collapse**: near‑zero variance in \(u_i\) or \(c\) channels (monitor \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{vf}} \) or per‑channel std).
- **Redundancy leakage**: high \( \text{NMI}(u_1,u_2) \) or low \( \text{NMI}(r_1,r_2) \) after training.
- **SUVR bias**: systematic under‑/over‑estimation in Bland‑Altman analysis (e.g., bias > 5%).
- **Poor Braak staging**: hard accuracy near chance (≈ 0.1) despite good reconstruction metrics.
- **Edge misalignment**: \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{skip}} \) remains high, producing blurry outputs.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Reconstruction benchmark**: Evaluate raw PET and SUVR metrics (MAE, MSE, PSNR, SSIM, MS‑SSIM) against VAE, VQ‑VAE, and UNet baselines to ensure fidelity is not catastrophically degraded.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question**: How can SUVR calibration be improved for late-stage Braak V/VI subjects, and what architectural or training modifications can reduce subject-dependent variability in regional error?
   **Basis in paper**: The discussion explicitly states: "Late-stage SUVR calibration and subject-dependent variability remain the primary limitations." Table 6 shows increasing absolute SUVR error for Braak V/VI across models, with DQ2H-MSE-Inf showing a jump to 28.8±18.7% error in that stage.
   **Why unresolved**: The paper identifies the problem but does not propose or test solutions for stage-specific calibration or individualized bias correction. The current framework treats all subjects uniformly, which may not account for heterogeneous tau distributions in advanced AD.
   **What evidence would resolve it**: Ablation studies incorporating subject-level normalization layers, staging-aware loss terms, or meta-learning approaches that adapt synthesis per subject. Evaluation would require reduced stage-stratified bias (Table 6) and narrower Bland-Altman limits (Table 4) specifically for Braak V/VI, without compromising earlier-stage accuracy.

2. **Question**: How does the PID-Shapley attribution from DisQ-HNet compare quantitatively and qualitatively to other post-hoc explainability methods (e.g., integrated gradients, attention rollouts) in terms of clinical plausibility and consistency with known tau pathology patterns?
   **Basis in paper**: The authors introduce PID-Shapley as their attribution method but do not benchmark against alternative XAI techniques. The introduction criticizes black-box synthesis pipelines for attribution ambiguity, yet the evaluation relies solely on their proposed PID framework without cross-validation against other interpretability paradigms.
   **Why unresolved**: Without comparative analysis, it is unclear whether PID-Shapley provides more faithful or clinically meaningful explanations than established methods. The claim that complementary information is dominant (Table 7) could be an artifact of the PID decomposition rather than a true reflection of multimodal synergy.
   **What evidence would resolve it**: A study where multiple XAI methods are applied to the same DisQ-HNet predictions, with attributions evaluated by neuroradiologists for alignment with expected tau spread (e.g., Braak stages). Correlation between different methods' heatmaps and ground-truth tau-PET uptake patterns (where available) would establish reliability.

3. **Question**: To what extent does the DisQ-HNet design generalize to other multimodal medical image synthesis tasks beyond tau-PET, such as amyloid-PET from MRI or synthetic CT from PET?
   **Basis in paper**: The conclusion claims the framework targets "a general class of multimodal synthesis problems," but validation is restricted to a single application (tau-PET from T1/FLAIR). No experiments on other modality pairs or targets are presented, leaving the broader applicability unverified.
   **Why unresolved**: Different synthesis tasks have varying degrees of cross-modal redundancy and complementary signal. For instance, amyloid-PET may have different spatial correlation with MRI contrasts than tau-PET. The PID decomposition and edge-conditioned decoder may not transfer straightforwardly.
   **What evidence would resolve it**: Replication studies on public datasets for other synthesis tasks (e.g., MR synthesis from CT, PET attenuation correction from MRI) with consistent evaluation of reconstruction fidelity and downstream clinical utility. Success would be indicated by maintained or improved performance relative to task-specific baselines and meaningful PID attributions (e.g., complementary signal still dominant).

4. **Question**: What are the optimal hyperparameter configurations (e.g., codebook size K, PID loss weights α,β,γ,δ,ϵ, variance floor λ_vf) for balancing disentanglement quality with reconstruction fidelity across diverse datasets and disease severities?
   **Basis in paper**: The methods section sets fixed values (e.g., K=4096, loss weights implied but not tuned systematically). The "Capacity matching" subsection notes parameter parity but does not explore sensitivity to architectural choices like codebook granularity or the relative weighting of PID constraints. The anti-collapse variance floor is introduced but its impact is not ablated.
   **Why unresolved**: The paper does not provide a systematic analysis of how these parameters affect the trade-off between information factorization (Table 7, Figure 6) and pixel-level accuracy (Table 2). For clinical deployment, understanding robustness to such choices is critical.
   **What evidence would resolve it**: A comprehensive ablation study sweeping key hyperparameters, measuring both disentanglement metrics (e.g., NMI between partitions) and downstream task performance (Braak accuracy). Optimal ranges would be identified that maximize complementary contribution without degrading reconstruction, potentially revealing task-specific tuning protocols.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter values (loss weights, variance floor, etc.) are not fully reported, hindering full reproducibility.
- Braak staging evaluation details (ROI definitions, SUVR thresholds) are ambiguous, making it hard to assess clinical relevance and replicate results.
- Validation is performed on a single cohort (n=83) without external testing or statistical significance, limiting generalizability.
