# Dynamic Mix Precision Routing for Efficient Multi-step LLM Interaction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02711
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02711
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** Achieves 88.8% success rate on ALFWorld with only 26.7% high‑precision usage (GHC = 19.85) for Qwen3‑8B, significantly improving accuracy‑cost trade‑offs.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the costly inference of large language models in multi‑step agentic tasks, where fully quantized models often fail on critical decisions. It introduces a dynamic mix‑precision routing framework that selectively invokes high‑precision or quantized LLMs at each interaction step. A lightweight router is trained in two stages: first via KL‑divergence‑based supervised learning to identify precision‑sensitive steps, then refined with Group‑Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to balance task success and high‑precision usage. Evaluated on the ALFWorld benchmark, the method attains superior Pareto frontiers across multiple model families, demonstrating that selective high‑precision usage can drastically cut costs with minimal accuracy loss.

## Method Summary
The framework centers on a router that, given the agent’s interaction history, decides per step whether to use a high‑precision (e.g., bf16) or quantized (e.g., 3‑bit) model. Training proceeds in two stages. Stage 1 (KL‑ST): successful rollouts from the high‑precision model are collected; for each step, the KL divergence between low‑ and high‑precision action distributions is computed. Steps with KL above a threshold (e.g., 85th percentile) are labeled as precision‑sensitive. A two‑layer Transformer router is trained via weighted cross‑entropy to predict these binary labels from step embeddings (produced by a frozen external encoder). Stage 2 (GRPO): the router policy is refined in a meta‑RL setup. For each task instance, groups of trajectories are sampled. The reward combines task success and a penalty for high‑precision calls. Group‑relative advantages are computed without a learned value function, and policy gradient updates are applied with a KL penalty to stay near the Stage 1 policy. At inference, the router processes the sequence of step embeddings and outputs a precision choice for the next step.

## Key Results
- For Qwen3‑8B, the router reaches 88.8% success rate using high‑precision on only 26.7% of steps, achieving a Gain per High‑Precision Call (GHC) of 19.85.
- It consistently outperforms random routing and single‑precision baselines (both full high‑precision and full quantized) across multiple model scales (Qwen3‑4B/1.7B, DeepSeek‑R1‑Distill‑Llama3‑8B).
- The method establishes a superior accuracy‑cost trade‑off frontier on the ALFWorld benchmark.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Step‑wise precision sensitivity heterogeneity
The router reduces inference cost by selectively applying high‑precision only at a small subset of steps where quantization would cause failure. It identifies these steps by computing the KL divergence between low‑ and high‑precision action distributions on successful high‑precision rollouts; steps with high KL are labeled sensitive. The router learns to predict this binary label from trajectory history. The core assumption is that KL divergence serves as a reliable proxy for steps where low‑precision execution leads to incorrect actions and eventual task failure. Evidence includes the observed heavy‑tailed, bimodal‑like KL distribution across steps (most near zero, a small distant tail), justifying a threshold around the 85th percentile. If the KL distribution loses its heavy‑tailed shape (e.g., quantization uniformly degrades all steps), the binary labeling becomes noisy and routing misallocates high‑precision calls.

### Mechanism 2: Two‑stage training with KL‑ST initialization and GRPO refinement
Combining supervised KL‑based initialization with RL‑based GRPO refinement yields better accuracy‑cost trade‑offs than either alone. KL‑ST provides a stable starting policy that already identifies many precision‑sensitive steps. GRPO then optimizes the router with respect to task‑level reward and cost: it samples groups of trajectories, computes group‑relative advantages (reward minus high‑precision usage penalty) without a learned value function, and performs policy gradient updates with a KL constraint to avoid deviating too far from the KL‑ST prior. This is particularly suitable for sparse‑reward, long‑horizon tasks. The assumption is that the KL‑ST prior is accurate enough to keep GRPO updates in a high‑performing region, and group‑relative advantage yields low‑variance gradients. Evidence includes ablations showing GRPO improves upon KL‑ST alone, reducing high‑precision usage for a given success rate. If GRPO causes too large a deviation (low KL penalty β) or if group size K is too small (high variance), the router may collapse to a suboptimal policy (e.g., always low‑precision).

### Mechanism 3: Lightweight step‑level router with Transformer encoder
A compact two‑layer Transformer encoder, fed with step embeddings and positional information, suffices for accurate routing without a large model. The router takes a sequence of pre‑computed embeddings (each summarizing an action‑observation pair), adds learnable positional embeddings, processes them, and uses the last valid hidden state to predict precision. With only a few hundred thousand parameters, its overhead is negligible. The core assumption is that the step embeddings contain enough information about the task state to predict precision sensitivity. Evidence includes the paper’s claim that effective routing does not require a full‑capacity LLM, and the success of the lightweight design. If the embeddings are too coarse or lack necessary details, the router’s predictions become inaccurate regardless of architecture.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map (inference flow):**  
  Observation -> Step Embedding Encoder -> Sequence of Step Embeddings -> Router -> Precision Decision -> LLM Agent (High‑Precision / Quantized) -> Action -> Environment -> Next Observation (loop).  
  Training Pipeline: Trajectory Buffer -> KL Computation -> KL‑ST Labels -> Router (KL‑ST Training) -> Initialized Router -> GRPO Updates -> Final Router.

- **Critical path (inference):**  
  1. Agent receives current observation *o_t* and retrieves history of previous (action, observation) pairs.  
  2. Each history step and current observation are encoded by the frozen encoder into embeddings *z_i*; concatenated into *X_t*.  
  3. Router processes *X_t* (with positional embeddings and masking) and produces routing decision *r_t* (high vs. low).  
  4. Selected LLM (high‑ or low‑precision) conditions on *o_t* and generates action *a_t*.  
  5. Execute *a_t* in the environment, receive *o_{t+1}*, append to history, repeat until termination.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Router capacity vs. overhead:** Deeper Transformer may capture longer dependencies but increases latency; two‑layer design balances performance and cost.  
  - **KL threshold τ:** Higher thresholds label fewer steps as sensitive, reducing high‑precision usage but risking missed critical steps; threshold selected from empirical CDF (e.g., 85th percentile) on validation.  
  - **GRPO parameters:** Group size *K* trades advantage variance for compute cost; KL penalty *β* controls deviation from KL‑ST prior—too small causes collapse, too large prevents adaptation.  
  - **Cost weighting λ:** Determines penalty for high‑precision calls; calibrates operating point on accuracy‑cost curve.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Over‑routing:** High‑precision usage ~100% with success rate matching full‑precision baseline; router never uses quantized model.  
  - **Under‑routing:** High‑precision usage ~0% with success rate near full‑quantized baseline; router never activates high precision even when needed.  
  - **GRPO instability:** KL divergence between current router policy and KL‑ST reference exceeds allowed range, or success rate fluctuates wildly across iterations.  
  - **Poor generalization:** On held‑out tasks, router’s success rate drops more than full‑precision model, indicating overfitting.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline comparison:** Evaluate router on ALFWorld test tasks; report success rate, high‑precision usage, and GHC. Compare against full high‑precision, full quantized, and random routing at fixed usage levels (20%, 40%, …). Establishes Pareto advantage.  
  2. **Ablation of GRPO:** Train router with KL‑ST only and evaluate. Plot success vs. usage alongside full model; gap demonstrates GRPO’s role in reducing high‑precision usage for a given success rate.  
  3. **Sensitivity to KL‑ST data scale:** Vary number of KL‑ST episodes (100, 200, 300, 400) used for supervision; measure success, usage, and GHC. Shows how much KL‑ST data is needed and whether more data consistently helps.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the proposed dynamic mix‑precision routing generalize to agentic tasks beyond ALFWorld?  
2. Can the manually selected KL‑divergence threshold for binary supervision be replaced by an adaptive or learned threshold?  
3. Does extending the framework to more than two precision levels (e.g., 8‑bit, 4‑bit, 3‑bit) yield better accuracy‑cost trade‑offs?  
4. How does the router behave when even the high‑precision model fails on a task, and can it gracefully degrade or signal impossibility?

## Limitations
- The design and dimensionality of the external step embedding encoder are unreported, which is crucial for router input quality.
- GRPO hyperparameters (group size *K*, KL penalty *β*, cost weighting *λ*) and sampling details are omitted, hindering faithful reproduction.
- Evaluation is restricted to the ALFWorld benchmark; transferability to other long‑horizon agentic environments is unexplored.
- The assumption that KL divergence predicts failure lacks causal validation (e.g., forcing high‑precision at low‑KL steps).
- No discussion of random seed variance; GRPO may be unstable with sparse rewards.

## Confidence
- **Heterogeneous step sensitivity**: Medium (evidence from ALFWorld KL distribution; generalizability unproven).
- **Two‑stage training (KL‑ST + GRPO)**: Medium (ablations show benefit; hyperparameters undisclosed).
- **Lightweight router sufficiency**: Medium (architecture not deeply justified; depends on embedding quality).
