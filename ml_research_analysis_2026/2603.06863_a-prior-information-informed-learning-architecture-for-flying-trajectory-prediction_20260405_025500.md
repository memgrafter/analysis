# A prior information informed learning architecture for flying trajectory prediction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.06863
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06863
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: PIDTC achieves MSE 372.39, RMSE 19.30 pixels, physical bias 17.07 cm, and 85.71% classification accuracy, reducing prediction error by over 40% compared to baseline RNN/LSTM/Transformer models.

## Executive Summary
Predicting landing points of flying objects like tennis balls is challenging due to complex aerodynamics and limited data. This paper introduces the Prior Information-Informed Dual-Transformer-Cascaded (PIDTC) architecture, which fuses environmental priors (court boundaries extracted from a single camera view via classical image processing) with flight coordinates detected by YOLO. A two-stage Transformer pipeline first classifies trajectories as “in” or “out” of bounds and then, conditioned on this label, predicts the precise landing coordinates. On a real-world dataset of 350 trajectories, PIDTC significantly outperforms baselines, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy and demonstrating the value of integrating geometric priors and modular reasoning.

## Method Summary
The method comprises three stages: (1) **Data acquisition** – a single industrial camera records ball flight; YOLOv10 detects per‑frame 2D coordinates. (2) **Prior extraction** – a reference court image is processed with Gaussian filtering, Canny edge detection, and Hough line detection to obtain two corner points that define the playing area. (3) **Dual‑Transformer cascade** – the first Transformer ingests 25 ball coordinates plus the two prior points to output a binary “in/out” label. This label is then concatenated with the ball coordinates and fed to the second Transformer, which uses cross‑attention (label as Query, trajectory as Key/Value) to regress the 2D landing point. Losses are binary cross‑entropy (classification) and mean squared error (regression). Final pixel predictions are converted to physical units via homography. The modular design isolates boundary reasoning and enables explicit conditioning.

## Key Results
- **Error metrics**: MSE = 372.39, RMSE = 19.30 pixels, physical bias = 17.07 cm.
- **Classification accuracy**: 85.71%.
- **Ablation**: Integrating prior information reduces prediction error by >40% compared to models without priors; the cascade design yields further gains over using priors alone.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Prior Extraction from Court Boundaries
**Claim**: Extracting court corners as geometric priors provides essential spatial constraints that inform the model about the legal playing area, thereby improving landing point prediction accuracy.  
**Mechanism**: The prior extraction module applies Gaussian filtering (noise reduction), Canny edge detection (edge pixel identification), and Hough line detection (line fitting). Intersections of detected lines yield corner points representing court boundaries in pixel coordinates.  
**Evidence**: The paper’s abstraction mentions integration of environmental priors; Section IV.A details the pipeline. Table IV (ablation) shows that using prior points alone (PMP) already reduces MSE relative to no prior (PMN), confirming the value of extracted priors.  
**Break condition**: Failure of edge detection due to occlusions, lighting changes, or camera motion produces incorrect priors, misleading the model.

### Mechanism 2: Dual‑Transformer Cascade
**Claim**: The two‑stage Transformer pipeline modularizes the problem; the classification stage outputs a binary label that conditions the subsequent regression, narrowing the plausible output space and reducing error.  
**Mechanism**: The first Transformer processes 25 ball coordinates and 2 prior points to produce a label. This label is then concatenated with the ball coordinates and fed to the second Transformer, which uses cross‑attention (label as Query, trajectory as Key/Value) to predict the landing point.  
**Evidence**: Table IV ablation demonstrates that including the classification label (PMC) reduces MSE by 68.53% relative to no prior (PMN) and outperforms using prior points without classification (PMP), highlighting the label’s unique contribution.  
**Break condition**: Misclassification (accuracy < ~80%) propagates errors to the prediction stage; the cascade introduces a dependency between modules.

### Mechanism 3: Cross‑Attention Conditioning
**Claim**: In the prediction decoder, cross‑attention where the label embedding queries the trajectory encoder output enables dynamic weighting of relevant time steps based on boundary context, leading to more precise regression.  
**Mechanism**: After positional encoding, the trajectory sequence is encoded via self‑attention. The classification label is embedded and used as Query in the decoder’s cross‑attention, with the encoder’s output as Key/Value. The resulting context vector passes through a feedforward network to produce the 2D landing point.  
**Evidence**: The improvement from PMC (with label) over PMP (only prior points) suggests that label‑based conditioning via cross‑attention is effective; Section IV.C describes the implementation.  
**Break condition**: If cross‑attention weights fail to capture meaningful temporal patterns (e.g., uniform distribution), the label does not provide useful conditioning, negating the cascade’s benefit.

## Foundational Learning
#### Transformer Encoder-Decoder
- **Why needed**: Both classification and prediction modules rely on Transformers to process sequential ball coordinates, capturing long‑range dependencies via self‑attention.
- **Quick check**: Why is positional encoding added to input embeddings in a Transformer, and how does self‑attention capture dependencies across time steps?

#### Cross‑Attention for Conditioning
- **Why needed**: The prediction module uniquely uses cross‑attention where the Query comes from the classification label, allowing the label to modulate attention over trajectory features.
- **Quick check**: In cross‑attention, if the Query originates from a different source than Key/Value, what capability does this give the model?

#### Edge Detection and Hough Transform
- **Why needed**: The prior information (court corners) is extracted from images using classical computer vision; understanding these steps is essential to follow how geometric priors are obtained.
- **Quick check**: What role does the Hough transform play in extracting straight lines from edge‑detected images?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Data Acquisition → Ball Detection → Prior Extraction → Classification Module → Prediction Module → Post‑processing
- **Critical path**:
  1. For each trajectory segment (25 frames before bounce), extract ball coordinates.
  2. Extract prior corner points from a reference court image (once per court or per trajectory).
  3. Feed ball coordinates and prior points into the classification module to obtain a binary label.
  4. Concatenate ball coordinates with the label and feed into the prediction module to obtain pixel landing point.
  5. Apply homography to convert pixel coordinates to physical bias (cm).
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Monocular camera vs multi‑camera: cheaper but loses depth; compensated by court geometry priors.
  - Cascade vs joint training: cascade enforces explicit boundary decision but risks error propagation; joint training could be more robust.
  - Minimal prior (2 corners) vs full line set: compactness vs completeness; corners suffice for classification but may miss nuanced boundary shape.
  - Transformer vs RNN/LSTM: Transformers handle long sequences via parallel attention but require more data; the paper shows effectiveness even with limited data (350 trajectories).
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Poor prior extraction: Hough lines deviate from true court lines → consistent bias in classification/prediction.
  - Low classification accuracy (<75% on validation): classification module fails → cascade benefits lost.
  - High prediction MSE but high classification accuracy: regression module insufficient or cross‑attention not functioning.
  - Training loss stagnation: possible issues with learning rate, model capacity, or data normalization.
  - Overfitting: large gap between training and validation losses.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Reproduce the data pipeline: using provided or simulated data, implement YOLO detection (or ground truth), prior extraction, and label generation. Visualize extracted corners to verify correctness.
  2. Train classification module in isolation: evaluate accuracy, precision, recall; ensure convergence and achieve ~85% accuracy as reported.
  3. Train full PIDTC: compare against ablations (PMN, PMP) and baselines (RNN, LSTM, Transformer) to replicate performance gains; also explore training set size effects.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Additional environmental priors**: Which other priors (e.g., wind, spin, player position) can improve accuracy, and how should they be integrated into PIDTC?
2. **Physics‑informed learning**: How to incorporate physical constraints (aerodynamics, collision physics) to ensure plausible outputs and better generalization?
3. **Cross‑domain generalization**: To what extent does PIDTC generalize to other flying objects (e.g., baseballs, soccer balls) and environments (e.g., irregular fields, indoor)?
4. **Robustness of prior extraction**: How does the extraction module perform under varying lighting, occlusions, and camera perspectives, and what are its typical failure modes?

## Limitations
- The original dataset of 350 trajectories is not publicly available, forcing reliance on simulation or new data collection that may not match real‑world distribution.
- Prior extraction assumes clear court lines; performance may degrade under occlusions, lighting changes, or camera motion.
- The cascade architecture introduces error propagation; classification mistakes directly affect prediction accuracy.
- Training on a small dataset (350) risks overfitting despite the use of Transformers.
