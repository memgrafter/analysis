# KALAVAI: Predicting When Independent Specialist Fusion Works -- A Quantitative Model for Post-Hoc Cooperative LLM Training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.22755
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22755
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Fusion gain is predictable via a linear model (gain = 0.82 × specialist divergence − 2.72, R²=0.856) and yields consistent improvements over the best specialist: +7.72% (410M), +7.49% (1B), +6.53% (6.9B); cross-lingual fusion achieves +21.76% with Yoruba perplexity dropping from 41.9 to 7.7; the learned router matches oracle routing (<10⁻⁵ nats gap).

## Executive Summary
KALAVAI addresses the challenge of democratizing large language model training by enabling privacy-preserving fusion of independently trained domain specialists. Contributors fine‑tune copies of a shared checkpoint on their private data; a lightweight learned mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) router is then trained post‑hoc to combine the specialists at inference. The protocol consistently outperforms any individual specialist across model scales (410M to 6.9B) and diverse domains, including low‑resource languages. A key contribution is a simple linear predictor that estimates the fusion gain from the specialists’ divergence (relative improvement over the base model), allowing practitioners to forecast cooperative value before committing resources.

## Method Summary
KALAVAI proceeds in four phases. First, a coordinator distributes an identical pre‑trained checkpoint (e.g., Pythia at step 10k) to all contributors. Second, each contributor independently fine‑tunes a copy on their domain‑specific data; optionally they freeze the first few transformer layers to curb over‑specialisation. Third, after all specialists are submitted, a trusted party trains a lightweight router (e.g., a linear layer) for a few hundred steps on a small mixed‑domain dataset. Finally, at inference, all specialists run in parallel and their logits are combined using softmax weights from the router. The router learns to assign each token to the most appropriate specialist, effectively recovering full‑domain coverage while avoiding each specialist’s out‑of‑domain weaknesses.

## Key Results
- Fusion gain is accurately predicted by a linear model: gain ≈ 0.82 × specialist divergence − 2.72 (R²=0.856).
- Improvements over the best specialist are consistent: +7.72% for 410M, +7.49% for 1B, +6.53% for 6.9B parameters.
- Cross‑lingual fusion (Tamil, Yoruba, Welsh, Code) yields a remarkable +21.76% gain, with Yoruba perplexity dropping from 41.9 to 7.7.
- The learned router saturates oracle performance, achieving a gap of less than 10⁻⁵ nats.
- A federation of 20 contributors produces a +16.71% gain, though two specialists with negative divergence due to data scarcity highlight scalability caveats.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Shared Initialization Preserves Representational Compatibility
All specialists start from identical weights, ensuring their internal representations occupy a coherent latent space defined by the base model’s generalist knowledge. Fine‑tuning moves each specialist along domain‑specific directions while maintaining geometric alignment, allowing a simple linear router to reliably distinguish domains based on token features. The core assumption is that the base checkpoint provides a stable, general representation that can be fine‑tuned along multiple complementary directions without destructive interference. Evidence: mismatched initialisation (e.g., specialists from different training steps) causes routing confusion (e.g., code expert receives 11% weight on fiction) and degrades absolute MoE quality (10.37% → 9.44% improvement). Break condition: if contributors use checkpoints from different training stages or models, the representational geometries become incommensurable, leading to mis‑assignment and collapsed gains.

### Mechanism 2: Catastrophic Forgetting Creates Complementary Specialisation
Independent domain‑specific fine‑tuning induces catastrophic forgetting on out‑of‑domain data. Each specialist reduces loss on its target domain while increasing loss elsewhere, producing a diagonal pattern of performance where each excels only on its own domain. This mutual exclusivity enables a router to suppress out‑of‑domain specialists per token and recover full‑domain coverage. The core assumption is that fine‑tuning overwrites domain‑general features in a specialist‑specific way, and the degree of degradation correlates with domain dissimilarity. Evidence: cross‑domain evaluation matrices show clear diagonals; e.g., the code specialist’s loss on science rises above the base (2.908 vs. 2.892). Break condition: if specialists are trained too briefly, divergence may be insufficient; if trained too long without freezing, they may over‑specialise and lose coherence (gain peaks around 5k–10k steps then may decline).

### Mechanism 3: Lightweight Learned Router Achieves Near‑Optimal Assignment
A router trained on a small mixed‑domain dataset (e.g., 500 gradient steps) learns to map token features to the correct specialist with near‑oracle accuracy. The router (e.g., a linear layer) takes pooled hidden states as input and outputs softmax weights; it discovers a nearly deterministic mapping because shared initialisation ensures domain‑discriminative features are encoded consistently across specialists. The core assumption is that the representations remain commensurable, making the router’s learning problem tractable with minimal data and a simple architecture. Evidence: the learned router matches the domain‑level oracle within <10⁻⁵ nats for 410M and 6.9B models; uniform routing underperforms the best specialist by −1.19%. Break condition: if base‑model representations are too similar across domains (low divergence), the router may fail to learn meaningful separation; skipping router training entirely yields no gain.

## Foundational Learning
- **Catastrophic forgetting in fine‑-tuning**: Why needed: The core mechanism relies on specialists degrading on non‑target domains, creating complementary strengths that routing can exploit. Without this, uniform averaging could work. Quick check: What pattern would you expect in a cross‑domain loss matrix if catastrophic forgetting did not occur? (Answer: a flat or near‑constant loss across domains, not a diagonal.)
- **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) gating and logit combination**: Why needed: The fusion protocol uses an MoE router to combine specialist logits at inference; understanding gating (softmax over a linear layer) and logit‑space mixing is key to implementation. Quick check: Why does the paper use logit‑space combination instead of probability‑space mixture? (Answer: Logit combination is standard in MoE because it preserves the relative scaling of the models and avoids probability‑space biases; it is equivalent to multiplying probabilities but more numerically stable.)
- **Representational divergence as a predictor**: Why needed: The divergence–gain relationship (gain ≈ 0.82×divergence − 2.72) allows practitioners to estimate cooperative value before training; divergence is defined as per‑domain loss improvement of a specialist over the base. Quick check: If a specialist achieves 20% lower loss than base on its domain, what is its divergence? What gain does the formula predict? (Answer: divergence = 20%; predicted gain = 0.82×20 − 2.72 = 14.0% − 2.72% = 11.28%.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Base checkpoint → Specialist models (fine‑tuned copies) → Router (lightweight learned module) → Inference engine (parallel specialist execution + logit combination).
- **Critical path**: 1. Coordinator distributes base checkpoint hash to contributors. 2. Each contributor fine‑tunes independently (optionally freezing first K layers) for a fixed number of steps. 3. Contributors submit specialist checkpoints. 4. A trusted party trains the router on a small mixed‑domain dataset (~500 steps). 5. Deploy fused model: all specialists loaded, router processes each token to produce weighted logit sum.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Frozen layers: Not needed below ~10,000 specialist training steps; beyond 10k steps, freezing the first 4 layers yields +2.4pp advantage at 20k steps. Optimal K is not highly sensitive.
  - Router architecture: Linear vs. MLP makes no meaningful difference; training is essential. Uniform averaging fails (−1.19% vs. best specialist).
  - Specialist training budget: Even 50 steps yield +4.0% gain; more steps increase divergence up to a peak (5k–10k) then may degrade if not frozen.
  - Checkpoint matching: Exact same base checkpoint required; even small training‑stage mismatches degrade routing clarity.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Routing collapse: One specialist dominates all domains (e.g., Yoruba routed to Tamil 99.84%). Check gate distributions per domain.
  - Negative specialist divergence: LoRA fine‑tuning often produces specialists worse than base (divergence <0%); fusion gain becomes negative. Use full fine‑tuning.
  - Shared init violation: Mismatched initial checkpoints cause routing confusion (e.g., code expert getting >10% weight on fiction) and reduce absolute MoE quality.
  - Data scarcity: Specialists with very few training chunks (<500) may degrade overall (e.g., dialogue, instructions in 20‑contributor experiment). Ensure minimal data volume.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Baseline replication: Train 3 specialists (code, science, fiction) on Pythia‑410M for 2,000 steps each (freeze=4). Train linear router 500 steps on mixed data. Expect ~+7.7% improvement over best specialist and oracle‑saturating routing (<10⁻⁵ nats gap). Verify router produces >99% one‑hot assignments.
  2. Divergence measurement: Compute each specialist’s per‑domain divergence (loss improvement over base). Check mean divergence against ~3.3% floor; predict gain using the linear formula. Validate that gain scales linearly with divergence across at least 3 points (e.g., vary training steps).
  3. Router necessity ablation: Compare uniform routing (no training) vs. trained linear router. Uniform should underperform best specialist (~−1.2%); trained should recover full gain. Also test hard routing (argmax) vs. soft; they should be equal, confirming weighting precision is irrelevant once domain assignment learned.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does incorporating base‑model competence alongside specialist divergence improve the predictive accuracy of fusion gains across diverse domains and model architectures? The current linear model (gain = 0.82×divergence − 2.72) is fitted on six conditions; base‑model perplexity is a secondary predictor that needs more validation.
2. What are the scalability limits of KALAVAI in terms of number of contributors, model size, and domain diversity, and does the protocol maintain benefits at larger scales? The paper tests up to 20 contributors and 6.9B parameters but does not claim scalability to frontier sizes; two specialists with negative divergence in the 20‑contributor experiment hint at potential issues.
3. Can the inference cost overhead of KALAVAI be reduced through sparse expert activation without sacrificing fusion quality? The protocol currently runs all specialists in parallel (N× cost); attempts at sparse top‑1 inference led to quality collapse despite correct routing agreement, leaving efficient sparse inference as future work.
4. How robust is KALAVAI to non‑IID data distributions and quality variations across real‑world contributors, and what failure modes emerge under such conditions? The paper tests heterogeneity in hyperparameters but assumes domain‑specific data of sufficient quality; data heterogeneity across contributors is an open engineering gap.

## Limitations
- **Implementation gaps**: Router training hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, scheduler) and exact composition of the mixed‑domain dataset are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- **Checkpoint dependency**: The requirement for *exact* base checkpoint matching is a practical barrier; the tolerance for minor weight differences is unknown.
- **Generalization bounds**: The divergence‑gain linear model (R²=0.856) is fitted on a specific set of domains and model scales (410M–6.9B); its predictive power for highly dissimilar domains or much larger models (e.g., 70B+) is not validated.

## Confidence
- **Predictable fusion gain (linear divergence model)**: High – Empirically validated across three model scales with strong R²; formula directly computed from reported metrics.
- **Shared initialization necessity**: Medium – Supported by clear ablation (mismatched init degrades routing), but the precise geometric conditions for “commensurable representations” remain theoretical.
- **Router saturation (<10⁻⁵ nats oracle gap)**: High – Directly measured; gap is vanishingly small, confirming learned routing is near‑optimal.
- **Catastrophic forgetting as driver**: Medium – Cross‑domain loss matrices show diagonal dominance, but the causal link between forgetting extent and router accuracy is not explicitly decomposed.

## Next Checks
1. **Checkpoint mismatch stress test**: Intentionally initialize specialists from different base snapshots (e.g., steps 5k, 10k, 20k). Measure (a) off‑diagonal routing weights per domain and (b) fusion gain relative to matched‑init baseline. Expected: rapid degradation in both metrics, confirming the shared‑init boundary condition.
2. **Router data ablation**: Systematically vary the size and domain balance of the router training set (e.g., 500, 1000, 2000, 4000 sequences; balanced vs. skewed). Track (a) final oracle gap and (b) fusion gain. Expected: performance plateaus after ~2000 sequences; severe imbalance (>80% one domain) increases off‑diagonal routing.
3. **Divergence predictor out‑of‑distribution test**: Train specialists on two held‑out domains (e.g., biomedical text, financial news) not used in the original divergence‑gain fitting. Compute their divergence and predicted gain from the published formula; then measure actual fusion gain. Expected: prediction error increases if domains are linguistically/topically distant from the original set, revealing model limits.
