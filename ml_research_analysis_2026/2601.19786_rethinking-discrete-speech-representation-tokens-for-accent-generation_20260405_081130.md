# Rethinking Discrete Speech Representation Tokens for Accent Generation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.19786
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19786
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Mid-early HuBERT layers encode accent information; ASR fine-tuning degrades accent encoding; proposed content and content-accent tokens outperform prior designs for accent-adaptive and accent-preserving voice conversion.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates the role of accent information in Discrete Speech Representation Tokens (DSRTs) for speech generation, a crucial aspect for controllable accent synthesis. It introduces a unified evaluation framework comprising an Accent ABX task to measure accent accessibility and cross-accent voice conversion resynthesis to assess recoverability.

Through systematic analysis of HuBERT, HuBERT fine-tuned for ASR, and Whisper across layers and codebook sizes, the study reveals that accent information is most salient in mid-early HuBERT layers, that ASR supervision significantly erodes accent encoding, and that naive codebook reduction fails to disentangle accent from phonetic and speaker attributes. Based on these insights, new DSRT variants are proposed: content tokens (from HuBERT-ft layer 18, codebook 256) for accent-adaptive VC, and content-accent tokens (from HuBERT layer 9, codebook 8192) for accent-preserving VC. These achieve superior performance over existing approaches like Vevo's, with higher objective accent similarity (e.g., A-SIM source 0.9541) and lower phonetic distance (PPG 0.1265), and are validated through subjective evaluations.

## Method Summary
The study employs a two-pronged evaluation framework. First, an Accent ABX task measures the discriminability of accent in discrete tokens: given a triplet of speech samples (source, target with same/different accent, and an unrelated third), the task evaluates whether tokens preserve accent information. Second, a cross-accent voice conversion (VC) pipeline resynthesizes speech using different token configurations; objective metrics (A-SIM for accent similarity, PPG for phonetic distance) quantify accent and content preservation. The analysis spans multiple DSRT sources—HuBERT, HuBERT fine-tuned for ASR (HuBERT-ft), and Whisper—across all layers and varying codebook sizes (e.g., 256, 1024, 8192). This allows probing the distribution of accent information within models and the impact of codebook compression.

## Key Results
- Accent information peaks in mid-early HuBERT layers; ASR fine-tuning substantially degrades accent encoding; naive codebook reduction fails to disentangle accent from phonetic and speaker information.
- New DSRTs (content tokens: HuBERT-ft L18, codebook 256; content-accent tokens: HuBERT L9, codebook 8192) achieve state-of-the-art accent and speaker similarity in voice conversion, outperforming prior designs (e.g., Vevo) with objective gains (A-SIM src up to 0.9541, PPG down to 0.1265).
- Subjective evaluations confirm improvements in perceived accent and speaker similarity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism leverages the natural distribution of linguistic information in self-supervised speech models. Mid-early layers of HuBERT capture acoustic and phonetic details, including accent-related features, without yet being dominated by lexical content. By selecting these layers and maintaining a sufficiently large codebook (8192), the content-accent tokens retain accent information while discarding speaker cues through later fine-tuning and clustering. Conversely, for accent-adaptive conversion, a deep HuBERT-ft layer (L18) with a small codebook (256) abstracts away accent, serving purely as content tokens. The ASR fine-tuning process filters out accent, underlining that task-oriented objectives can erase useful variability. Thus, controlling layer depth and codebook size allows deliberate trade-offs between accent retention and speech content representation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Discrete Speech Representation Tokens (DSRTs)**: Vector-quantized representations derived from pretrained speech encoders (e.g., HuBERT). *Why needed*: Enable speech manipulation with text-like token sequences. *Quick check*: Can you convert a continuous speech embedding to its nearest codebook index using cosine distance?
- **Accent ABX**: An evaluation task inspired by ABX discrimination, adapted for accent. *Why needed*: Quantify accent information in representations without human listening. *Quick check*: For a set of speech triples, does the token distance between same-accent pairs fall below that of different-accent pairs?
- **Cross-accent Voice Conversion**: Resynthesizing speech while preserving content but changing accent. *Why needed*: Test recoverability of accent from discrete tokens in a downstream task. *Quick check*: Convert a source utterance to a target accent using learned tokens and measure accent similarity.
- **Codebook size reduction**: Simplifying the discrete vocabulary by clustering codebook entries. *Why needed*: Study information loss and compression trade-offs. *Quick check*: Apply k-means to original codebook vectors and compare ABX scores before and after reduction.
- **ASR fine-tuning impact**: Supervised training on automatic speech recognition data. *Why needed*: Observe how task-specific supervision erases non-task-relevant attributes like accent. *Quick check*: Compare accent ABX scores between pretrained and fine-tuned model checkpoints.
- **Layer-wise probing**: Analyzing representations at each network layer. *Why needed*: Identify where linguistic attributes (accent, phonetics) are most salient. *Quick check*: Extract hidden states per layer and compute accent ABX for each.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Input Audio -> HuBERT/Whisper Encoder -> Layer Selection -> Codebook Lookup (quantization) -> Discrete Tokens -> (Optional) Voice Conversion Decoder -> Output Speech

Critical path:
1. Choose pretrained model (HuBERT, HuBERT-ft, Whisper) and target layer.
2. For each input audio frame, extract continuous feature vector from the chosen layer.
3. Quantize the vector to the nearest codebook entry (using L2 distance) to produce a token index.
4. For voice conversion, feed the token sequence into a decoder (e.g., Hifi-GAN) trained to reconstruct speech from tokens; optionally condition on a target accent embedding.
5. Evaluate using Accent ABX (token distances) and resynthesis metrics (A-SIM, PPG).

Design tradeoffs:
- Earlier layers retain more accent but may include speaker variability; deeper layers become more abstract and lose accent.
- Larger codebooks preserve finer-grained distinctions (including accent) but increase memory and computational cost; smaller codebooks may merge accent with other factors.
- Using HuBERT-ft (ASR-fine-tuned) gives cleaner content but strongly attenuates accent.
- The choice of codebook (original vs. reduced) balances information retention vs. disentanglement.

Failure signatures:
- Low Accent ABX score (near chance) indicates accent information is lost at the token level.
- High PPG (phonetic distance) after voice conversion indicates content degradation.
- Small codebook size combined with high accent ABX suggests accent features are being compressed together with content or speaker traits, leading to poor accent transfer.
- Significant drop in accent ABX when moving from mid to deep layers signals that accent information is not represented in deeper representations.

First three experiments:
1. Layer-wise Accent ABX: Extract tokens from each layer of HuBERT (and variants) with a fixed codebook size (e.g., 8192). Compute ABX scores to locate the accent peak.
2. Codebook reduction impact: For the peak accent layer, vary codebook sizes (e.g., 8192 → 1024 → 256) via k-means clustering. Measure ABX and PPG trade-offs.
3. Voice conversion resynthesis: Using the identified optimal token configurations (content tokens and content-accent tokens), perform cross-accent VC on a held-out speaker set. Evaluate with A-SIM and PPG, and compare against a baseline (e.g., Vevo tokens).

## Limitations
- Generalizability across languages is limited; analysis only covers English accents (L1/L2), not necessarily applicable to other language families or tonal accents.
- Ecological validity of the Accent ABX task is constrained by controlled minimal triplets, which may not reflect real-world accent perception in free-form synthesis.
- The voice conversion experiments use a small, possibly non-representative speaker set; performance may degrade with larger speaker variability or unseen accents.
- Architecture dependence: conclusions drawn from HuBERT and Whisper may not hold for other self-supervised models with different pretraining objectives.
- Subjective evaluation sample size is modest (20-30 raters per test), yielding moderate confidence in perceived accent similarity.

## Next Checks
1. Cross-linguistic accent ABX: Replicate the Accent ABX analysis on at least two non-English language families (e.g., Romance, Slavic, tonal languages) to test generalizability of the mid-early layer peak and codebook dynamics.
2. Robustness test of proposed tokens: Perform accent-preserving VC with the new tokens on a held-out set of speakers with extreme accent/speaker variability (e.g., low-resource L1 accents, non-native speakers with diverse L1 backgrounds).
3. Architecture comparison: Apply the same layer/codebook analysis pipeline to WavLM-base and data2vec-Audio to verify whether the "ASR supervision degrades accent" pattern holds for models with different pretraining objectives (contrastive vs. masked prediction).
