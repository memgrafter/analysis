# Beyond Mimicry: Toward Lifeless Adaptability in Imitation Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19930
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19930
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** Introduces Compositional Repertoire Learning (CRL) and the generalisation boundary metric to measure compositional understanding, shifting imitation learning from trajectory memorisation toward lifelong adaptability.

## Executive Summary
Current imitation learning agents often succeed on training data but fail in novel contexts because they rely on trajectory memorisation rather than learning reusable behavioural structures. This paper argues for a paradigm shift toward lifelong adaptability, where agents generalise by composing learned primitives. To operationalise this, the authors propose Compositional Repertoire Learning (CRL) as a research agenda. They formalise Goal‑conditioned Contextual MDPs (GCMDPs) to separate controllable context from goals and ensure deterministic transitions. A new metric, the generalisation boundary δπθ(p), quantifies the maximum contextual distance at which an agent maintains a specified success rate. The framework evaluates three compositional dimensions—systematicity, productivity, and substitutivity—and calls for benchmarks with fixed primitives, isolated dimensions, and interpretable failures to drive progress.

## Method Summary
The method begins by defining a benchmark as a GCMDP. The environment state is factored into an observation ϕ(s,c) that depends on a context parameter *c* and a distinct goal *g*. Transitions are deterministic to isolate the agent’s policy. Demonstrations *D*ₜᵣₐᵢₙ are collected from an expert operating across a set of training contexts *C*ₜᵣₐᵢₙ, covering necessary primitives and simple compositions. An agent (e.g., trained via behavioural cloning) learns a goal‑conditioned policy πθ(a|o,g). To evaluate compositional generalisation, a suite of test contexts *C*ₜₑₛₜ is generated at varying contextual distances *d(c,c′)* from training contexts. For each distance *d*𝓬, the agent’s success rate SRπθ(*d*𝓬) is measured. The generalisation boundary δπθ(p) is the supremum of distances where SRπθ(*d*𝓬) ≥ *p*. This approach allows systematic probing of systematicity (recombining known parts), productivity (handling longer sequences), and substitutivity (replacing components) while keeping primitive semantics fixed.

## Key Results
- CRL reframes imitation learning success as compositional generalisation rather than trajectory reproduction.
- GCMDPs provide a clean formalism to separate context and goals, enabling deterministic evaluation of policy composition.
- The generalisation boundary metric δπθ(p) distinguishes memorisation from genuine understanding and can profile performance across compositional dimensions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Separating controllable context from goals in a formal MDP extension enables the isolation and measurement of compositional generalisation.  
**Mechanism:** GCMDPs decompose the environment state into an observation filtered by a context parameter and a distinct goal specification. Deterministic transitions ensure failures are attributable to the agent’s policy rather than environmental stochasticity.  
**Core assumption:** The compositional structure of a task is embedded in the systematic variation of context parameters, and this structure can be exposed by holding primitive semantics fixed while varying combinatorial rules.  
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract and Section 3.1 formalise GCMDPs with ϕ(s,c) and deterministic *T*.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** The generalisation boundary metric δπθ(p) distinguishes trajectory‑level memorisation from genuine compositional understanding by measuring performance decay as a function of contextual distance.  
**Mechanism:** For a fixed success threshold *p*, the metric finds the maximum contextual distance *d*𝓬 from the training distribution where the agent maintains at least *p* success rate. Different decay profiles reveal different failure modes.  
**Core assumption:** Contextual distance *d(c,c′)* is a meaningful proxy for compositional challenge (e.g., Levenshtein distance on symbolic context vectors reflects structural change).  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.3 define δπθ(p) and discuss profiling.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Benchmarks with fixed primitive semantics and isolated compositional dimensions are necessary to diagnose which compositional capabilities an agent has actually learned.  
**Mechanism:** By engineering test contexts that vary only one compositional factor at a time (e.g., sequence length for productivity, component substitution for substitutivity), failure analysis can pinpoint whether breakdowns occur in primitive execution, rule recombination, or contextual adaptation.  
**Core assumption:** Failures in current IL are often due to a lack of compositional understanding rather than perceptual or motor deficiencies, and these can be separated via controlled contextual manipulation.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.4 list four benchmark requirements.

## Foundational Learning
- **Compositional Generalisation**  
  *Why needed:* CRL’s core thesis is that adaptability comes from recombining known primitives. Understanding systematicity, productivity, and substitutivity is required to design targeted benchmarks and interpret the generalisation boundary’s shape per dimension.  
  *Quick check:* If an agent succeeds at stacking 4 blocks and stacking blue‑on‑red after red‑on‑blue training, but fails when given a new “orange” block of identical physics, what does this reveal about its learned repertoire?

- **Goal‑conditioned Policies (π(a|s,g))**  
  *Why needed:* GCMDPs use explicit goals to define trajectory success, shifting evaluation from action‑wise imitation to outcome achievement, necessary to assess whether a recombined plan actually solves a novel goal.  
  *Quick check:* In a navigation task, if an agent reaches the intended goal location via a novel path (same goal, different context), is this evidence of compositional adaptation or just local memorisation?

- **Contextual MDPs (C‑MDPs)**  
  *Why needed:* GCMDPs extend C‑MDPs. The notion of a controllable context parameter *c* is fundamental to systematically generating the test distribution and defining distance *d(c,c′)*.  
  *Quick check:* In a C‑MDP, if the reward function also depends on context, how might that conflate goal achievement with context adaptation in evaluation?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Demonstration Encoder -> Primitive Repertoire Learner -> GCMDP Policy -> Contextual Distance Calculator -> Evaluation Harness

### Critical Path
Data Collection (Dₜᵣₐᵢₙ with contexts) → Primitive Extraction → Policy Training on GCMDP → Systematic Testing across Cₜₑₛₜ → Generalisation Boundary Profiling.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Deterministic vs. Stochastic Transitions:** Determinism ensures clean attribution but loses realism; may not transfer to stochastic deployment.
- **Goal Specification Type:** Terminal, landmark, or ordered landmark goals. Simpler goals test simpler composition; complex ordered goals stress planning but may require more sophisticated policies.
- **Context Vector Design:** Symbolic (Levenshtein‑friendly) vs. continuous. Symbolic enables clean distance but may limit domain applicability; continuous needs a meaningful metric.

### Failure Signatures
- **Sharp boundary drop:** δπθ(p) plummets at a small *d*𝓬. Signature of surface‑level mimicry/overfitting.
- **Asymmetric dimensions:** High boundary for substitutivity but low for systematicity. Signature: learned primitives but not combination rules.
- **Contextual leakage:** Performance degrades if ϕ is not observation‑independent (i.e., agent confuses states from different *c*). Signature: failure to respect context boundary.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Systematicity Test:** Train on stack(A,B) and stack(C,D) with distinct primitives. Test on stack(B,A) and stack(D,C). Measure success vs. distance (order change). A compositional agent maintains high boundary.
2. **Productivity Test:** Train on stacks of height 2. Test on heights 3,4,5 without new primitives. Boundary should degrade gracefully with length, not catastrophically at height 3.
3. **Substitutivity Test:** Train with red, blue blocks. Test with functionally identical green, yellow blocks. Distance is colour substitution. Boundary should be high if colour is irrelevant; low boundary indicates agent treated colour as semantically meaningful.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can meaningful contextual distance metrics be defined for continuous or perceptual context spaces, beyond symbolic edit‑distance?
- To what extent can the deterministic transition assumption in GCMDPs be relaxed while retaining interpretability of the generalisation boundary?
- What mechanisms ensure primitive semantics remain stable and reusable across widely varying contexts, preventing contextual interference?
- How should benchmarks balance ecological validity (e.g., realistic stochasticity) against the need for isolated, diagnosable compositional dimensions?

## Limitations
- **Context disentanglement:** Separating context *c* from goal *g* may not hold in many real‑world tasks, confounding distance‑success profiling.
- **Distance metric validity:** *d(c,c′)* must correlate with compositional challenge; for continuous/perceptual contexts, defining such a metric remains open.
- **Primitive semantics stability:** Ensuring fixed primitive behaviour across contexts is non‑trivial; subtle physics changes could cause false failure attribution.

## Confidence
- **GCMDP separation of context and goals:** *Medium* confidence. The formalism is clear and useful for controlled evaluation, but its applicability to domains with entangled context-goal dependencies is questionable and requires further validation.
- **Generalisation boundary δπθ(p) as a diagnostic:** *High* confidence. The metric is well‑defined, interpretable, and directly probes compositional generalisation decay; its primary limitation lies in the choice of distance metric.
- **Three‑dimensional compositional evaluation:** *Medium* confidence. The framework for systematicity, productivity, and substitutivity is sound conceptually, but constructing benchmarks that isolate these dimensions without confounding factors is an open engineering challenge.

## Next Checks
1. Construct a symbolic block‑stacking benchmark with a precise context vector (e.g., `[num_blocks, block_colors, order]`) and edit‑distance metric; verify that *d* correlates with human‑judged compositional complexity.
2. Run ablations on primitive extraction: train agents on varied contexts; measure asymmetry in generalisation boundaries across dimensions to confirm that failures map to specific compositional deficits (e.g., low systematicity but high productivity).
3. Stress the deterministic assumption: introduce stochastic transitions and compare boundaries to the deterministic case to test whether conclusions about compositional understanding hold under noise.
