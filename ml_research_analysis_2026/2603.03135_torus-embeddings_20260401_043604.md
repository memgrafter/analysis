# Torus embeddings

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03135
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03135
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: Toroidal embeddings enable efficient integer-based deployment on standard hardware with minimal performance loss, especially under quantization.

## Executive Summary
Deep learning embeddings typically use hyperspherical or unconstrained Euclidean topologies, but the most efficient hardware representation—integer arithmetic with overflow—naturally forms a torus. This mismatch motivated the authors to propose two simple methods for training embeddings with inherent toroidal topology within standard frameworks: Clifford projection (`torusC`) and pairwise L2 normalization (`torusN`). The latter proved stable and achieved performance comparable to hyperspherical embeddings on CIFAR image retrieval and BIRB few-shot audio classification. Moreover, under extreme quantization (1‑bit grid and product quantization), toroidal embeddings retained strong fidelity, often matching or slightly outperforming hyperspherical embeddings at low dimensions. The core contribution is a direct, efficient pathway to integer-based deployment without significant task performance degradation, particularly for compressed representations.

## Method Summary
The authors integrate toroidal topology into deep embeddings via two projection methods. The first, `torusC`, maps each pair of embedding dimensions to a circle using sine and cosine functions (Clifford projection), but this approach often diverges during training. The second, `torusN` (L2p), applies pairwise L2 normalization across dimension pairs, which is simpler and yields robust convergence. Both are trained with supervised contrastive learning (SupCon) using an Adam optimizer, gradient clipping (threshold 100), and optional KoLeo regularization to encourage uniform coverage on the manifold. For inference, `torusC` embeddings are converted to a flat torus via `arctan2` to enable efficient integer arithmetic. Quantization experiments cover both grid‑based (e.g., 8‑bit, 1‑bit) and product quantization (PQ) using the Faiss library. Critical hyperparameters like KoLeo strength (log10 values -3, -1, 0) are varied, but exact learning rates and batch sizes are not fully specified.

## Key Results
- `torusN` achieves stable training and matches hyperspherical embedding performance on CIFAR‑10/100 retrieval (Precision@1) and BIRB few‑shot classification.
- Under extreme quantization (1‑bit grid) and product quantization, toroidal embeddings retain high fidelity, often matching or slightly outperforming hyperspherical embeddings at low embedding dimensions.
- The Clifford projection (`torusC`) exhibits convergence issues, especially in low dimensions, despite gradient clipping.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The toroidal topology mirrors the circular nature of integer overflow: each dimension pair forms a circle, and their product yields a flat torus. Pairwise L2 normalization (`torusN`) enforces that each coordinate pair lies on a circle without explicit trigonometric functions, leading to stable gradients. In contrast, the Clifford projection (`torusC`) uses sine/cosine mappings that can amplify gradients and cause instability. Because toroidal embeddings naturally align with integer arithmetic, distances computed via modular arithmetic remain faithful after aggressive quantization, whereas hyperspherical embeddings suffer discontinuities when mapped to integers. KoLeo regularization further promotes uniform distribution across the torus, preventing collapse and improving robustness to low‑bit quantization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Torus topology**  
  *Why needed*: Matches the circular behavior of integer overflow, enabling efficient integer‑based distance computation.  
  *Quick check*: Verify that adding integers modulo 2^b corresponds to moving on a circle; e.g., 255 + 1 ≡ 0 (mod 256).

- **Clifford projection**  
  *Why needed*: Provides a smooth, differentiable mapping from Euclidean space to a product of circles.  
  *Quick check*: Apply sin and cos to a pair (x,y); the resulting point lies on the unit torus S¹×S¹.

- **Pairwise L2 normalization (L2p)**  
  *Why needed*: Simpler, more stable alternative to Clifford projection; directly constrains each dimension pair to the unit circle.  
  *Quick check*: Normalize a pair of numbers to unit L2 norm; the normalized values should wrap around like angles.

- **KoLeo regularization**  
  *Why needed*: Encourages uniform coverage of the embedding manifold, preventing collapse and enhancing quantization robustness.  
  *Quick check*: Monitor the circular variance of embeddings during training; it should increase or stabilize.

- **Supervised contrastive learning (SupCon)**  
  *Why needed*: Standard framework for learning discriminative embeddings by pulling together same‑class samples and pushing apart different classes.  
  *Quick check*: Ensure the loss decreases on a held‑out set and that nearest‑neighbor retrieval improves.

- **Product quantization (PQ)**  
  *Why needed*: Efficiently compresses high‑dimensional vectors by splitting into sub‑spaces and assigning codebook indices.  
  *Quick check*: Use Faiss to train PQ codebooks and measure reconstruction error; lower error indicates better retention.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Data -> Torus Projection (torusN or torusC) -> Backbone (ResNet) -> Contrastive Loss + KoLeo -> Training Loop -> Checkpoint.  
Inference: Checkpoint -> (if torusC) arctan2 conversion -> Integer quantization -> Retrieval/Classification.

**Critical path**  
Select a projection method → train with SupCon and KoLeo → validate performance → if satisfactory, proceed to quantization testing and final evaluation.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- torusC vs torusN: theoretical elegance vs training stability.  
- KoLeo strength: stronger regularization promotes uniformity but may cause gradient explosion.  
- Quantization bitrate: lower bits increase compression but risk fidelity loss.  
- PQ configuration: number of sub‑spaces and bits per sub‑space balance memory and accuracy.

**Failure signatures**  
- NaN loss or exploding gradients → reduce KoLeo strength or increase gradient clipping.  
- Low validation Precision@1 → check projection implementation or adjust hyperparameters.  
- Catastrophic drop after 1‑bit quantization → ensure flat torus conversion (arctan2) is applied and PQ is trained on the correct representation.

**First experiments**  
1. Baseline: Train hypersphere embedding on CIFAR‑10 with SupCon; record Precision@1.  
2. TorusN replication: Replace L2 norm with pairwise L2 normalization; compare training stability and validation accuracy against baseline.  
3. Quantization test: Apply 1‑bit grid quantization to both models; measure retrieval performance to confirm torus robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What causes training instability in the Clifford projection (torusC) method, especially at low embedding dimensionalities?**  
   Basis: The authors note that torusC "did not always converge" and "the reasons for this are unclear, though potentially influenced by the difference in the intrinsic dimensionality." (Page 9)  
   Why unresolved: The instability was observed empirically but not diagnosed; mitigation relied on gradient clipping, which may mask rather than solve the underlying issue.  
   Evidence needed: Controlled experiments measuring gradient norms, loss landscape curvature, and intrinsic dimensionality effects; theoretical analysis of how the Clifford projection affects backpropagation.

2. **Why does KoLeo regularization exert a stronger influence on toroidal embeddings compared to hyperspherical embeddings?**  
   Basis: "The KoLeo regularisation appears to have a slightly stronger influence on the performance of torus representations than the equivalent hyperspherical representation. The reasons for this are unclear." (Page 10)  
   Why unresolved: The phenomenon was reported without explanation; KoLeo’s gradient depends on nearest‑neighbor distances, which may distribute differently on a torus.  
   Evidence needed: Analytical derivation of KoLeo gradients in toroidal vs. spherical geometries; empirical measurement of neighbor distance statistics; ablation studies with matched intrinsic dimensionalities.

3. **Can the topological match between toroidal embeddings and binary integer representations be exploited to improve binary neural networks (BNNs) via knowledge distillation?**  
   Basis: "If torus and binary representations have matching topologies, this may be fruitful in future e.g. to distil toroidal representations onto BNNs." (Page 15)  
   Why unresolved: This remains a speculative suggestion without any implementation or evaluation.  
   Evidence needed: Implement a distillation pipeline where a toroidal teacher guides a binary student; evaluate accuracy, convergence, and robustness on standard BNN benchmarks (e.g., CIFAR‑10/100 with binary CNNs).

## Limitations
- Incomplete hyperparameter details: Learning rate, batch size, weight decay, and exact KoLeo schedule are omitted.
- Product quantization ambiguity: Notation `PQ(8,16)` lacks clear interpretation (subspaces and bits); unclear whether PQ training uses full‑precision or quantized vectors.
- Clifford projection instability: `torusC` is reported unstable; precise conditions leading to divergence are not fully characterized.
