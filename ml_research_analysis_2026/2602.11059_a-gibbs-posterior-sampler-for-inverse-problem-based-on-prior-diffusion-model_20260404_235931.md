# A Gibbs posterior sampler for inverse problem based on prior diffusion model

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11059  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11059  
- **Reference count:** 26  
- **Primary result:** A parameter-free Gibbs sampler for linear inverse problems using diffusion priors achieves accurate posterior sampling (pixel errors ~0.001–0.075), correct uncertainty quantification, and rapid convergence (~1000 iterations) with ~85% of time spent on a single neural network pass per iteration.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a Gibbs sampling algorithm for Bayesian inference in linear inverse problems (e.g., deconvolution) when the prior is a diffusion model. The method alternately samples each latent variable in the diffusion chain from its exact conditional posterior. For the target image \(x_0\), this combines a learned diffusion denoiser \(\mu_\theta\) with the linear Gaussian likelihood, yielding a Gaussian posterior whose precision is diagonalized by the Fourier transform under convolutional operators. For intermediate latents \(x_t\) (\(t=1,\dots,T-1\)), the conditionals use the forward diffusion transitions only, which are independent of the data \(y\). The approach is parameter-free, computationally efficient (most runtime is the neural network evaluation), and empirically converges quickly. However, the theoretical justification relies on the near equivalence of the learned backward diffusion process and the forward prior.

## Method Summary
The algorithm constructs a Gibbs sampler over the extended state \(x_{0:T}\) (the image \(x_0\) and diffusion latents \(x_1,\dots,x_T\)). It exploits the Markov structure of the diffusion prior and the conditional independence of \(y\) given \(x_0\). Each conditional posterior is Gaussian:
- For \(x_0\): The posterior combines the prior conditional \(N(\mu_{\theta_0}(x_1), v_0^- I)\) from the backward diffusion step and the likelihood \(N(y; Hx_0, v_e I)\). The resulting mean and covariance are derived via Bayesian updating; when \(H\) is convolutional, the precision matrix is diagonal in the Fourier domain, enabling \(O(P \log P)\) sampling via FFTs.
- For \(x_t, t=1,\dots,T-1\): The conditional uses two forward diffusion transitions: \(p^+(x_t|x_{t-1})\) and \(p^+(x_{t+1}|x_t)\). Since these are independent of \(y\), the conditional is simply \(N(\varepsilon_t, \gamma_t^{-1} I)\) with \(\varepsilon_t\) and \(\gamma_t\) computed from the neighboring latents and diffusion coefficients.
- For \(x_T\): Only the forward transition \(p^+(x_T|x_{T-1})\) is used, giving \(N(k_T x_{T-1}, v_T^+ I)\).

By repeatedly sweeping through \(x_0, x_1, \dots, x_T\) in fixed order, the chain is designed to target the true posterior \(p(x_{0:T}|y)\), though the convergence argument assumes the forward and backward diffusion processes are closely aligned.

## Key Results
- **Accurate recovery:** On MNIST deconvolution, pixel errors between posterior samples and ground truth are very low, ranging from 0.001 to 0.075.
- **Proper uncertainty quantification:** True underlying images fall within ±2 posterior standard deviations in repeated experiments, indicating calibrated credible intervals.
- **Rapid convergence:** The Gibbs chain stabilizes after approximately 1000 full sweeps, allowing efficient posterior approximation.
- **Computational efficiency:** Roughly 85% of the runtime per iteration is spent on a single forward pass of the neural network \(\mu_\theta\), with the remaining updates (FFTs and Gaussian draws) being cheap.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The Gibbs sampler targets the true posterior by iteratively sampling each variable from its exact conditional posterior, all of which are Gaussian.
- **Mechanism:** The joint posterior factorizes due to the diffusion prior’s Markov property and conditional independence of \(y\) given \(x_0\). For \(x_0\), the conditional is a product of two Gaussians (denoiser prior and likelihood). For \(x_t\) (\(t=1,\dots,T-1\)), it is a product of two forward diffusion conditionals, independent of \(y\). For \(x_T\), it is a single forward conditional. All are Gaussian.
- **Core assumption:** The learned backward process \(p^{-,\theta}\) is approximately equal to the forward prior \(p^+\) after training, so the mixed conditionals collectively define a joint distribution close to the true posterior.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: "alternately updates each latent variable... For \(x_0\), combines backward diffusion denoiser... with likelihood (Wiener solution); for other latents, uses forward diffusion transitions. All conditionals are Gaussian."  
  - [section III-1]: Shows \(\pi_{0|*}(x_0|y,x_{*0}) \propto N(x_0; \mu_0(x_1), v_0^-I)N(y;Hx_0, v_e I)\).  
  - [section III-2.1]: For \(x_t\) (\(t=1,\dots,T-1\)), \(\pi_{t|*}(x_t|y,x_{*t}) \propto N(x_t;k_t x_{t-1}, v_t^+I)N(x_{t+1};k_{t+1}x_t, v_{t+1}^+I)\).  
  - Corpus: GibbsDDRM and related works similarly exploit Gaussian conditionals for diffusion posteriors.
- **Break condition:** Significant divergence between \(p^+\) and \(p^{-,\theta}\) (due to poor training or distribution shift) will cause the Gibbs chain to converge to a biased joint distribution, yielding incorrect samples.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Sampling \(x_0\) can be performed in \(O(P \log P)\) time via FFTs when \(H\) is a convolutional operator.
- **Mechanism:** The \(x_0\) posterior is Gaussian with precision matrix \(\Gamma_0 = H^TH/v_e + I/v_0^-\). If \(H\) is block-circulant (e.g., convolution with periodic boundaries), \(H^TH\) is diagonalized by the discrete Fourier transform (DFT). Thus, sampling reduces to independent Gaussian draws per Fourier coefficient, with means and variances computed from FFTs of \(H^Ty/\sigma_e^2\) and \(\mu_{\theta_0}(x_1)/v_0^-\).
- **Core assumption:** \(H\) is exactly or approximately circulant, i.e., convolution with periodic boundary conditions.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section III-1]: "Sampling is particularly effective in the Fourier plane: the components are independent and Gaussian, and their mean and variance are easily obtained by simple FFT."  
  - [section IV-D]: Notes that 85% of time is neural network passes, implying FFT steps are computationally negligible.  
  - Corpus: Common assumption in diffusion-based inverse methods for computational tractability.
- **Break condition:** For non-circulant operators (e.g., asymmetric point spread functions, non-periodic boundaries), the diagonalization fails, requiring \(O(P^3)\) direct linear algebra and destroying scalability.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The Gibbs chain converges to the intended posterior because the conditionals are consistent with a joint density that approximates the true posterior, and Gibbs sampling is ergodic for such decompositions.
- **Mechanism:** Standard Gibbs theory guarantees convergence to a joint distribution \(\tilde{\pi}(x_{0:T})\) defined by the set of conditionals used, provided the chain is irreducible and aperiodic. Here, \(\tilde{\pi}\) is a hybrid of the forward prior \(p^+_{0:T}\) (used for \(x_t, t>0\)) and the backward posterior \(p^{-,\theta}_{0:T}\) (used for \(x_0\)). The authors argue that learning aligns \(p^+\) and \(p^{-,\theta}\), so \(\tilde{\pi} \approx \pi(\cdot|y)\). Empirical diagnostics (stabilization after ~1000 iterations, decaying autocorrelation) support practical convergence.
- **Core assumption:** The discrepancy between \(p^+\) and \(p^{-,\theta}\) is small enough that the marginals of \(\tilde{\pi}\) match those of the true posterior \(\pi(\cdot|y)\) (or at least the chain is \(\pi\)-irreducible).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section III, Convergence property]: "strictly speaking, the result does not apply here since some conditionals are deduced from the forward (joint) pdf and others from the backward (joint) pdf. That said, by its nature, the learning stage brings the forward and backward pdfs close together, so it is considered that guarantee is provided."  
  - [abstract]: "Convergence guarantee is suggested but relies on forward/backward prior similarity."  
  - [section IV-C]: Presents empirical convergence diagnostics (e.g., running mean stabilization).  
  - Corpus: Neighbors (e.g., [10,12,13]) provide theoretical guarantees for different diffusion posterior samplers, but this Gibbs mixing approach lacks a formal proof.
- **Break condition:** If \(p^+\) and \(p^{-,\theta}\) are significantly mismatched (e.g., due to model misspecification or insufficient training data), the Gibbs chain may converge to a biased \(\tilde{\pi} \neq \pi\), leading to erroneous posterior inference.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Diffusion models as Markov processes (forward/backward).**  
  *Why needed:* The prior is defined via a forward noising process (adding noise) and a learned backward denoising process. The Gibbs updates for \(x_t\) (\(t>0\)) use forward transitions; \(x_0\) uses the backward denoiser. Understanding both processes explains why conditionals are Gaussian and how they’re combined.  
  *Quick check:* Given a variance-preserving schedule (\(k_t, v_t^+, v_t^-\)), derive the forward conditional \(p^+_t(x_t|x_{t-1})\) and backward conditional \(p^{-,\theta}_t(x_t|x_{t+1})\). What makes them Gaussian?

- **Concept: Gibbs sampling for block updates.**  
  *Why needed:* The algorithm is a block-Gibbs sampler where each block is an entire latent image \(x_t\). Correctness and convergence depend on sampling each block from its conditional under the joint posterior (or an approximation). Knowing the conditions for Gibbs convergence (positive recurrent, aperiodic) is key to assessing the claimed guarantee.  
  *Quick check:* If you have a joint density \(\pi(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) and you update each \(x_i\) from \(\pi(x_i|x_{-i})\) in a fixed scan order, what are the minimal conditions for the chain to converge to \(\pi\)? How does mixing conditionals from two different joint densities (\(p^+\) and \(p^{-,\theta}\)) affect this?

- **Concept: Linear-Gaussian inference (Wiener filter).**  
  *Why needed:* The conditional posterior for \(x_0\) is Gaussian because both the likelihood (Gaussian, linear \(H\)) and the prior conditional (from backward diffusion, Gaussian) are Gaussian. Its mean and covariance are derived via standard Bayesian precision addition. The FFT acceleration relies on the convolutional structure of \(H\).  
  *Quick check:* For \(y = Hx_0 + \epsilon\), \(\epsilon \sim N(0,\sigma_e^2 I)\), and prior \(x_0|x_1 \sim N(\mu_{\theta_0}(x_1), \sigma_0^{-2} I)\), derive the posterior mean and precision matrix. Under what condition on \(H\) is the precision diagonal in the Fourier domain?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Pre‑trained neural network \(\mu_{\theta_t}\) (denoiser) → Latent chain \(x_{0:T}\) → FFT module (for \(x_0\) update when \(H\) is convolutional) → Observed data \(y\) via linear operator \(H\) and noise variance \(v_e\).
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Initialize \(x_0\) (e.g., from \(y\) or Wiener filter) and forward‑diffuse to obtain \(x_{1:T}\).  
  2. For each Gibbs iteration:  
     a. Update \(x_0\): Compute precision \(\Gamma_0 = H^TH/v_e + I/v_0^-\) and mean \(\varepsilon_0 = \Gamma_0^{-1}(H^Ty/v_e + \mu_{\theta_0}(x_1)/v_0^-)\) in Fourier domain; sample \(x_0\).  
     b. For \(t=1\) to \(T-1\): Compute \(\gamma_t = 1/v_t^+ + k_{t+1}^2/v_{t+1}^+\), \(\varepsilon_t = \gamma_t^{-1}(k_t x_{t-1}/v_t^+ + k_{t+1} x_{t+1}/v_{t+1}^+)\); sample \(x_t \sim N(\varepsilon_t, \gamma_t^{-1} I)\).  
     c. Update \(x_T\): Sample \(x_T \sim N(k_T x_{T-1}, v_T^+ I)\).  
  3. Repeat sweeps until convergence (e.g., change in running mean of \(x_0 < 10^{-2}\)).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Mixing forward/backward conditionals vs. pure backward:** Using forward transitions for \(x_t\) (\(t>0\)) avoids needing backward conditionals (not directly available from the trained model), simplifying implementation but introducing potential bias if \(p^+ \not\approx p^{-,\theta}\).  
  - **FFT acceleration vs. generality:** FFTs give \(O(P \log P)\) sampling for \(x_0\) but require \(H\) to be circulant; for general linear operators, direct \(O(P^3)\) solvers are needed, limiting scalability.  
  - **Parameter‑free vs. adaptive inference:** The algorithm requires no tuning (except iteration count) but cannot adapt unknown hyperparameters (e.g., \(v_e\), \(H\)) automatically—extensions for joint estimation are proposed as future work.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Bias in posterior mean:** Systematic deviation of empirical \(x_0\) mean from ground truth; indicates mismatch between \(p^+\) and \(p^{-,\theta}\).  
  - **Poor uncertainty calibration:** True \(x_0\) falling outside ±2 posterior SD intervals; may arise from non‑Gaussian conditionals if diffusion prior is poorly trained.  
  - **Slow mixing or non‑convergence:** High autocorrelation of \(x_0\) samples (lag‑1 >0.9 after many iterations) or running mean not stabilizing; could be due to multimodal posteriors or weak coupling from \(y\) to \(x_t\) (\(t>0\)).  
  - **FFT artifacts:** Ringing or boundary artifacts in \(x_0\) reconstructions; suggests \(H\) is non‑circulant (e.g., asymmetric PSF). Switching to a direct solver or using boundary handling is needed.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Sanity check on linear Gaussian case:** Set \(H = I\), \(v_e\) small. Posterior for \(x_0\) should equal \(N(\mu_{\theta_0}(x_1), v_0^- I)\). Verify Gibbs samples match (mean ≈ \(\mu_{\theta_0}(x_1)\), variance ≈ \(v_0^-\)).  
  2. **Deconvolution with known PSF:** Use MNIST with a \(3\times3\) uniform blur, \(v_e=0.05\). Measure RMSE (posterior mean vs. ground truth) and ±2 SD coverage. Compare to baselines like DPS if available.  
  3. **FFT vs. direct solver comparison:** For a small image (e.g., \(8\times8\)), implement both FFT‑based and direct Cholesky sampling for \(x_0\). Verify statistical indistinguishability (e.g., two‑sample KS test per pixel) for circulant \(H\). Then test with a non‑circulant \(H\) (asymmetric PSF) to show direct solver becomes necessary.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What is the rigorous convergence guarantee for the Gibbs sampler when conditionals are derived from a mixture of forward and backward joint distributions?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The authors acknowledge that the standard Gibbs theory does not strictly apply because some conditionals come from the forward pdf and others from the backward pdf. They rely on the heuristic that learning aligns the two, and note that work is ongoing to obtain a full convergence proof.  
   *Why unresolved:* No formal proof exists; the asymptotic correctness remains heuristic.

2. **How does G-DPS comparatively perform against state-of-the-art diffusion posterior sampling methods (e.g., DPS, Π-GDM, TDS) in terms of accuracy, uncertainty quantification, and computational efficiency?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The conclusion explicitly identifies evaluation against other methods as a future perspective, citing recent works [23,24].  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper provides only qualitative results and simple baselines; no systematic benchmark on standard inverse problems is presented.

3. **Can G-DPS be extended to jointly estimate unknown hyperparameters such as noise variance or point spread function within the Bayesian framework?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The conclusion suggests including estimation of additional parameters like noise level or instrument parameters, noting that this is partly addressed in a submitted work [22].  
   *Why unresolved:* The current algorithm assumes these parameters are known; joint estimation requires designing efficient Gibbs conditionals (e.g., Metropolis‑within‑Gibbs) and may increase computational cost.

## Limitations
- **Forward/backward equivalence assumption:** Mixing forward and backward conditionals assumes the diffusion processes are nearly identical; significant deviation biases the posterior.
- **FFT dependency for \(x_0\):** The FFT acceleration requires \(H\) to be circulant (e.g., periodic boundaries). Non‑circulant operators (non‑symmetric PSF, zero padding) break diagonalization, forcing costly \(O(P^3)\) solvers or causing boundary artifacts.
- **Convergence guarantee:** Only a heuristic argument is provided; no rigorous proof that the Gibbs chain converges to the true posterior. Mixing may be slow, and the claimed ~1000‑iteration stabilization is not universally assured.

## Confidence
- **Posterior correctness & uncertainty quantification** – *Medium*: Conditionals are Gaussian, but correctness hinges on forward/backward match. Empirical results on MNIST are limited in scope.
- **FFT acceleration for circulant \(H\)** – *High*: Mathematically rigorous; the precision matrix is diagonalized by DFT, guaranteeing \(O(P \log P)\) sampling.
- **Parameter‑free efficiency** – *High*: Algorithm requires no tuning; ~85% runtime per iteration is one neural network pass, as expected.
- **Scalability to large images** – *Medium*: FFTs enable scalability for convolutional \(H\), but for general \(H\) the direct solver becomes infeasible. Parallelization potential is high but unevaluated.
- **Robustness to model mismatch** – *Low*: Sensitivity to deviations between \(p^+\) and \(p^{-,\theta}\) is a major theoretical and practical concern; no extensive robustness tests provided.

## Next Checks
1. **Mismatch sensitivity test:** Deliberately mismatch the diffusion schedule (e.g., use a variance‑preserving schedule different from training) or train on a dataset distinct from the test image domain. Run Gibbs on deconvolution with known \(x_0^*\). Measure bias in posterior mean and coverage of ±2 SD intervals; expect degradation if forward/backward equivalence breaks.
2. **Non‑circulant operator test:** For a small image (e.g., \(8\times8\)), compare FFT‑based \(x_0\) sampling against direct Cholesky sampling for a non‑circulant \(H\) (e.g., asymmetric \(3\times3\) PSF). Verify that FFT samples show statistical differences (e.g., Kolmogorov‑Smirnov test per pixel) and that the direct solver remains accurate but scales poorly to larger images.
3. **Extended convergence diagnostics:** Run 10,000 Gibbs iterations on MNIST and CIFAR‑10 deconvolution. Compute effective sample size (ESS) and autocorrelation for \(x_0\). Check whether the running mean stabilizes within 1000 iterations and whether autocorrelation at lag 1000 is near zero. If not, the claimed convergence may be problem‑dependent.
