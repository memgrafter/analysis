# Uncertainty Drives Social Bias Changes in Quantized Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06181  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06181  
- **Reference Count:** 40  
- **Primary Result:** Post-training quantization (PTQ) reduces LLM computational cost but alters social biases via "quantization-induced masked bias flipping"—up to 21% of individual responses flip between biased/unbiased states despite stable aggregate metrics, driven by model uncertainty and asymmetric across demographic groups.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how post-training quantization (PTQ), a common technique for reducing LLM computational costs, inadvertently changes social bias patterns. The authors constructed PostTrainingBiasBench, a unified framework evaluating 50 quantized models across 13 bias datasets via paired response analysis. They discovered that PTQ causes individual responses to flip between biased and unbiased states—a phenomenon they term "quantization-induced masked bias flipping"—even when aggregate bias scores remain unchanged. These flips are strongly predicted by model uncertainty, with high-uncertainty responses 3–11× more likely to change. Stronger 4-bit quantization amplifies effects (4–6× more changes than 8-bit), and impacts are asymmetric: within the same model, bias can worsen by up to 18.6% for one demographic group while improving by 14.1% for another. Model size shows no consistent robustness, and relative model rankings become unpredictable post-quantization.

## Method Summary
The study employs a paired evaluation framework: for each of 10 instruction-tuned base models (e.g., LLaMA 3.1, Qwen 2.5) and their 5 quantized variants (RTN, GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant at 4/8-bit), responses are generated on identical prompts from 13 social bias benchmarks (≈85K questions). Closed-ended tasks use geometric mean token probabilities to select answers and compute Shannon entropy as uncertainty; open-ended tasks use greedy decoding followed by LLaMA Guard 3 8B for safety labeling. Response flips are detected by comparing base vs. quantized outputs (choice or safety label). Aggregate bias scores (e.g., BBQ, StereoSet ICAT) are computed pre/post-quantization, and statistical significance is assessed via permutation tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Preference tuning (SimPO) on a subset of BBQ modulates pre-quantization uncertainty to test causality.

## Key Results
- Quantization induces response-level bias flips in up to 21% of samples while aggregate metrics often remain stable.
- High-uncertainty responses (entropy >0.66) are 3–11× more likely to flip than confident ones.
- 4-bit PTQ causes 4–6× more behavioral changes than 8-bit; advanced methods (AWQ, SmoothQuant) reduce but do not eliminate effects.
- Asymmetric group impacts: for a single quantized model, bias can worsen by 18.6% for one demographic group while improving by 14.1% for another.
- Model size does not confer consistent robustness; relative model rankings become unpredictable after quantization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper identifies that PTQ alters LLM social bias through perturbations in probability distributions that disproportionately affect uncertain predictions, leading to masked bias flipping at the response level.

**Mechanism 1: Uncertainty-driven response vulnerability**  
Quantization noise injects perturbations into weight and activation tensors. For predictions where the base model is uncertain (small probability margin between top choices), this noise tips the balance enough to change the selected response, while confident predictions (large margin) remain stable. High-uncertainty responses (Shannon entropy >0.66) show 3–11× higher flip rates; low-uncertainty flip <2% (Figure 2a, Table S8).

**Mechanism 2: Quantization-strength amplification**  
Lower bit-widths increase quantization error magnitude. Larger noise more frequently pushes probability distributions past decision thresholds, especially in uncertain regions. 4-bit PTQ yields 4–6× more behavioral changes than 8-bit (Figure 4a); RTN W4A16 shows 2-3× larger entropy shifts than W8A16 (Figure S2).

**Mechanism 3: Asymmetric group impacts via heterogeneous uncertainty**  
Different demographic groups exhibit different pre-quantization uncertainty distributions in the benchmark questions. Since uncertainty drives flipping, groups with higher uncertainty experience more flips, causing divergent bias metric changes post-quantization. For Qwen 2.5 14B, flipping rates vary by group (e.g., "short" -10.3% vs. "male" +7%; Figure 5b) and by dataset context (e.g., "male" 2.1% vs. 18%; Figure 5c).

## Architecture Onboarding
For a new engineer joining the *PostTrainingBiasBench* project:

**Component map:**  
Dataset wrappers (13 benchmarks) -> Response extractor (closed-ended: geometric mean probabilities; open-ended: greedy decode + LLaMA Guard) -> Uncertainty calculator (Shannon entropy for closed-ended) -> Model zoo (10 base models × 5 PTQ methods = 50 quantized variants) -> Evaluation pipeline (paired generation, flip detection, aggregate metric computation, permutation testing) -> Utility scripts (social group extraction via GPT-4o, statistical analysis with Cohen’s d/FDR).

**Critical path:**  
1. Setup: Install dependencies (vLLM, transformers, llm-compressor), download models (HuggingFace paths in Table S7).  
2. Generation: Run inference on base and quantized models with identical prompts; closed-ended computes geometric mean probabilities per option, open-ended generates text then LLaMA Guard.  
3. Pairing: Align responses by prompt ID; record choice/entropy (closed-ended) or safety label (open-ended).  
4. Flip detection: Flag if choice/safety label changed; for bias flips, check bias label change.  
5. Aggregation: Compute dataset-level bias scores pre/post-quantization; run permutation tests (1000 swaps) for significance.  
6. Analysis: Plot flipping vs. entropy (Figure 2), behavioral changes by model/quantization (Figure 4), group asymmetry (Figure 5).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Closed-ended metrics allow precise uncertainty measurement but may not reflect real-world open-ended usage; open-ended relies on LLaMA Guard with low PPV (40–55% on some datasets), introducing classification noise.  
- Paired evaluation controls for prompt difficulty but assumes identical prompt parsing; quantization could alter instruction adherence, confounding effects.  
- Social group extraction uses GPT-4o with regex cleanup; may miss nuanced references and intersectional groups due to dataset limitations.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Unexpectedly low flipping rates: Check entropy calculation (range 0–1) and model loading/prompt consistency.  
- High flipping on SocialStigmaQA: Dataset typically shows <1% flipping (models always select "cannot answer" with entropy≈0); >5% suggests parsing error (e.g., mislabeling "cannot answer" as biased).  
- LLaMA Guard inconsistency: Low PPV/NPV variation across datasets; manually annotate samples if flipping rates seem implausible.  
- Quantization mismatch: Verify bit-widths (W4A16 vs. W8A16) and calibration; SmoothQuant models should have "SmoothQuant-RTN" in path.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate uncertainty-flipping correlation on LLaMA 3.1 8B + BBQ: bin by entropy, compute flip rates; expect high-entropy (>0.66) to flip ~20× more than low-entropy (<0.33).  
2. Test quantization strength: Compare RTN W8A16 vs. RTN W4A16 flipping on a closed-ended dataset (e.g., BiasLens-Choices); expect 4-bit to show 4–6× more flipping.  
3. Spot-check group asymmetry: On Qwen 2.5 14B + BBQ, extract social groups for flipped questions; compute flipping rate per group; expect significant variation (e.g., "short" vs. "male" as in Figure 5b) via bootstrap CI.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** What is the causal mechanism by which model uncertainty exacerbates quantization-induced bias flipping? The study shows correlation (3–11× higher flipping for high-uncertainty responses) and causality via preference tuning, but internal model analyses (e.g., attention, activations) are needed to explain representational vulnerability.
**Open Question 2:** How do quantization-induced bias changes generalize to multilingual settings and intersectional demographic groups? Benchmarks are English-only and target single demographic axes; impacts on non-English queries or overlapping identities (e.g., race + gender) remain unknown.
**Open Question 3:** How does stochastic decoding (e.g., sampling with temperature > 0) interact with quantization to affect social bias stability? Analyses used deterministic generation; real-world deployments often use sampling, so persistence or worsening of uncertainty-driven flipping is unclear.
**Open Question 4:** To what extent do current automated bias detectors (like LLaMA Guard) misestimate quantization-induced bias flipping in open-ended generations? LLaMA Guard shows low PPV (40–55% on some datasets), implying reported flipping rates may be upper bounds, but the magnitude of overestimation is unquantified.
**Open Question 5:** What model architectures or training procedures (e.g., alignment methods, data mixtures) confer robustness against quantization-induced bias changes, beyond parameter count? The study finds no consistent scaling advantage and unpredictable ranking changes; factors like architecture or fine-tuning techniques require controlled comparison.

## Limitations
- Calibration data specifics for quantization (e.g., WikiText-2 splits) are not fully specified, potentially causing minor quantitative differences in quantized model parameters.
- Social group extraction prompts and regex cleanup for BiasLens/FMT10K are not detailed; subtle variations could affect subgroup asymmetry results.
- Open-ended evaluation relies on LLaMA Guard 3 8B with low positive predictive value (40–55% on some datasets), so bias flipping rates may be noisy upper bounds.
- Models are instruction-tuned English LLMs; generalization to base models, non-English contexts, or intersectional groups is limited.
- Paired evaluation assumes identical prompt parsing; quantization might alter adherence to complex instructions, confounding observed effects.

## Confidence
- **Uncertainty-driven flipping:** High confidence. Strong empirical anchor (Figure 2a, Table S8) and aligned with probability margin sensitivity theory.
- **Quantization-strength amplification:** High confidence. Consistent 4–6× effect across models/datasets (Figure 4a) supported by prior softmax sensitivity work.
- **Asymmetric group impacts:** Medium confidence. Evident in data (Figure 5b) but relies on potentially noisy group extraction; weaker direct corpus support.
- **Aggregate metrics masking flips:** Medium confidence. Logically sound and demonstrated via case studies, but systematic prevalence across all metrics needs broader validation.
- **Model size non-robustness:** Low confidence. Mixed results in Figure 4b; may be confounded by architecture/training differences.
