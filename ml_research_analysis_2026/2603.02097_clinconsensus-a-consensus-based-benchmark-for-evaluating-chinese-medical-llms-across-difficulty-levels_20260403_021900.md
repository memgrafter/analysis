# ClinConsensus: A Consensus-Based Benchmark for Evaluating Chinese Medical LLMs across Difficulty Levels

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.02097  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02097  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: ClinConsensus reveals a persistent usability gap (12.5–15.5 points) and treatment planning bottleneck across 22 evaluated Chinese medical LLMs.

## Executive Summary
ClinConsensus addresses the inadequacy of static, task-isolated medical benchmarks for evaluating LLMs in real-world clinical workflows. The authors present a Chinese-language benchmark comprising 2,500 expert-curated, open-ended cases that span prevention, treatment, and long-term care across 36 medical specialties and 12 task types. Cases are stratified into three complexity levels (L1–L3) to reflect progressively challenging clinical scenarios. Evaluation employs a rubric-based protocol and the Clinically Applicable Consistency Score (CACS@k), a threshold-calibrated metric that penalizes responses falling below a usability threshold set at k=7 based on expert responses. A dual-judge framework combines a high-capability LLM-as-judge with a distilled 8B model for scalable, physician-aligned scoring. Assessment of 22 leading LLMs uncovers substantial heterogeneity in reasoning, evidence use, and longitudinal follow-up, with performance degrading as complexity increases and treatment stages showing the weakest results. A persistent gap between standard rubric accuracy and CACS@7 indicates that many responses are partially correct but clinically insufficient, highlighting actionable treatment planning as a key unsolved challenge.

## Method Summary
The benchmark construction involved clinical experts generating or curating de-identified cases covering the full continuum of care. Each case includes a multi-turn conversation context and 30 binary rubric criteria aligned with salient clinical considerations. A two-stage quality control pipeline discards cases where top LLMs achieve ≥50% rubric accuracy (likely too easy) and audits 20% of cases for clinical soundness. Complexity levels (L1–L3) are assigned via hierarchical rules based on task count, specialty count, and inclusion of reasoning/planning components. For evaluation, each target LLM produces a single response per case given the conversation history. Responses are scored independently against each rubric item by a dual-judge system: a high-capability LLM-as-judge (e.g., GPT-5.1) using a fixed prompt and schema-constrained JSON output, and a distilled 8B SFT model trained on physician-validated HealthBench data. Per-case rubric-hit scores are aggregated into Rubric Accuracy (mean % of criteria met) and CACS@k, which computes the area under the reverse-ECDF curve with truncation at threshold k=7, emphasizing consistent generation of clinically usable outputs. Validation includes physician meta-evaluation on a stratified 285-case subset to measure judge alignment.

## Key Results
- **Top model performance**: Frontier models like GPT-5.2 (34.7% CACS@7) and Qwen3-Max (33.3%) achieve comparable aggregates but diverge sharply in reasoning, evidence integration, and longitudinal follow-up capabilities.
- **Complexity and task effects**: Performance drops monotonically with complexity (L1→L3 penalty ~8–10 points for top models) and is lowest in treatment stages. Extreme specialty-wise volatility exists (e.g., GPT-5.2: 47.8% in Sports Medicine vs. 22.9% in Organ Transplantation).
- **Usability gap**: Standard Rubric Accuracy consistently overestimates clinical utility by 12.5–15.5 absolute percentage points relative to CACS@7, indicating many responses are partially correct but fail to meet minimum usability thresholds; actionable treatment planning remains a persistent bottleneck.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Rubric-based evaluation with CACS@k ensures clinically usable LLM responses by thresholding on expert-calibrated standards. CACS@k computes a truncated area under the reverse-ECDF curve of rubric-hit scores, assigning zero credit to responses below a clinical threshold k (set to 7 from expert mean scores). This emphasizes consistency in producing outputs that meet minimum usability, based on the assumption that expert-authored responses define a realistic baseline for salient clinical considerations; partial correctness below this threshold is unsafe or impractical in real workflows. Evidence includes the abstract statement and Section 4.3 detailing k calibration from 250 expert responses. Break condition: If expert calibration is unrepresentative or k misaligns with deployment needs, CACS@k may not correlate with real-world utility.

### Mechanism 2
The dual-judge framework (LLM-as-judge and distilled model) enables scalable, reproducible evaluation aligned with physician judgment. A high-capability LLM-as-judge grades each rubric criterion independently for fidelity; a distilled SFT model (8B scale) is trained on physician-validated data to approximate this at lower cost, both outputting structured JSON decisions. The core assumption is that the distilled model generalizes from training data to new cases with fidelity comparable to the LLM-as-judge and physicians. Evidence includes the abstract and Section 4 describing the input–output contract and training data. Section 6 reports Macro-F1 agreement of 0.775 (LLM-as-judge) and 0.765 (distilled) with physicians. Break condition: If the distilled model drifts due to distribution shift or fails on nuanced cases, alignment degrades.

### Mechanism 3
Expert-curated, complexity-stratified cases capture the longitudinal, open-ended, and safety-critical nature of real clinical workflows. Clinical experts create or transform de-identified cases covering prevention, treatment, and long-term care across 36 specialties and 12 task types, with complexity levels (L1–L3) assigned via hierarchical rules based on task count, specialty count, and inclusion of reasoning/planning. The core assumption is that expert curation ensures clinical validity and that complexity rules reflect increasing real-world difficulty. Evidence includes the abstract, Section 3 detailing curation and quality control, and Section 5.1 showing monotonic performance drop from L1 to L3. Break condition: If case creation introduces bias or complexity rules fail to capture key challenges (e.g., rare diseases), generalizability is limited.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Case Curation Module -> Quality Control Pipeline -> Model Inference Layer -> Dual-Judge Framework -> Metrics Engine -> Validation Module.  
- **Critical path**: Case curation → Quality control → Model response collection → Rubric grading (parallelized by judge) → Score aggregation → CACS@k calculation. Key dependency: Judge reliability directly impacts final scores; quality control ensures case validity.  
- **Design tradeoffs**: Fidelity vs. cost: LLM-as-judge offers high accuracy but expensive; distilled judge reduces cost but risks drift—use LLM-as-judge for validation, distilled for scaling. Granularity vs. scalability: 30 criteria provide fine-grained insights but increase grading time; consider sampling for faster iteration. Complexity stratification enables diagnostics but may underrepresent edge cases; ensure L3 cases include diverse specialty combinations.  
- **Failure signatures**: Low physician-grader agreement (Macro-F1 < 0.7) indicates misalignment—retrain distilled judge or adjust prompts. Performance collapse on L3 or specific specialties (e.g., Organ Transplantation) reveals model weaknesses—targeted data augmentation. Large gap between rubric accuracy and CACS@7 (>15 points) suggests threshold miscalibration—revisit expert calibration. Inconsistent model rankings across judges or runs—investigate judge stability and case distribution.  
- **First 3 experiments**: 1) Validate grader alignment: Run physician meta-evaluation on a stratified 285-case subset; ensure Macro-F1 > 0.75 for both judges. 2) Test complexity stratification: Evaluate a sample model on L1/L2/L3 cases; confirm monotonic performance degradation (e.g., >5 point drop per level). 3) Quantify usability gap: Compare CACS@7 vs. rubric accuracy for top 5 models; expect gap of 12–15 points; if gap narrows, threshold may be too lenient.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why is clinically actionable treatment planning a persistent bottleneck?** Despite strong overall performance, frontier models plateau in the low-to-mid 30s on personalized treatment planning. The paper identifies the symptom but not underlying causes—whether deficits in multi-system constraint synthesis, guideline adherence, personalization trade-offs, or other reasoning gaps. Resolution requires fine-grained error analysis comparing model outputs to expert plans and controlled ablations isolating components of treatment planning.

2. **What drives monotonic performance degradation from L1 to L3?** Performance drops 8–10 points for top models as complexity increases (L3 defined by ≥4 tasks and ≥4 specialties). The authors hypothesize "synthesizing interacting constraints" is challenging but do not disentangle whether the drop stems from combinatorial reasoning explosion, cross-specialty knowledge integration, longitudinal state maintenance, or a combination. Diagnostic experiments varying complexity dimensions orthogonally and analyzing failure modes on L3 cases are needed.

3. **Why the extreme specialty-wise volatility?** GPT-5.2 scores range from 47.8% (Sports Medicine) to 22.9% (Organ Transplantation), with domestic models showing "unique regional resilience" in areas like Anesthesiology. Potential factors include training data distribution, reasoning style differences, or cultural/guideline alignment, but the paper does not explore causes, leaving deployment safety uncertain. Evidence: Table 4 and Figure 2 show capability collapses in surgical specialties.

4. **What accounts for the persistent 12.5–15.5 point usability gap?** Standard Rubric Accuracy overestimates clinical utility relative to CACS@7. Case studies show responses can achieve multiple correct rubrics yet miss critical risk stratification and timelines. The metric highlights the gap but does not diagnose why models accumulate partial correctness without crossing the usability threshold—whether due to missing safety items, lack of prioritization, or failure to address multiple dimensions simultaneously. Comparing responses just below vs. above the threshold to identify discriminative criteria would clarify.

5. **How reliable is the dual-judge framework for nuanced criteria?** Overall agreement with physicians is high (Macro-F1 ~0.765–0.815), but agreement is consistently lower on tasks like Medical NER (Figure 6). The distilled 8B model is cost-effective but its per-task limitations are unexplored, potentially biasing results for precision-critical tasks. Extended meta-evaluation with more physician labels per task, error analysis of judge mistakes, and targeted training data augmentation are needed.

## Limitations
- The k=7 threshold is based on a single expert cohort; generalizability to other clinical settings is uncertain.
- Dual-judge fidelity depends on the unspecified LLM-as-judge model and may introduce artifacts; distilled model drift risk remains.
- Expert curation may harbor selection bias and lacks rare conditions; the benchmark's Chinese specificity limits cross-cultural generalization.

## Confidence
- **CACS@k ensures usable outputs**: Medium – threshold lacks external clinical validation.
- **Dual-judge aligns with physicians**: Medium – moderate agreement (Macro-F1 0.775) but distilled model drift risk.
- **Complexity stratification captures workflows**: Medium – supported by difficulty gradients but no human realism study.

## Next Checks
1. **Judge robustness**: Replicate results with alternative LLM-as-judge (e.g., GPT-4, Claude) and quantify score shifts and ranking stability.
2. **Threshold sensitivity**: Vary k across a plausible range (5–9) to test impact on model rankings and identify optimal cutoff.
3. **External physician validation**: Conduct blinded assessment of LLM responses by independent clinicians to confirm CACS@7 correlates with perceived actionability.
