# SoK: The Pitfalls of Deep Reinforcement Learning for Cybersecurity

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.08690  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08690  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Identified eleven methodological pitfalls in DRL for cybersecurity; fixing state representation in an XSS fuzzer increased vulnerabilities found by 10.1% (75.2% → 85.3%).

## Executive Summary
This SoK paper systematically examines the use of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) in cybersecurity. Through a literature review of 66 papers (2018–2025), it uncovers eleven common methodological pitfalls, with an average of 5.8 pitfalls per work. Controlled experiments validate their real-world impact: correcting state representation in an XSS fuzzer boosts vulnerability discovery by 10.1%, sanitizing SQLi inputs reduces performance by 47.4%, and violating assumed action orders in a network defense simulator can degrade rewards by up to -72.7%. The study concludes with actionable recommendations to enhance rigor and deployability.

## Method Summary
The authors combine a systematic review with empirical case studies. The review identifies and classifies methodological pitfalls across DRL‑cybersecurity literature. For the experiments, they use three representative domains: autonomous defense, malware generation, and web security. The reproduction notes detail the XSS fuzzing experiment: it compares two state representations for the Link environment (original 47‑dimensional vs. distinct 53‑dimensional) by training 20 PPO agents (3.5 M timesteps, learning rate 5×10⁻⁴, γ=0.95) from different seeds and evaluating the percentage of 32 WAVSEP vulnerabilities found (VF%).

## Key Results
- Eleven pitfalls discovered; average 5.8 per paper; most frequent: policy convergence (71.2% lacking evidence), partial observability (60.6% unaddressed), variance analysis (66.7% neglected).
- XSS fuzzer: fixing state representation raised VF% from 75.2% to 85.3% (absolute 10.1% gain).
- SQLi testing: using sanitized inputs caused a 47.4% performance drop.
- Network defense: violating assumed action orders reduced rewards by as much as -72.7%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper succeeds by coupling a comprehensive literature review with concrete, controlled experiments. The review surfaces hidden flaws, while the experiments isolate each pitfall in realistic cybersecurity tasks, quantifying its performance impact. This dual approach transforms abstract methodological concerns into measurable losses, motivating practitioners to adopt the provided best‑practice recommendations. The clear cause‑effect demonstration makes the pitfalls tangible and the remediation guidance actionable.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Deep Reinforcement Learning**  
   *Why needed:* DRL underpins many automated cybersecurity agents; its hyped results often ignore subtle assumptions.  
   *Quick check:* Verify stable learning curves and convergence across multiple random seeds.

2. **State Representation**  
   *Why needed:* The state encodes environment observations; conflating distinct situations leads to partial observability and erroneous policies.  
   *Quick check:* Ensure all relevant features are present, properly normalized, and that semantically different inputs map to distinct states.

3. **Policy Convergence**  
   *Why needed:* Training must converge to a reliable policy; without evidence, reported results are unsubstantiated.  
   *Quick check:* Plot moving average of rewards; look for plateau across training and consistency over repeated runs.

4. **Variance Analysis**  
   *Why needed:* DRL algorithms exhibit high variance; statistical rigor is essential to avoid false conclusions.  
   *Quick check:* Compute confidence intervals (e.g., 95%) over multiple seeds; compare using non‑overlapping intervals or statistical tests.

5. **Controlled Experimentation**  
   *Why needed:* Isolating the effect of one factor (e.g., a specific pitfall) requires holding all else constant.  
   *Quick check:* Identify independent and dependent variables; confirm no confounding changes between experimental conditions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Link Environment → Agent (PPO with MLP actor‑critic) → Action (payload modification) → Next State (updated payload features) → Reward (binary XSS trigger) → Termination (vulnerability found or max steps) → Evaluation on WAVSEP dataset.

**Critical path**  
Train PPO on Link environment with a given state representation → Save trained policy → Run evaluation episodes on WAVSEP vulnerabilities → Compute VF% per run → Aggregate across 20 runs.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- State dimensionality: Higher dimension (53 vs. 47) improves feature discrimination but increases sample complexity and training time.  
- Training timesteps: 3.5 M balances convergence risk with computational cost.  
- Network capacity: Three hidden layers (128 neurons each) provide modeling power but may overfit on limited data.  
- Evaluation episodes: More episodes per vulnerability increase detection confidence at the expense of evaluation duration.

**Failure signatures**  
- Missing Link environment code or WAVSEP dataset.  
- Incorrect distinct‑state transformation (wrong feature separation, dimensions not 53).  
- Excessive variance across runs (overlapping confidence intervals), indicating unstable training.  
- Mismatched evaluation logic (e.g., XSS detection criterion differs from the original).

**Three first experiments**  
1. Replicate baseline: train 20 PPO agents with the original 47‑dimensional state, evaluate VF%.  
2. Replicate modified: train 20 PPO agents with the distinct 53‑dimensional state, evaluate VF%.  
3. Compare: perform a paired statistical test (e.g., t‑test) on the two sets of VF% scores to validate the reported 10.1% improvement.

## Limitations
- Incomplete reporting: The Link state encoding and WAVSEP evaluation protocol are underspecified, preventing faithful replication.  
- Systematic review lacks transparency: No PRISMA flow diagram, inclusion/exclusion criteria, or inter‑rater reliability provided.  
- Other claims (SQLi sanitization impact, network‑defense action‑order impact) are presented without supporting methodology or raw data.

## Confidence
- Pitfall prevalence (systematic review findings): **Medium**  
- XSS state‑representation impact: **Low**  
- SQLi sanitization impact: **Low**
