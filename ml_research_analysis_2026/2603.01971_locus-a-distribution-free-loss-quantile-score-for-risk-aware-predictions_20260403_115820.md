# LOCUS: A Distribution-Free Loss-Quantile Score for Risk-Aware Predictions
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.01971
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01971
- Reference count: 34
- Primary result: LOCUS is a distribution‑free wrapper that outputs per‑input loss quantile scores; on 13 regression benchmarks, its tuned variant reduced conditional large‑loss rate compared to Isolation Forest and label‑variance baselines while maintaining similar acceptance rates.

## Executive Summary
LOCUS provides a modular calibration layer that transforms any black‑box engine producing a predictive cumulative distribution function (CDF) for the realized loss \(Z = L(g(X),Y)\) into a finite‑sample, distribution‑free upper loss quantile score \(U_\alpha(x)\). This score can be thresholded to flag inputs likely to incur large losses, with a guaranteed bound on the joint probability that an unflagged input suffers an unacceptable loss. In empirical evaluations across 13 tabular regression datasets, LOCUS‑Tuned (with a tuned threshold to target ~70% acceptance) lowered the conditional large‑loss rate relative to strong baselines while keeping acceptance rates comparable. The method optionally inflates the predictive CDF in data‑sparse regions via a k‑nearest‑neighbor density proxy to enhance robustness without breaking calibration guarantees.

## Method Summary
Given a fixed predictor \(g\) and any engine that outputs a predictive CDF \(\hat{F}(z|x)\) for the realized loss \(Z = L(g(X),Y)\), LOCUS splits a calibration dataset into two parts. On the first part \(\mathcal{D}_1\), the engine is trained to obtain \(\hat{F}\). On the second part \(\mathcal{D}_2\), it computes probability integral transform (PIT) values \(W_i = \hat{F}(Z_i|X_i)\) and extracts their empirical \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile \(t\). The calibrated upper loss score is defined as \(U_\alpha(x) = \hat{F}^{-1}(t|x)\). This split‑conformal construction guarantees \(P(Z \le U_\alpha(X)) \ge 1-\alpha\) under exchangeability. To increase conservativeness in low‑density regions, LOCUS can modulate \(\hat{F}\) with an envelope \(\hat{F}_\gamma(z|x)\) derived from a kNN‑based density proxy \(\gamma(x)\). For deployment, a user specifies an unacceptable loss tolerance \(\tau\); inputs with \(U_\alpha(x) \le \tau\) are flagged (or the threshold \(\lambda\) may be tuned on a validation set to target a desired conditional exceedance rate). The approach requires no distributional assumptions and provides finite‑sample validity.

## Key Results
- LOCUS‑Tuned (fixed acceptance ~70%) reduced the conditional large‑loss rate compared to Isolation Forest and label‑variance baselines on all 13 regression benchmarks.
- Acceptance rates of LOCUS‑Tuned were similar to those baselines, demonstrating practical utility.
- Test marginal coverage consistently met or exceeded the target \(1-\alpha\) level, confirming the calibration guarantee.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: From Loss Predictive CDF to Calibrated Score
- Claim: Any black‑box engine that produces a predictive CDF for the realized loss \(Z = L(g(X),Y)\) can be converted into a finite‑sample, distribution‑free, calibrated upper loss bound \(U_\alpha(x)\).
- Mechanism: Split‑calibration transforms the engine’s output \(\hat{F}(\cdot|x)\) into a score with marginal validity. On a hold‑out calibration set \(\mathcal{D}_2\), compute PIT values \(W_i = \hat{F}(Z_i|X_i)\). The empirical \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile \(t_{1-\alpha}\) of these PIT scores is used to define \(U_\alpha(x) = \hat{F}^{-1}(t_{1-\alpha}|x)\). By exchangeability, at least \(1-\alpha\) probability mass of \(Z\) falls below \(U_\alpha(X)\).
- Core assumption: The calibration data are i.i.d. and exchangeable with deployment data; the predictive CDF engine is deterministic given its training data; PIT values are continuous (no ties).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: "...applies split‑calibration to obtain a calibrated upper loss quantile. Thresholding \(U_\alpha(x)\) yields a flagging rule with finite‑sample control."
  - [section 2.1]: Theorem 1 states \(P(Z \leq U_\alpha(X)) \geq 1-\alpha\) under i.i.d. sampling, and describes the calibration construction with PIT values.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors (e.g., "Leverage‑Weighted Conformal Prediction") discuss split conformal principles; evidence for *this paper’s specific mechanism* is only in the paper itself.
- Break condition: Violations of exchangeability, or deterministic failures in \(\hat{F}\) (e.g., CDF inversion errors, degenerate PIT distributions).

### Mechanism 2: Epistemic Uncertainty Inflation via Density‑Modulated Envelope
- Claim: The base loss predictive CDF can be inflated conservatively in data‑sparse regions without breaking the finite‑sample calibration guarantees, improving robustness in extrapolation.
- Mechanism: A kNN‑based density proxy (radius \(r_k(x)\) in a representation space) maps to a trimming level \(\gamma(x)\). On posterior samples of loss CDFs \(\{F(\cdot|x,\theta^{(s)})\}\), the calibrated predictive CDF becomes the \(\gamma(x)\)-quantile envelope: \(\hat{F}(z|x) = \text{quantile}_{\gamma(x)}(\{F(z|x,\theta^{(s)})\})\). Smaller \(\gamma(x)\) in sparse regions yields a heavier right tail (more conservative upper bounds).
- Core assumption: kNN radius in feature space is a meaningful proxy for prediction uncertainty; the posterior captures aleatoric variability; the envelope remains a valid CDF.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 2.1.1]: Explicit formula for \(\gamma(x)\) via logistic transform of standardized kNN radius, and statement that “smaller \(\gamma(x)\) in low‑density regions… inflates the resulting bound \(U_\alpha(x)\)”.
  - [section 2.1]: “Our framework is modular… the optional \(\gamma(x)\)‑inflated envelope increases conservativeness in data‑scarce regions without changing… the finite‑sample guarantees.”
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; epistemic‑aware conformal methods (e.g., EPICSCORE) exist but not linked to this exact inflation.
- Break condition: Poor feature representation \(\phi\) making kNN density proxy irrelevant; kNN radius fails to correlate with true loss variability; envelope quantile step introduces numerical instability.

### Mechanism 3: Flagging with Distribution‑Free Large‑Loss Control
- Claim: Thresholding \(U_\alpha(x)\) at \(\lambda = \tau\) (the unacceptable loss tolerance) yields a rule that controls the joint probability of “trusted but bad” events: \(P(Z > \tau, U_\alpha(X) \leq \tau) \leq \alpha\).
- Mechanism: Define acceptance region \(A_{\tau;\alpha} = \{x: U_\alpha(x) \leq \tau\}\). Since \(U_\alpha(X) \leq \tau\) implies (by construction) \(Z \leq U_\alpha(X)\) with probability at least \(1-\alpha\), the event \(\{Z > \tau, X \in A_{\tau;\alpha}\}\) is a subset of \(\{Z > U_\alpha(X)\), which has probability at most \(\alpha\) by Theorem 1.
- Core assumption: The tolerance \(\tau\) is fixed and meaningful for deployment; the calibration procedure is valid; the loss \(L\) is non‑negative or bounded appropriately (not explicitly required, but standard).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: “…thresholding \(U_\alpha(x)\) yields a transparent flagging rule with distribution‑free control of large‑loss events.”
  - [section 2.2]: Theorem 3 explicitly states \(P(Z > \tau, X \in A_{\tau;\alpha}) \leq \alpha\).
  - [section 2.2.1]: “In this case we obtain the following guarantee: Theorem 3. Under the construction above, \(P(Z > \tau, X \in A_{\tau;\alpha}) \leq \alpha\).”
  - [corpus]: Related to risk‑controlling prediction sets [Bates et al., 2021] but focuses on *joint* event control rather than average risk.
- Break condition: If the calibration marginal validity bound \(P(Z \leq U_\alpha(X)) \geq 1-\alpha\) is loose (i.e., actual coverage much higher), the joint control may be conservative but not false; break would require marginal validity failure.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Split Conformal Prediction
- Why needed here: The core calibration mechanism (PIT‑based quantile lookup) is a direct application of split conformal prediction (ICP), which provides finite‑sample, distribution‑free marginal validity.
- Quick check: Why is exchangeability of \(\{(X_i,Z_i)\}\) crucial for the marginal coverage guarantee \(P(Z \leq U_\alpha(X)) \geq 1-\alpha\)?

### Concept: Realized Loss vs. Label Uncertainty
- Why needed here: LOCUS models the distribution of \(Z = L(g(X),Y)\)—the actual error incurred—instead of \(Y|X\), aligning the score directly with deployment cost.
- Quick check: Give an example where high label uncertainty \(\text{Var}(Y|X)\) does **not** imply high realized loss \(L(g(X),Y)\) (see Figure 1 left panel for intuition).

### Concept: Conditional vs. Marginal Coverage
- Why needed here: The default \(\lambda=\tau\) rule guarantees a *joint* event bound. Achieving a *conditional* exceedance rate \(P(Z>\tau|X\in A) \approx \eta\) requires tuning (\(\lambda\) or \(\alpha\)) on a validation set.
- Quick check: What is the key difference between Theorem 3’s guarantee and the approximate conditional statement \(P(Z > \tau|X \in A_{\tau;\alpha}) \lesssim \alpha\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Loss model engine (trained on D1) → Predictive CDF \(\hat{F}(\cdot|x)\) → Calibration layer (uses D2 to compute PIT quantile t) → \(U_\alpha(x) = \hat{F}^{-1}(t|x)\). Optional: Epistemic modulator (kNN‑based \(\gamma(x)\)) adjusts the CDF envelope before inversion. Flagging rule compares \(U_\alpha(x)\) to threshold λ (either fixed τ or tuned on validation set). Validation tuner selects λ to match target acceptance/conditional exceedance.

### Critical path
1. Split calibration data into D1 and D2.
2. Train loss model on D1 to obtain \(\hat{F}(\cdot|x)\).
3. Compute PIT values on D2 and find empirical \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile t.
4. For new input x, compute \(U_\alpha(x) = \hat{F}^{-1}(t|x)\).
5. Flag input if \(U_\alpha(x) \le \lambda\) (λ chosen by user or via validation‑tuning).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Loss model choice**: BART vs. MC‑Dropout. BART may be more sample‑efficient but slower; MC‑Dropout scales better but requires tuning dropout rate. Evidence: Table 2 shows alternating winners.
- **Epistemic inflation (\(\gamma(x)\))**: Adds robustness in low‑density regions but may over‑inflate and reduce power. Evidence: Table 2 shows \(\gamma\)-inflated variants sometimes better, sometimes similar.
- **Fixed \(\lambda=\tau\) vs. tuning**: Guarantees joint control but conditional exceedance may deviate from \(\alpha\); tuning matches the conditional target but loses finite‑sample guarantee. Evidence: Section 3.2 reports Locus‑Tuned outperforms baselines; Appendix D/E provide alternative tunings.

### Failure signatures
- **Calibration failure**: Marginal coverage \(P(Z \le U_\alpha(X))\) falls below \(1-\alpha\) on test (check Table 4). Causes: non‑exchangeable data, severe \(\hat{F}\) misspecification, small D2.
- **Inflated accept‑rate mismatch**: Very low acceptance for some BART runs (Table 5) indicates \(\hat{F}\) too conservative; consider reducing \(\gamma_{\max}\) or using a different loss model.
- **Tuning instability**: When scanning λ over validation grid, the conditional exceedance estimate \(\hat{q}(\lambda)\) jumps erratically (small \(n_\lambda\)). Enforce minimum acceptance rate (e.g., \(\rho_{\min}=0.05\)).
- **Epistemic proxy failure**: kNN radius not correlated with true loss heterogeneity. Diagnose by plotting \(U_\alpha(x)\) vs. \(r_k(x)\) on held‑out data.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Marginal coverage sanity check**: Compute empirical coverage \(P(Z \le U_\alpha(X))\) on a held‑out test set. Should be ≥ \(1-\alpha\) (e.g., ≥ 90% for \(\alpha=0.1\)). See Table 4.
2. **Loss‑score interpretability**: For a few test points, compare \(U_\alpha(x)\) with label uncertainty estimate \(\hat{\sigma}(x)\) and realized loss \(Z\). Verify cases where \(Z\) is large but \(\hat{\sigma}(x)\) is small (misfit)—\(U_\alpha(x)\) should be large (see Table 1 example, row 3).
3. **Conditional exceedance with fixed \(\lambda=\tau\)**: Compute \(P(Z > \tau|X \in A_{\tau;\alpha})\) on test. Expect around \(\alpha\) (e.g., ∼10%), not wildly larger. Check Table 6; many datasets achieve ≤ α but some (concrete, protein) show 0% or 100% due to tiny acceptance—indicates \(\gamma\)-inflation may be too strong.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
#### Open Question 1
- Question: Does Locus work for classification?
- Basis in paper: The authors claim natural extension to classification but provide no experiments or analysis.
- Why unresolved: Classification loss is discrete, which may violate the no‑ties assumption (Theorem 1) and affect conditional validity.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluate Locus on binary/multiclass datasets with 0‑1 loss, report marginal/conditional exceedance, and test tie‑handling strategies.

#### Open Question 2
- Question: Which base loss model yields the most efficient Locus scores?
- Basis in paper: The method is modular and uses any predictive CDF, but only BART and MC dropout are examined.
- Why unresolved: Unclear trade‑offs between model flexibility, accuracy of quantile estimates, and resulting acceptance rates.
- What evidence would resolve it: Benchmark a diverse set of density estimators (normalizing flows, ensembles, FlexCode) on multiple datasets, compare acceptance vs. conditional exceedance.

#### Open Question 3
- Question: Can we achieve finite‑sample conditional coverage guarantees for the accepted set?
- Basis in paper: Locus provides marginal validity (Theorem 1) and asymptotic conditional coverage (Theorem 2), but conditional exceedance control is only heuristic.
- Why unresolved: Finite‑sample control of \(P(Z > \tau \mid X\in A)\) is stronger and desirable for risk‑sensitive applications.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop a concentration inequality for conditional exceedance, or prove finite‑sample bounds under smoothness assumptions on the conditional loss distribution.

#### Open Question 4
- Question: How does Locus compare to conformal risk control and selective prediction methods?
- Basis in paper: The related work highlights theoretical differences, but no head‑to‑head empirical study exists.
- Why unresolved: Unknown which method yields higher acceptance for a given conditional risk or performs better under different loss functions and thresholds.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct a comprehensive benchmark across datasets and losses, compare acceptance rates, conditional exceedance, and computational cost.

## Limitations
- **Implementation gaps**: Missing specifics for MDN training (optimizer, LR, epochs), BART MCMC (burn‑in, thinning), and bisection bounds for CDF inversion create reproducibility risks.
- **Weak external evidence**: The method lacks validation in related literature; performance may not generalize beyond the 13 tabular benchmarks.
- **Failure modes**: Marginal coverage can drop below \(1-\alpha\) if the loss model is misspecified or data violate exchangeability; kNN density estimates may degrade in high dimensions.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (split‑calibration to \(U_\alpha\)): High
- Mechanism 2 (epistemic inflation): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (joint flagging control): High
- Benchmark results (Locus‑Tuned vs. baselines): Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Marginal coverage audit**: On a held‑out test set compute \(P(Z \le U_\alpha(X))\); it should be ≥ \(1-\alpha\). A significant shortfall indicates calibration failure.
2. **Joint control verification**: With fixed \(\lambda=\tau\), estimate \(P(Z > \tau, X\in A_{\tau;\alpha})\); it must be ≤ α. Large excess suggests violations (e.g., non‑exchangeability).
3. **Density‑inflation sanity check**: On a validation set, correlate kNN radius \(r_k(x)\) with \(U_\alpha(x)\). A clear positive correlation confirms the epistemic proxy works; absence indicates ineffective inflation.
