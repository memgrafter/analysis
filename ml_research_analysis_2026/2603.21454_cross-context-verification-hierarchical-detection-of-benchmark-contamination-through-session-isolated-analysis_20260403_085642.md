# Cross-Context Verification: Hierarchical Detection of Benchmark Contamination through Session-Isolated Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv:** 2603.21454  
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21454  
- **Reference count:** 4  
- **Primary result:** CCV with HCCA achieves perfect separation between contaminated and genuine reasoning on 9 SWE-bench Verified problems; contamination is binary, absence of analytical preamble perfectly discriminates recall from reasoning, and 33% of prior contamination labels are false positives.

## Executive Summary
Large language model coding benchmarks suffer from solution contamination and test flaws, which undermine their validity. This work introduces Cross-Context Verification (CCV), a method that detects contamination by repeatedly solving the same problem in independent sessions and measuring solution diversity—contaminated models produce identical outputs across sessions while genuine reasoning yields diverse solutions. Combined with the Hierarchical Cross-Context Architecture (HCCA), which prevents analyst bias through strict information restriction, CCV achieves near-perfect statistical separation on a set of SWE-bench Verified problems. Key findings reveal that contamination is a binary phenomenon, the absence of an analytical preamble in model outputs perfectly distinguishes memorized recall from reasoning, and a significant fraction (33%) of previously reported contamination labels are false positives.

## Method Summary
The task is to identify benchmark contamination and test flaws in coding problems. The evaluation uses nine SWE-bench Verified problems: three suspected of contamination, three with known test flaws, and three clean controls. For each problem, the model (Claude Opus 4.6) generates five independent solutions at temperature 0.0, with only the issue text provided and no prior repository context. No training is performed; all analysis is post‑hoc.

CCV quantifies solution diversity via three similarity metrics: weighted Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) structural similarity, line‑level BLEU, and normalized edit distance. These are combined into a Contamination Score (CS) using a weighted formula (AST 0.5, BLEU 0.3, edit distance 0.2) with a threshold of 0.6 to separate contaminated from genuine behavior. A complementary classifier checks whether the first 100 tokens of the output begin with an analytical preamble (reasoning) or jump directly to a diff (recall). HCCA organizes the verification process into four hierarchical layers—Designer, Executor, Analysts, Integrator—with strictly unidirectional information flow to eliminate confirmation bias and enable detection of composite contamination‑flaw cases.

## Key Results
- Perfect separation (Mann‑Whitney U=0, p≈0.012, effect size r=1.0) between contaminated (CS≥0.6) and genuine (CS<0.6) behaviors on the 9‑problem set.  
- Contamination exhibits a binary pattern; no intermediate CS values were observed.  
- The absence of an analytical preamble perfectly discriminates recall from reasoning (100% accuracy on their trials).  
- 33% of prior contamination labels are re‑classified as false positives.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Session‑isolated solution diversity distinguishes memorized recall from genuine reasoning.  
- **Mechanism:** Contaminated models reproduce identical outputs across independent sessions (deterministic recall), while genuine reasoning yields varied solutions due to inherent non‑determinism even at temperature 0.  
- **Core assumption:** Memorization produces byte‑identical patches; reasoning admits multiple valid implementations.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "solve the same problem in N independent sessions and measure solution diversity—contaminated models produce identical outputs while genuine reasoning yields diverse solutions."  
  [section 3.1] "Core assumption. Memorized recall is deterministic... Genuine reasoning is non‑determinism... natural variation in LLM generation produces diverse solutions even at temperature 0."  
- **Break condition:** If models learn to generate "fake diversity" (surface variation with identical logic), or if genuine reasoning on certain problems is inherently deterministic (e.g., trivial bugs), the metric fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Absence of analytical preamble perfectly discriminates recall from reasoning.  
- **Mechanism:** Memorized solutions are retrieved and output immediately; reasoning requires analysis, producing an initial analytical preamble.  
- **Core assumption:** Contaminated models skip problem analysis; reasoning models begin with descriptive, analytical text.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "the absence of analytical preamble perfectly discriminates recall from reasoning."  
  [section 3.4] "Our classifier examines the first 100 tokens: NO REASONING: Output begins with diffs... FULL REASONING: Output begins with analytical phrases... This simple heuristic achieves 100% accuracy on our 45 trials."  
  [section 6.3] "Response time provides independent corroboration: contaminated problems average 5.77s versus 18.45s for genuine reasoning."  
- **Break condition:** Models adversarially prepend synthetic reasoning traces to memorized outputs, or reasoning models are prompted for concise responses without preamble.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** HCCA's hierarchical, information‑restricted structure prevents confirmation bias in analysis, enabling detection of contamination‑flaw composites.  
- **Mechanism:** Strict unidirectional information flow (Designer → Executor → Analysts → Integrator) with no lateral sharing between analysts prevents anchoring and sycophancy.  
- **Core assumption:** Independent analytical perspectives, unexposed to prior interpretations, reveal nuances (e.g., composite cases) that a single sequential analysis would miss.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "HCCA prevents analyst bias via strict information restriction... HCCA's independent analysis structure discovers contamination‑flaw composite cases that single‑analyst approaches miss."  
  [section 4.3] "The Layer 2 analyst for Group B classified astropy‑7606 as a pure test flaw... The independent Layer 2 analyst for Group C... computed a contamination score of 0.641 and flagged it as a contamination‑flaw composite."  
  [section 4.4] Pilot: "Worker→Verifier→Director pipeline... yields 100% sycophantic confirmation" when conclusions are shared, confirming information restriction is key.  
- **Break condition:** If the Integrator layer introduces bias by overruling independent analysts without robust cross‑validation, or if problem assignments to analysts are not randomized, the structure fails.

## Foundational Learning
### Non‑deterministic LLM generation at temperature 0
**Why needed:** The CCV mechanism relies on inherent variation across sessions even with deterministic sampling settings. Understanding that hardware/infrastructure factors cause this variation is crucial for trusting the diversity signal.  
**Quick check:** Why might two identical prompts to an LLM at temperature 0 produce different outputs, and how does this enable CCV?

### AST‑based code similarity metrics
**Why needed:** CCV's diversity score uses weighted AST structural similarity (0.4) to detect functionally identical code despite cosmetic differences. This requires understanding that syntax trees capture logic over formatting.  
**Quick check:** Why is AST similarity weighted higher than line‑level BLEU in the contamination score derivation?

### Information restriction as an anti‑bias mechanism in multi‑agent systems
**Why needed:** HCCA's design is a deliberate inversion of typical multi‑agent frameworks (which maximize sharing). The principle that independent judgment requires context isolation must be understood to implement HCCA correctly.  
**Quick check:** How does HCCA's "unidirectional information flow" differ from the "sycophantic confirmation" failure mode in the Worker→Verifier→Director pilot?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Layer 0 (Designer) → Layer 1 (Executor) → Layer 2 (Analysts) → Layer 3 (Integrator).  
Data flow: L1 markdown files → L2 separate markdown files → L3 consolidated markdown. No lateral L2→L2.

**Critical path:**  
1. Executor generates trial data (N=5 per problem).  
2. Each Analyst independently processes their group’s data using the scoring formulas (Eqs 2, 3).  
3. Integrator aggregates scores, runs statistical tests (Mann‑Whitney, etc.), validates cross‑group consistency (e.g., checks if a problem flagged by Group B as test‑flaw is independently high‑contamination in Group C's raw metrics).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Overhead vs. rigor: HCCA requires 6 separate LLM sessions (3 analysts + Integrator) versus 1–3 for simple CCV. Worth it for ambiguous cases (composites) or high‑stakes decisions.  
- Information restriction vs. collaboration: HCCA forbids sharing; typical multi‑agent systems rely on discussion. HCCA's pilot shows shared conclusions destroy filtering.  
- A priori group assignment: Problems are pre‑categorized (contamination‑suspect, test‑flaw, control). This allows analysts to use targeted heuristics but risks expectation bias if group labels leak (prevented by strict context isolation).

**Failure signatures:**  
- CCV failure: All N sessions produce different but incorrect patches (high diversity, low gold BLEU) – could be a hard problem or test flaw, not contamination.  
- HCCA failure: Any lateral communication (e.g., an analyst referencing another's report) or Integrator overriding independent scores without L1 data review.  
- Composite detection failure: If Group C analyst does not manually compute contamination score for test‑flaw problems, the composite (e.g., astropy‑7606) is missed.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Replicate CCV on 9‑problem set:** Run 5 sessions per problem at temperature 0. Compute diversity and gold BLEU. Confirm perfect separation (CS ≥0.6 for known contaminated, <0.6 for controls).  
2. **Validate reasoning classifier on held‑out problems:** Apply the first‑100‑token heuristic to new problems not in the 9‑set. Check if NO REASONING still perfectly correlates with zero code diversity (d=0) and high gold BLEU.  
3. **Reproduce HCCA pilot sycophancy:** Implement Worker→Verifier→Director with shared conclusions (Verifier sees Worker's findings). Apply to a sample of CCR artifacts. Expect 100% confirmation rate, confirming information restriction is the active ingredient.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Binary score distribution:** Does the binary pattern of contamination scores persist with more contaminated problems and diverse models? (Basis: only n=3 contaminated problems; intermediate cases may exist at larger scale.)  
- **Preamble classifier reliability:** Is the NO REASONING vs FULL REASONING classifier reliable on held‑out benchmarks and robust against adversarial synthetic prefixes? (Needs validation on unseen problems and adversarial training.)  
- **Cross‑domain applicability:** Can CCV's session‑isolated diversity detection work for other programming languages (e.g., JavaScript) and non‑coding tasks (e.g., math proofs)? (Metrics like AST are Python‑specific; other domains may require adaptation.)

## Limitations
- Small sample (9 problems) limits generalizability of thresholds and binary claim.  
- Incomplete method specifications (AST normalization details, classifier lexicon, isolation protocol) hinder exact replication.  
- Evaluation restricted to Claude Opus 4.6; results may not transfer to other models or open‑source LLMs.

## Confidence
- CCV core mechanism (diversity + preamble): **Medium** – perfect separation on small set but vulnerable to adversarial outputs.  
- HCCA bias prevention: **Medium** – pilot shows sycophancy without isolation, but full‑scale evidence lacking.  
- Binary contamination claim: **Low** – threshold CS≥0.6 derived from limited data; bimodality may not hold broadly.  
- 33% false positive rate: **Low** – unclear prior label set and labeling procedure.

## Next Checks
1. **Full‑suite replication:** Apply CCV to all SWE‑bench Verified problems (500+) across multiple LLMs to validate generalizability of CS threshold and preamble classifier.  
2. **Adversarial robustness:** Prompt models to prepend synthetic reasoning and generate cosmetic code variations; measure resulting false negative rates and score shifts.  
3. **HCCA vs. sequential analysis:** Compare HCCA to a shared‑context analyst pipeline on 50 ambiguous problems; quantify differences in composite detection and inter‑analyst agreement.
