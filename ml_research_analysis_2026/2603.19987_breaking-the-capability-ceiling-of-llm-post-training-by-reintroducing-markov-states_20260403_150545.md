# Breaking the Capability Ceiling of LLM Post-Training by Reintroducing Markov States

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.19987
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19987
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Introducing explicit Markov state representations breaks the performance ceiling in RL post‑training of LLMs on complex logic puzzles, yielding substantially higher success rates and robust out‑of‑distribution generalization compared to standard action‑history conditioning.

## Executive Summary
Standard RL post‑training for large language models (LLMs) uses the expanding action history as the state, which causes an exponential growth in the effective state space and leads to a capability ceiling. This paper reintroduces explicit Markov states, where a compact state representation is updated by a deterministic transition function rather than by concatenating histories. The method is evaluated on three challenging logic puzzles (Sudoku, Sokoban, Futoshiki) using Qwen models with the GRPO algorithm. Markov models consistently surpass action‑sequence baselines, achieving dramatically higher Pass@128 scores and maintaining performance on out‑of‑distribution puzzles where baselines collapse. Theoretical analysis shows that the Markov property reduces sample complexity exponentially by avoiding the exponential blow‑up of history‑based state spaces. The results demonstrate that structured state representations can unlock genuinely new reasoning capabilities in LLM post‑training.

## Method Summary
The approach replaces the expanding action‑history buffer with a compact Markov state that is updated after each step via a transition function. During RL post‑training (GRPO), the policy network conditions on the current state instead of the history. For training, the true environment dynamics are used to update the state; for deployment, a separate state predictor is trained via supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) on (state, action → next state) triples to simulate the environment. The pipeline begins with an SFT warm‑up on optimal trajectories to initialize the policy, followed by GRPO where trajectories are collected using group‑relative advantages. The method is applied to deterministic, fully observable puzzles (Sudoku, Sokoban, Futoshiki) where the board configuration serves as a natural Markov state. Evaluation uses Pass@k metrics over 128 sampled trajectories per test instance.

## Key Results
- Markov models break the capability ceiling observed with action‑sequence conditioning, achieving significantly higher Pass@128 and average success rates on in‑distribution puzzles.
- On out‑of‑distribution puzzles, Markov models retain strong performance (e.g., Futoshiki OOD: 77.8% vs. 0.0% for action‑sequence with Qwen3‑4B), while action‑sequence models fail completely.
- Theoretical analysis confirms that Markovian learning reduces sample complexity exponentially, and empirical results on the combination lock task show convergence in ~30k steps versus failure after 800k steps for history‑based agents.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Replacing the expanding action history with a compact Markov state reduces the effective state space size from exponential to polynomial in the horizon, lowering sample complexity.
- **Mechanism**: The policy conditions on a succinct state representation (updated via a transition function) rather than the full history. This collapses an exponentially growing history tree into a linear state chain, so coverage requirements scale with the number of distinct Markov states, not the number of action sequences.
- **Core assumption**: The underlying task has a compact, sufficient Markov state representation (i.e., the current state encapsulates all information needed for optimal future decisions).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "current formulations use the expanding action history as the state, rather than compact Markov states."
  - [section 3.2] On the Combination Lock task with horizon H=10, the Markov agent converges in ~30k steps while the action-sequence agent fails after 800k steps.
  - [section 5] Proposition 2 shows the performance bound depends on density ratios over true Markov states, while Proposition 1 (action-sequence) depends on exponentially large history space ratios.
- **Break condition**: If the environment is non‑Markovian (the current compact state is insufficient), the state representation will fail to capture necessary history, leading to suboptimal policies or instability.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The Markov property enables generalization across trajectories that lead to the same state, improving out‑of‑distribution (OOD) performance on deeper or more complex instances.
- **Mechanism**: By treating all histories that arrive at an equivalent state as identical, the policy learns a single mapping from state to action. This avoids the need to separately learn behaviors for every possible history, which is infeasible for long horizons or novel configurations.
- **Core assumption**: OOD problems share the same underlying Markov state structure as in‑distribution problems (i.e., the transition dynamics and state representation are consistent).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.1] Markov models achieve substantially higher Pass@128 on OOD puzzles (e.g., Futoshiki OOD: 77.8% vs. 0.0% for action‑sequence with Qwen3‑4B), while action‑sequence models collapse.
  - [section 4.3] Even with ground‑truth advantages (A*) to simplify credit assignment, Markov models (πA⋆_mkv) consistently outperform action‑sequence models (πA⋆_act‑seq) on generalization benchmarks.
- **Break condition**: If OOD instances require state representations not covered during training (e.g., new board sizes or transition dynamics), the Markov state may not transfer, and performance gains could diminish.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Explicit Markov state conditioning reduces noise in the learning signal by decoupling state tracking from action selection, leading to more reliable reward attribution and faster convergence.
- **Mechanism**: The policy receives a clean, environment‑updated state instead of having to infer the current state from a noisy history of tokens. This prevents error accumulation from mistaken history interpretation and enables the RL algorithm to assign credit based on true state‑action pairs.
- **Core assumption**: The external state transition function (P or ˆP) is accurate enough to provide a faithful representation of the underlying environment state.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.2] State‑action‑sequence models (which see the true state sequence but still condition on full history) underperform Markov models, suggesting that discarding redundant history improves learning efficiency.
  - [section 4.2] Ablation: removing current state access from state‑action‑sequence models causes performance to collapse, while keeping only the last state retains much of the accuracy, indicating the current state is the dominant informative feature.
  - [section 5] Theoretical bound includes an additive term O(H³εP) for transition model error, showing robustness to small inaccuracies.
- **Break condition**: If the state transition model accumulates significant error (high εP), the additive bias term may dominate the bound, causing performance to degrade below that of history‑based methods.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  - **Base LLM (π)**: Acts as the policy, taking a state (Markov or history) and outputting an action (token/semantic step).
  - **State transition module (P/ˆP)**: Updates the state given the previous state and action. Can be the true environment dynamics (during training) or a learned/predicted model (for deployment).
  - **RL trainer (GRPO)**: Collects trajectories using the current policy, computes advantages (either estimated or ground‑truth A*), and updates π. The trainer remains largely unchanged from standard implementations.
  - **State encoder/decoder (implicit)**: For discrete tasks, the state is a structured representation (board configuration) that must be serialized into a textual or numerical input for the LLM.

- **Critical path**:
  1. Replace the history buffer (concatenated tokens) with a compact state variable that is updated after each action.
  2. Implement or obtain a state transition function that maps (state, action) → next_state.
  3. Modify the environment interaction loop to feed the current state (not history) to the policy, then update state via the transition function.
  4. Keep the RL algorithm (GRPO) identical, but ensure the trajectory buffer stores (state, action) pairs rather than history‑indexed steps.
  5. For test‑time deployment without environment access, train a separate state prediction model (ˆP) via SFT on (state, action, next_state) triples.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **True environment vs. learned transition model**: Using the true P during training and testing is exact but requires environment access. Using a learned ˆP allows deployment in environments without step‑wise state feedback but introduces approximation error (εP). The paper shows robustness to moderate ˆP error.
  - **State representation complexity**: The state must be compact yet sufficient. Overly simplified states may lose information (non‑arkov), while overly detailed states may reintroduce history‑like explosion. Task‑specific engineering is needed (e.g., board grids for puzzles).
  - **Training stability**: Markov models converge faster, but require an accurate initial SFT warm‑up to bootstrap state understanding? The ablation (Appendix C.4) shows Markov models need less SFT to start learning, suggesting they are more data‑efficient in RL.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - **State collapse**: The learned transition model produces identical states for distinct configurations, causing the policy to lose situational awareness (detect by monitoring state diversity).
  - **Credit assignment degradation**: If the state is not truly Markov, the policy may still rely on spurious historical cues, leading to inconsistent action selection for the same state (check via state‑action visitation diversity).
  - **Domain shift**: When deploying with ˆP, if the training distribution of (state, action) pairs differs from test‑time, the state prediction error εP increases, causing performance drops (measure by state prediction accuracy on held‑out trajectories).

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Combination Lock replication**: Train small MLP agents on the 10‑step lock with both Markov and action‑sequence inputs using DQN. Verify the ~30k vs. 800k step gap to confirm the basic mechanism in a controlled setting.
  2. **LLM‑scale pilot on single task**: Fine‑tune a small instruct‑tuned LLM (e.g., Qwen2.5‑3B) on a single puzzle (e.g., Sudoku) with both paradigms, using GRPO with true environment dynamics. Compare Pass@128 and training curves to ensure the LLM can learn the Markov formulation.
  3. **State transition model accuracy sweep**: Train ˆP with varying amounts of data (e.g., 10k, 50k, 100k state‑action triples). Evaluate Markov policy performance when switching from P* to ˆP at test time to quantify robustness to εP.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Do Markov state representations yield similar performance gains in complex, open‑domain reasoning tasks (e.g., multi‑step coding, mathematical proofs) as observed in synthetic logic puzzles?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The authors claim broader applicability to coding and mathematical reasoning but only evaluate on three grid‑based puzzles (Sudoku, Sokoban, Futoshiki) with fully observable, deterministic states.
- **Why unresolved**: The controlled puzzle environments have explicit, compact state definitions; real‑world tasks often lack such clear state boundaries and may involve partial observability or semantic complexity.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replication of the Markov vs. action‑sequence paradigm on diverse open‑domain benchmarks (e.g., competitive programming, formal theorem proving) with learned or automatically extracted state representations.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can effective Markov states be learned end‑to‑end within the RL policy rather than relying on an external, separately trained state predictor (like the SFT‑based ˆP)?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The method requires a pre‑trained state transition model ˆP to replace the environment during deployment, but the paper does not explore training the policy and state estimator jointly.
- **Why unresolved**: A separate predictor introduces potential distribution shift and limits applicability to domains where ground‑truth Markov states are not readily definable.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Architectural designs where the policy network outputs both actions and a latent state representation, trained via RL objectives that ensure the latent state serves as a sufficient statistic for future decisions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How robust is the Markovian approach to stochastic or non‑Markovian environments where the true state cannot be perfectly captured by a compact deterministic representation?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Theoretical analysis assumes deterministic transitions (P⋆) and a compact true state; the empirical tasks are deterministic. The paper acknowledges many real‑world settings have stochastic dynamics.
- **Why unresolved**: The benefit of Markov states may diminish if the estimated transition model ˆP is inaccurate due to stochasticity, or if the environment fundamentally requires history for optimal control.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Introducing controlled stochasticity (e.g., random moves in Sokoban, noisy rewards) or using partially observable variants of puzzles, then measuring performance degradation relative to action‑sequence models.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Is the theoretically predicted exponential reduction in sample complexity empirically observable across task families with varying horizon lengths and state‑space sizes?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Proposition 2 shows sample complexity dependence on state‑space coverage versus exponential history coverage, but empirical results only report final success rates on fixed‑horizon puzzles.
- **Why unresolved**: Without scaling experiments that systematically vary horizon H or problem complexity, the practical magnitude of the sample complexity advantage remains unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Training curves showing number of samples required to reach a target success rate as H increases, comparing Markov vs. action‑sequence models on puzzle families with configurable horizons.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: Do Markov state representations facilitate better multi‑task learning or transfer across related reasoning problems compared to action‑sequence policies?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper focuses on single‑task post‑training; compact Markov states might provide a reusable abstraction that speeds learning on new tasks with similar underlying dynamics.
- **Why unresolved**: If Markov states capture task‑agnostic structure (e.g., board constraints in puzzles), policies conditioned on them could generalize faster to new problems within the same domain family.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Multi‑task experiments where models are trained on a distribution of puzzle variants (e.g., Sudoku of different sizes) and evaluated on zero‑shot transfer to unseen variants, comparing Markov and action‑sequence architectures.

## Limitations
- Exact system prompts, board formatting, and action representations for Sokoban/Futoshiki are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- SFT data composition and GRPO hyperparameters (e.g., clipping range, PPO epochs) are missing, requiring assumptions that could affect results.
- Theoretical gains rely on a perfect Markov representation; insufficient state may degrade performance in complex domains.

## Confidence
- **Empirical breakthrough on puzzles**: High
- **Exponential sample complexity reduction**: Medium
- **Out‑of‑distribution generalization**: High
