# Benchmarking Political Persuasion Risks Across Frontier Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09884
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09884
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Frontier LLMs produce significantly larger political attitude shifts (~0.20 on a 5‑point scale) than actual campaign advertisements (~0.14), with substantial variation across models.

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates the political persuasion capabilities of seven frontier large language models (Claude Sonnet 4/4.5, GPT‑4.1/5, Gemini 2.5 Flash/3, and Grok 4) through two large‑scale survey experiments on minimum wage and immigration policies. With a total sample of 19,145 participants, the study finds that LLM‑generated messages induce greater attitude change than professionally produced campaign ads. Model performance varies: Claude models are most persuasive, while Grok is least. An information‑focused prompt boosts persuasion for Claude and Grok but sharply reduces it for GPT, highlighting model‑specific sensitivities. Conversation analysis reveals that the inclusion of call‑to‑action language is strongly associated with higher persuasion, whereas citing sources or numbers shows no clear benefit. The findings underscore the need for ongoing benchmarking of AI‑driven persuasion risks and suggest that LLM‑assisted content analysis can uncover effective persuasive strategies.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted two randomized survey experiments targeting U.S. adults recruited via Prolific. Participants were exposed to persuasive messages about either minimum wage or immigration policies, generated by one of seven frontier LLMs using standard and information‑focused prompts. Attitudes were measured on a 5‑point scale before and after exposure, enabling calculation of within‑subject attitude shifts. The experimental design also included a benchmark condition with real political campaign advertisements. To probe the drivers of persuasion, the researchers transcribed and coded the LLM conversations, examining linguistic and structural features such as call‑to‑action usage, source citation, and numerical claims. The large sample size (N=19,145) provided high statistical power to detect differences across models, prompts, and message characteristics.

## Key Results
- LLMs produced average attitude shifts of approximately 0.20 points on a 5‑point scale, significantly exceeding the ~0.14 shift from actual campaign ads.
- Model ranking: Claude (most persuasive) > GPT/Gemini > Grok (least persuasive).
- The information‑focused prompt increased persuasion for Claude and Grok but sharply decreased effectiveness for GPT.
- Conversations containing call‑to‑action messages were strongly associated with greater persuasion; explicit source citation and number usage showed no significant relationship.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not experimentally isolate the causal mechanisms behind LLM persuasion. However, correlational analysis suggests that interactive, action‑oriented language (call‑to‑action) may enhance persuasive impact, possibly by increasing user engagement or commitment. The model‑dependent effect of the information‑focused prompt indicates that underlying architectural differences or training objectives (e.g., alignment, safety fine‑tuning) could shape how models leverage factual appeals. The authors note that understanding *why* some models are more persuasive than others requires further investigation into their internal reasoning and output distribution characteristics.

## Foundational Learning
Though not elaborated in the paper, several concepts are foundational to understanding its findings:

**Concept 1: Political Persuasion**  
- *Why needed*: Central to the paper’s research question; defines the phenomenon being measured.  
- *Quick check*: persuasion involves a source (LLM), a message, and a receiver (user) leading to attitude/behavior change.

**Concept 2: Survey Experiments**  
- *Why needed*: The core methodology; enables causal inference about message effects.  
- *Quick check*: random assignment to message conditions with pre‑ and post‑measures of attitudes.

**Concept 3: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**  
- *Why needed*: Explains how frontier LLMs are aligned; may inadvertently enhance persuasive ability.  
- *Quick check*: RLHF trains a reward model on human preferences, then optimizes the LLM policy via PPO or similar.

**Concept 4: Call‑to‑Action (CTA)**  
- *Why needed*: Identified as a strong correlate of persuasion; a classic persuasive technique.  
- *Quick check*: CTA prompts the audience to take a concrete step (e.g., “Sign the petition”, “Donate now”).

## Architecture Onboarding
The evaluated models share a common transformer‑based architecture, though implementation details vary. For onboarding:

**Component Map**  
User Prompt -> Tokenizer -> Token Embeddings -> Positional Encoding -> Transformer Blocks (Self‑Attention + MLP) -> Final Layer Norm -> Output Logits -> Sampling (e.g., nucleus) -> Response.

**Critical Path**  
The sequential processing through each transformer layer for each token constitutes the critical path. Latency scales with model depth and per‑layer compute; parallelization across layers is not possible, making depth a primary determinant of inference speed.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Model size vs. inference cost: larger models are more capable but slower and more expensive.  
- Alignment vs. capability: safety fine‑tuning (e.g., RLHF) may reduce helpfulness or alter output style.  
- Context length vs. memory: longer contexts increase computational and memory demands quadratically for attention.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Hallucination: generation of factually incorrect statements.  
- Bias: unequal persuasion effects across demographic groups or stances.  
- Sycophancy: excessive agreement with the user to maintain engagement.  
- Refusal failures: unnecessary blocking of legitimate queries.

**First Experiments**  
1. Subgroup analysis: measure persuasion effects by participant demographics (age, education, political affiliation) to identify vulnerable populations.  
2. CTA causality test: randomly instruct models to include or omit explicit call‑to‑action language while holding content constant, then assess persuasion differences.  
3. Mechanistic probe: use activation patching or feature attribution to compare internal representations between high‑persuasion and low‑persuasion outputs across model families.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the persuasive risk of frontier LLMs compare specifically to benchmark human campaign advertisements across a broader range of policy domains and demographic groups?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper explicitly benchmarks LLMs against human ads for two issues but notes this comparison "warrants further study" and suggests externally valid generalizations require testing across more policies and populations.
- **Why unresolved:** The study was limited to two bipartisan issues (minimum wage, immigration) and a Prolific sample. Unknown if findings hold for other salient policies (e.g., climate, healthcare) or more representative voter demographics.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication experiments on diverse policy issues with nationally representative samples, directly comparing LLM persuasion to professionally produced human campaign ads in identical survey settings.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the causal mechanism behind the model‑dependent effect of information‑focused prompts on persuasiveness (e.g., why does it boost Claude/Grok but reduce GPT‑5’s effectiveness)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state "the effectiveness of information‑based prompts is model‑dependent" and that this interaction "deserves further attention," noting it contradicts prior work.
- **Why unresolved:** The study design only compares prompt conditions *between* models; it cannot identify whether the prompt changed model reasoning, output style, or user reception differently per model. The observed effect is correlational.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An experiment that randomly assigns the same conversation *content* (e.g., identical text generated by one model) to different model "personas" or fine‑tunes a single model to mimic the response distributions of others, then tests persuasion effects.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent do LLMs contribute to affective or ideological polarization through asymmetric persuasion (i.e., larger effects for pro‑Democratic vs. pro‑Republican arguments)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note "all models exhibit substantially larger effects when arguing in favor of the policy" (the Democratic position) and explicitly list "benchmarking against LLM polarization risk" as an open extension.
- **Why unresolved:** The study design could not randomize stance (pro/anti policy) independently of participant ideology, so the observed asymmetry conflates model behavior, participant susceptibility, and selection into stances. It remains unclear if LLMs are inherently more persuasive for liberal arguments or if conservative participants are harder to persuade.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A fully crossed experiment randomizing both participant baseline ideology and the assigned AI stance (pro/anti) on a neutral issue, measuring differential attitude change and content analysis to see if models generate different arguments by stance.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the identified association between "call‑to‑action" messaging and persuasion reflect a causal lever, or is it merely a correlate of other persuasive traits (e.g., increased interactivity or enthusiasm)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conversation analysis finds call‑to‑action "strongly associated with greater persuasion" but explicitly acknowledges these are "correlational relationships" and that "causal effects would require designs that randomly assign strategy use."
- **Why unresolved:** Strategy use was not randomized; more persuasive conversations may naturally incorporate more calls to action, or a third factor (e.g., model confidence) may drive both. The analysis cannot isolate the effect of the strategy itself.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An experiment where conversations are instrumented: randomly prompting models to include (or omit) specific call‑to‑action language while holding all other content constant, then measuring persuasion outcomes.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How stable are cross‑model persuasion rankings (Claude > GPT/Gemini > Grok) over time as models are updated, and what architectural or training differences explain persistent gaps?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state "continuous benchmarking will be necessary to track how AI‑driven political persuasion evolves" and note the "substantial leap" between prior models and the 2025 frontier models, but the underlying drivers of model heterogeneity remain unexplored.
- **Why unresolved:** The study is a snapshot of late 2025 models. Unknown whether observed ranking differences are temporary (prompt‑tuning artifacts) or stable (reflecting fundamental capability gaps in reasoning, alignment, or dialogue policy). No analysis links model internals (e.g., reinforcement learning setup, safety fine‑tuning) to persuasion metrics.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal benchmarking of updated model versions using identical protocols, combined with red‑teaming or mechanistic interpretability studies to identify which model components (e.g., chain‑of‑thought depth, refusal behavior) correlate with persuasion scores.

## Limitations
- External validity: Attitude shifts observed in controlled survey experiments may not translate to real‑world political behavior or voting decisions.
- Sample representativeness: Participants were recruited from Prolific, which may not reflect the demographic and ideological diversity of the broader electorate.
- Correlational message analysis: Observed associations between conversation features (e.g., call‑to‑action) and persuasion cannot establish causality due to lack of experimental manipulation.

## Confidence
- LLMs produce larger shifts than real campaign ads: **Medium**
- Persuasion varies significantly across frontier models: **High**
- Prompt effects are model‑specific: **Medium**
- Call‑to‑action association with persuasion: **High**

## Next Checks
1. Replicate with broader policy domains and real‑world ad datasets: Test if LLM persuasion superiority holds across diverse issues (e.g., climate, healthcare) using matched real campaign content.
2. Demographic stratum analysis: Report persuasion effects by participant political affiliation, age, education, and digital literacy to assess heterogeneity and targeting vulnerability.
3. A/B test message elements: Isolate call‑to‑action phrasing from content (e.g., identical arguments with/without CTA) in randomized conversations to establish causal contribution.
