# Voxtral Realtime
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11298
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11298
- Reference count: 33
- Primary result: Achieves a macro-average WER of 8.72% at 480 ms delay on FLEURS, competitive with offline Whisper (8.23%).

## Executive Summary
Voxtral Realtime tackles the latency‑accuracy trade‑off in streaming automatic speech recognition (ASR). Traditional methods that adapt offline models to streaming often suffer degraded accuracy at sub‑second latencies. This work introduces a natively streaming architecture with a causal audio encoder, a temporal adapter, and a transformer decoder conditioned on target delay via Adaptive RMS‑Norm. The model is trained end‑to‑end on a large multilingual (13‑language) dataset using frame‑synchronous targets and uniformly sampled delays. At a 480 ms delay it reaches 8.72% WER on FLEURS, compared to Whisper’s 8.23% at zero latency, and improves to 7.70% at 960 ms. The system also outperforms other open‑source streaming ASR models and can be served with low latency through vLLM enhancements.

## Method Summary
The architecture processes audio in a fully causal manner: a 50 Hz audio encoder (log‑Mel spectrogram input) extracts features, which are downsampled four‑fold by a temporal adapter to 12.5 Hz. A transformer decoder (initialized from Ministral 3B) is applied to this sequence. Delay conditioning is achieved by embedding the target delay (τ) and adding it via Adaptive RMS‑Norm in each transformer layer’s feed‑forward branch. Training uses frame‑synchronous tokenization, where each audio frame aligns with either a padding token [P] or a word‑boundary token [W]; subsequent subwords form the recognized word. Two‑phase training first warms up the encoder (5% of steps, LR 4e‑4) then trains end‑to‑end (95%, LR 6e‑5) with a z‑loss penalty on the logits to stabilize norms. During training, the delay τ is uniformly sampled between 80 ms and 2400 ms. At inference, the model accepts audio in 80 ms chunks, left‑pads the sequence with 32 frames of silence and corresponding [P] tokens, and the delay is specified as a multiple of 80 ms.

## Key Results
- FLEURS macro‑average WER: 8.72% at 480 ms delay, 7.70% at 960 ms.
- Matches or exceeds offline Whisper’s accuracy while operating in real‑time (sub‑second).
- Outperforms other open‑source streaming ASR models on English and multilingual benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The native streaming design avoids the accuracy drop seen in cascaded or adapted systems. Causality guarantees low inference latency; the adapter balances temporal resolution and computational cost. Adaptive RMS‑Norm injects delay information without altering the attention mechanism, letting the decoder adjust its prediction horizon. Frame‑synchronous training aligns textual tokens with audio frames, enabling precise timing and natural integration of word boundaries. Delay sampling exposes the model to a wide range of latency constraints, improving generalization. Left‑padding introduces stable “sink” tokens that promote consistent attention patterns (attention sink effect). The z‑loss penalty controls logit magnitudes, preventing the audio‑text embedding norm imbalance that could otherwise cause degenerate outputs. Together, these elements yield high accuracy at very low delays.

## Foundational Learning
- **Streaming ASR latency‑accuracy trade‑off**: In streaming, each output token must be produced before the full audio is seen, creating a direct link between delay and contextual availability. Low delays limit future context, increasing error rates. *Why needed*: Understanding this trade‑off frames the contribution. *Quick check*: Compare WER of an offline model at varying input lengths; observe performance degradation as context shrinks.
- **Causal modeling**: All operations (e.g., convolutions, attention) must respect temporal order, only using past and present information. *Why needed*: Guarantees real‑time operation. *Quick check*: Implement a causal convolution and verify its output at time t does not depend on future samples.
- **Frame‑synchronous tokenization**: Text is aligned to audio frames using [P] for silence and [W] for word onsets; subwords follow. *Why needed*: Enables training with exact frame‑level targets and natural word segmentation. *Quick check*: Create a dummy audio‑text pair and manually assign frame‑level tokens.
- **Adaptive RMS‑Norm for conditioning**: Normalization statistics are scaled by a delay‑dependent vector `g(τ)`. *Why needed*: Provides a lightweight way to modulate activations based on τ without adding attention‑level complexity. *Quick check*: Inspect norm outputs for two different τ values and verify they differ.
- **Delay sampling**: During training, τ is drawn uniformly from a range. *Why needed*: Models multiple latency points with a single model. *Quick check*: Train a small model with fixed τ versus random τ and evaluate across τ generalization.
- **Z‑loss regularization**: Penalty on squared logit norms. *Why needed*: Stabilizes training, preventing audio‑text embedding magnitude mismatch. *Quick check*: Monitor logit norm variance with/without z‑loss.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Audio Input -> Log‑Mel Spectrogram -> Causal Audio Encoder -> Temporal Adapter -> Transformer Decoder (with Ada RMS‑Norm) -> Token Stream.
**Critical path**: The audio flows through the encoder and adapter to produce a sequence of hidden states H. Each transformer layer normalizes H using Ada RMS‑Norm (conditioned on τ), then applies self‑attention and feed‑forward. The decoder autoregressively generates tokens using cross‑attention to H.
**Design tradeoffs**:
- Causal encoder vs non‑causal: Causality imposes latency but ensures real‑time; using non‑causal would break streaming.
- Downsampling factor 4 reduces sequence length (computational savings) but discards fine‑grained timing; larger factor may harm accuracy at low delays.
- Conditioning in feed‑forward branch (not attention) simplifies training and avoids modifying attention matrices, at the cost of potentially less direct influence on content selection.
- Left‑padding adds upfront latency (2560 ms of silence) but improves stability; too much padding increases response time.
**Failure signatures**:
- High latency: audio chunks not processed within 80 ms; likely resumable request misconfiguration.
- Degraded accuracy: mismatch in Mel preprocessing, missing left‑padding, incorrect τ setting.
- Repetitive or audio‑ignorant outputs: z‑loss missing or insufficient, leading to decoder collapse.
**First three experiments**:
1. Run the model on a known 10‑second audio clip at 480 ms delay; compare WER against the reported 8.72% on FLEURS to verify base functionality.
2. Ablate left‑padding by removing the 32‑frame silence; measure WER change to quantify its impact.
3. Sweep τ across 240–2000 ms on a held‑out set; plot WER vs delay to confirm expected trend and check that the model generalizes across the sampling range.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What is the mechanism behind left‑padding’s accuracy gains, and does it relate to attention sinks?
2. Does conditioning the decoder’s attention branch with delay information improve streaming accuracy at low latencies?
3. Is uniform delay sampling during training optimal for the full latency range, or could biased sampling improve low‑latency performance?
4. Why is encoder warm‑up required for stable training, and can it be avoided with better initialization?

## Limitations
- Training dataset composition (exact sources, language proportions, preprocessing) is not disclosed, limiting assessment of coverage and biases.
- Hyperparameter details (z‑loss coefficient/schedule, Ada RMS‑Norm MLP configuration) are omitted, hindering full reproducibility.
- vLLM real‑time serving contributions (paged attention details, WebSocket API usage, exact flags) lack documentation and may evolve.
- Uniform delay sampling may underrepresent low‑delay regimes, possibly affecting sub‑second performance.
