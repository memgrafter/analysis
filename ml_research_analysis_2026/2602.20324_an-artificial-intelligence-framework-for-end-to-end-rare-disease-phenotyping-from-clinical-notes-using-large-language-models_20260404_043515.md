# An artificial intelligence framework for end-to-end rare disease phenotyping from clinical notes using large language models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20324
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20324
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** RARE-PHENIX achieved a higher ontology-based similarity score (0.70) than the PhenoBERT baseline (0.58) on external validation of 16,357 clinical notes.

## Executive Summary
RARE-PHENIX is an end-to-end AI framework designed to automate rare disease phenotyping from unstructured clinical notes. It addresses the scalability limitations of manual curation by implementing a three-stage pipeline: extraction of phenotype mentions using large language models (LLMs), standardization of these mentions to Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms via retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and prioritization of diagnostically informative terms using a supervised ranking model. External validation on a held-out cohort demonstrated superior performance over a state-of-the-art baseline, with ablation studies confirming that each module contributed incremental gains. The work highlights the value of modeling the complete clinical phenotyping workflow rather than isolated components.

## Method Summary
The framework processes clinical note chunks through three sequential modules. First, an LLM-based extractor (either a LLaMA model fine-tuned via QLoRA or a few-shot prompted ChatGPT-4o) identifies phenotype spans from text. Second, a RAG system retrieves the top-10 semantically similar HPO terms for each extracted string using a vector store of HPO embeddings and employs an LLM to select the most accurate match. Third, an XGBoost pairwise learning-to-rank model prioritizes the standardized HPO terms based on features like information content and gene/disease associations. The system is trained on a combination of expert-annotated rare disease documents and synthetic notes derived from Undiagnosed Diseases Network (UDN) HPO terms, with external validation performed on notes from a separate site (VUMC).

## Key Results
- RARE-PHENIX significantly outperformed the PhenoBERT baseline on external validation, achieving an ontology-based Lin similarity score of 0.70 versus 0.58.
- Ablation analyses demonstrated that each module (extraction, standardization, prioritization) provided measurable performance improvements, validating the end-to-end design.
- The full pipeline improved precision in top-k ranked HPO lists, aiding the identification of diagnostically relevant phenotypes for rare disease cases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** LLM-based extraction identifies rare disease phenotypes from unstructured clinical text with sufficient recall to capture most clinician-curated terms.
- **Mechanism:** Instruction fine-tuning (QLoRA on LLaMA variants) or few-shot prompting (ChatGPT-4o) maps raw clinical narratives to annotated phenotype spans, using HTML tags to delineate entities. Training corpora (RareDis expert annotations + synthetic UDN notes) teach domain-specific patterns.
- **Core assumption:** Clinically relevant phenotypes are expressed in the text with discernible linguistic patterns learnable by LLMs.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "extracting phenotypes with LLMs"
  - [section 2.3] Details fine-tuning with QLoRA on LLaMA and few-shot prompting with ChatGPT-4o; uses 2,671 synthetic UDN documents.
  - [corpus] Neighbors show LLMs applied to phenotyping (e.g., "Large Language Models with Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Zero-Shot Disease Phenotyping"), but direct validation for rare diseases is paper-specific.
- **Break condition:** If clinical notes use extreme linguistic variation or neologisms absent from training corpora, extraction recall drops (evidenced by systematic error analysis: 97% of false negatives were due to indirect expression, not extraction failure).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) standardizes extracted phenotypes to accurate HPO terms, improving precision and F1 by reducing non-specific or incorrect mappings.
- **Mechanism:** Semantic retrieval (cosine similarity on HPO embeddings) fetches top-10 candidate HPO terms, then an LLM selects the best match based on definitional context, outputting "none" if no suitable term exists.
- **Core assumption:** Ontologically valid mappings exist within the top-N semantically similar HPO terms for a given phenotype string.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "standardizing them to HPO terms via retrieval-augmented generation"
  - [section 2.4] Describes vector store construction with `text-embedding-3-small` and LLM selection.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "LLM-Augmented Computational Phenotyping" and "Hybrid Framework with Large Language Models for Rare Disease Phenotyping" use RAG for phenotyping, but implementation details vary.
- **Break condition:** If phenotype descriptions are vague or ambiguous (e.g., "cardiac issue"), retrieval may not contain the precise HPO term, leading to "none" or incorrect parent-term selection (error analysis cites ontology granularity differences as a false positive source).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A supervised learning-to-rank model prioritizes diagnostically informative HPO terms (e.g., specific, rare, low gene association) over common/non-specific ones, increasing precision in top-k lists.
- **Mechanism:** XGBoost pairwise ranking trained on UDN patient data; positive labels = clinician-curated HPO terms; negative labels = mixture of easy/medium/difficult/implausible HPO negatives sampled from ontology structure. Features include information content, gene/disease association counts, IDF metrics.
- **Core assumption:** Clinician-curated HPO terms are more diagnostically informative than non-curated terms, and this informativeness is captured by knowledgebase-derived features (gene/disease counts, information content).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "prioritizing diagnostically informative phenotypes using a supervised ranking model"
  - [section 2.5] Details negative sampling strategy and feature engineering (OMIM/Orphanet annotations); XGBoost selected (MAP@30=0.85).
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Survey and Improvement Strategies for Gene Prioritization with Large Language Models" address ranking, but not specifically for HPO term prioritization from extraction.
- **Break condition:** If clinician curation itself is biased toward certain phenotype types (e.g., omitting common symptoms), the ranking model may under-prioritize clinically relevant but common terms (error analysis notes common symptoms like "nausea" were false positives because not curated).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**
  - **Why needed here:** Mitigates LLM hallucination of invalid HPO terms and grounds standardization in the official ontology structure.
  - **Quick check question:** How does providing retrieved HPO candidates with definitions to the LLM during generation reduce invalid term output compared to asking the LLM to generate a term from scratch?
- **Concept: Learning-to-Rank (Pairwise)**
  - **Why needed here:** Phenotypes must be ordered by diagnostic utility, not just extracted; the model learns relative ordering (clinician-curated > non-curated) rather than absolute classification.
  - **Quick check question:** Why is pairwise ranking (comparing pairs of HPO terms) more suitable than pointwise scoring for this prioritization task?
- **Concept: Ontology-Based Semantic Similarity (Lin measure)**
  - **Why needed here:** Primary evaluation metric; quantifies how close the predicted HPO set is to the clinician-curated set, accounting for HPO's hierarchical structure (shared ancestor specificity).
  - **Quick check question:** Why can't simple precision/recall against exact HPO matches fully capture "concordance" when clinicians might choose a sibling term in the ontology?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Extractor -> Standardizer -> Prioritizer
- **Critical path:** Extraction quality sets an upper bound; without sufficient recall in Module 1, downstream modules cannot recover missing terms. Standardization's precision directly impacts the noise fed to the ranker. The ranker's feature engineering (gene/disease counts) is static and does not depend on note content.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Extractor:** Fine-tuned open-source (LLaMA) vs. few-shot closed-source (ChatGPT-4o). Tradeoff: deployment control/cost vs. performance and ease of use.
  - **RAG:** Vector store size vs. retrieval speed. Using `text-embedding-3-small` balances quality and efficiency; top-10 retrieval is a fixed cost.
  - **Ranker:** Feature complexity vs. generalizability. Using OMIM/Orphanet static features avoids per-note computation but may become outdated as knowledgebases update.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Module 1:** Low recall across all notes → check training data diversity (synthetic notes may not cover all real-world expressions).
  - **Module 2:** Many "none" outputs or parent-term substitutions → review top-10 retrieval quality; increase N or adjust embedding model.
  - **Module 3:** No improvement over random ordering → verify negative sampling strategy; check if knowledgebase features distinguish curated vs. non-curated terms in training data.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Extraction sanity check:** Run Module 1 on 10 manually reviewed notes. Verify that clearly stated phenotypes (e.g., "microcephaly") are extracted. If not, inspect prompt/fine-tuning data.
  2. **RAG retrieval validation:** For 50 extracted phenotype strings, manually check if the correct HPO term is in the top-10 retrieved candidates. If hit rate <80%, consider hybrid retrieval (e.g., BM25 + vector) or synonym expansion.
  3. **Ranking baseline test:** Compare XGBoost's MAP@30 on validation set against a random ordering baseline and an IDF-only scorer. If gains are minimal, revisit feature engineering (add phenotype prevalence from UDN training data?).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- To what extent does modeling the full clinical phenotyping workflow (extraction, standardization, prioritization) improve diagnostic utility compared to optimizing only isolated components, and which module contributes most to real-world decision-making?
- How well does RARE-PHENIX generalize to rare disease populations beyond the complex, multi-system cases enriched in the Undiagnosed Diseases Network (UDN) cohort?
- What is the impact of using clinician-curated HPO terms as an incomplete and variable ground truth on the measured performance and perceived limitations of RARE-PHENIX?
- Can the prioritization module’s learned ranking of diagnostically informative phenotypes be effectively interpreted and validated by clinicians as being aligned with clinical reasoning for narrowing a differential diagnosis?
- What are the most efficient and practical deployment strategies for RARE-PHENIX in resource-constrained clinical environments, given that top performance relies on large LLMs (70B parameters)?

## Limitations
- External validation gold standard (VUMC patient-level HPO mappings) not publicly shared, hindering independent replication.
- Critical hyperparameters undisclosed: RAG LLM prompt/temperature, negative sampling ratios for ranking model.
- Potential data leakage in synthetic note generation or patient splits not ruled out.

## Confidence
- **Architecture design (3-module pipeline):** High. Components clearly specified, code public.
- **External validation superiority (Lin score 0.70 vs 0.58):** Medium. Cohort described but exact inputs/gold standard inaccessible.
- **Ablation gains (each module contribution):** Low. Unknown if training data contamination or sampling biases explain incremental improvements.
