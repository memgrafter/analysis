# HeatPrompt: Zero-Shot Vision-Language Modeling of Urban Heat Demand from Satellite Images

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.20066  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20066  
- **Reference count**: 37  
- **Primary result**: HeatPrompt improves heat demand prediction R² by 93.7% (from 0.32 to 0.62) and reduces MAE by 30% compared to the baseline simulation model.

## Executive Summary
Municipalities often lack detailed building-level data needed for effective heat demand mapping, which hampers decarbonization planning. HeatPrompt addresses this by using a zero-shot vision-language framework: satellite images overlaid with region masks are fed to a pretrained VLM (e.g., GPT-4o) via an “energy planner” prompt to extract semantic attributes like roof age and density. These captions are embedded (Nomic Embed) and combined with basic GIS and building features in a simple MLP regressor. The method achieves a large performance gain (R² 0.62 vs. 0.32) while remaining interpretable and data-scarce, offering a practical solution for urban heat planning.

## Method Summary
The pipeline starts by acquiring satellite imagery (Esri World Imagery) and rasterizing heat demand isoline masks to create region-specific RGBA composites. Each composite is sent to a pretrained vision-language model with a fixed prompt that instructs the model to act as an energy planner. The VLM returns a free-form caption describing relevant urban features (e.g., building materials, vegetation, density). A text encoder transforms the caption into a fixed-length semantic vector (512-d). This vector is concatenated with deterministic GIS metrics (area, perimeter) and aggregated OpenStreetMap/LOD2 building-type ratios and heights. A regularized MLP (two hidden layers, dropout 0.2) is trained to predict heat demand, using stratified 5-fold cross-validation and early stopping. No task-specific fine-tuning or pixel-level labels are required.

## Key Results
- **R² uplift**: from 0.32 (baseline simulation) to 0.62 (HeatPrompt) – a 93.7% relative improvement.  
- **MAE reduction**: approximately 30% lower than the baseline.  
- **Interpretability**: VLM-generated tokens (e.g., “old roof”, “minimal vegetation”) align with high-demand zones, providing human-readable explanations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: VLM Captures Energy‑Relevant Semantics
- **Claim**: A pretrained VLM, when prompted as an “energy planner,” can generate captions containing latent visual cues correlated with heat demand.
- **Mechanism**: The VLM processes the composite image+mask; its joint vision-language representation activates descriptors that, though not explicitly trained for energy modeling, align with thermal properties (e.g., aged roofs → higher demand).
- **Core assumption**: The VLM’s pretraining corpus contains sufficient co‑occurrence patterns between visual urban elements and concepts that proxy for heat loss/gain.
- **Evidence**: Section III.B describes the prompt‑driven caption extraction; Table II shows performance gains across VLM backbones.  
- **Break condition**: Captions lack consistency in energy‑relevant features across similar‑demand regions, or ablation (removing VLM features) collapses performance to baseline.

### Mechanism 2: Semantic Fusion Enhances Regression
- **Claim**: The semantic embeddings from VLM captions complement basic GIS and building composition features, significantly improving demand regression.
- **Mechanism**: The MLP learns to weight the high‑dimensional semantic vector (z_sem) alongside structured features; z_sem encodes implicit factors (e.g., visual “agedness”) not fully captured by simple metrics.
- **Core assumption**: The VLM’s latent space organizes urban concepts in a way that linearly separable features for heat demand exist.
- **Evidence**: Abstract and Section IV.B report R²/MAE gains; Section IV.C notes that high‑demand areas show lower vegetation, fewer solar panels, higher density – all VLM‑inferred.  
- **Break condition**: An MLP on structured features alone performs no worse (p≥0.05) than one with semantic features in cross‑validation.

### Mechanism 3: Zero‑Shot Deployment in Data‑Scarce Contexts
- **Claim**: The zero‑shot VLM approach eliminates the need for costly energy audits or task‑specific training labels.
- **Mechanism**: Off‑the‑shelf foundation VLMs supply feature engineering automatically; only basic GIS masks are needed.
- **Core assumption**: The study region’s imagery distribution lies within the VLM’s pretraining data manifold.
- **Evidence**: Abstract calls it a “lightweight, data‑scarce solution”; Section I highlights elimination of manual labeling.  
- **Break condition**: Performance degrades severely in regions with visually distinct urban typologies not represented in pretraining.

## Foundational Learning
- **Vision‑Language Models (VLMs)** – Why needed: They convert raw satellite pixels into semantically meaningful captions that serve as input features. Quick check: Changing the prompt to “describe the image generally” should reduce overlap with energy‑related terms.  
- **Semantic Embeddings** – Why needed: Captions must be turned into numeric vectors (z_sem) that retain energy‑relevant information; the choice of text encoder (Nomic Embed, 512‑d) influences what the MLP can learn. Quick check: Would CLIP’s text encoder yield similar gains, or does a generative VLM’s caption diversity help?  
- **Stratified Cross‑Validation for Spatial Data** – Why needed: Heat demand is spatially autocorrelated and often skewed; stratification by demand quintiles ensures each fold reflects the distribution and prevents leakage. Quick check: A random split would likely increase R² variance across folds.  
- **GIS Rasterization and Masking** – Why needed: Precise alignment of vector isolines to the satellite pixel grid is essential to isolate the region of interest for the VLM; misalignment propagates errors. Quick check: For irregular isolines, the 5% margin (si = 1.05·max(width,height)) might include extraneous context, altering caption content.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data loader → VLM inference module → Text embedding module → Feature fusion → MLP regressor → Evaluation  
**Critical path**: Satellite image + mask → VLM caption → Text embedding → Concatenation with structured features → MLP prediction. Any break in caption quality or embedding relevance fails the pipeline.  
**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Interpretability vs. automation: Captions provide explanations but VLM reasoning is opaque.  
- VLM choice: Proprietary (GPT‑4o) vs. open (Qwen2.5‑VL) involves cost, reproducibility, and fine‑tuning potential.  
- Feature dimensionality: High‑d semantic vector (512) risks overfitting with N=1,677; dropout and weight decay are essential.  
**Failure signatures**:  
- High training R² but low cross‑validation R² (<0.4) → overfitting.  
- Inconsistent caption terms across similar‑demand zones → unstable semantic extraction.  
- High MAE on low‑demand regions → bias toward outliers.  
**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Baseline replication: Train MLP on only GIS + building composition to reproduce Table I’s ~R² 0.51.  
2. Ablation study: Add VLM embeddings one by one (GPT‑4o, CLIP, ResNet50) to confirm textual embeddings outperform CNN latents.  
3. Qualitative audit: Manually inspect 50 captions from low/medium/high demand terciles; verify high‑demand captions consistently mention “old roof,” “minimal vegetation,” “dense packing.”

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Q1**: How can VLMs be specialized for urban energy semantics without extensive fine‑tuning? The paper suggests future work on energy‑aware embeddings and lightweight tuning.  
- **Q2**: To what extent do learned semantic features generalize across geographies? The study is limited to Rheinland‑Pfalz, Germany; cross‑regional robustness remains unknown.  
- **Q3**: What is the optimal VLM prompt and model selection for extracting energy‑relevant attributes? The current work uses a single prompt; systematic ablation is needed.  
- **Q4**: Would multi‑spectral or thermal satellite data improve estimation? The pipeline currently uses RGB only.

## Limitations
- **Geographic specificity**: Validation confined to one German state; performance may drop in areas with different architectural styles or climates.  
- **Prompt engineering dependency**: The “energy planner” prompt’s design is critical and not systematically evaluated.  
- **Caption consistency & hallucination**: Free‑form VLM outputs introduce variability and potential hallucination, with no quantified consistency metric.

## Confidence
- **VLM extracts energy‑relevant semantics**: Medium (supported by qualitative alignment and gains, but lacks large‑scale human validation).  
- **Semantic fusion improves regression**: High (quantitative results robust across VLM backbones and validated with CV and statistical tests).  
- **Zero‑shot deployability in new regions**: Medium (promising in theory but only one region tested).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑region test**: Apply the trained model (GPT‑4o captions) to a distinct urban area (e.g., Mediterranean city) and measure R²/MAE drop versus local retraining.  
2. **Prompt sensitivity ablation**: Run the pipeline with three prompt variants (“Describe this image,” “List urban features,” and the original) and compute Jensen‑Shannon divergence between caption token distributions and correlated R² changes.  
3. **Caption consistency audit**: For 100 pairs of visually similar high‑demand buildings (SSIM >0.9), calculate ROUGE‑L token overlap; low average (<0.4) would indicate unstable extraction.
