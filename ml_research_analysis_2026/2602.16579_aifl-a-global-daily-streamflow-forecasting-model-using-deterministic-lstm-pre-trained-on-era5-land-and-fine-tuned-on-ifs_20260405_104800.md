# AIFL: A Global Daily Streamflow Forecasting Model Using Deterministic LSTM Pre-trained on ERA5-Land and Fine-tuned on IFS
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16579
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16579
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Achieves median modified Kling-Gupta Efficiency (KGE') of 0.66 and median Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) of 0.53 across 2,003 basins in the 2021–2024 test set; perfect precision (1.0) in flood event detection (1.5–50 year return periods).

## Executive Summary
AIFL is a global daily streamflow forecasting model that addresses the performance drop observed when transitioning from historical reanalysis to operational numerical weather prediction (NWP) forecasts. It employs a deterministic LSTM trained end-to-end on 18,588 basins using a two-stage strategy: first pre-trained on 40 years of ERA5-Land reanalysis to learn robust hydrological processes, then fine-tuned on operational Integrated Forecasting System (IFS) control forecasts to adapt to forecast-specific biases. On an independent 2021–2024 test set covering 2,003 basins, AIFL attains a median KGE' of 0.66 and median NSE of 0.53. The model exhibits perfect precision in detecting flood events of 1.5–50 year return periods, with conservative recall, and matches or exceeds the benchmark Google global model at 42.9% of shared evaluation stations. The work demonstrates that targeted fine-tuning can effectively bridge the domain shift between reanalysis and operational NWP, enabling skillful global streamflow prediction.

## Method Summary
AIFL uses specific discharge (mm d⁻¹) as the target variable, normalized by basin area, to align with precipitation forcing units and simplify the learning task. Inputs comprise five dynamic meteorological variables (surface solar radiation, surface thermal radiation, surface pressure, 2m air temperature, total precipitation) from ERA5-Land during pre-training and IFS control forecasts during fine-tuning and testing; and 203 static catchment attributes (physiography, soil, land cover, etc.) from HydroATLAS and CARAVAN. The architecture consists of separate three-layer MLP embedding networks for static and dynamic features, a single-layer LSTM with 1024 hidden units that fuses the embeddings, and a linear output head that predicts specific discharge over a 10‑day horizon given a 180‑day input window. Training proceeds in two stages: (1) pre‑training on ERA5‑Land sequences (1980–2019) from all 18,588 basins using Adam (learning rate 4e‑4) and a per‑basin variance‑normalized mean squared error loss, with cosine annealing and gradient clipping; (2) fine‑tuning on IFS control forecasts (2016–2019) from a subset of 10,149 basins (those overlapping with operational records) using Adam (learning rate 1e‑4) for 30 epochs, with all weights unfrozen. The model is evaluated on a temporal hold‑out (2021–2024) using IFS LT1 forcings at 2,003 basins, assessing KGE', NSE, and flood detection skill against observed discharge.

## Key Results
- Median KGE' = 0.66, median NSE = 0.53 across 2,003 basins in 2021–2024.
- Perfect precision (1.0) for flood events of 1.5–50 year return periods; recall remains conservative.
- Matches or surpasses the Google global model at 42.9% of shared stations; notably underperforms in arid/semi‑arid and large (>10,000 km²) basins.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Two‑stage training bridges the reanalysis‑to‑forecast domain shift
Pre‑training on 40 years of physically consistent ERA5-Land data allows the LSTM to internalize universal hydrological rainfall‑runoff relationships. Fine‑tuning on shorter‑term IFS forecasts then adapts model parameters to the specific statistical biases (e.g., precipitation wet bias) and error structures present in operational NWP streams, without discarding the core physical dynamics. This transfer learning approach resolves the distribution shift between historical reanalysis and real‑time forecasts.

Evidence: [abstract] and [section 3.2] explicitly describe this strategy; neighboring work (e.g., domain adaptation in forecasting) confirms the problem and approach.

Break condition: If the discrepancy between reanalysis and IFS is non‑stationary or irreducible (e.g., due to model physics updates), fine‑tuning may fail or overwrite essential priors.

### Mechanism 2: Specific discharge target simplifies the regression task
By normalizing discharge by basin area, the target variable shares units (mm d⁻¹) with precipitation inputs. This removes the need for the network to learn the linear area scaling factor implicitly, letting it focus on modeling complex, nonlinear rainfall‑runoff transformations. Basin area is still provided as a static attribute to capture linear effects.

Evidence: [section 2.1] and [section 2.3] discuss this design choice and inclusion of area as a static feature.

Break condition: If basin area exerts significant nonlinear influences (e.g., threshold effects in infiltration or channel routing), the specific discharge target may obscure those relationships, requiring the model to infer them solely from static attributes, potentially limiting performance in basins with extreme sizes.

### Mechanism 3: Separate MLP embeddings integrate heterogeneous features
Static attributes (203 features) are embedded via a three‑layer MLP into a fixed 64‑dimensional vector, while dynamic forcings at each timestep undergo an identical MLP embedding. These embeddings are concatenated and fed to the LSTM, which jointly considers the basin’s identity and the current meteorological state. This structure keeps the high‑dimensional input manageable and allows the recurrent core to modulate static characteristics with dynamic information at every step.

Evidence: [section 3.1] describes the architecture; similar patterns appear in neighboring models (e.g., Hydra‑LSTM, CNN‑LSTM).

Break condition: If critical hydrological processes rely on spatial heterogeneity within the basin (e.g., orographic precipitation gradients, sub‑basin soil moisture patterns) that are not captured by basin‑averaged inputs, the lumped representation becomes a fundamental limitation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Normalized loss function (MSE):** Multi‑basin training uses a variance‑scaled MSE to prevent large, high‑flow basins from dominating the loss gradient. Without normalization, small basins would be systematically under‑fit. Quick check: Removing per‑basin variance normalization would bias the model toward large basins, widening the performance gap between small and large catchments.
- **Domain adaptation via fine‑tuning:** The reanalysis‑to‑forecast distribution shift is addressed by first learning on abundant reanalysis data, then adapting the model to the target forecast domain using a smaller set. Quick check: Why not train exclusively on the IFS period? Because the reanalysis record is much longer (40 years vs. 4) and provides a richer basis for learning robust hydrological dynamics; fine‑tuning then specialises the model without sacrificing generalisation.
- **Specific discharge as target:** Aligning units with precipitation simplifies the regression problem and avoids the need for the network to learn a linear scaling by basin area. Quick check: Switching back to raw discharge would require either (a) scaling precipitation inputs by basin area, or (b) adding an explicit multiplicative area factor in the output layer to preserve the same learning efficiency.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Input Pipeline (dynamic forcings, static attributes) -> Separate MLP embedding networks (static & dynamic) -> Concatenation -> LSTM Core -> Output Head (linear projection) -> Training Controller (manages pre‑training and fine‑tuning stages)

### Critical Path
1. **Data curation:** Deduplicate basins (polygon overlap ≥0.7 & KGE≥0.95) and apply quality control (flatline ratio), yielding 18,588 basins. This step prevents spatial leakage.
2. **Pre‑training:** Train on 1980–2019 ERA5‑Land sequences; this is where the model acquires core hydrological dynamics.
3. **Fine‑tuning:** Train on 2016–2019 IFS control forecasts; this adapts the model to operational forecast biases.
4. **Temporal evaluation:** Use IFS LT1 forcings on held‑out 2021–2024 to assess generalisation to future forecasts.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Simplicity vs. fidelity:** A single‑layer, lumped LSTM is chosen over spatially explicit or hybrid physics‑based architectures, sacrificing sub‑basin heterogeneity for global scalability, computational efficiency, and a transparent baseline.
- **Deterministic vs. probabilistic:** Use of deterministic MSE loss simplifies training and inference but provides no inherent uncertainty estimates.
- **Two‑stage vs. single‑stage:** The added complexity of two‑stage training is necessary to handle the reanalysis‑forecast domain shift; a single‑stage model trained only on IFS would have less data and likely worse skill.

### Failure Signatures
- **Spatial:** Low KGE' (<0) in arid/semi‑arid regions and highly intermittent streams (Section 4.1).
- **Temporal/event:** Low recall (<0.5) for rare floods despite perfect precision (Section 4.2), indicating over‑conservatism.
- **Basin scale:** Relative underperformance in very large basins (>10,000 km²) compared to the Google model (Section 4.3), pointing to limitations of lumped structure for distributed routing.
- **Fine‑tuning:** Performance degradation in ~22.7% of basins after fine‑tuning (Section 3.2), possibly due to overfitting or catastrophic forgetting.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Sanity check on a single basin:** Train a minimal model (e.g., 64‑unit LSTM) on a well‑gauged basin with ERA5‑Land. Verify it learns the hydrograph. Then force it with IFS LT1 data without fine‑tuning and quantify the performance drop to isolate the domain shift effect.
2. **Ablation of two‑stage strategy:** Train an identical model only on the IFS 2016–2019 period (no pre‑training). Compare its 2021–2024 test performance against the published AIFL results to quantify the contribution of pre‑training.
3. **Embedding capacity test:** Train models with different LSTM hidden sizes (e.g., 256 vs. 1024 units) and evaluate both spatial generalisation and temporal skill to justify the choice of 1024 units for global deployment.

## Limitations
- **Data/preprocessing opacity:** Exact list of static attributes, their imputation/scaling, and the criteria for selecting the 10,149 fine‑tuning basins are unspecified, hampering full reproduction.
- **Performance boundaries:** The model underperforms in arid/semi‑arid and large catchments, exposing limitations of the lumped architecture and specific discharge target for such regimes.
- **Fine‑tuning instability:** A non‑trivial fraction (~22.7%) of basins experience skill degradation after fine‑tuning, indicating risk of overfitting to the short IFS record or catastrophic forgetting of robust reanalysis‑learned dynamics.
- **Extreme event handling:** Perfect precision but low recall for rare floods suggests over‑conservatism inherent to the MSE loss, limiting the model’s utility for flood early warning.

## Confidence
- **High confidence:** The two‑stage training strategy effectively bridges the reanalysis‑to‑forecast domain shift. Supported by explicit ablation rationale, neighboring literature, and the presented performance gains.
- **Medium confidence:** Predicting specific discharge simplifies learning by aligning units with precipitation. The logic is sound and supported by design rationale, though the potential break condition (non‑linear area effects) is not empirically validated.
- **Medium confidence:** The separate MLP embedding plus shared LSTM architecture sufficiently integrates static and dynamic features for global application. This is a standard, interpretable design, but its limitation for spatially heterogeneous basins (noted in failure signatures) suggests it may not be universally sufficient.

## Next Checks
1. **Spatial leakage audit:** Re‑run the basin deduplication using the exact criteria (polygon overlap ≥0.7 & KGE≥0.95) on the full CARAVAN/GRDC dataset. Quantify removed basins and inspect nested pairs in the final test set to confirm no spatial autocorrelation inflates metrics.
2. **Ablation of two‑stage strategy:** Train a model identical to AIFL but only on the IFS 2016–2019 fine‑tuning period (no pre‑training). Directly compare its 2021–2024 temporal test KGE'/NSE distribution against the published AIFL results to isolate the contribution of the pre‑training phase.
3. **Static attribute ablation in arid basins:** For a held‑out set of arid/semi‑arid basins, systematically remove static attribute groups (e.g., soil, land cover, topography) and measure the drop in KGE'. This tests whether the poor performance stems from missing/less‑informative static features versus an inherent limitation of the lumped architecture.
