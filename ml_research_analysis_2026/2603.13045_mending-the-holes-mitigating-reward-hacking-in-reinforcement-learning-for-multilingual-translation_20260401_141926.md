# Mending the Holes: Mitigating Reward Hacking in Reinforcement Learning for Multilingual Translation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13045
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13045
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** WALAR's composite reward yields large improvements in multilingual translation quality on low-resource directions (average xCOMET* +6.37) and near-perfect language consistency.

## Executive Summary
Multilingual translation via reinforcement learning (RL) often relies on source-based quality estimation (QE) models as reward signals, but these models contain failure modes ("holes") that allow reward hacking, such as generating wrong-language output, over-/under-translation, and self-repetition. To address this, the paper proposes WALAR, a composite reward that combines a base QE score (MetricX), a word alignment score (BGE-M3 F1) to penalize coverage errors, and a language alignment score (GlotLID with MaskLID) to enforce correct target language generation, all integrated within the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) framework. Training LLaMAX3-8B-Alpaca on monolingual data for 22 source languages and 1,016 directions yields large improvements on Flores-101 across 1,414 directions, with average xCOMET* rising from 64.97 to 71.34 (+6.37), especially on low-resource languages, and near-perfect language consistency. Human evaluations show win rates of 42% and 51% against the baseline on Azerbaijani-Portuguese and English-Kannada pairs, respectively, and the approach generalizes to unseen target languages.

## Method Summary
WALAR post-trains a multilingual LLM (LLaMAX3-8B-Alpaca) using reinforcement learning on monolingual source sentences only. The reward function combats known QE failure modes by integrating three signals: a quality estimation score from MetricX; a word alignment F1 score derived from BGE-M3 embeddings to ensure coverage; and a language alignment check using GlotLID (with MaskLID to handle code-switching) that enforces generation in the correct target language. The training employs the GRPO algorithm with a group-relative advantage and a KL penalty to maintain stability, using a batch of 1024 sentences, 8 rollouts per batch, and hyperparameters ϵ=0.2, β=0.01, and alignment weight α=20. The model is evaluated on the Flores-101 test suite across 1,414 translation directions using a suite of automatic metrics and language consistency rate.

## Key Results
- WALAR improves average xCOMET* from 64.97 to 71.34 (+6.37) on Flores-101, with especially large gains on low-resource language pairs (e.g., Swahili-x: 54.00→60.31).
- Language Consistency Rate (LCR) approaches 100% for many low-resource target languages, effectively eliminating wrong-language outputs.
- Human evaluation shows win rates of 42% (Azerbaijani→Portuguese) and 51% (English→Kannada) against the baseline model.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
WALAR mitigates reward hacking through three complementary mechanisms:

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A source‑based quality estimation model (MetricX) provides a coarse reward signal that correlates with translation quality, enabling reinforcement learning without parallel references.
- **Mechanism:** The source sentence *x* and candidate translation *y* are concatenated and fed to MetricX, which outputs a scalar score *r_qe(x,y)* in [−25, 0].
- **Core assumption:** MetricX’s scores remain predictive of human judgment even when only the source is available.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “WALAR introduces a composite reward for RL training that combines (1) a base QE score (MetricX)…”  
  - [section] “3.2 Quality Estimation Score… r_qe(x,y)=MetricX(x,y)”  
  - [corpus] “The Energy Loss Phenomenon in RLHF” identifies reward hacking in RLHF; WALAR’s composite reward directly counters such hacking.
- **Break condition:** If MetricX consistently assigns high scores to degenerate outputs (e.g., wrong‑language or repeated text), RL will optimize for those holes and degrade translation quality.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A word alignment score penalizes both over‑translation (extra words) and under‑translation (missing content), ensuring the hypothesis covers the source without hallucination.
- **Mechanism:** BGE‑M3 embeddings are extracted for source and translation tokens; a similarity matrix is thresholded to obtain alignment pairs WA. Precision (|WA|/n) and recall (|WA|/m) yield an F1 score *r_wa*.
- **Core assumption:** Semantic similarity above a small threshold correctly captures word‑level correspondences across many languages, including low‑resource ones.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “…(2) a word alignment score (F1 from BGE‑M3 embeddings) to penalize coverage errors.”  
  - [section] “3.2 Word Alignment Score… r_wa(x,y)=2·P·R/(P+R)… penalizes both over‑translation and under‑translation.”  
  - [corpus] “M‑RewardBench” shows that QE‑only rewards can be gamed; alignment adds an orthogonal coverage signal.
- **Break condition:** If the embedding model fails to produce meaningful similarities for certain language pairs (e.g., very different scripts), the alignment score becomes noisy and may misguide learning.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A language alignment score enforces generation in the intended target language, fixing a critical hole where QE models ignore language mismatch.
- **Mechanism:** GlotLID detects the language of the full translation; MaskLID first masks code‑switched segments to avoid contamination. The binary reward *r_la*=1 only if the cleaned translation’s language matches the target.
- **Core assumption:** GlotLID is accurate enough for short, possibly imperfect translations, and MaskLID reliably isolates non-target language tokens.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “…(3) a language alignment score (GlotLID with MaskLID) to enforce correct target language generation.”  
  - [section] “3.2 Language Alignment… r_la = I(Lang_detect(y)=tgt) … using GlotLID and MaskLID.”  
  - [corpus] “M‑RewardBench” highlights the lack of language consistency checks in existing reward models; WALAR adds this dimension.
- **Break condition:** Misidentification by GlotLID (e.g., for very low‑resource languages or mixed content) will incorrectly penalize valid translations, potentially reducing language fidelity.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Data pipeline (WMT News Crawl) → source sentences → Base LLM (LLaMAX3-8B-Alpaca) → candidate translations → [QE module (MetricX), Word alignment module (BGE-M3), Language alignment module (GlotLID+MaskLID)] → composite reward → GRPO trainer (OpenRLHF) → updated Base LLM; Data pipeline also provides language-direction sampling.

### Critical Path
1. Sample a batch of source sentences with designated source/target languages.
2. Append a translation instruction prompt and sample G candidate translations from the current policy.
3. For each candidate:
   - Compute r_qe by concatenating source + hypothesis and feeding to MetricX.
   - Compute embeddings of source and hypothesis tokens with BGE-M3; derive similarity matrix; threshold (c=1e-3) to get alignment pairs WA; calculate precision/recall and F1 r_wa.
   - Run MaskLID on hypothesis to mask code-switched tokens; pass masked text to GlotLID; set r_la=1 if detected language equals target, else 0.
4. Form final reward: if r_la=0 → -25; else r = r_qe + α * r_wa.
5. Compute group-relative advantage A_k = [reward − mean(rewards)] / std(rewards) over the G rollouts.
6. Perform GRPO update: minimize clipped surrogate loss plus β·KL(π_θ || π_ref).
7. Repeat for next batch.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **α (alignment weight):** Larger values reduce over-/under-translation but may hurt fluency; α=20 chosen optimal.
- **QE model size:** MetricX is slow; smaller QE could speed up training but risk more holes.
- **Alignment threshold c (1e-3):** Lower c increases recall but adds noise; higher c may miss valid alignments, especially in low-resource languages.
- **Language identification:** GlotLID is resource-intensive; MaskLID adds latency but is necessary for code-switching robustness.
- **GRPO hyperparameters (ε=0.2, β=0.01):** Require tuning; too high β stifles learning, too low leads to collapse.

### Failure Signatures
- **Wrong-language ratio** remains high → GlotLID/MaskLID misconfiguration or insufficient language alignment reward weight.
- **Reward collapse** (near-zero advantage variance) → rollout diversity too low or KL penalty too high; increase temperature or decrease β.
- **Length deviation** (hypotheses much longer/shorter than references) → Word alignment component under-weighted (α too small).
- **Validation spBLEU drops** while xCOMET rises → potential metric over-optimization; consider early stopping or adjusting α.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

#### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can we dynamically balance the contributions of quality estimation, word alignment, and language alignment rewards (e.g., adjusting α) to optimize for multiple evaluation metrics simultaneously across diverse language pairs?
- **Basis in paper:** The ablation study (Table 4) shows that increasing the word alignment weight α improves spBLEU but degrades neural metric scores (MetricX*, xCOMET*). The authors note metric over-optimization risks and selected α=20 based on validation spBLEU, implying a trade‑off that may not generalize optimally across all 1,414 directions.
- **Why unresolved:** A fixed α may be suboptimal for languages with different typological features or resource levels. The paper does not explore adaptive or per‑language weighting strategies.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying α per language family or resource tier, measuring performance on a comprehensive suite of metrics (including human evaluation) to identify an optimal scheduling or meta‑controller for α.

#### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent do WALAR's improvements generalize to languages with extremely scarce monolingual data (e.g., <1k sentences) and to domains beyond news (e.g., social media, technical texts)?
- **Basis in paper:** Training data is filtered from WMT News Crawl and includes only 22 source languages with sufficient data. The generalization analysis (Figure 5) tests unseen target languages but assumes seen source languages and a news-domain test set (FLORES-101). The paper acknowledges low-resource gains but does not analyze data scarcity thresholds or domain shift.
- **Why unresolved:** WALAR's reliance on word and language alignment models (BGE-M3, GlotLID) may fail on noisy, informal, or highly divergent monolingual sources not covered in the curated training set.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Training and evaluating WALAR on additional monolingual corpora from diverse domains (e.g., web‑text, forum posts) for a subset of extremely low‑resource languages, measuring degradation in reward stability and translation quality.

#### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can WALAR's architecture be decoupled from GRPO and effectively combined with alternative RLHF algorithms (e.g., PPO, DPO) or non‑RL fine‑tuning methods to further reduce reward hacking?
- **Basis in paper:** WALAR is implemented within the GRPO framework, chosen for its stability and KL penalty. The paper does not compare against other RL algorithms or explore whether the composite reward alone could be used in a supervised setting (e.g., as a filtered data selector).
- **Why unresolved:** GRPO is one specific approach to policy optimization; other methods might handle the composite reward's different scales (e.g., -25 penalty vs. continuous QE scores) differently, possibly with better sample efficiency or reduced overfitting to alignment heuristics.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation where the WALAR reward is used with PPO, DPO, and a simple supervised fine‑tuning pipeline (using reward‑filtered pseudo‑references as in +WALAR‑SFT), comparing sample complexity, final quality, and reward hacking prevalence.

#### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How robust is WALAR's language alignment component (GlotLID + MaskLID) to nuanced code‑switching patterns and languages with high intra‑sentential mixing, and does it inadvertently penalize legitimate multilingual output?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state: "GlotLID alone struggles to reliably identify such code‑switching translations" and thus incorporate MaskLID. However, they do not evaluate MaskLID's precision/recall on code‑switched generation or discuss whether the masking approach might over‑penalize transliteration or loanwords.
- **Why unresolved:** The LCR metric (Figure 3) measures language consistency but cannot distinguish between "pure" target language output and code‑switched output that is still dominantly in the target language. The reward design might suppress useful code‑switching common in some low‑resource language communities.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis of generated outputs flagged by MaskLID, with human annotation to区分 legitimate code‑switching versus incorrect language generation, followed by a variant of WALAR that uses a softer language alignment penalty (e.g., proportional to non‑target token fraction) rather than a binary reject.

#### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Do the observed "holes" in source‑based QE models (like MetricX) reflect fundamental architectural biases (e.g., training on reference‑based datasets) that cannot be fully patched by adding auxiliary scores, or are they correctable via improved QE pre‑training?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper identifies holes (self‑reference, wrong language, over/under‑translation) and shows WALAR's components mitigate them. However, the authors note that MetricX is a hybrid model trained on both source‑based and reference‑based data, and its failure mode for "self‑generated reference" stems from this design (Section 5.1).
- **Why unresolved:** WALAR applies a post‑hoc fix to the reward signal but does not address whether the QE model itself could be fine‑tuned on multilingual, source‑only scenarios to be intrinsically robust. The holes might be symptomatic of a deeper misalignment between QE training objectives and open‑vocabulary generation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A study fine‑tuning MetricX (or a similar QE) on a dataset of "hacked" translations (wrong language, self‑reference) with source‑only inputs, then using the refined QE within WALAR without word/language alignment. If performance remains competitive, it would suggest QE model improvements could supplant heuristic aligners.

## Limitations
- Insufficient specification of training schedule, convergence criteria, and prompt formats hinders exact replication.
- Hyperparameter tuning details (α, alignment threshold) are sparse, and default values may not yield the reported gains.
- Evaluation relies heavily on Flores‑101 and only two human‑evaluated language pairs, leaving real‑world domain generalization and computational cost unexamined.
