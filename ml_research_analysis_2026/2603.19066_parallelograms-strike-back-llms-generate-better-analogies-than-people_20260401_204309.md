# Parallelograms Strike Back: LLMs Generate Better Analogies than People

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19066
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19066
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** LLMs generate analogies that receive higher human relational similarity ratings than human-generated analogies, a advantage driven by humans producing a long tail of weak completions; both systems' high-quality analogies are better predicted by parallelogram alignment and lower word frequency than by local similarity heuristics.

## Executive Summary
This study revisits the parallelogram model for four-term word analogies (A:B::C:D), comparing human and large language model (LLM) completions on a standard dataset. LLM-generated analogies were rated as more relationally similar by human judges. Critically, LLM completions showed greater alignment with the parallelogram vector difference in static GloVe embeddings and used less frequent words. However, this overall LLM advantage disappeared when restricting analysis to each system’s most frequent (modal) responses, indicating the human long tail of low-quality completions drives the difference. For both humans and LLMs, higher ratings were consistently predicted by greater parallelogram alignment and lower word frequency, not by local C:D similarity. The results support the parallelogram model as an accurate description of quality analogies but reveal that humans often fail to generate such analogies in practice, while LLMs do so more consistently.

## Method Summary
The study re-used 846 analogy stems from SemEval-2012 and prior work. Human completions (D terms) came from an existing dataset (26,265 responses). Six LLMs were prompted with four template variations per stem, generating ~203k responses at temperature=1.0. A new set of human participants (n=390) rated the relational similarity of constructed analogies using human and LLM completions on a 7-point scale. All words were mapped to 300-dimensional GloVe embeddings. For each (stem, D) pair, metrics were computed: parallelogram alignment (cosine similarity between vectors (B-A) and (D-C)), C:D local similarity, a nearest neighbor (NN) heuristic score, and word frequency. Frequency-weighted average metrics per stem were compared between humans and LLMs. Paired t-tests assessed rating differences. Multiple regression models predicted rating differences (LLM minus human) from differences in the metrics. A “modal-only” analysis restricted responses to the most frequent completion per stem. Cumulative Proportion Retrieved (CPR) analysis ranked the full vocabulary by each heuristic to see how well it captured observed responses.

## Key Results
- LLM completions received significantly higher human relational similarity ratings than human completions for most models.
- Higher ratings were robustly predicted by greater parallelogram alignment in GloVe embeddings and by lower word frequency; local C:D similarity was a weaker predictor.
- LLMs showed greater average parallelogram alignment and used less frequent words than humans.
- The LLM rating advantage was driven by humans producing a long tail of weak, infrequent completions; when comparing only modal (most frequent) responses, the LLM advantage was eliminated.
- For both systems, local similarity heuristics (C:D, NN) better captured the full distribution of responses than the parallelogram model, despite parallelogram alignment predicting quality judgments.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Greater alignment with the parallelogram vector model in GloVe embeddings predicts higher human relational similarity ratings for analogies.
- **Mechanism:** Analogy quality is determined by the cosine similarity between the relational vector derived from the stem (v(B) – v(A)) and the candidate completion vector (v(D) – v(C)). A higher cosine similarity indicates the D term preserves the relational structure.
- **Core assumption:** Semantic relations are linearly representable in distributional embedding spaces, and human judgments of analogy quality are sensitive to this geometric constraint.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - LLM completions showed greater parallelogram alignment; this alignment strongly predicted which LLM completions were rated higher (β=0.205, p < .001).
  - The effect holds in regression models controlling for other predictors.
- **Break condition:** If human ratings are unrelated to parallelogram alignment in other embedding spaces or with different analogy types.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Use of lower-frequency (less accessible) words is associated with higher-rated analogies, and LLMs tend to generate such words more than humans.
- **Mechanism:** Lexical accessibility creates a retrieval bias: humans default to high-frequency words during free generation, but these are judged as weaker analogies. Lower-frequency words, while harder to retrieve, often provide tighter relational fits.
- **Core assumption:** Perceived analogy quality is inversely related to word accessibility, independent of relational alignment.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - LLM completions used less frequent words; Δlog(freq) was a negative predictor of rating advantage (β=–0.192, p < .001).
  - Lower frequency predicted higher ratings within each system.
- **Break condition:** If frequency effects are eliminated when controlling for word concreteness, imageability, or domain specificity.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The overall LLM advantage in analogy quality is primarily driven by humans producing a long tail of low-quality completions, not by LLMs uniformly superior modal responses.
- **Mechanism:** Human response distributions are highly dispersed, with many rare, weak completions. LLM distributions are more peaked on a few high-quality answers. The mean superiority of LLMs is an artifact of response distribution shape.
- **Core assumption:** The prototypical (modal) analogies generated by humans and LLMs are of comparable quality; the difference lies in the prevalence of poor completions in human data.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Human modal responses accounted for 64% of total responses, compared to 85% for LLMs.
  - In modal-only analysis, no model showed a statistically significant overall advantage over humans.
- **Break condition:** If humans produce similarly peaked distributions under time pressure or with training, or if LLMs' modal responses are consistently worse in new datasets.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Four-term word analogy (A:B::C:D)**
  - *Why needed here:* The task structure defines the problem space; the stem-completion format is the input-output framework being studied.
  - *Quick check:* What does the notation "king:queen::man:woman" represent? A relationship (gender) applied to a new pair.
- **Concept: Parallelogram model (vector offset)**
  - *Why needed here:* The core theoretical model; it posits that D should satisfy v(D) – v(C) ≈ v(B) – v(A).
  - *Quick check:* If v(king) – v(queen) ≈ v(man) – v(woman), what vector operation defines the target for D? v(C) + (v(B) – v(A)).
- **Concept: Local similarity heuristics (C:D similarity, NN)**
  - *Why needed here:* The competing explanation from prior work; these ignore relational structure and rely on semantic similarity to C or to A/B.
  - *Quick check:* What is the "C:D similarity" heuristic, and why might it outperform the parallelogram model? It picks D most similar to C; works because many analogies involve semantically related pairs.
- **Concept: Distributional semantics (GloVe embeddings)**
  - *Why needed here:* The geometric models are operationalized using pre-trained word vectors; GloVe provides the shared space.
  - *Quick check:* Why use cosine similarity rather than Euclidean distance in a high-dimensional embedding space? Cosine focuses on directional alignment (relation) rather than magnitude (frequency/usage).
- **Concept: Cumulative Proportion Retrieved (CPR)**
  - *Why needed here:* Measures how well a ranking heuristic (e.g., parallelogram score) captures the observed responses across the entire vocabulary.
  - *Quick check:* What does a CPR curve show? The proportion of observed D terms that fall within the top τ% of ranked candidate words.
- **Concept: Modal response**
  - *Why needed here:* The most frequent completion per stem; used to compare prototypical system outputs independent of long-tail effects.
  - *Quick check:* Why compare only modal responses? To isolate the quality of the most typical answer each system generates, removing dispersion effects.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Stimulus Generation -> Response Collection -> Human Rating -> Embedding Analysis -> Regression Modeling -> Internal Representation Check
- **Critical path:** Extract 846 stems → gather human responses & generate LLM completions → collect human ratings → compute GloVe-based metrics per (stem, D) → aggregate by stem → regress ΔRating on ΔMetrics → restrict to modal responses for secondary analysis.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Embedding space:* GloVe (static, corpus-derived) vs. LLM-internal (contextual). GloVe chosen for compatibility and interpretability; internal checks showed weaker alignment→rating link.
  - *Response aggregation:* Frequency-weighted averages reflect overall performance but obscure distribution shape; modal-only analysis isolates typical response quality.
  - *Heuristic comparison:* CPR across rank thresholds captures full distributional fit; single-threshold approaches might miss tail behavior.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - LLM modal responses rated lower than human modal responses for some models (e.g., Qwen3-32B, Gemma-3-27B).
  - Parallelogram alignment fails to predict ratings when local similarity is very high (multicollinearity managed by separate regression models).
  - Internal LLM representations show near-zero alignment→rating slopes, contradicting a "geometric internal process" narrative.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. *Human–LLM convergence:* Compute centroid similarity in GloVe space for each stem’s responses; test if LLM responses are more similar to humans than chance.
  2. *Rating comparison:* Paired t-tests on frequency-weighted mean ratings per stem comparing each LLM to human benchmark.
  3. *Predictor regression:* Linear models predicting rating difference (LLM – human) from ΔParallelogram, ΔC:D, ΔNN, Δlog(freq); identify significant predictors.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Why do local similarity heuristics (e.g., C:D similarity) better predict analogy completions for both humans and LLMs than the parallelogram model, despite parallelogram alignment correlating with quality judgments?
  - *Basis:* Local similarity outperforms in CPR capturing response distributions; parallelogram alignment predicts human quality ratings.
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper speculates co-occurring relational and semantic similarity but doesn’t test decoupling.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Construct analogies where relational and semantic similarity are intentionally decoupled; test if local similarity still predicts completions and parallelogram alignment predicts quality independently.

- **Open Question 2:** What cognitive or computational factors cause humans to produce a long tail of weak analogy completions, while LLMs generate more concentrated, parallelogram-aligned responses?
  - *Basis:* Human response distributions are more dispersed; LLM modal responses account for a higher share of total responses.
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper attributes human failures to difficulties in generating relation-preserving analogies but doesn’t investigate underlying causes (e.g., retrieval inefficiencies).
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Manipulate human cognitive resources (time pressure, working memory load) to see if dispersion decreases; analyze LLM training objectives.

- **Open Question 3:** Do LLMs' internal representations during analogy generation reflect parallelogram geometry, or is their success an artifact of external embedding spaces like GloVe?
  - *Basis:* Internal representation checks show weaker alignment→rating prediction compared to GloVe embeddings.
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper concludes LLMs don’t rely on observable geometric structures but doesn’t identify what internal features drive generation.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Probe LLM activations to extract relational vectors and compare to parallelogram predictions; perform layer-wise interventions.

- **Open Question 4:** Why do certain semantic relation types (e.g., Time:Associated Item) show low human-LLM convergence in analogy completions?
  - *Basis:* Figure 1 shows variation in convergence across 20 relation types; some relations diverge consistently across all LLMs.
  - *Why unresolved:* The pattern is noted but not explained; may relate to linguistic encoding or conceptual ambiguity.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Analyze low-convergence relations’ distributional properties in training corpora; test relation-specific probes.

## Limitations
- Unavailability of the exact human response dataset (Peterson et al., 2020) and unspecified GloVe file version hinder full replication.
- Potential out‑of‑vocabulary words and unstated handling could skew metric calculations.
- Reliance on static GloVe embeddings may not capture contextual nuance; internal LLM representations showed weaker predictive power.
- Possible confounds between word frequency and other lexical properties (e.g., concreteness) not fully controlled.
- Arbitrary definition of “modal” responses; sensitivity to aggregation thresholds not explored.

## Confidence
- **Parallelogram alignment predicts analogy quality** – Medium (strong within-study evidence but untested across embeddings/languages).
- **Lower word frequency improves ratings; LLMs use less frequent words** – Medium (frequency effect may be entangled with other lexical factors).
- **LLM advantage stems from human long tail, not superior modal responses** – Medium (modal analysis sensitive to response cutoff; needs robustness checks).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-embedding replication:** Rerun main regression using alternative embedding spaces (fastText, BERT CLS, GPT internal embeddings) to test robustness of parallelogram predictor.
2. **Lexical confound control:** Construct matched analogies with fixed D-term frequency but varying parallelogram alignment; collect fresh human ratings to test causality.
3. **Modal definition sensitivity:** Repeat LLM vs. human comparison using top-2 or top-3 responses per stem and analyze response-distribution shape (e.g., entropy) to characterize the long tail more precisely.
