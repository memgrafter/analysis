# Wear Classification of Abrasive Flap Wheels using a Hierarchical Deep Learning Approach

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12852
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12852
- **Reference count:** 37
- **Primary result:** A hierarchical deep learning framework classifies flap wheel wear with 87.5% accuracy on unseen tools, with sub-models achieving 93.8–99.3% test accuracy.

## Executive Summary
This research tackles the automation of wear condition monitoring for flexible abrasive flap wheels, which exhibit complex and variable geometric wear patterns that resist traditional inspection. The proposed solution is a vision-based hierarchical deep learning system that breaks down the classification problem into three sequential levels: distinguishing new versus used wheels, identifying the geometric profile (rectangular, concave, convex) and detecting flap tears, and assessing deformation severity (partial or complete) for non-rectangular profiles. Using a custom dataset of 13,240 real images, the authors train EfficientNetV2 networks via transfer learning for each sub-task. The system achieves high accuracy on individual sub-models and an overall 87.5% accuracy on a test set of previously unseen tools. The authors project that adding logical consistency checks and confidence-based thresholding could theoretically increase overall accuracy to between 93.6% and 94.7%.

## Method Summary
The core method is a hierarchical classification framework that decomposes the multi-attribute wear state into three consecutive decision levels, each handled by a separate convolutional neural network. This decomposition reduces the complexity and class imbalance per model compared to a monolithic approach. Five EfficientNetV2 models (variants S and L) are trained individually on a custom dataset of 13,240 images from 105 flap wheels, with a tool-wise split to ensure generalization. Inputs are 512×512 pixel images: radial views for profile/severity and axial views for tear detection. Transfer learning from ImageNet pretrained weights is employed to enable robust learning from the limited domain-specific data. The final classification path is governed by a logical rule engine that resolves conflicts between levels based on semantic constraints (e.g., a "new" wheel cannot have a tear or non-rectangular profile).

## Key Results
- Individual sub-models demonstrate high robustness, with test accuracies ranging from 93.8% (flap tear detection) to 99.3% (concave severity assessment).
- The complete hierarchical classification on unseen tools achieves 87.5% accuracy, with primary error sources being misclassifications in the usage (Level 1) and tear detection (Level 2) models.
- The authors estimate that implementing logical consistency checks and confidence-thresholding could theoretically raise overall accuracy to 93.6–94.7%, though this is not empirically validated.
- Grad-CAM visualizations confirm that the networks learn physically relevant features such as flap contours and gaps, supporting model interpretability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Decomposing the complex wear classification into a sequential hierarchy reduces per-model complexity and mitigates data fragmentation for rare wear patterns.
*   **Mechanism:** The problem is split into three logical levels: (1) new vs. used, (2) profile type & tear detection, (3) severity assessment. Each sub-network is trained on a focused, less class-imbalanced task. For example, all images showing a flap tear—regardless of profile—train the tear detector, enlarging its training set compared to a monolithic model where tear samples would be scattered across many combined classes.
*   **Core assumption:** Wear features (state, profile, tear, severity) are semi-independent, allowing decoupled training without catastrophic information loss. Conflicts between levels can be used as error signals.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "decomposes the problem into three logical levels... reduces the necessary selectivity of each network... enables modularity... avoids combinatorial data fragmentation."
    *   [section 3.2] "In a flat structure, the few available images of defects would be divided into different geometric subclasses... Hierarchical decoupling... allows all images showing a tear... to be used to train the flap tear network."
*   **Break condition:** If wear features are strongly coupled (e.g., a specific tear type only occurs with a specific severity of concavity), decoupling harms accuracy. The paper's logical inconsistency checks are a workaround, but the fundamental assumption of feature independence would be violated.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Transfer learning with EfficientNetV2 provides robust feature extraction from a relatively small, real-world dataset (13,240 images) by leveraging pre-trained general visual features.
*   **Mechanism:** Models initialized with ImageNet weights are fine-tuned on the specific flap wheel images. The architecture's compound scaling and attention mechanisms allow efficient learning. This avoids training from scratch, which would require orders of magnitude more domain-specific data.
*   **Core assumption:** Generic mid-level visual features (edges, textures, contours) learned on ImageNet are sufficiently transferable to the specialized domain of abrasive tool surfaces under varying lighting and perspective.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "transfer learning approach with EfficientNetV2 architecture was used."
    *   [section 4.2] "A transfer learning approach is pursued to ensure a high degree of generalizability... with limited domain-specific data sets."
*   **Break condition:** The domain shift is too large. If the texture, lighting, and geometry of flap wheels are fundamentally unlike natural ImageNet images, transfer learning gains diminish. The use of ambient/test hall lighting introduces variability that might challenge this assumption.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Logical consistency checks between hierarchical levels and confidence-thresholding can catch and reduce error propagation from lower-level misclassifications.
*   **Mechanism:** The hierarchy enforces semantic rules (e.g., a "new" wheel cannot have a "tear" or "concave" profile). Predictions with low confidence from Level 1 or Level 2 can trigger a re-evaluation (e.g., by analyzing a second image). The paper estimates this could raise overall accuracy from 87.5% to 93.6-94.7%.
*   **Core assumption:** Classification errors in lower levels are not perfectly correlated with errors in higher levels, and confidence scores are well-calibrated (low confidence correlates with incorrect predictions).
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section 3.3] "Possible paths... can be divided into consistent or contradictory results using logical checks... logical inconsistencies... can serve as an indication of misclassifications."
    *   [section 5.4] "By implementing the conflict and confidence mechanisms... a theoretical accuracy between 93.6% and 94.7% could be expected."
*   **Break condition:** Errors are perfectly correlated or confidence is poorly calibrated. If almost all low-confidence predictions are actually correct, thresholding would increase errors. The paper notes misclassifications often have lower confidence, supporting the assumption, but this is not rigorously proven.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Hierarchical / Multi-Level Classification**
    - **Why needed here:** The wear state is a composition of orthogonal characteristics (usage status, geometric profile, defect presence, severity). A flat multi-class classifier would require a separate class for every possible combination, leading to extreme data sparsity. Hierarchical decomposition allows independent optimization for each characteristic.
    - **Quick check question:** Can the problem be broken into decisions where the output of an earlier decision constrains or informs the options for a later one? (Yes: only concave/convex tools require severity assessment.)
- **Concept: Transfer Learning with Convolutional Neural Networks**
    - **Why needed here:** The custom dataset (13k images) is small for training a deep CNN from scratch to achieve robust generalization across variable lighting, wheel orientation, and wear stages. Transfer learning bootstraps learning with features from a large generic dataset (ImageNet).
    - **Quick check question:** Is the input domain (images of physical objects) semantically similar to the source domain of the pre-trained model (natural images)? (Partially: both are RGB images of textured objects, but abrasive surfaces and controlled industrial imagery differ from everyday scenes.)
- **Concept: Confidence Calibration & Thresholding for Reliability**
    - **Why needed here:** The system must be trusted for autonomous operation. Raw softmax scores are used to identify uncertain predictions for human review or secondary analysis, improving safety and robustness in a production environment.
    - **Quick check question:** Does the model's confidence score differentiate between correct and incorrect predictions on a validation set? (Paper shows misclassifications had lower average confidence, suggesting partial calibration.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** 
    Image Acquisition Module (2 cameras, ambient lighting) -> Preprocessing Pipeline (cropping, resizing, normalization, augmentation) -> Hierarchical Inference Engine (three sequential EfficientNetV2 sub-models: Usage, Tear/Profile, Severity) -> Logical Rule Engine (consistency checks) -> Confidence & Re-evaluation Module (thresholding, multi-image ensemble)
- **Critical path:**
    1.  Acquire a pair of images (radial + axial) of a wheel.
    2.  Run Usage Classifier (L1). If "new", stop. If "used", proceed.
    3.  Run Tear Classifier (L2) on axial image. Run Profile Classifier (L2) on radial image.
    4.  Check L1-L2 consistency (e.g., "used" + "no tear/rectangular" is fine; "new" + "tear" is conflict). If conflict, flag for re-evaluation.
    5.  If profile is "concave" or "convex", run corresponding Severity Classifier (L3).
    6.  Output final wear state from the consistent path.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
    - **Accuracy vs. Speed:** Hierarchical evaluation is sequential but with small models it is fast. Re-evaluation for low-confidence/conflict cases adds significant latency.
    - **Modularity vs. Integration:** Each sub-model can be updated independently (pro). But integrating them requires maintaining consistent preprocessing and a robust rule engine (con). A single monolithic model would be simpler but requires vastly more combined training data.
    - **Single vs. Multi-view:** Using both radial (profile) and axial (tear) views improves accuracy but doubles hardware cost and acquisition time. A single-view model would be cheaper but less robust.
- **Failure signatures:**
    - **High L1 (usage) error:** Model confuses lightly used rectangular wheels with new ones due to retained fringes. Fix: incorporate usage tracking metadata or train on wheels after "break-in" period.
    - **High L2 Tear error:** Misses single flap tears or confuses irregular flap spacing with tears. Fix: augment with more single-tear examples; investigate image capture during wheel rotation.
    - **Low L3 Convex severity accuracy:** Tends to classify "fully convex" as "partially convex". Fix: gather more data on the transition boundary; consider a regression output instead of classification.
    - **Inconsistent final output:** Logical conflicts occur, often from L1 or L2 errors. Fix: implement the proposed confidence-thresholding and multi-image ensemble voting.
- **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Baseline Monolithic Classifier:** Train a single EfficientNetV2 to classify all 11 combined states. Compare accuracy & data efficiency to the hierarchical approach. *Goal: Quantify the cost of not decoupling features.*
    2.  **Hierarchical without Consistency Checks:** Run the sequential models but ignore logical rules. Measure the rate of contradictory outputs (e.g., "new" + "concave"). *Goal: Validate the need for the rule-based layer.*
    3.  **Confidence Threshold Tuning:** For L1 and L2 tear models, sweep confidence thresholds on the validation set. Plot the trade-off between flagged samples (for re-evaluation) and captured misclassifications. *Goal: Find the Pareto-optimal operating point for reliability vs. automation rate.*

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can dynamic confidence thresholds and logical consistency checks be practically implemented to reduce error propagation in the hierarchical classification, and what accuracy gains can be achieved beyond the theoretical 93.6%-94.7%?
    - **Basis in paper:** The authors propose that implementing logical consistency checks and confidence-thresholding could theoretically raise overall accuracy from 87.5% to 93.6%-94.7%, but this remains unimplemented and unvalidated.
    - **Why unresolved:** The theoretical improvement assumes perfect re-examination of uncertain predictions; practical issues like optimal threshold selection, conflict resolution, and integration overhead are not addressed.
    - **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement these mechanisms in the hierarchical pipeline, conduct experiments on unseen flap wheels with varying confidence thresholds, and measure actual accuracy improvement, false positive/negative trade-offs, and computational cost.
- **Open Question 2:** How should the hierarchical wear classification be integrated with quantitative wear measurement to provide a holistic, actionable wear assessment for adaptive process control in automated grinding?
    - **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states that future work will link classification to explicit wear measurement to generate a holistic wear picture and cross-validate results, but no integration method is specified.
    - **Why unresolved:** Classification yields discrete categories (e.g., concave), while measurement requires continuous geometric parameters; the semantic context use and algorithm selection (e.g., fitting methods) are undefined.
    - **What evidence would resolve it:** Design and test a coupled system where classification outputs guide measurement algorithms (e.g., triggering circular arc fitting for concave profiles), validate on grinding experiments with known wear states, and assess process consistency and tool life prediction accuracy.
- **Open Question 3:** Does the custom dataset of 13,240 images from 105 flap wheels sufficiently cover the natural variability of wear patterns, including rare or concurrent phenomena like parallel concavity-convexity, to ensure robust industrial deployment?
    - **Basis in paper:** The paper notes that parallel concavity and convexity are theoretically possible but excluded due to rarity; the dataset size is moderate, but wear diversity across manufacturing, process parameters, and time is not quantified.
    - **Why unresolved:** Unrepresented wear combinations could lead to systematic failures; the dataset's comprehensiveness is assumed but not statistically validated against real-world distributions.
    - **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform a gap analysis comparing dataset wear patterns against industrial case studies or failure reports; augment with targeted samples of rare/mixed wear, retrain models, and evaluate performance on a diverse, external validation set.
- **Open Question 4:** Can the classification models generalize to variations in flap wheel manufacturing tolerances, imaging setups (e.g., lighting, rotation), and tool dimensions without retraining or significant accuracy loss?
    - **Basis in paper:** Grad-CAM analysis indicated that irregular flap inclination from manufacturing tolerances caused errors; image acquisition used ambient light and static poses, with plans for controlled lighting and rotating flaps, implying current data may not capture all variations.
    - **Why unresolved:** The models are trained on a specific dataset and setup; real-world deployment will encounter unseen tolerances and conditions, risking robustness.
    - **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct stress tests by introducing synthetic variations (e.g., noise, blur, perspective shifts) and using physically distinct flap wheels from different sources; measure accuracy decay and identify critical factors for domain adaptation.

## Limitations
- **Hierarchical Decomposition:** The core assumption of semi-independent wear features is not rigorously proven; strong couplings between wear attributes could violate the decomposition's validity.
- **Transfer Learning:** The significant domain shift from natural ImageNet images to textured, repetitive industrial parts under ambient lighting may limit the generalizability benefits of transfer learning; no comparison to from-scratch training is provided.
- **Consistency Check & Theoretical Accuracy:** The projected 93.6-94.7% accuracy gain from confidence-thresholding is theoretical only, based on example post-hoc thresholds; the practical implementation, threshold optimization, and actual performance improvement remain unvalidated.

## Confidence
- **Hierarchical Decomposition Justification:** Medium confidence. Internally supported by data usage arguments and ablation logic, but the independence assumption lacks external or rigorous validation.
- **Transfer Learning Generalizability:** Medium confidence. Plausible for a 13k-image dataset, but the domain shift is substantial and unbenchmarked against alternative training strategies.
- **Consistency Check Projected Accuracy:** Low confidence. Relies on unimplemented mechanisms and example thresholds; no empirical evidence supports the specific accuracy gain figures.
