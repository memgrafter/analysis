# Flow-Based Density Ratio Estimation for Intractable Distributions with Applications in Genomics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.24201
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24201
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** scRatio efficiently estimates likelihood ratios between intractable distributions via a single ODE trajectory, achieving lower estimation error than baselines and improved performance in single-cell genomics analyses.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the problem of estimating density ratios between two intractable data distributions—a key task for comparing samples under different conditions—without requiring costly per-distribution likelihood evaluations using normalizing flows. The proposed method, scRatio, employs conditional flow matching to construct a unified generative process that tracks the log-density ratio along a single trajectory by solving an ordinary differential equation (ODE) that combines learned velocity fields and score functions. This approach eliminates the need for separate integral computations for each distribution. Experiments on synthetic data show that scRatio yields lower mean squared error compared to existing methods like Time Score Matching, particularly in high dimensions when using appropriate noise schedules. Real-world applications in single-cell genomics demonstrate gains in differential abundance analysis and batch correction, while drug combination studies reveal strong correlation with classifier-based distribution separation, underscoring scRatio's utility for detecting treatment interactions.

## Method Summary
scRatio builds on normalizing flows and conditional flow matching. It trains separate neural networks to approximate the velocity field (governing the generative ODE) and the score function (gradient of log-density). Given two distributions, the method defines a conditional path connecting them and estimates the log-density ratio by integrating an ODE whose derivative equals the difference between the learned score and the velocity field along that path. This allows the ratio to be computed without separately evaluating the density of each distribution. The ODE is solved once per pair of distributions, making the approach more efficient than naive flow-based ratio computation that requires two separate likelihood evaluations.

## Key Results
- Synthetic benchmarks: scRatio achieves lower mean squared error than Time Score Matching and naive flow evaluation, with larger improvements in higher dimensions and when using adaptive noise schedules.
- Single-cell genomics: scRatio improves differential abundance analysis (higher AUC and Normalized Abundance Ratio) and yields more accurate batch correction (smaller absolute log-ratios after correction).
- Drug combination studies: scRatio's log-ratio scores strongly correlate with classifier-based measures of distribution separation, indicating reliable detection of synergistic or antagonistic drug effects.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works because it reformulates density ratio estimation as an ODE along a single trajectory connecting the two distributions. The ODE's derivative is the difference between the learned score (oriented towards density increase) and the velocity field (oriented along the generative path). This difference captures how the density changes relative to the path, and integrating it accumulates the log-ratio. By using conditional flow matching, the same generative path can be used for both distributions, avoiding separate integration. Moreover, the learned score and velocity are derived from the same flow model, ensuring consistency. The approach reduces computational overhead and improves accuracy by focusing on the relative change rather than absolute densities.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data (samples from two distributions) → Train conditional flow matching (CFM) → Obtain score network \(s_\theta(x,t)\) and velocity network \(v_\phi(x,t)\) → For a pair, solve ODE \(d\log r_t / dt = (s_\theta - v_\phi)^T v_\phi\) from \(t=0\) to \(t=1\) → Log-density ratio \(\log r(x)\).

**Critical path:**  
Training phase: learn \(s_\theta\) and \(v_\phi\) from data using CFM loss. Inference phase: for a new pair, initialize ratio at \(t=0\) as 0 (if source densities equal or known) and integrate ODE to \(t=1\) to obtain log-ratio \(\log r(x) = \int_0^1 (s_\theta - v_\phi)^T v_\phi \, dt\).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Two-network vs. unified: training separate networks adds overhead but may avoid optimization conflicts; merging networks could reduce memory and speed up inference but risk accuracy drop.
- Noise schedule: the choice of \(\alpha_t, \sigma_t\) in CFM affects path geometry; more aggressive noise can improve stability but may blur information.
- ODE solver: fixed-step Euler is fast but may accumulate error; adaptive step solvers are more accurate but slower. Tradeoff between speed and precision.

**Failure signatures:**
- Numerical instability or large variance when the two distributions barely overlap; the ODE integration may become ill-conditioned.
- Poor performance if the noise schedule is inappropriate (e.g., too little noise in high dimensions leads to high curvature ODEs).
- Degenerate ratio estimates if score or velocity networks are not well-trained (e.g., high training loss, poor generalization).

**First three experiments:**
1. **Synthetic Gaussian mixtures:** Validate scRatio on 2D and 10D Gaussian mixtures with tunable mean separation to probe robustness to overlap.
2. **Noise schedule ablation:** Compare three schedules (linear, cosine, and square-root) on a high-dimensional synthetic benchmark to measure MSE and stability.
3. **Single-cell pilot:** Apply scRatio to a small public single-cell RNA-seq dataset (e.g., PBMC) for differential abundance between two cell types, report AUC and compare to ground truth (if available) or competing methods.

## Limitations
- The method depends on accurate score and velocity estimates; errors in these networks propagate to ratio estimates.
- Performance is sensitive to the choice of noise schedule; only "appropriate" schedules yield gains, but selection is currently heuristic.
- Evaluation has been limited to specific single-cell and drug datasets; broader generalizability across domains and platforms remains to be tested.
