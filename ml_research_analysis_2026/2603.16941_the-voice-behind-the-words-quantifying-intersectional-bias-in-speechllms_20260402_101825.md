# The Voice Behind the Words: Quantifying Intersectional Bias in SpeechLLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.16941
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16941
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: SpeechLLMs exhibit intersectional accent and gender bias, with Eastern European–accented speech—especially female-presenting voices—receiving lower helpfulness scores.

## Executive Summary
SpeechLLMs that process direct audio input may inadvertently encode speaker identity cues, leading to biased response quality. This paper quantifies intersectional bias (accent × gender) across three SpeechLLMs using a controlled experiment with voice-cloned prompts. By holding linguistic content constant, they isolate the effect of acoustic speaker identity. Results show consistent disparities: Eastern European accents are penalized, and the gap is largest for female voices. Human evaluations via Best-Worst Scaling reveal sharper bias than automated LLM judges, which only capture the directional trend. The findings highlight the need for robust bias audits in spoken conversational AI.

## Method Summary
The authors built a synthetic speech dataset to causally isolate speaker identity effects. Starting from 40 textual prompts (with and without hesitations), they used MegaTTS3 voice cloning to generate audio in six English accents (Chinese, Eastern European, Indian, Latin American, US, Southern British) and two gender presentations, using reference recordings from the Edinburgh International Accents corpus. This yielded 960 prompts per variant, for a total of 2,880 model interactions per SpeechLLM (LFM2-Audio, OmniVinci, Qwen3-Omni). Responses were evaluated by an LLM judge (gemini-3-flash-preview) using pointwise ratings (1–5) on helpfulness, competence, formality, and condescension, as well as pairwise and Best-Worst Scaling (BWS) tasks. A human BWS study on Prolific (N=18) provided ground-truth validation. Plackett–Luce models fitted to BWS choices estimated worth parameters per accent×gender subgroup, while statistical tests (Kruskal–Wallis, binomial) assessed significance.

## Key Results
- Eastern European accented speech received lower helpfulness scores; the gap was most pronounced for female-presenting voices (mean helpfulness: 3.15 vs. 3.62 for Southern British female).
- LLM judges captured the directional trend but underestimated bias magnitude; human BWS showed significantly sharper intersectional disparities (Eastern European worth parameter 0.114 vs. US English 0.202, p<0.001).
- Bias operated implicitly: models’ accent identification was near chance (19.4% accuracy) and responses remained polite but less actionable for marginalized accents.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** SpeechLLMs may encode speaker identity (accent/gender) in latent representations that indirectly shape response content, even without explicit demographic recognition.
- **Mechanism:** The end-to-end processing of raw audio preserves paralinguistic features (prosody, spectral cues). These features influence the model’s internal state during generation, leading to systematic variations in output helpfulness that correlate with social categories.
- **Core assumption:** Speaker identity cues are not filtered out by the model’s front-end and affect the contextual representations used for response generation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.1] Models show near-chance accent identification (19.4% accuracy) and default to predicting US English, indicating bias is not driven by an explicit classification step.
  - [abstract] "SpeechLLMs process spoken input directly, retaining cues such as accent and perceived gender... This introduces speaker identity dependent variation in responses."
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers (e.g., "VoiceBBQ", "Acoustic-based Gender Differentiation") investigate similar acoustic-based bias in spoken LMs, supporting the plausibility of implicit encoding.
- **Break condition:** If acoustic frontends were replaced with anonymized or normalized features (e.g., ASR text-only input), identity-dependent variation should diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Training data imbalances may cause representational disparities for certain accent-gender intersections, leading to lower-quality outputs for underrepresented groups.
- **Mechanism:** Societal and dataset biases result in uneven distribution of speech samples across accents and perceived genders in pretraining corpora. Models learn to associate certain voice identities with lower-resource or stereotyped contexts, degrading response utility for those groups.
- **Core assumption:** The models’ training data reflects real-world inequalities in spoken English representation (e.g., less Eastern European female speech in high-quality instructional contexts).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2] Dataset construction uses six accents from the Edinburgh International Accents corpus, but the models’ training data composition is not analyzed.
  - [abstract] "holding linguistic content constant via voice cloning" implies the observed bias stems from speaker identity, not content differences.
  - [corpus] Evidence is weak; neighboring papers focus on evaluation, not data composition. No direct anchor in this paper.
- **Break condition:** Training on a perfectly balanced, intersectionally representative speech corpus should eliminate the disparity, all else equal.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** LLM-as-a-judge evaluations may underestimate the magnitude of implicit bias due to limited sensitivity to subtle quality degradations in AI-generated responses.
- **Mechanism:** Automated judges (even advanced LLMs) lack human-like perceptual and social sensitivity to detect nuanced differences in actionability and depth. They are more likely to rate responses as interchangeable on coarse scales, missing intersectional patterns.
- **Core assumption:** Human evaluators have a lower detection threshold for subtle helpfulness gaps that correlate with social identity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "LLM judges capture the directional trend of these biases, human evaluators exhibit significantly higher sensitivity, uncovering sharper intersectional disparities."
  - [section 4.3] Human BWS worth parameters show significant Eastern European disadvantage (π=0.114) vs. US English (0.202, p<0.001), while LLM judge BWS found no significant contrasts.
  - [section 4.4] "LLM-as-a-judge frameworks... LLM judges can reliably mirror human perceptions of bias up to a degree."
- **Break condition:** Using evaluation prompts with finer-grained, concept-guided criteria or ensemble human judgments should align LLM-as-a-judge sensitivity closer to human results.

## Foundational Learning
- **Intersectionality in bias measurement**: Needed because the study hypothesizes multiplicative rather than additive combination of accent and gender biases. *Quick check*: How would results differ if analysis only considered main effects of accent or gender separately?
- **Voice cloning for causal isolation**: Voice cloning holds linguistic content constant while varying speaker identity, critical to attribute response differences to acoustic-identity cues rather than lexical variations. *Quick check*: What alternative explanation for bias would be possible without voice cloning, and how does cloning rule it out?
- **Best-Worst Scaling (BWS) and Plackett–Luce modelling**: BWS yields comparative rankings (not absolute scores) and requires Plackett–Luce to estimate significance, key to interpreting human results on subtle bias. *Quick check*: Why might BWS be more sensitive than pointwise Likert ratings for detecting subtle bias?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Voice cloning system (MegaTTS3) → SpeechLLM models (LFM2-Audio, OmniVinci, Qwen3-Omni) → Evaluation suite (LLM judge + Human BWS) → Analysis pipeline (statistics, Plackett–Luce)

**Critical path:** Dataset generation → SpeechLLM inference → LLM judge scoring → Human BWS → Plackett–Luce analysis → Bias detection.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Synthetic vs. natural speech: Voice cloning ensures content control but may not capture all naturalness variations; natural recordings risk content confounding.
- LLM judge vs. human: LLM judges are cheap and scalable but less sensitive; humans are costly but provide ground truth for validation.
- Scale: 2,880 interactions balances model coverage with computational/human evaluation cost.

**Failure signatures:**
- High WER variance across accents (e.g., LFM2-Audio’s 116.2% for Indian English) indicates transcription failures that could confound response quality analysis.
- LLM judge and human BWS worth parameters diverge significantly; low agreement on best/worst selections (<60%).
- Bias only appears in pairwise/BWS but not pointwise scores, suggesting scale insensitivity.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Preliminary Check 1 (Accent/Gender ID): Prompt each SpeechLLM to identify accent/gender from audio to confirm bias is implicit, not explicit.
2. Pointwise LLM-Judge Ratings: Rate each of 2,880 responses on helpfulness, competence, formality, condescension (1–5) to identify coarse patterns.
3. LLM Judge BWS + Human Validation: Run BWS with LLM judge and a separate human BWS on overlapping subsets; compare Plackett–Luce worth parameters to assess judge validity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Why do LLM-as-a-judge frameworks systematically underestimate the magnitude of intersectional bias compared to human evaluators?  
  *Basis in paper:* Human BWS revealed significant accent disparities while LLM judge BWS showed near-uniform worth parameters, despite directional agreement.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper documents the underestimation but does not investigate underlying causes, such as differences in perceptual sensitivity or evaluation criteria between LLMs and humans.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Comparative studies analyzing LLM and human judgment processes, e.g., via think-aloud protocols or probing judge LLM responses, to identify specific bias dimensions that humans detect more acutely.

- **Open Question 2:** To what extent does the intersectional bias generalize beyond the three tested SpeechLLMs (LFM2-Audio, OmniVinci, Qwen3-Omni) and the specific English accents evaluated?  
  *Basis in paper:* The study is limited to three models and six English accents from the EdAcc dataset; all showed bias patterns, but broader applicability is not examined.  
  *Why unresolved:* Without evaluating a wider array of models (e.g., open-source, multilingual) and accents (e.g., African, Southeast Asian), it is unclear if findings are universal or context-specific.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Replicating the controlled evaluation with diverse SpeechLLMs and accent-gender combinations, potentially extending to non-English languages, to test robustness of the bias.

- **Open Question 3:** Which specific acoustic or prosodic features in accented speech correlate with reduced helpfulness in SpeechLLM responses, and how are these features internally represented?  
  *Basis in paper:* Bias is implicit; models do not explicitly recognize accents (identification accuracy near chance) yet produce lower-quality responses. Voice cloning controlled linguistic content, but feature-level drivers are unanalyzed.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper focuses on aggregate outcomes without decomposing accent cues; thus, the precise paralinguistic triggers (e.g., vowel formants, intonation patterns) and their model encoding remain unknown.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Acoustic analyses linking input speech features to response scores, or representational similarity analyses of model hidden states across accent conditions.

- **Open Question 4:** Can bias mitigation strategies effectively reduce the intersectional helpfulness gap in SpeechLLMs without compromising overall response quality?  
  *Basis in paper:* The conclusion advocates shifting to in-domain bias evaluations, implying mitigation is needed, but no interventions are tested or proposed.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper identifies the problem but does not explore solutions; effectiveness of methods like data balancing, prompt engineering, or adversarial training for this bias is unexamined.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Implementing and evaluating mitigation techniques (e.g., fine-tuning on balanced datasets, test-time prompt adjustments) on the same benchmark, measuring changes in disparity and utility metrics.

## Limitations
- Synthetic speech quality may drift across accents/genders in voice cloning, potentially introducing confounds unrelated to identity cues.
- LLM-as-a-judge evaluation shows directional consistency but systematically underestimates bias magnitude; the reliability of automated bias audits is questionable.
- External validity is limited: the study tests only three proprietary SpeechLLMs and six accents from one corpus; generalization to other models, languages, or real-world interactions remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- Existence of intersectional bias: **High** (consistent disparities across multiple metrics and human validation)
- Implicit mechanism (acoustic cues → response variation): **Medium** (supported by near-chance accent ID but lacking direct representation evidence)
- Magnitude of bias (especially intersectional gaps): **Low to Medium** (human BWS shows sharp gaps, but LLM judge discrepancy and synthetic data concerns limit confidence in absolute effect sizes)
- LLM judge reliability for bias detection: **Low** (captures direction but not magnitude; underestimation suggests low trustworthiness)
