# CRaFT: Circuit-Guided Refusal Feature Selection via Cross-Layer Transcoders

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2604.01604  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01604  
- **Reference count**: 32  
- **Primary result**: CRaFT increases attack success rate from 6.7% to 48.2% across multiple jailbreak benchmarks and achieves significantly higher judge scores for specificity and convincingness.

## Executive Summary
CRaFT is a method for identifying the causal features within a language model that govern its refusal to comply with harmful requests. It replaces the conventional reliance on activation magnitude with a circuit-guided approach: using a cross-layer transcoder (CLT) to decompose the model’s computations, constructing an attribution graph of direct effects, and ranking features by their multi‑hop influence on the refusal/compliance logits. Boundary‑critical prompts—where both refusal and compliance tokens are plausible—ensure the analysis isolates the actual decision boundary rather than superficial correlations. On Gemma-3-1B-it, CRaFT dramatically improves jailbreak attack success (from 6.7% to 48.2%) and produces outputs that are rated as more specific and convincing by an LLM judge, outperforming activation‑based and other steering baselines.

## Method Summary
CRaFT first applies a pre-trained CLT to replace each MLP output with a sparse linear combination of features from all layers. For a set of boundary‑critical prompts (selected by the minimum probability of the model’s typical refusal token “I” and compliance token “Okay”), it runs a forward pass with the CLT reconstruction and computes a directed attribution graph: nodes are CLT features and output logits; edge weights are direct effects (partial derivatives) conditioned on cached attention and normalization patterns. The graph is normalized to a matrix \(\tilde{A}\), and each feature’s influence is computed via a Neumann series \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty \tilde{A}^k\) multiplied by the output logit vector. Feature influence scores are averaged across prompts, the top‑ranked feature is selected, and at inference its activation is scaled by a layer‑dependent factor (stronger in later layers) to steer the model toward compliance.

## Key Results
- Attack success rate (LG4-ASR) rises from a baseline of 6.7% to **48.2%** using CRaFT.
- LLM‑as‑a‑Judge (StrongREJECT) scores are significantly higher, indicating more **specific** and **convincing** jailbreak responses.
- CRaFT outperforms activation‑based selection and other steering baselines across WildJailbreak, HarmBench, AdvBench, and SorryBench.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Activation magnitude captures superficial correlations (e.g., topic markers) rather than the causal features that directly influence the refusal/compliance decision. Features strongly activated on harmful prompts may be downstream byproducts of input content, not nodes in the decision circuit. Evidence: [abstract] and [Section 2.2] state that activation‑based selection tends to capture superficial signals.

### Mechanism 2
The CLT‑based attribution graph quantifies multi‑hop influence, capturing cross‑layer dependencies missed by single‑layer or contrastive methods. Direct effects are aggregated via a Neumann series to measure each feature’s total contribution, including indirect pathways. Evidence: [abstract] emphasizes ranking by multi‑hop influence; [Section 3.2] defines the graph and influence computation.

### Mechanism 3
Boundary‑critical prompts—where both refusal and compliance tokens have high probability—isolate the decision boundary within a single semantic context. Tracing both pathways for the same prompt highlights features that tip the balance, avoiding confounds like topic differences between prompt groups. Evidence: [abstract] introduces boundary‑critical prompts; [Section 4.1] defines the boundary score \(s(p)\) and argues against cross‑group confounding.

## Foundational Learning

### Cross‑Layer Transcoder (CLT)
**Why needed**: Enables cross‑layer attribution by decomposing each MLP output into sparse contributions from features in all previous layers.  
**Quick check**: Given a CLT’s decoder weights \(W^{j\to\ell}_{\text{dec}}\), what does a large magnitude value for feature \(k\) at layer \(j\) in the reconstruction of layer \(\ell\)’s MLP output imply about the computational role of feature \((j,k)\)?

### Attribution Graph & Direct Effect
**Why needed**: Formalizes “influence” and allows computation of multi‑hop contributions; without this graph, CRaFT could not differentiate causal from correlative features.  
**Quick check**: If the direct effect from feature node \(s\) to output node \(t\) is near zero, what does that imply about the necessity of feature \(s\) for producing the output associated with \(t\)?

### Boundary‑Critical Prompt
**Why needed**: Shifts from inter‑prompt contrast (harmful vs. benign) to intra‑prompt decision analysis, ensuring identified features are relevant to the refusal *decision* for a given request.  
**Quick check**: Would a prompt with \(P_R=0.99\) and \(P_C=0.01\) be a good boundary‑critical prompt? Why or why not?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Prompt Sampler → Circuit Extractor (using CLT Model) → Influence Scorer → Feature Aggregator → Steering Module

**Critical path**: Boundary prompt selection → Circuit extraction per prompt → Influence computation → Feature ranking → Steering intervention. The path is linear; failure in any step (e.g., CLT reconstruction error) breaks the pipeline.

**Design tradeoffs**
- **CLT vs. SAE**: CLTs enable cross‑layer tracing but require model‑specific training; SAEs are per‑layer and readily available but cannot directly model cross‑layer dependencies.
- **Boundary vs. Cross‑group**: Boundary‑critical sampling avoids topic confounds but is limited to the distribution of the boundary set; cross‑group comparisons cover broader scenarios but mix topic and decision effects.
- **Single vs. Multiple Features**: Steering the top‑1 feature avoids conflicts; intervening on multiple features could amplify effect but risks instability and incoherent outputs.

**Failure signatures**
1. **Degenerate outputs** when steering strength \(\gamma\) is too high (gibberish/repetition), often high LG4‑ASR but low judge scores.
2. **No layer shift**: Selected features remain in high layers (L22+), indicating flawed sampling or influence computation.
3. **Cross‑group contamination**: Using cross‑group influence on boundary prompts yields lower judge scores, suggesting features that only suppress surface refusal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Cost of CLT training**: To what extent can CRaFT be applied to models without pre‑trained CLTs, and how costly is it to train a suitable CLT for a new target model? The paper notes this as a key limitation but does not quantify the trade‑off.
2. **Generalization across harm categories**: Do the refusal features identified by CRaFT generalize across different categories of harmful requests, or are they highly specific to the boundary‑critical prompts used for selection?
3. **Steering multiple features**: What is the effect of steering multiple top‑ranked refusal features simultaneously, and why does the paper default to intervening only on the top‑1 feature? The claim of conflict and instability is not empirically validated.
4. **Judge scores vs. real‑world harm**: Does a higher LLM‑as‑a‑Judge score necessarily correspond to a more harmful or useful jailbreak response, or does CRaFT primarily optimize for superficial coherence?

## Limitations
- Requires a pre‑trained CLT matched to the target model; such resources are scarce and model‑specific.
- Relies on linearization of non‑linear components (attention, normalization) and leaves adjacency normalization/Neumann series convergence details unspecified, introducing unquantified approximation errors.
- Boundary‑critical prompt selection is novel; its robustness across model scales, modalities, and diverse harm types remains unproven.

## Confidence
- **High**: CRaFT improves attack success and judge scores (strong empirical results across multiple benchmarks).
- **Medium**: Activation magnitude fails to identify causal refusal features; cross‑layer multi‑hop influence uncovers refusal circuits (theoretically plausible but limited independent validation).
- **Low**: Boundary‑critical prompts isolate decision‑controlling features (novel approach with weak prior support; improvement could stem from dataset artifact).
