# Infeasibility Aware Large Language Models for Combinatorial Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.01455
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01455
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Infeasibility-aware fine-tuning of Llama 3.1 8B achieves up to 30% accuracy improvement over GPT-5.2 on minor-embedding and yields up to 2× speedup in Feasibility Jump local search.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces an infeasibility-aware framework for applying large language models (LLMs) to combinatorial optimization, focusing on minor-embedding and k-coloring. Existing LLM approaches often ignore infeasibility detection, leading to inefficiencies. The proposed method combines scalable dataset construction using a novel MILP formulation and zero-phase screening, supervised fine-tuning of an LLM to jointly predict solutions or infeasibility, and using the model's outputs as warm starts for a local search heuristic called Feasibility Jump. Experiments show that the fine-tuned model significantly outperforms GPT-5.2, achieving near-perfect accuracy with Best-of-8 sampling, and that warm-starting Feasibility Jump reduces runtime by up to half. The approach generalizes to k-coloring, maintaining balanced performance on satisfiable and unsatisfiable instances.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a three-component pipeline. First, they generate a balanced dataset of 10,000 labeled instances for minor-embedding, spanning multiple graph families (Erdős–Rényi, Barabási–Albert, Watts–Strogatz, random regular). Instances are screened via necessary conditions (zero-phase) to filter obvious infeasibilities, then solved exactly with Gurobi or heuristically with minorminer. Inputs are formatted as edge lists with neighbor-degree statistics, and outputs encode either a feasible embedding or an infeasibility flag. Second, they fine-tune Llama 3.1 8B using LoRA (rank 32, alpha 32) to perform joint solution generation and infeasibility detection. Third, during inference, the model produces multiple candidate outputs (Best-of-N) which are parsed and fed as warm starts to the Feasibility Jump local search algorithm, which repairs or improves solutions within a 30-second budget. The framework is evaluated on both minor-embedding and k-coloring tasks.

## Key Results
- Fine-tuned Llama 3.1 improves overall accuracy by up to 30% compared to GPT-5.2 on minor-embedding, reaching 99.9% accuracy with Best-of-8 sampling.
- LLM-guided warm starts for Feasibility Jump achieve up to 2× speedup over random initialization in reaching feasible solutions.
- The method generalizes to k-coloring, showing balanced accuracy on satisfiable and unsatisfiable instances.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The infeasibility-aware training enables the model to learn both the structure of feasible solutions and the patterns that lead to infeasibility, reducing wasted effort on impossible outputs. Zero-phase screening ensures the training data contains clear examples of both classes, improving the model's discriminative ability. By outputting structured embeddings rather than unstructured text, the model provides high-quality initial points that align with the problem's constraints, allowing the Feasibility Jump heuristic to converge faster. Best-of-N sampling further boosts reliability by selecting the most promising candidate among multiple attempts. The combination of explicit infeasibility detection and warm-starting leverages the LLM's pattern recognition while compensating for its lack of guarantee via classical search.

## Foundational Learning
We outline key concepts underlying the work:

- **Minor-embedding**: Mapping a source graph onto a hardware graph using chains of vertices. *Why needed*: It is the primary optimization problem addressed. *Quick check*: Can you explain how a chain represents a vertex and why chains must not overlap?
- **Infeasibility detection**: Determining whether a problem instance has no solution. *Why needed*: Prevents futile computation and improves decision-making. *Quick check*: Design a necessary condition that could quickly rule out unsatisfiable k-coloring instances.
- **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with LoRA**: Low-rank adaptation of a pre-trained LLM to a downstream task. *Why needed*: Efficiently specialize the model without full retraining. *Quick check*: What are the trade-offs between LoRA and full fine-tuning in terms of memory and performance?
- **Local search (Feasibility Jump)**: Iterative repair heuristic that adjusts assignments to satisfy constraints. *Why needed*: Converts LLM’s approximate outputs into feasible solutions quickly. *Quick check*: Describe the two phases of Feasibility Jump and their purposes.
- **Best-of-N sampling**: Generating N outputs and selecting the best according to a scorer (e.g., feasibility). *Why needed*: Increases probability of obtaining a correct solution from a stochastic model. *Quick check*: How does Best-of-N differ from beam search in terms of diversity?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Graph generation -> Zero-phase screening -> Exact/heuristic solver -> Labeled dataset -> Llama 3.1 + LoRA fine-tuning -> Best-of-N inference -> Feasibility Jump -> Evaluation metrics.

**Critical path**: The main deployment flow: (1) Receive problem instance (graph); (2) Format prompt with edge list and neighbor-degree info; (3) Perform LLM inference with Best-of-N; (4) Parse outputs into embedding or infeasibility flag; (5) If a feasible embedding is produced, pass it to Feasibility Jump as warm start; (6) Run FJ for up to 30 seconds; (7) Return final solution or report failure.

**Design tradeoffs**: Choosing LoRA over full fine-tuning reduces compute and memory at the cost of some expressiveness; Best-of-N improves accuracy but increases inference latency; zero-phase screening accelerates dataset creation but may discard some borderline feasible instances; warm-starting FJ leverages LLM knowledge but depends on output quality.

**Failure signatures**: (a) Model consistently outputs “yes” with invalid embeddings (low UNSAT accuracy); (b) Parsing errors due to format deviations; (c) FJ fails to improve or converges to infeasible states from poor warm starts; (d) Runtime exceeds expected bounds.

**First experiments**: 
1. Verify dataset generation by reproducing MILP solutions on a small subset (e.g., 10 instances) and checking zero-phase filter recall.
2. Evaluate the fine-tuned model on a held-out validation set, measuring SAT/UNSAT accuracy and observing the effect of Best-of-N.
3. Compare warm-start FJ against random starts on a diverse sample of 50 instances, recording time-to-solution and success rate to confirm speedup.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to other NP-hard problems**: To what extent does the infeasibility-aware framework extend to problems like traveling salesman or scheduling, which have structurally different constraints? The current evaluation covers only minor-embedding and k-coloring. Systematic empirical evaluation on a diverse suite would be needed to assess transferability.
2. **Theoretical limits of zero-phase screening**: Can the necessary conditions used for minor-embedding be adapted to other combinatorial problems, and how tight and computationally efficient are such conditions? Deriving analogous properties for canonical problems (e.g., SAT, set cover) and measuring early infeasibility detection rates would help.
3. **Scalability of LLM warm-starts**: How does the performance of LLM-guided warm starts change with problem size and complexity? The paper explores instances up to a few hundred vertices; scaling to thousands may degrade LLM output quality and affect downstream heuristic performance. Scaling experiments across orders of magnitude are required.
4. **Impact of training data distribution**: Does the balance between SAT and UNSAT instances, or the mix of graph families, affect generalization to out-of-distribution problems? The fixed 50/50 split may not be optimal. Ablation studies varying the distribution and curriculum/active learning could improve robustness.
5. **Integration with reinforcement learning or search**: Can reinforcement learning fine-tuning or search-time algorithms (e.g., MCTS) further improve solution quality, especially on hard instances where supervised outputs are often incorrect? The authors suggest this direction but did not implement it; empirical comparison would be valuable.

## Limitations
- Dataset generation details (chain enumeration parameters, graph distribution specifics) are not fully specified, making exact replication challenging.
- Prompt template, tokenization, and output parsing rules are ambiguous, which may cause implementation variance.
- Feasibility Jump parameters and reliance on a proprietary baseline (GPT-5.2) limit reproducibility of speedup claims.
