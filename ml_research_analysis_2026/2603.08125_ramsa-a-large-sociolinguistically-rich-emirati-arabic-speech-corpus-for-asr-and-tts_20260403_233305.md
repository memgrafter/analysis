# Ramsa: A Large Sociolinguistically Rich Emirati Arabic Speech Corpus for ASR and TTS
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.08125
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08125
*   **Reference count:** 23
*   **Primary result:** A 41-hour, sociolinguistically diverse Emirati Arabic corpus establishes zero-shot ASR/TTS baselines; Whisper-large-v3-turbo achieves WER 0.268 and MMS-TTS-Ara achieves WER 0.285 on a 10% test subset.

## Executive Summary
Ramsa is the first large-scale, publicly documented speech corpus designed to support speech technology for Emirati Arabic, a low-resource dialect. It addresses critical gaps in prior resources by providing 41 hours of audio from 157 speakers, with deliberate attention to subdialect variation (Urban, Bedouin, Mountain/Shihhi) and gender balance. The data is sourced from both structured interviews and national television programs, encompassing monologic and dialogic speech formats. The paper establishes initial performance benchmarks through zero-shot evaluation of several ASR and TTS systems on a held-out 10% subset, revealing significant challenges, especially in spontaneous, overlapping speech. This work provides a foundational resource and clear performance targets for future research in Emirati Arabic speech processing.

## Method Summary
The Ramsa corpus combines two primary data sources: (1) interviews conducted by the University of Sharjah, offering controlled monologic speech, and (2) recordings from four national television shows, providing naturalistic dialogic speech with varied acoustic conditions. A 10% subset (246 minutes) was held out for evaluation, featuring 22 speakers (13 male, 9 female) and a mix of subdialects and formats. All audio was segmented into utterances and transcribed using a dialectal orthography that captures phonetic reductions and lexical substitutions as spoken. Benchmark evaluation was conducted in a zero-shot manner: ASR models were used to transcribe the test audio directly, while TTS models generated speech from gold transcripts, which were then back-transcribed by a high-performing ASR system. Performance was measured using word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER) after text normalization.

## Key Results
*   For **ASR**, the best zero-shot performance was achieved by **Whisper-large-v3-turbo** (WER: 0.268, CER: 0.144).
*   For **TTS**, the best results were from **MMS-TTS-Ara** (WER: 0.285, CER: 0.081).
*   Performance varied dramatically by source; dialogic programs with rapid turn-taking (e.g., *Maṭbakh al-Dār*) exhibited error rates exceeding 0.70 for WER, while more structured monologue sources performed substantially better.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Including diverse subdialects and genders enhances model generalization across Emirati Arabic variations.
*   **Mechanism:** Exposure to a wide range of phonetic, lexical, and prosodic features helps models learn invariant representations and reduce dialect-specific bias.
*   **Core Assumption:** Subdialect (Urban, Bedouin, Mountain/Shihhi) and gender-driven variations systematically affect speech characteristics and influence ASR/TTS accuracy.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** [abstract] "spans subdialects such as Urban, Bedouin, and Mountain/Shihhi" and "balanced gender representation"; [section] Section 1 critiques prior corpora for lacking this metadata; Table 3 shows speaker counts and source associations, though Mountain/Shihhi evidence is weak in TV data.
*   **Break Condition:** If underrepresented subdialects (e.g., Mountain/Shihhi) are inadequately sampled, models may fail to generalize to those varieties.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Varied interactional formats and recording conditions improve robustness to real-world speech variability, particularly spontaneous phenomena.
*   **Mechanism:** Monologic recordings provide clear, structured speech for baseline learning, while dialogic segments introduce overlaps and rapid turn-taking, forcing models to handle conversational noise and improve segmentation.
*   **Core Assumption:** Emirati speech in natural settings includes both monologue and dialogue, requiring exposure to this spectrum for reliable performance.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** [abstract] "consists of 91 monologic and 79 dialogic recordings"; [section] Section 3.2 describes format variations; Section 6.1.3 shows higher error rates for dialogic sources like *Maṭbakh al-Dār* (WER ~0.73–0.79).
*   **Break Condition:** If dialogic segments are excessively noisy or poorly transcribed, models may overfit to monologic speech.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Dialectal orthography transcription aligns text with acoustic reality, reducing mismatch between training targets and spoken signals.
*   **Mechanism:** By retaining phonological reductions (e.g., "mashāllah") and substitutions (e.g., /q/→/g/) as produced, transcriptions mirror surface speech, enabling models to learn dialect-specific patterns without normalization noise.
*   **Core Assumption:** MSA-normalized transcriptions would distort dialectal speech; faithful dialectal representation is crucial for low-resource dialects.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** [section] Section 4.2 provides detailed transcription guidelines and examples; the 10% subset follows these (Table 4), though inter-annotator agreement is unreported.
*   **Break Condition:** If transcription consistency is low, models may learn spurious patterns; if downstream tasks require MSA compatibility, this orthography may need alignment steps.

## Architecture Onboarding
The core components are: Audio Files (MP3, variable sampling) -> Utterance Segmentation -> Dialectal Orthography Transcriptions -> Metadata (speaker ID, gender, subdialect, source show, format, acoustic notes). Access paths differ: interview data requires a DUA; broadcast data access is copyright-limited, often providing metadata/analytical files but not full gold transcripts.

**Critical Path:** 1) Request access to the 10% transcribed subset (or full corpus as authorized). 2) Set up evaluation infrastructure (API keys for commercial models, download open-source checkpoints). 3) For ASR: process audio through all models and save outputs. For TTS: generate speech from gold transcripts, then back-transcribe with Whisper. 4) Compute WER/CER by comparing normalized (lowercase, no punctuation) system outputs to gold references.

**Design Tradeoffs:** Dialectal orthography preserves authenticity but increases vocabulary sparsity; normalizing to MSA could improve compatibility with MSA-trained models but loses dialectal fidelity. Zero-shot evaluation offers clean baselines but may not reflect fine-tuned potential; fine-tuning requires more data and compute.

**Failure Signatures:** Disproportionately high error rates in dialogic segments (e.g., *Maṭbakh al-Dār*) indicating poor overlap handling; low performance on Mountain/Shihhi-associated data due to scarcity; annotation inconsistencies inflating WER/CER.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1.  Replicate ASR benchmarks: Run Whisper-large-v3-turbo on the 10% subset with default settings to verify average WER 0.268 and CER 0.144, and per-source trends.
2.  Subdialect sensitivity analysis: Group test utterances by self-identified subdialect (from metadata) and compare error rates to identify challenging varieties, acknowledging underrepresentation.
3.  Format robustness test: Compare model performance (WER/CER) between monologic and dialogic segments using metadata to validate the hypothesis that dialogic speech is harder, and investigate failure cases in overlap-rich clips.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
*   **Question:** How can Ramsa be expanded to achieve truly balanced representation of all Emirati subdialects (Urban, Bedouin, Mountain/Shihhi), genders, and age groups in both data collection and evaluation (e.g., MOS listening tests)?
*   **Basis:** The paper notes current imbalance: Urban speech is overrepresented, while Bedouin and Mountain/Shihhi are underrepresented in TV sources and interview pools. MOS evaluation was impossible due to lack of expert listeners from all subdialects. The conclusion calls for expanded collection and diversified evaluation.
*   **Why Unresolved:** Constraints include University of Sharjah demographics (skewed Urban/Mixed, female), lack of identified TV shows with Mountain/Shihhi speakers, and annotators primarily aged 20–40 from limited backgrounds.
*   **What Evidence Would Resolve:** A feasibility study with strategies for targeted recruitment (e.g., collaborations in Ras Al Khaimah), identification of new media sources, and a plan for recruiting a diverse listener panel. Success measured by revised corpus metadata showing proportional targets and a completed, diverse MOS cohort.

### Open Question 2
*   **Question:** What are the precise acoustic-prosodic and conversational-discourse features in overlap-rich, rapid-turn formats (e.g., *Maṭbakh al-Dār*) that cause the dramatic performance drop, and how can models be specifically adapted?
*   **Basis:** Results show uniformly high error rates for *Maṭbakh al-Dār* (WER ~0.73–0.79 ASR; 0.455–0.579 TTS), described as having "dense, rapid turn-taking with frequent overlaps." The pattern is consistent but not analyzed.
*   **Why Unresolved:** Benchmarking was zero-shot with off-the-shelf models. No fine-grained error analysis (overlap regions, disfluencies) isolates causes (acoustic separation, linguistic parsing, discourse modeling). Adaptation strategies remain speculative.
*   **What Evidence Would Resolve:** Dedicated analysis of high-error segments: (1) automated diarization/overlap detection; (2) prosodic annotation of turn boundaries; (3) error type classification. Model adaptation could then be tested with overlap-augmented data or turn-taking architectures.

### Open Question 3
*   **Question:** To what extent does the choice of dialectal orthography versus normalized orthography (e.g., MSA-based or CODA) impact performance and generalization of zero-shot multilingual ASR/TTS for Emirati Arabic?
*   **Basis:** The paper justifies dialectal orthography for "close correspondence between transcription and acoustic signal" and notes CER is "especially informative" under it. However, benchmark models are likely trained on mixed normalization schemes. Low WER/CER on monologic sources might reflect easier mapping to canonical forms; high errors in conversational speech could be exacerbated by transcriptional variation.
*   **Why Unresolved:** Only one transcription convention was used for training reference and evaluation. No controlled comparison with alternative schemes exists, so reported baselines may reflect transcription artifacts.
*   **What Evidence Would Resolve:** Parallel re-transcription (or post-hoc mapping) of the 10% subset into a normalized scheme (e.g., CODA). Re-evaluate the same models against both versions. Differences would quantify orthography effect. Analyzing model outputs for convergence toward normalized/dialectal forms would reveal biases.

### Open Question 4
*   **Question:** Is the reported difficulty some young annotators had with certain lexical items evidence of active lexical attrition or change in Emirati Arabic, and how does this inter-generational variance impact corpus consistency and model training?
*   **Basis:** The conclusion states: "Recruited annotators fell within the 20–40 age range, and some reported difficulty interpreting some lexical items. This suggests ongoing language change and possible attrition... future annotation efforts would benefit from including speakers and annotators from a wider range of age groups."
*   **Why Unresolved:** The observation is anecdotal, not systematically documented. The paper does not specify which items were problematic, whether they were subdialect-specific, or if difficulty was in comprehension vs. transcription. Scale of attrition and impact on consistency are unknown.
*   **What Evidence Would Resolve:** Sociolinguistic analysis of annotation logs: (1) quantify and catalog "best-guess" transcriptions and disputes tied to low-frequency/archaic terms; (2) correlate items with speaker age/region to map lexical change; (3) measure inter-annotator agreement on these items vs. the whole lexicon. This would define a "variable lexicon" subset for specialized modeling.

### Open Question 5
*   **Question:** What is the upper bound of performance for Emirati Arabic ASR/TTS achievable through targeted fine-tuning (vs. zero-shot), and how does the benefit of Emirati-specific fine-tuning compare to fine-tuning on closely related Gulf dialects or larger multidialectal corpora like Casablanca?
*   **Basis:** The paper establishes zero-shot baselines and states they "leave substantial room for improvement." It references prior work where fine-tuning yielded gains (e.g., on Alsanaa, Casablanca) but provides no fine-tuning experiments on Ramsa itself. The potential for intra-Gulf transfer is unknown.
*   **Why Unresolved:** Without empirical fine-tuning results on the expanding Ramsa corpus, adaptation efficiency and required data size are unknown. Positive transfer from other Gulf dialects is plausible but unmeasured; risk of detrimental interference from dominant dialects (Egyptian) is also unknown.
*   **What Evidence Would Resolve:** Controlled fine-tuning experiments using Ramsa (e.g., 50%, 100%) for ASR/TTS. Compare against models fine-tuned on (a) other Gulf dialect subsets from Casablanca/ADI17, (b) a combined Gulf dataset, and (c) a large multidialectal corpus (e.g., MMS data). Measure absolute improvement, data efficiency, and cross-dialect generalization to held-out Emirati speakers/subdialects.

## Limitations
*   **Subdialect Representation Gaps:** Mountain/Shihhi is absent from TV data and only present as "Mixed" in interviews; gender balance is not equal (59F/98M); self-identified "Mixed" category composition is underspecified.
*   **Transcription Consistency:** No quantitative inter-annotator agreement metrics are reported, leaving the internal consistency of the core dialectal orthography unvalidated.
*   **Zero-Shot Benchmark Scope:** Results are from a small, non-disclosed 10% test subset and do not reflect potential gains from fine-tuning on the full 41-hour corpus.

## Confidence
*   **Claim: The corpus is sociolinguistically rich and diverse.** **Confidence: Medium.** Evidence for Urban/Bedouin is solid, but representation for Mountain/Shihhi is weak; gender balance is present but not equal.
*   **Claim: Varied interactional formats increase robustness but also challenge models.** **Confidence: High.** Direct evidence correlates source/format with performance, showing clear error rate disparities (e.g., *Maṭbakh al-Dār*).
*   **Claim: Dialectal orthography transcription is key to performance.** **Confidence: Low.** This is a core design rationale, but its empirical benefit is unproven due to lack of an ablation study and missing agreement metrics. Error rates could partly reflect transcription noise.
*   **Claim: Reported zero-shot WER/CER values are valid baselines for Emirati Arabic.** **Confidence: Medium.** Methodology is clear and reproducible in principle, but tempered by small/non-disclosed test subset and unavailability of full reference transcripts for exact replication.

## Next Checks
1.  **Audit Subdialect Metadata Distribution:** Request from authors the complete speaker-level metadata for the 10% benchmark subset. Explicitly verify counts and proportions for self-identified Urban, Bedouin, Mountain/Shihhi, and Mixed categories, cross-referenced with gender and source (interview/TV). This validates claimed coverage and identifies skewed test splits.
2.  **Measure Transcription Consistency:** Propose a collaborative re-annotation exercise on a shared sample of the 10% subset audio. Calculate Cohen's Kappa or token error rate between original annotators and a new annotator (or between annotator pairs). A target agreement >85% on tokenization and dialectal representation decisions is needed to support the "faithful transcription" mechanism.
3.  **Conduct a Fine-Tuning Experiment:** Using the full 41-hour corpus (or a larger authorized fraction), fine-tune Whisper-large-v3 on Emirati Arabic. Compare resulting WER/CER on the held-out 10% test set against reported zero-shot scores. A significant improvement (>15-20% relative WER reduction) would empirically demonstrate the corpus's value for in-domain training and contextualize the zero-shot baseline.
