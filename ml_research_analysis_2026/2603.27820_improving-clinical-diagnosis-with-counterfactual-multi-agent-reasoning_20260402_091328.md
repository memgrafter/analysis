# Improving Clinical Diagnosis with Counterfactual Multi-Agent Reasoning
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.27820
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27820
*   **Reference count:** 40
*   **Primary result:** Proposes a counterfactual multi-agent framework that consistently improves diagnostic accuracy over baselines (e.g., +13.2% for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct vs. zero-shot CoT) and yields superior reasoning quality in human evaluation.

## Executive Summary
Clinical diagnosis systems based on large language models often reason over fixed evidence without explicitly testing how findings support a diagnosis, limiting interpretability and reliability. This paper introduces a counterfactual multi-agent framework where specialist agents modify clinical findings (e.g., negate, remove) and quantify the resulting diagnostic confidence change using a Counterfactual Probability Gap (CPG) metric. These counterfactual signals guide multi-round discussions, enabling agents to challenge hypotheses and refine differentials. Evaluated on three clinical benchmarks with seven LLMs, the method achieves consistent accuracy gains over prompting and multi-agent baselines, with the largest improvements in complex cases. Human evaluation by physicians confirms enhanced reasoning quality, including fewer factual errors and higher logical coherence. The CPG metric effectively identifies critical evidence, and the approach works across open-source and closed models, enhancing reliability for potential clinical deployment.

## Method Summary
The system begins with a triage agent that selects up to five relevant specialist roles from a pool of 43 and generates a top-3 differential diagnosis. Specialist agents then receive the case presentation and differential. Each specialist performs counterfactual editing on evidence groups linked to the diagnoses using six operations (negate, remove, replace, weaken, intensify, insert), filters edits with a semantic similarity score, and computes the Counterfactual Probability Gap (CPG) to measure confidence shifts. A discussion manager coordinates multi-round cycles: specialists share CPG-backed arguments, debate, and update diagnoses. After each round, consensus is checked (≥75% agreement); if no consensus after a maximum number of rounds, a judge agent selects the final diagnosis from the initial top-3. A summarizer agent condenses prior rounds to manage context length. The framework requires access to model token-level log probabilities for CPG calculation and uses carefully crafted prompts for all agents.

## Key Results
*   Diagnostic accuracy improved across all three benchmarks (MIMIC, MedCaseReasoning, ER-Reason) and seven LLMs compared to zero/few-shot CoT and multi-agent baselines, with up to +13.2% absolute gain for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on complex cases.
*   Human evaluation by physicians showed the method produces reasoning traces with fewer factual errors, stronger logical coherence, and a 66.7% trust preference over zero-shot CoT.
*   The Counterfactual Probability Gap (CPG) effectively identifies critical evidence; ablation studies removing counterfactual editing caused the largest accuracy drop (e.g., -19.7% on MedCaseReasoning).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Counterfactual case editing forces explicit hypothesis testing.** Specialists generate edited case variants (e.g., negating a symptom) and observe diagnostic confidence shifts. If removing a finding drastically lowers confidence, that finding is critical evidence. This exposes over-reliance on spurious correlations and makes reasoning interpretable. Core assumption: Clinically relevant findings have a measurable causal impact on model diagnostic probability. Evidence anchors: Most probability shifts are positive (diagnosis probability decreases after editing) in 78.8% of MIMIC cases and 89.5% in ER-Reason. Break condition: If clinical findings are highly correlated or redundant, edits may not shift probability despite clinical importance, leading to false negatives in evidence identification.

**Mechanism 2: CPG provides an interpretable signal to guide evidence-grounded discussion.** CPG scores from all specialists are shared; agents prioritize features with high CPG (critical evidence) and challenge hypotheses that rely on low-CPG features. Core assumption: High CPG correlates with clinical necessity; features whose removal collapses a diagnosis are likely essential. Evidence anchors: The Negate operation produces the largest median shift (ΔP=0.408); ablation removing CF case editing causes the largest accuracy drop. Break condition: CPG relies on model probability calibration; poorly calibrated models may produce misleading shifts. CPG may also not capture synergistic evidence where multiple moderate features combine critically.

**Mechanism 3: Multi-round discussion with role-playing refines diagnoses by reconciling conflicting evidence interpretations.** Specialists present CPG-backed arguments; disagreements are debated; a final judge synthesizes evidence, overriding individual biases when counterfactual evidence is strong. Core assumption: Diverse specialist perspectives, when grounded in shared counterfactual evidence, will converge on the diagnosis that best fits all critical findings. Evidence anchors: Specialists correct more incorrect diagnoses than they convert correct ones into incorrect ones (32.3% in MIMIC); reasoning traces have fewer factual errors and higher coherence. Break condition: If specialists misinterpret counterfactual results or CPG signals are noisy, discussion may entrench rather than resolve disagreements. Judge selection from fixed DDx may force suboptimal choices.

## Architecture Onboarding
Triage Agent → Specialist Agents → Discussion Manager → Judge Agent → Summarizer Agent

Critical path: Case input → Triage (selects specialists + top-3 DDx) → Specialist report generation → **Round 0**: Each specialist runs counterfactuals, computes CPG, proposes initial diagnosis → Summarizer updates history → Consensus check (≥75%) → If no consensus: **Round ≥1**: Specialists review summary, answer directed questions, generate new counterfactuals, update diagnosis → Repeat until consensus or max rounds → Judge final selection (from initial DDx) → Output final diagnosis + reasoning trace.

Design tradeoffs: More specialists/rounds improve evidence coverage but increase cost/latency (system averages ~2.3 rounds, caps specialists at 5). Restricting judge to initial top-3 DDx prevents degeneration but may miss rare diseases outside initial list. CPG requires token-level log probabilities, excluding many closed APIs without probability outputs. Automatically grouping findings for editing may miss nuanced clinical relationships; manual curation would be more accurate but not scalable.

Failure signatures: Low consensus rate + high diagnosis change rate indicates unstable reasoning or noisy CPG signals. High CPG but wrong final diagnosis suggests counterfactual editing identified critical features but synthesis failed. Judge override of strong specialist consensus without clear CPG justification. All specialists anchoring on same non-discriminating feature. Insert operation causing largest CPG (as in MIMIC) may indicate model oversensitivity to new information.

First 3 experiments:
1. Ablation: Remove counterfactual editing → Expect largest accuracy drop (per paper: -19.7% absolute on MedCaseReasoning). Validates CPG-driven evidence testing as core contribution.
2. Vary number of discussion rounds (R) → Test if performance plateaus; paper uses up to 3 rounds. Expect marginal gains after 2 rounds, excessive rounds cause drift.
3. Compare CPG selection strategies: Random edits vs. high-CPG vs. low-CPG → High-CPG should drive best performance, confirming that informative counterfactuals are key.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   How can counterfactual case editing be adapted for multi-modal clinical data (medical images, time-series vital signs) where textual "edits" are not straightforward?
*   How can structured medical knowledge (e.g., disease ontologies, causal graphs, clinical guidelines) be integrated to ensure the medical correctness of counterfactual reasoning and prevent implausible hypothesis testing?
*   What alternative methods can approximate the Counterfactual Probability Gap (CPG) for LLMs that do not expose token-level probability outputs, enabling broader adoption of the approach?
*   Does the counterfactual multi-agent approach systematically reduce specific cognitive biases (e.g., anchoring, premature closure) in clinical reasoning compared to standard prompting, and through what mechanism?
*   How robust are the reported performance gains to larger-scale evaluations with more diverse clinical populations and to variations in hyperparameters (e.g., number of specialists, discussion rounds, counterfactual thresholds)?

## Limitations
*   CPG may fail to identify critical evidence when clinical findings are highly correlated or redundant, potentially leading to false negatives.
*   Final diagnosis is restricted to the initial top-3 differential, limiting recall for rare or unexpected conditions if the triage agent misses the correct diagnosis.
*   System relies on multiple specialist agents and multi-round discussion, incurring potentially prohibitive latency and API costs for real-world deployment; scalability is not fully quantified.

## Confidence
*   **Mechanism 1 (Counterfactual editing forces hypothesis testing):** Medium-High. Strong evidence shows probability shifts after edits, but threat from feature correlation remains unresolved.
*   **Mechanism 2 (CPG guides evidence-grounded discussion):** Medium-High. Ablation results and shift statistics support CPG’s role, but confidence is tempered by reliance on well-calibrated model probabilities.
*   **Mechanism 3 (Multi-round discussion refines consensus):** Medium. Human evaluation supports improved reasoning quality, but disentangling the contribution of multi-agent structure versus counterfactual signal is difficult.

## Next Checks
*   Test CPG under feature correlation: Construct synthetic cases with highly correlated findings (e.g., fever & tachycardia) and measure whether CPG correctly identifies both as critical or discounts one due to redundancy.
*   Analyze judge agent bias: Audit cases where the judge overrides specialist consensus. Determine how often the judge’s decision is correct and whether it correlates with specific CPG patterns or initial DDx rankings.
*   Measure cost/latency vs. accuracy trade-off: Systematically vary the number of specialists (1–5) and maximum discussion rounds (1–5). Plot accuracy gains against estimated token cost and wall-clock time to identify practical deployment sweet spots.
