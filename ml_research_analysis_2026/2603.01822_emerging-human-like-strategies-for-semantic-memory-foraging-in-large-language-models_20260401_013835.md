# Emerging Human-like Strategies for Semantic Memory Foraging in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01822
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01822
- **Reference count:** 34
- **Primary result:** LLMs replicate human-like semantic foraging patterns but switch categories less frequently, with distinct internal signatures in mid-to-late layers.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) may internalize human-like memory search strategies, a hypothesis tested via the Semantic Fluency Task (SFT). In this paradigm, participants (or models) generate lists of items (e.g., animals) and the sequence of category switches reflects foraging behavior. The authors analyze Llama‑3 instruct models of various sizes and find that LLMs produce transition matrices highly correlated with human data (ρ=0.701), indicating a shared preference for within‑category clustering. However, LLMs switch semantic categories less often than humans (mean switch ratio 0.4 vs. 0.55). Mechanistic interpretability reveals a layer‑dependent pattern: in mid‑to‑late layers, the model’s output distribution suppresses within‑category probability amplification when a switch occurs, while between‑category probabilities rise modestly. This behavioral regularity is mirrored internally: linear probes trained on residual stream activations can classify switch versus non‑switch events with AUROC up to 0.98, especially when trained on contrastive data designed to isolate convergent and divergent decisions. These findings suggest that LLMs not only mimic human foraging at the output level but also encode such strategic decisions in separable internal states, opening possibilities for targeted steering.

## Method Summary
The study uses human SFT sequences from prior work (699 animal lists) and category norms (SNAFU) to define semantic clusters and switch events. LLM data comes from instruction‑tuned Llama‑3 models (1B, 3B, 8B, 70B) prompted with a standardized template: “Without repeating yourself, continue your response as a list of comma separated animal names that come to mind: <Animal 1>, …”. The model generates up to 35 comma‑separated responses; only sequences where all items are valid animal names present in the norms are retained.

Behavioral metrics include the transition matrix (category co‑occurrences) and the switch ratio (fraction of consecutive items with disjoint category sets), compared across humans and LLMs. Mechanistic analysis employs logitlens: at every generation step, the final unembedding matrix is applied to each layer’s residual stream to obtain a full vocabulary distribution. The vocabulary is split into within‑category tokens (belonging to the current animal’s cluster) and between‑category tokens; average probabilities are computed and aligned around switch events. Additionally, linear probes (logistic regression after PCA) are trained on residual activations to predict whether the next token is a switch. Two data regimes are used: natural human sequences (labels from norms) and engineered contrastive data where the next animal is deliberately chosen as either highly similar (convergent) or dissimilar (divergent) with prompts encouraging staying or switching. Performance is evaluated via Spearman correlation, Mann‑Whitney tests, layer‑wise z‑scored probability differences, and classification AUROC.

## Key Results
- Human and LLM transition matrices show a strong positive correlation (ρ=0.701), confirming convergent clustering.
- LLMs switch categories significantly less often than humans (mean switch ratio 0.4 vs. 0.55).
- Mid‑to‑late layers exhibit a suppression of within‑category probability amplification during switch events; this signature is linearly decodable (probe AUROC up to 0.98 on contrastive data).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Output distribution signature during switches
- **Claim:** LLMs exhibit a distinct output distributional signature during category switches, where the probability mass for within‑category tokens is suppressed and between‑category tokens rises.
- **Mechanism:** At the point of a switch event (as defined by category norms), the model’s final‑layer logitlens shows a reduction in the averaged probability of tokens belonging to the previous semantic cluster and a relative increase for tokens belonging to new clusters.
- **Core assumption:** The model’s output distribution over the vocabulary reflects an internal competition between continuing a convergent cluster and initiating a divergent switch.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2.1, Figure 2B‑D] Shows z‑scored probability differences centered on switch events and layer‑wise separation of within/between category probabilities.
  - [abstract] Mentions “within‑category probability amplification during convergence is suppressed during switches.”
- **Break condition:** If the probability distributions for within‑category and between‑category tokens do not diverge significantly at switch points compared to non‑switch points (e.g., Mann‑Whitney p > 0.05), the identified signature is absent.

### Mechanism 2: Linear probe decodability of strategic states
- **Claim:** Switch (divergent) versus non‑switch (convergent) events are linearly decodable from mid‑layer residual stream activations, with performance peaking in intermediate layers.
- **Mechanism:** A simple linear probe (logistic regression after PCA) trained on contrastive data (prompts explicitly instructing convergence/divergence) can classify the nature of the next token’s semantic relationship with high accuracy (AUROC up to 0.98), indicating these strategic states form distinct, separable representations.
- **Core assumption:** The model’s internal representations (pre‑final layer) encode a strategic dimension related to semantic foraging that is accessible via linear readouts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2.2, Figure 3] Presents layer‑wise AUROC curves for probes trained on neutral, convergent, and divergent prompt datasets, showing peak performance in middle layers.
  - [abstract] States “Linear probes trained on contrastive data classify switch versus non‑switch events from internal representations with high accuracy (AUROC up to 0.98).”
- **Break condition:** Probe performance close to chance (AUROC ~0.5) across all layers, especially in the mid‑to‑late layers where effects are reported.

### Mechanism 3: Behavioral replication of human foraging patterns
- **Claim:** At a population/behavioral level, LLMs replicate the human pattern of preferring within‑category transitions (clustering) but do so more extremely, resulting in fewer switches.
- **Mechanism:** Analysis of full generated sequences shows a strong correlation (ρ=0.701) in category transition matrices between humans and LLMs, but a significantly lower mean switch ratio for LLMs (0.4 vs. human 0.55).
- **Core assumption:** The frequency of category switches is a behavioral proxy for the balance of convergent/divergent strategies, and alignment in transition probabilities indicates shared underlying search dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.1, Figure 1B‑D] Reports the transition matrix correlation and switch ratio distributions with statistical tests.
  - [abstract] Summarizes “LLMs replicate human‑like transition patterns (correlation ρ=0.701) but switch categories less frequently.”
- **Break condition:** No significant difference in switch ratios between LLMs and humans (Mann‑Whitney p > 0.05), or a negative correlation in transition matrices.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept 1: Semantic Fluency Task (SFT)**
- **Why needed here:** It is the experimental paradigm defining the “memory foraging” behavior under study. Understanding its constraints (timed, list generation, category norms) is essential to interpret switch/clustering definitions and sequence analysis.
- **Quick check question:** What are the two primary behavioral signatures (convergent/divergent) derived from human SFT performance and what cognitive theory explains them?

**Concept 2: Mechanistic Interpretability (Logitlens & Probing)**
- **Why needed here:** These are the core methods used to move from behavioral observation to internal mechanism. Logitlens tracks distributional changes across layers; probing tests for decodable representations. Without this, claims remain at the output level only.
- **Quick check question:** What is the key methodological difference between using logitlens (examining output distributions at each layer) and training a linear probe (learning a classifier on activations)?

**Concept 3: Category Norms & Switch Definition**
- **Why needed here:** The entire analysis hinges on objectively labeling sequence tokens as “within‑category” or “between‑category” relative to the preceding context. This operationalization ties model outputs to human semantic structure.
- **Quick check question:** According to the paper, how is a “switch event” formally defined using the SNAFU animal category norms?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input prompt → Llama Transformer (embedding → multiple attention+MLP blocks in layers → residual stream at each layer) → Final layer norm → Output logits. Key inspection points: residual stream activations at each layer and final logitlens applied to those activations to get per‑layer probability distributions.

**Critical path:** To replicate the core analysis: (1) Generate sequences for a given prompt set. (2) For each token in a sequence, use category norms to label the next token as `switch` or `non‑switch`. (3) For logitlens: at each generation step, apply the final layer’s unembedding matrix to *every* layer’s residual stream to get per‑layer vocab distributions. (4) Partition the vocabulary at each step into `within‑category` and `between‑category` sets based on the *current* category. (5) Average probabilities within these sets and align by relative position to switch events. (6) For probing: extract a fixed‑layer residual stream vector for each `(context, next‑token)` pair from contrastive datasets, reduce dimensionality (PCA), and train/test a linear classifier.

**Design tradeoffs:** Using natural human sequences provides ecological validity but introduces noise in switch labels (due to norm incompleteness). Using engineered contrastive prompts (convergent/divergent instructions) creates cleaner training data for probes but may not reflect the model’s default, un‑prompted strategy. The paper uses both to triangulate.

**Failure signatures:**
- No clear layer‑wise separation in logitlens plots (Figure 2C).
- Probe AUROC stays near 0.5 across layers and prompt types.
- The model’s switch ratio is identical to or higher than the human mean, contradicting the main behavioral finding.
- Transition matrix correlation between humans and LLMs is low (ρ < 0.3).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Behavioral Replication:** Generate sequences for the “neutral” prompt across model sizes. Compute per‑sequence switch ratios and the population transition matrix. Replicate the finding: LLMs show a strong positive within‑category diagonal and a switch ratio significantly below the human mean (0.55).
2. **Logitlens Signature:** For the 70B model, apply logitlens to a sample of sequences containing clear switch events. Replicate Figure 2C/D: show that in late layers (>39), within‑category probability is high during non‑switches but suppressed during switches, while between‑category probability shows a smaller relative increase.
3. **Probe Decodability:** Using the provided contrastive dataset generation logic (Section A.2), create a dataset of `(context, next‑token, label)` pairs where the label is `convergent` or `divergent`. Train a logistic regression on PCA‑reduced activations from a mid‑layer (e.g., layer 40). Measure AUROC on held‑out data; expect >0.95 for the 70B model on data generated with congruent prompts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How do human response times during the Semantic Fluency Task correlate with internal layer activation patterns in LLMs performing the same task?**  
   - *Basis in paper:* The conclusion states that “Future work will relate human processing details, e.g., time to generate responses, to the patterns we reported…”  
   - *Why unresolved:* The current study did not collect or analyze human response time data alongside model internal states, leaving the temporal dynamics unconnected.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Simultaneous recording of human response times and comparison to time‑synchronized layer activations in LLMs generating SFT sequences, possibly using dynamic time warping or cross‑correlation analysis.

2. **Why do LLMs switch semantic categories less frequently than humans in SFT, despite similar transition probability patterns?**  
   - *Basis in paper:* Results show LLMs have a lower mean switch ratio (0.4) than humans (0.55), but the paper does not investigate the underlying cause of this quantitative difference.  
   - *Why unresolved:* The behavioral observation is documented, but no hypotheses are tested regarding whether this stems from differences in training objectives, dataset statistics, or optimization pressures.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Comparative analyses across models with varying training regimes, or controlled experiments manipulating factors like sequence length penalties or diversity incentives, to isolate what drives the reduced switching.

3. **Which specific architectural components (e.g., attention heads, MLP layers) within transformer models causally generate convergent versus divergent semantic search behaviors?**  
   - *Basis in paper:* The study uses probing and logitlens to identify layer‑wise patterns, but does not pinpoint the precise mechanisms or sub‑circuits responsible for switch/non‑switch decisions.  
   - *Why unresolved:* Probing reveals correlations but not causation; the paper acknowledges “identifiable patterns” but not the underlying computational primitives.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Causal intervention studies (e.g., ablating specific heads, steering vectors) targeting layers where switch classification peaks, to see if these manipulations selectively disrupt convergence/divergence without affecting overall fluency.

4. **Are the identified semantic foraging signatures (e.g., mid‑to‑late layer amplification of within‑category probabilities) generalizable beyond Llama models to other transformer families?**  
   - *Basis in paper:* The analysis is restricted to Llama‑3.3 models of various sizes; no other architectures (e.g., GPT, Claude) are examined, limiting claims about universality.  
   - *Why unresolved:* The paper suggests these patterns may be “identifiable” in LLMs generally, but without cross‑architectural replication, it’s unclear if they are specific to Llama’s training or design.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it:* Replicating the logitlens and probing pipeline on multiple model families, checking for consistent layer‑wise patterns and classifier performance across architectures.

## Limitations
- Human SFT sequences and SNAFU norms are not publicly available, hindering exact replication.
- LLM generation settings (temperature, tokenization, comma handling) are unspecified, potentially affecting sequence validity and switch ratio measurements.
- Binary switch definition ignores partial category overlap, and logitlens approximations may introduce noise into the distribution analysis.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate behavioral analyses using an independent set of animal category norms (e.g., Battig & Montague standards) and compute switch ratios with a definition that acknowledges partial overlaps to test robustness of the human‑LLM difference.
2. Extend the logitlens pipeline to at least two non‑Llama architectures (e.g., GPT‑2, GPT‑3.5) to verify whether the within‑category probability suppression during switches generalizes across model families.
3. Train nonlinear probes (e.g., shallow MLP) on residual activations from natural human sequences (without contrastive augmentation) to gauge the intrinsic linear separability of switch states.
