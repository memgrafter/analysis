# PLACID: Privacy-preserving Large language models for Acronym Clinical Inference and Disambiguation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.23678
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23678
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: A cascaded pipeline using a general detection model and a biomedical expansion model achieves ~0.81 expansion accuracy on the GLADIS biomedical benchmark without transmitting PHI.

## Executive Summary
Clinical acronym disambiguation is critical for patient safety, but cloud-based LLMs risk leaking protected health information (PHI). PLACID proposes a privacy-preserving, on-device solution that decomposes the task into two stages: first, a general-purpose small LLM detects acronyms in clinical text; second, a biomedical fine-tuned model expands those acronyms using context. Evaluated on the GLADIS benchmark, the cascaded pipeline reaches expansion accuracy of ~0.81 (ROUGE-L), substantially outperforming a single-pass approach (~0.655) while keeping all data on the device. The work demonstrates that accurate clinical NLP can be achieved with small, locally-run models, offering a viable path for HIPAA-compliant deployment.

## Method Summary
PLACID employs a zero-shot, two-mode inference framework. In single-pass mode, a single model (e.g., Gemma2, Llama, Mistral) receives a clinical text and must detect and expand acronyms in one step. In cascaded mode, a general model (2–7B parameters) first identifies acronym occurrences, then a domain-specific biomedical model (e.g., MedGemma, BioMistral) performs expansion using the original context and the detected acronym. Both modes use strict JSON output prompts with no exemplars, mimicking real-world constraints. Models run on Apple Silicon (M4-Max) via the MLX framework, with 4-bit quantization for 7B models to fit on-device memory. Performance is measured with BLEU, METEOR, and ROUGE-L against GLADIS ground truth.

## Key Results
- General-purpose models achieve high acronym detection accuracy (~0.988) but low expansion accuracy (~0.655) on biomedical data.
- Biomedical fine-tuned models alone achieve higher expansion accuracy (~0.77–0.81 ROUGE-L).
- The cascaded pipeline (general detector + biomedical expander) yields expansion accuracy of ~0.81, demonstrating that the two-stage approach effectively combines broad detection with domain expertise while preserving privacy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Decomposing acronym disambiguation into detection (general model) and expansion (biomedical model) leverages the strengths of each, improving accuracy while maintaining privacy.
- **Mechanism**: A general-purpose small model first isolates acronym strings; its broad linguistic training yields high detection accuracy. The detected acronym and original context are then passed to a biomedical fine-tuned model, whose specialized training on medical literature enables accurate, context-aware expansion.
- **Core assumption**: Detection is primarily syntactic/pattern-matching, which general models excel at, whereas expansion requires deep semantic and domain knowledge exclusive to biomedical models.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract describes general detection and biomedical expansion; results show high detection (~0.988) but low expansion for general models (~0.655), and improved expansion (~0.81) with biomedical models in the cascade.
- **Break condition**: Failure in the detection stage (missed or false acronym) halts the cascade; sequential latency also increases.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Biomedical fine-tuning imbues small models with the structured knowledge of UMLS and clinical notation, enabling them to resolve ambiguous acronyms by weighing contextual cues against a vast internal sense inventory.
- **Mechanism**: Fine-tuning on datasets like MedMentions teaches models associations between acronyms, possible expansions (senses), and clinical contexts that differentiate them (e.g., "MS" as "Mitral Stenosis" vs. "Multiple Sclerosis"). This creates a dense, context-sensitive mapping in the model’s weights.
- **Core assumption**: General models severely under-represent clinical jargon and UMLS-linked concepts, so their parametric knowledge is insufficient for reliable expansion.
- **Evidence anchors**: Results show expansion accuracy remains robust for biomedical fine-tuned models; the paper notes general models lack deep domain knowledge; example logs show MedGemma weighing semantic expansions against context.
- **Break condition**: Encountering an acronym with an out-of-vocabulary sense or context too sparse/novel for the model to disambiguate.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Strict zero-shot prompting, without task-specific examples or chain-of-thought, forces models to rely solely on parametric knowledge and raw inference, providing a valid assessment of real-world utility given limited input.
- **Mechanism**: The evaluation uses minimalist prompt templates requesting only JSON output, with no exemplars or detailed reasoning steps. This mirrors a live system receiving raw clinician notes and prevents performance inflation from prompt engineering.
- **Core assumption**: In clinical use, clinicians provide raw text; system performance must derive from the model’s inherent knowledge and the given context alone.
- **Evidence anchors**: The paper explicitly states they employed zero-shot to avoid artificial inflation and to mimic real-world limited input; shown prompt templates are minimal; neighboring papers often use few-shot, highlighting the contrast.
- **Break condition**: Acronyms requiring multi-hop reasoning or external knowledge beyond the model’s training may fail where RAG-augmented or few-shot systems could succeed.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input Parser & Formatter -> General Detection Model -> Biomedical Expansion Model -> Output Validator & Formatter -> Inference Runtime (MLX on Apple Silicon)
- **Critical path**: Clinical Text -> (Prompt 1) -> General Model (detect acronym) -> (Prompt 2 with acronym) -> Biomedical Model (expand) -> JSON Output
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Cascaded vs. Single-Pass: Cascaded (~0.81 accuracy) needs two inferences; Single-Pass (~0.655) is faster but weaker.
  - Model size: 2B–10B balances on-device feasibility and capability; 7B models require 4-bit quantization.
  - Zero-shot vs. Fine-tuning: Zero-shot simplifies deployment but caps performance; future work aims for a single fine-tuned model to remove cascading overhead.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Detection failure: No acronym returned or non-acronym string; cascade halts.
  - Expansion failure: Low ROUGE-L/BLEU; plausible but incorrect expansion (especially for "overshadowed" instances).
  - Confidence mismatch: High confidence for incorrect expansions (observed overconfidence).
  - System failure: Apple Foundation model safety guardrails blocking output on clinical text.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline Single-Pass**: Run all general models (Gemma2:2B, Llama3.2:3B, Mistral7B, Foundation) on the 1K general-domain and ~2K biomedical single-acronym subsets. Measure detection and expansion accuracy. Expect high detection, low expansion on biomedical data.
  2. **Cascaded Implementation**: Wire Gemma2:2B (detector) to MedGemma (expander). Run on full 6.3K biomedical cascaded dataset. Measure expansion accuracy (ROUGE-L). Expect jump to ~0.81.
  3. **Quantization & Hardware Check**: Convert Mistral7B and BioMistral7B to 4-bit MLX. Run 20 sample prompts comparing full-precision (if possible) vs. quantized outputs. Document memory usage and latency per inference to validate deployment feasibility.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1**: Can a single, fine-tuned on-device model match the expansion accuracy of the cascaded pipeline without sacrificing detection performance?  
  *Basis*: The authors propose future work on a "fully fine-tuned, single-pass model".  
  *Why unresolved*: Unified model may struggle with both tasks due to domain-knowledge deficit in general models.  
  *What evidence would resolve*: Compare a hybrid fine-tuned model against the cascade on the GLADIS benchmark under identical constraints.
- **Question 2**: How can overconfidence and lack of interpretability in model-generated confidence scores be mitigated for safe clinical deployment?  
  *Basis*: The paper observes pronounced overconfidence and black-box internals.  
  *Why unresolved*: Uncalibrated scores could mislead clinicians; no calibration methods tested.  
  *What evidence would resolve*: Evaluate confidence calibration techniques (e.g., temperature scaling) and conduct a human study on trust and error detection.
- **Question 3**: To what extent does the PLACID pipeline generalize beyond the biomedical GLADIS dataset to other clinical domains (e.g., primary care notes, surgical reports) and non-English texts?  
  *Basis*: Evaluation was restricted to biomedical subset; general-domain test set was small and non-clinical.  
  *Why unresolved*: Clinical language varies across specialties; performance on PubMed abstracts may not reflect real-world EHR diversity.  
  *What evidence would resolve*: Cross-domain evaluation on multiple clinical corpora (e.g., MIMIC-III) and multilingual datasets, measuring accuracy degradation.
- **Question 4**: Does integrating Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) into the cascaded pipeline significantly improve expansion accuracy for rare or low-resource acronyms compared to the current zero-shot approach?  
  *Basis*: Authors propose RAG integration as future work to bridge the domain-knowledge gap.  
  *Why unresolved*: RAG could provide dynamic, context-specific knowledge missing from static model weights.  
  *What evidence would resolve*: An ablation study augmenting the biomedical model with a RAG module using a medical knowledge base, comparing accuracy on low-frequency acronyms.

## Limitations
- The GLADIS dataset is not publicly released, hindering direct verification of reported results.
- Cascaded prompt format is underspecified (e.g., exact placement of the detected acronym), introducing ambiguity in replication.
- Apple Foundation model is proprietary and inaccessible, making that comparison point irreproducible.
- Decoding parameters (temperature, top_p) and the exact JSON output schema are omitted, risking parsing inconsistencies.
- Quantization effects on 7B models were not rigorously benchmarked; potential accuracy degradation remains unquantified.
- Zero-shot evaluation may understate model capabilities; few-shot or fine-tuning could improve results.
- Generalizability beyond the GLADIS biomedical subset is unexplored.
