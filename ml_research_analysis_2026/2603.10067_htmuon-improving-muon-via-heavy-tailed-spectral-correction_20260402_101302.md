# HTMuon: Improving Muon via Heavy-Tailed Spectral Correction

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.10067  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10067  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: HTMuon modifies Muon by raising momentum matrix singular values to a power p∈(0,1), yielding heavier-tailed updates and improving perplexity by up to 0.98 on LLaMA-60M while matching convergence theory.

## Executive Summary
HTMuon is a plug‑in enhancement to the Muon optimizer that corrects its tendency to produce orthogonalized, light‑tailed updates, which the authors argue limits generalization under heavy‑tailed spectral regularization (HT‑SR) theory. By applying a power transformation p∈(0,1) to the singular values of the momentum matrix, HTMuon preserves the inter‑dependency structure captured by the singular vectors while inducing a heavier‑tailed distribution of updates. Experiments on LLaMA (60M) pretraining and ResNet/ViT image classification show consistent gains over Muon, Adam, and COSMOS, with perplexity reductions of up to 0.98 and higher accuracy. The method retains Muon’s theoretical convergence rate, and efficient SVD approximations keep overhead manageable.

## Method Summary
HTMuon builds directly on Muon, which uses momentum and an orthogonalization step to produce updates that are uncorrelated across parameters. The authors observe that this orthogonalization suppresses heavy‑tailedness in the spectrum of weight updates, potentially harming generalization according to HT‑SR theory. HTMuon intervenes after the momentum matrix M_t is formed: it computes the singular value decomposition M_t = U Σ V^T, raises the singular values to a power p ∈ (0,1) to obtain Σ' = Σ^p, and reconstructs the update using the original singular vectors U and V. This operation skews the distribution of updates toward heavier tails while keeping the directions determined by the data‑driven singular vectors. The resulting optimizer is a drop‑in replacement for Muon, with the same hyperparameters except for the new exponent p, which is typically set to 0.125 after a modest grid search. Theoretical analysis shows that the convergence rate remains comparable to Muon’s under standard assumptions.

## Key Results
- On LLaMA‑60M (C4), HTMuon reduces perplexity by up to 0.98 relative to Muon and outperforms Adam and COSMOS.
- HTMuon yields higher test accuracy on ResNet and ViT image classification benchmarks.
- The method preserves Muon’s convergence rate theory and acts as a plug‑in for Muon variants; efficient SVD approximations incur only minor performance trade‑offs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Muon generates updates via an orthogonalization process that, while improving conditioning, inadvertently flattens the heavy‑tailed spectral distribution of the gradients. HT‑SR theory suggests that heavier‑tailed update distributions correlate with better generalization. HTMuon corrects this by directly manipulating the singular values of the momentum matrix: raising them to a power p<1 amplifies smaller singular values relative to large ones, thereby creating a heavier tail in the reconstructed update matrix. This retains the beneficial interdependencies captured by the singular vectors (which encode the geometry of the gradient space) but counteracts Muon’s over‑smoothing of the spectrum. As a result, HTMuon maintains the convergence properties of Muon while aligning the update distribution with the desiderata of HT‑SR, leading to empirical improvements across language and vision tasks.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (data flow):  
Input gradients → Momentum accumulation (M_t) → SVD: M_t = U Σ V^T → Exponentiate singular values (Σ' = Σ^p) → Reconstruct update matrix = U Σ' V^T → Apply learning rate → Weight update

**Critical path**: The sequence from gradient computation to weight update; the SVD and power step are essential for HTMuon’s effect.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Accuracy vs. cost*: Exact SVD is computationally heavy; approximations (randomized SVD, power iteration) can reduce overhead but may distort the heavy‑tailed property.
- *Choice of p*: Smaller p yields heavier tails but may destabilize training; p requires tuning per task.
- *Memory*: Storing U, Σ, V triples memory compared to Muon; trade‑off with model size.

**Failure signatures**:
- Divergence or plateauing if p is too small (over‑heavy tails) or too large (insufficient correction).
- Excessive runtime or memory leading to out‑of‑memory errors on large models.
- Degraded performance when using low‑quality SVD approximations that misrepresent singular vectors.

**First experiments**:
1. **Main result replication**: Train LLaMA‑60M on C4 with HTMuon (p=0.125) and compare perplexity against Muon, Adam, and COSMOS.
2. **p‑sensitivity sweep**: Evaluate a range of p values (e.g., 0.05, 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0) on at least two tasks (language, vision) to assess robustness.
3. **Approximation benchmark**: Replace exact SVD with randomized SVD or iterative methods, measure wall‑clock time and final accuracy to quantify the cost‑benefit trade‑off.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How does HTMuon scale to models larger than 1B parameters and larger datasets?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "Due to limited computational resources, we have not evaluated HTMuon on larger models than 1B or larger-scale datasets; we leave this to future work."  
- **Why unresolved**: Current experiments stop at 1B; spectral dynamics and per‑step overhead could differ dramatically at greater scales, potentially altering the observed gains.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Train HTMuon on standard large‑scale benchmarks (e.g., LLaMA‑7B/13B on The Pile) and compare perplexity, stability, and convergence speed against Muon and other optimizers.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can we further reduce the SVD overhead of HTMuon while preserving its performance benefits?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] "Exploring better numerical methods to accelerate HTMuon is also part of future work."  
- **Why unresolved**: HTMuon incurs higher per‑step cost than Muon; more efficient approximations (e.g., randomized SVD, power iteration) might lower cost but their impact on the heavy‑tailed correction effect is unknown.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implement and test alternative low‑complexity approximations (e.g., iterative methods, Chebyshev polynomials) and measure the trade‑off between runtime and final model quality across multiple tasks.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What is the theoretical justification for choosing the hyperparameter p, and can it be set adaptively?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper sets p=0.125 after limited grid search; Lemma 4.1 links p to the PL exponent but offers no guidance for an optimal value.  
- **Why unresolved**: p is manually tuned; its ideal value may depend on model size, layer depth, or training stage. A principled method for selecting p—either static or dynamic—is lacking.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Derive a generalization bound that explicitly involves p; or propose an adaptive scheme that adjusts p based on spectral statistics during training and demonstrates improved results without extra tuning.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Is the improvement from HTMuon solely due to inducing heavy‑tailed updates, or does preserving data‑driven singular values contribute additional gains?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] HTMuon HT (fixed heavy‑tailed spectrum) outperforms Muon but underperforms HTMuon, suggesting both heavy tails and data‑driven singular values matter.  
- **Why unresolved**: The relative contribution of each factor is unclear; understanding this could lead to simpler or more effective designs.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Run controlled experiments comparing Muon, HTMuon, HTMuon HT, and hybrids that mix data‑driven and fixed spectra; analyze the alignment of singular vectors with signal vs. noise; develop theory on how the power operation interacts with gradient structure.

- The hyperparameter p requires task‑specific tuning and its sensitivity is not thoroughly analyzed.
