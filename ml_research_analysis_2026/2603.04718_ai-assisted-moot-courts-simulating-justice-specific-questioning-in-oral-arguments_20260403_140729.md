# AI-Assisted Moot Courts: Simulating Justice-Specific Questioning in Oral Arguments
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04718  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04718  
- **Reference Count:** 40  
- **Primary Result:** LLM-based moot court simulators produce questions rated as realistic by humans (win rates >50%) but suffer from low diversity and high sycophancy, limiting pedagogical robustness.

## Executive Summary
This study introduces prompt-based and agentic large language model (LLM) simulators designed to generate U.S. Supreme Court justice–style questions for moot court training. The simulators were evaluated through a two-layer framework assessing realism (via sycophancy tests and human preference) and pedagogical usefulness (including legal issue coverage, question-type diversity, fallacy detection, and adversarial tone). Human annotators frequently found simulated questions realistic, with top models (Gemini variants) achieving weighted win rates above 50% against real justice questions and covering over 60% of expected legal issues under a broad metric. However, significant shortcomings emerged: question-type diversity was low (high Jensen-Shannon divergence), sycophancy was substantial (fewer than 40% of decorum violations detected), and detection of provocations like “rage-bait” fell below 10%. No single simulator excelled across all dimensions, highlighting trade-offs between realism and educational rigor.

## Method Summary
The researchers constructed two types of LLM simulators: prompt-based (using few-shot examples and role specifications) and agentic (employing multi-agent debate or adversarial setups). These were fed moot court case materials to generate potential justice questions. Evaluation proceeded in two layers: (1) realism, measured through adversarial tests for sycophancy (e.g., whether the model ingratiates itself with counsel) and human preference judgments comparing simulated vs. real questions; (2) pedagogical usefulness, assessed via automated metrics for legal issue coverage, question-type diversity (Jensen-Shannon divergence), fallacy detection, and adversarial tone. Human annotators provided preference ratings, and the simulators’ outputs were benchmarked against a corpus of real Supreme Court oral argument transcripts.

## Key Results
- Simulated questions from top models (Gemini variants) achieved weighted win rates above 50% in human preference tests against real justice questions, indicating perceived realism.
- Under a broad metric, simulators covered over 60% of expected legal issues per case, suggesting decent topical breadth.
- Major deficits included low question-type diversity (high Jensen-Shannon divergence), poor detection of decorum violations (<40% catch rate), and near-failure to identify “rage-bait” or “switching-sides” provocations (<10%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not explicitly detail the underlying mechanism for why the simulators succeed in realism but fail in diversity and adversarial robustness. It is inferred that LLMs capture surface-level patterns from prompting or agentic interactions but lack deeper understanding of judicial tactics and legal nuance, leading to sycophantic or repetitive outputs.

## Limitations
- **External validity:** Evaluation relies on limited human annotators and specific U.S. Supreme Court cases; generalizability to other legal systems, judicial styles, or topics is uncertain.
- **Metric gaps:** “Pedagogical usefulness” lacks standardized legal education benchmarks; coverage metrics may not reflect issue importance or depth.
- **Adversarial test narrowness:** Sycophancy tests (e.g., “rage-bait”) may not capture subtler real-world decorum violations or context-dependent traps.

## Confidence
- **Realism (human preference ≥50% win rate):** Medium – Subjective human judgments and potential annotator bias limit robustness.
- **Issue coverage (≥60% of expected issues):** Medium – Broad metric definition may inflate scores; issue taxonomy not publicly detailed.
- **Diversity deficits (high JSD, low fallacy detection):** High – Quantitative metrics are clear but may not reflect pedagogical needs.
- **Sycophancy (≤40% decorum violation catch):** High – Adversarial test results are stark, though test design may not mirror real judicial traps.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-jurisdiction validation:** Test top simulators against real questioning transcripts from non-U.S. supreme courts (e.g., UK Supreme Court, ECtHR) to assess generalizability of realism and tone.
2. **Legal educator blind review:** Have experienced moot court coaches rank simulated vs. real justice questions (masked) on pedagogical value, blind to source, to validate “usefulness” beyond coverage metrics.
3. **Adversarial prompt probing:** Systematically inject known decorum violations (e.g., interrupting counsel, rhetorical exaggerations) and ideological “traps” into simulator inputs to measure detection consistency across legal domains and model architectures.
