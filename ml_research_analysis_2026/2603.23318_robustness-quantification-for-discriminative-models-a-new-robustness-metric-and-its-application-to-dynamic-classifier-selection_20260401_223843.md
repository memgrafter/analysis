# Robustness Quantification for Discriminative Models: a New Robustness Metric and its Application to Dynamic Classifier Selection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23318
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23318
- **Reference count:** 24
- **Primary result:** A novel robustness metric (rdCOR) applicable to any probabilistic discriminative classifier, with a closed-form expression based on the top two class probabilities, shows strong correlation with accuracy and improves dynamic classifier selection.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a new robustness metric, rdCOR, for discriminative classifiers that overcomes limitations of prior work requiring generative models. rdCOR is derived from Constant Odds Ratio perturbations and depends solely on the probabilities of the top two predicted classes, giving a closed-form expression. Empirical evaluations across 15 datasets demonstrate that rdCOR correlates well with accuracy (via Accuracy Rejection Curves) and outperforms a local robustness metric (rGeF). When used in dynamic classifier selection, robustness-based strategies (RS-D, RS-I) achieve higher mean accuracy than competing methods (e.g., META-DES) and exhibit greater resilience under label corruption.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a robustness metric called rdCOR that quantifies a classifier’s sensitivity to distributional perturbations under the Constant Odds Ratio (COR) model. For a given input, rdCOR is computed as the relative difference between the probabilities assigned to the top two predicted classes: rdCOR(x) = (p(ŷ₁|x) - p(ŷ₂|x)) / p(ŷ₁|x). This metric is model-agnostic, applies to any feature type, and avoids optimization required by prior metrics. To evaluate its utility, the paper uses Accuracy Rejection Curves (ARCs) that plot accuracy as a function of rejecting low-robustness samples; higher ARC indicates better correlation between robustness and correctness. Additionally, the metric is applied to dynamic classifier selection (DCS) where two candidate models are compared per instance using a robustness ratio threshold. Two strategies, RS-D (threshold tuned on validation accuracy) and RS-I (threshold based on ARC crossovers), are introduced. Experiments compare these against standard DCS baselines (Single Best, META-DES, etc.) on 15 datasets with both clean and label-corrupted training. The robustness-based DCS methods consistently achieve higher accuracy and maintain performance under corruption.

## Key Results
- rdCOR exhibits a strong monotonic relationship with accuracy across diverse classifiers and datasets, outperforming the local robustness metric rGeF in ARC analysis.
- In dynamic classifier selection, RS-D and RS-I achieve the highest mean accuracy in most of the 15 datasets and under label corruption rates (ρ=0.05–0.2), while competing methods (e.g., META-DES) degrade more significantly.
- The closed-form rdCOR metric is computationally efficient and applicable to any probabilistic discriminative classifier, removing the need for generative modeling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Closed-form robustness for discriminative classifiers
The rdCOR metric provides a computationally efficient robustness score without optimization, derived from Constant Odds Ratio perturbations. Theorem 1 shows that for a given input x, robustness simplifies to the relative difference between the top two class probabilities: rdCOR(x) = [p(ŷ₁|x) - p(ŷ₂|x)] / p(ŷ₁|x). This expression depends only on the classifier’s output probabilities, making it model-agnostic. The core assumption is that perturbation effects on decision boundaries are dominated by the two most likely classes. Evidence includes the explicit derivation in Section 2 and the contrast with prior epsilon-contamination methods that require generative models. A break condition arises if probability estimates are poorly calibrated or when there is a tie for the second class.

### Mechanism 2: Robustness as an accuracy predictor
Higher rdCOR values correlate with higher prediction accuracy, enabling reliability assessment via rejection curves. The paper orders test samples by increasing rdCOR and plots Accuracy Rejection Curves (ARCs). Discarding low-robustness samples systematically improves accuracy, indicating rdCOR effectively separates reliable from unreliable predictions. This links perturbation stability to generalization: predictions robust to distributional shifts under COR are more likely correct under the original distribution. Evidence is provided by ARCs (Figures 1–2) where rdCOR ordering improves accuracy faster than random or rGeF ordering. The correlation may weaken under extreme aleatoric uncertainty, as even robust predictions could be incorrect due to inherent noise.

### Mechanism 3: Robustness-based dynamic classifier selection
RS-D and RS-I leverage per-instance rdCOR differences to select between two models, assuming the model with higher robustness (relative to the other) is more likely to predict correctly. For two models ranked by validation accuracy, they compute the robustness ratio r₂(x)/r₁(x); if above a threshold t, they select M₂, else M₁. RS-D tunes t to maximize validation accuracy; RS-I sets t where M₂’s ARC overtakes M₁’s. This approach yields higher mean accuracy than traditional DCS methods, especially under label corruption where other methods degrade. Evidence includes Tables 2–5 and Figure 4 showing consistent gains across 15 datasets. The strategy may underperform if models have very similar robustness profiles or if validation sets are small/thresholds poorly calibrated.

## Architecture Onboarding
Input feature vector x → Classifier module (probabilistic discriminative model) outputs conditional probabilities p(y|x) → Robustness calculator identifies top two classes ŷ₁, ŷ₂ and computes rdCOR = (p(ŷ₁|x) - p(ŷ₂|x)) / p(ŷ₁|x) → For DCS, compare robustness ratios between two candidate models to threshold t → Output selected model’s prediction.

**Critical path:** The classifier must produce valid probability estimates (non-negative, sum to 1). Numerical stability is critical when p(ŷ₁|x) is near zero (rdCOR becomes unstable). Edge cases like equal top probabilities require careful handling.

**Design tradeoffs:** rdCOR’s simplicity and model-agnostic nature come at the cost of potentially overlooking architecture-specific robustness (e.g., neural network boundary geometry). The metric focuses only on output probabilities, ignoring internal uncertainty. For DCS, RS-D optimizes validation accuracy but may overfit; RS-I is more conservative but requires clear ARC crossovers.

**Failure signatures:** 
- rdCOR ≈ 0 for many samples indicates high uncertainty and low reliability.
- In DCS, an unrepresentative validation set may yield mis-calibrated threshold t, causing poor selection.
- Under extreme distribution shift, rdCOR may not deteriorate, leading to overconfident incorrect predictions.

## Limitations
- rdCOR assumes perturbation effects are dominated by top-two class probabilities; this may not hold if more classes become competitive or if probabilities are miscalibrated.
- The strong correlation between rdCOR and accuracy is validated on 15 datasets but may weaken under extreme aleatoric noise or distribution shift.
- Exact hyperparameter grids for base models and tie-handling in rdCOR are unspecified, potentially affecting reproducibility and performance.
