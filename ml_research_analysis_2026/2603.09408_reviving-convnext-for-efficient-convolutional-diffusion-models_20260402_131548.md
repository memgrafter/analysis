# Reviving ConvNeXt for Efficient Convolutional Diffusion Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.09408  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09408  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: FCDM-XL achieves ImageNet FID 2.03 at 256×256 with 50% fewer FLOPs than DiT-XL/2, 7× faster convergence, and 3–4× higher throughput; trainable on 4 consumer GPUs.

## Executive Summary
Transformer-based diffusion models deliver strong generative performance but are computationally expensive. This paper revives convolutional architectures for diffusion by proposing the Fully Convolutional Diffusion Model (FCDM), which replaces transformer blocks with ConvNeXt blocks. FCDM incorporates Adaptive LayerNorm (AdaLN) for conditioning and adopts a scalable U-shaped design controlled by only two hyperparameters (block count L and channel width C). The resulting models achieve competitive ImageNet FID scores (e.g., 2.03 at 256×256) while significantly reducing computational cost—using half the FLOPs of DiT-XL/2, converging much faster, and offering higher throughput. The work demonstrates that carefully designed convolutional networks can match transformer efficiency in generative modeling.

## Method Summary
FCDM is a fully convolutional diffusion model built from ConvNeXt blocks modified with AdaLN to inject timestep and class conditioning. Each block performs a depthwise 7×7 convolution followed by AdaLN, a pointwise expansion (ratio 3), a Channel-wise Reinhorn Normalization (GRN), and a pointwise projection. Blocks are arranged in a symmetric U-shaped encoder-decoder architecture: the encoder progressively downsamples, the decoder upsamples with skip connections from the encoder, and both branches use the same block pattern. The number of blocks L and hidden channels C are the only scaling hyperparameters, doubled at each downsampling stage to yield models S, B, L, and XL. Training follows the iDDPM framework with a linear noise schedule, AdamW (lr=1e-4, no weight decay), batch size 256, and EMA. The design leverages convolutional efficiency—depthwise separable convolutions with large kernels—while maintaining competitive generation quality.

## Key Results
- FCDM-XL attains FID 2.03 on ImageNet 256×256, comparable to DiT-XL/2 but with 50% fewer FLOPs.  
- Training convergence is 7× faster at 256×256 and 7.5× faster at 512×512 relative to DiT-XL/2.  
- Throughput is 3–4× higher, and the model can be trained on 4 consumer-grade GPUs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Convolutional Efficiency via Locality Bias
Convolutional operations reduce computational cost and improve hardware utilization. Depthwise separable convolutions with large kernels (7×7) capture local patterns efficiently, avoiding the quadratic complexity of self-attention. The locality bias aligns with image statistics, allowing fewer parameters to learn effective representations. Core assumption: local receptive fields suffice for high-quality image generation and convolutions are hardware-friendly. Evidence: the abstract highlights locality bias and hardware friendliness; Section 3.1 details the ConvNeXt block; related works like DiCo explore convolutions for diffusion. Break condition: if global context becomes critical for complex datasets, convolutions may plateau compared to transformers, though FCDM remains competitive on ImageNet.

### Conditional Injection via AdaLN
Adaptive LayerNorm (AdaLN) enables stable conditioning on timestep and class labels. Conditioning vectors from time and class embeddings are mapped by an MLP to γ, β, and zero-initialized α parameters that modulate the output of LayerNorm, adapting block computations based on conditions while stabilizing training. Core assumption: conditioning can be injected through normalization layers without disrupting feature extraction, and zero-initialized α prevents early instability. Evidence: Section 3.1 describes replacing LayerNorm with AdaLN and zero-initializing α; the abstract mentions conditional injection via AdaLN; DiT’s use of AdaLN-zero shows cross-architecture applicability. Break condition: for rich conditioning like text descriptions, simple AdaLN may not capture semantics; Section H extends the conditioning module, indicating limitations for multi-modal inputs.

### Scalable U‑shaped Design with Minimal Hyperparameters
Organizing ConvNeXt blocks in a U‑shape controlled by only two hyperparameters (L and C) simplifies scaling and maintains efficiency across model sizes. Blocks are arranged in an encoder‑decoder with skip connections; at each downsampling stage, L and C are doubled, ensuring balanced capacity and avoiding resolution‑specific designs. Core assumption: a symmetric U‑shape with consistent block types captures global context (downsampling) and local details (skip connections), with complexity governed primarily by width and depth. Evidence: the abstract states the two‑hyperparameter control; Section 3.1 explains the generalized U‑shape and scaling rule; Section 4.1 shows scalability from S to XL with consistent FID improvements and FLOPs efficiency; U‑Nets are standard in diffusion but FCDM’s simplification is novel. Break condition: if asymmetric compute allocation or more complex hierarchies are needed for extreme scales, but ablations (Table 14) indicate the symmetric design is effective; future tasks may require modifications.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Conditioning Module → FCDM Blocks (depthwise 7×7 conv → AdaLN → pointwise expansion (ratio 3) → GRN → pointwise reduction) arranged in symmetric U‑shaped encoder‑decoder with downsampling/upsampling and skip connections → Output Head (1×1 conv predicting noise).

**Critical Path**:  
1. Define the FCDM block with AdaLN replacing LayerNorm and zero‑initialized modulation scale α.  
2. Assemble blocks into a symmetric U‑shaped encoder‑decoder: encoder progressively downsamples, decoder upsamples with skip connections; the number of blocks L and channels C are doubled at each downsampling stage.  
3. Integrate the conditioning module to produce a conditioning vector for each block (combining timestep and class embeddings via an MLP).  
4. Use the final decoder features to predict noise via a 1×1 convolution head.  
5. Train with diffusion loss (e.g., iDDPM) using standard hyperparameters (AdamW, lr=1e‑4, batch size 256, EMA).

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- Kernel size: 7×7 depthwise conv increases receptive field but may reduce hardware efficiency; ablations confirm 7×7 optimal versus smaller kernels.  
- GRN vs. CCA: GRN is parameter‑free and promotes channel diversity efficiently; it outperforms the more complex CCA used in DiCo.  
- Symmetric U‑shape: Simplifies scaling with only L and C but may slightly underperform asymmetric designs; chosen for ease of scale.  
- Channel expansion ratio (r=3): Balances representation richness and FLOPs; altering it affects performance.  
- Zero‑initialized α: Stabilizes early training but may limit initial conditioning strength.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- High FID at convergence: Check AdaLN integration or block capacity; conditioning might not propagate.  
- Poor throughput: Ensure depthwise convolutions are used efficiently; large kernels or improper padding may slow down execution.  
- Scalability issues (FID doesn’t improve with L/C): Verify skip connections and correct downsampling rates; the U‑Net may be too shallow or deep.  
- Conditioning failure (samples ignore class): Verify the conditioning vector is correctly computed and applied in AdaLN.

**First 3 Experiments**:  
1. **Baseline replication**: Train FCDM‑S on ImageNet 256×256 for 400K steps; measure FLOPs (~3.1G) and throughput vs. DiT‑S; expect ~50% FLOPs reduction and comparable FID.  
2. **Kernel size ablation**: Train FCDM‑L with 3×3, 5×5, and default 7×7 depthwise conv; expect FID degradation with smaller kernels.  
3. **Latent VAE test**: Replace SD‑VAE with EQ‑VAE; expect improved FID, demonstrating compatibility with advanced VAEs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Can FCDM match or exceed the FID scores of state‑of‑the‑art diffusion models like EDM‑2 or Simpler Diffusion through further scaling or advanced training?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors state that FCDM does not yet surpass the latest state‑of‑the‑art results but suggest further scaling and more advanced training frameworks could improve performance.  
*Why unresolved:* Current FCDM‑XL achieves 2.03 FID at 256×256, while EDM‑2 and Simpler Diffusion report lower numbers; it is unclear if scaling FCDM (e.g., larger models, longer training) can close this gap without sacrificing efficiency.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Train larger FCDM variants (e.g., with increased depth/width) for more iterations, incorporate techniques from SOTA models (e.g., improved noise schedules, distillation), and benchmark FID on ImageNet at multiple resolutions.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How can FCDM be effectively extended to use full text embeddings (token‑level) for text‑to‑image generation, similar to MMDiT, while maintaining its efficiency?  
*Basis in paper:* The paper notes that extending FCDM to support joint conditioning on full text embeddings represents an important direction for future work.  
*Why unresolved:* Current text‑to‑image experiment uses only pooled text embeddings; joint conditioning on all text tokens may require architectural modifications (e.g., cross‑attention) that could break the simplicity and efficiency of the fully convolutional design.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Design a conditioning module that injects token‑level text features into FCDM blocks without excessive overhead, train on MS‑COCO or LAION, and compare generation quality (FID, CLIP score) against models like MMDiT.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What is the theoretical explanation for the consistently higher spectral energy in FCDM’s predicted noise compared to DiT, and how does it correlate with final image quality?  
*Basis in paper:* Frequency analysis shows FCDM has higher log‑magnitude spectrum sums across diffusion steps, and the authors state “further theoretical analysis is required” to understand this.  
*Why unresolved:* The relationship between spectral properties of intermediate noise predictions and final generation fidelity is not established; higher energy might indicate better high‑frequency preservation but could also lead to instability or artifacts.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct theoretical analysis linking spectral energy to denoising dynamics; perform controlled experiments manipulating frequency components (e.g., low‑pass filtering predictions) and measure impact on FID, IS, and human evaluation.

## Limitations
- VAE checkpoint ambiguity: It is unclear which VAE (SD‑VAE or EQ‑VAE) was used for the main reported results, affecting reproducibility.
- Conditioning module specifics: Exact embedding dimensions, MLP architecture, and combination method are not detailed.
- Evaluation limited to ImageNet: Performance on other datasets or higher resolutions remains unknown.
