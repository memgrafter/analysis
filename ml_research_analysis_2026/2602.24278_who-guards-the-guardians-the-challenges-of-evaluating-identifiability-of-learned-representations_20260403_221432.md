# Who Guards the Guardians? The Challenges of Evaluating Identifiability of Learned Representations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.24278  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24278  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Standard identifiability metrics (MCC, R², DCI-D) are structurally misspecified, producing false positives/negatives when latent factor structure or encoder geometry violates their implicit assumptions; a two-axis taxonomy and practitioner checklist are provided to expose these failures.

## Executive Summary
This paper systematically examines the validity of standard metrics used to evaluate learned representations for identifiability of ground-truth latent factors. Through controlled synthetic experiments and theoretical analysis, it demonstrates that common metrics—Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), DCI Disentanglement (DCI-D), and R² with a linear probe—are each vulnerable to specific, structurally rooted failure modes. These failures arise from mismatches between metric design assumptions and realistic data-generating processes, such as correlated factors, functional redundancies, or overcomplete encodings. The authors introduce a two-axis taxonomy characterizing latent factor dependencies and encoder geometry, use it to isolate each metric’s breaking points, and provide a practical checklist to guide appropriate metric selection and interpretation.

## Method Summary
The study employs a controlled framework where ground-truth latent factors \(z \in \mathbb{R}^d\) are generated from one of four data-generating process (DGP) classes: independent factors (D⊥), correlated factors (D𝜌), single-factor deterministic constraint (D𝑓), or multi-factor deterministic constraint (D𝐹). Representations \(\hat{z} \in \mathbb{R}^m\) are produced by synthetic encoders belonging to one of ten geometry classes (E1–E10), specifying the equivalence class (e.g., permutation, linear mixing) and dimension ratio \(m/d\). Standard metrics are computed on paired \((z, \hat{z})\) datasets. Theoretical results complement the empirical analysis, proving, for instance, that MCC under a null encoder exhibits a non-vanishing false-positive floor scaling with \(\sqrt{2\log(m)/n}\). A practitioner checklist maps combinations of DGP and encoder geometry to valid metrics based on four desired properties: invariance to latent correlation (P1), faithfulness to effective dimensionality (P2), invariance to overcompleteness (P3), and robustness to uninformative encoders (P4).

## Key Results
- MCC is inflated by latent factor correlation (P1) and by high representation-to-sample ratios \(m/n\) (P4), making it unsuitable for correlated DGP or overcomplete settings.
- DCI-D fails to detect multi-factor redundancy (P2) and spuriously increases for overcomplete linearly entangled encoders (P3), misreporting completeness and disentanglement.
- R² with a linear probe is robust to correlation, overcompleteness, and high \(m/n\) but cannot capture nonlinear functional constraints (P2), yielding low scores even when factors are perfectly predictable via a nonlinear relationship.
- No existing metric satisfies all four properties; evaluating representations requires matching metric validity to the underlying DGP and encoder geometry.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
MCC conflates latent factor correlation with representation quality and is inflated by high representation-to-sample ratios. It computes optimal one-to-one matching via the Hungarian algorithm on absolute sample correlations. Under correlated latent factors (D𝜌), entangled encodings yield spuriously high correlations because each code’s correlation with a factor is amplified by inter-factor dependencies. Under a null encoder (independent of ground truth), the maximum correlation across random codes follows an extreme-value distribution scaling with \(\sqrt{2 \log(m)/n}\), producing a non-vanishing false positive floor when \(m/n\) is large. Core assumptions: latent factors are independent (D⊥), each factor is encoded by exactly one code (elementwise linear recovery, E1), and sufficient samples \(n \gg m\). Break condition: when factors are correlated (D𝜌) or \(m/n \gtrsim 0.1\).

### Mechanism 2
DCI-D cannot detect multi-factor redundancy (lossless compression when a factor is deterministic from multiple others) and spuriously increases for overcomplete linearly entangled encoders. It trains a nonlinear probe (e.g., gradient boosted trees) to predict each factor from all codes, then computes disentanglement as the average concentration of feature importances per code. Under multi-factor redundancy (D𝐹), a redundant factor predicted using multiple codes spreads importance, lowering completeness even if all independent factors are present. Conversely, for overcomplete linear entanglement (E7), the probe’s flexibility lets it assign different code subsets to different factors, artificially raising disentanglement as \(m/d\) grows despite persistent entanglement. Core assumptions: each factor should be predictable from a small, dedicated code subset and the probe’s importance matrix accurately reflects factor-code alignment; overcompleteness does not alter identifiability status. Break condition: when deterministic constraints involve ≥2 factors (D𝐹) or when the encoder is overcomplete with linear mixing (E7) and \(m/d > 1\).

### Mechanism 3
R² (with a linear probe) is robust to latent correlation, overcompleteness, and high \(m/n\), but fails to recognize lossless compression under multi-factor redundancy because it cannot model nonlinear relationships. R² measures variance explained in each factor by a linear combination of codes. Linear probing insulates it from nonlinear statistical dependencies (P1) and extreme-value false positives (P4). Overcomplete copies (E5) are ignored by the linear fit, preserving stability (P3). However, when a factor is a nonlinear function of multiple independent factors (D𝐹), the linear probe cannot capture the relationship, yielding low R² even though the factor is perfectly predictable (violating P2). Core assumption: the mapping from representation to factors is linear (or a linear probe suffices) and each factor’s variance should be individually explained. Break condition: when the data-generating process includes multi-factor deterministic constraints (D𝐹) inducing nonlinear factor relationships.

## Foundational Learning
- **Identifiability up to an equivalence class**: Understanding what transformations (e.g., permutation, scaling) leave a representation meaningfully equivalent is central to defining what a metric should measure. Quick check: What is the difference between identifiability up to \(G_{\text{perm}}\) (permutations) and up to \(G_{\text{aff}}\) (affine transformations)?
- **Disentanglement metrics (MCC, DCI-D, R²)**: The paper analyzes failure modes of these standard metrics; understanding their computations is essential to grasp why they fail. Quick check: How does DCI-D compute disentanglement from the feature importance matrix?
- **Effective dimensionality of latent factors**: Metrics must account for the true number of independent degrees of freedom (\(d_{\text{eff}}\)) to avoid false positives when factors are functionally redundant. Quick check: Given factors \(I, R, V\) with \(V = I \cdot R\), what is the effective dimensionality of \(\{I, R, V\}\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
Data generator -> Encoder factory -> Metric module -> Validity checker -> Reporting

Critical path: (a) Characterize latent factor structure (independence, correlation, constraints) and encoder geometry (\(m/d\), equivalence class). (b) Select metrics per checklist/table ensuring the regime lies in their validity domain. (c) Compute metrics on the target representation and on a null encoder baseline. (d) Interpret scores only if the null baseline is low and validity conditions hold; otherwise flag as potentially deceptive.

Design tradeoffs: Single metric vs. suite; probe choice (linear vs. nonlinear); sample efficiency vs. stability.

Failure signatures:
- MCC score > 0.3 when \(m/n > 0.1\) even for a random encoder.
- DCI-D score increases with \(m/d\) for linearly entangled overcomplete encoders (E7).
- R² score near 1 despite known multi-factor functional constraints (D𝐹).
- Any metric returns perfect score (1.0) under D𝐹 with \(m = d_{\text{eff}}\).

First 3 experiments:
1. **Correlation sensitivity**: Vary latent correlation \(\rho\); under linearly entangled encoding (E3), MCC inflates with \(|\rho|\) while DCI-D remains low.
2. **Redundancy detection**: Under single-factor constraint (D𝑓) vs. multi-factor constraint (D𝐹), test if metrics distinguish lossless compression from lossy omission when varying \(m\). R² and DCI-D should plateau near 1 at \(m = d_{\text{eff}}\) for D𝑓 but drop for D𝐹.
3. **Null encoder floor**: Under a random encoder (E9), sweep \(m/n\) and measure MCC inflation; verify R² stays near 0.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Can a single identifiability metric be designed to simultaneously satisfy invariance to latent correlation (Property 1), sensitivity to effective dimensionality under functional constraints (Property 2), invariance to overcompleteness (Property 3), and robustness to null encoders (Property 4)? The paper's Table 3 and conclusion state that no existing metric satisfies all four properties and call this a fundamental limitation requiring new design principles. Unresolved because the failures stem from deep structural mismatches: correlation-based metrics conflate inter-factor covariance with identifiability; regression-based metrics cannot detect multi-factor redundancy; and all metrics except R² inflate under high \(m/n\). A metric would need to combine orthogonal strengths without inheriting flaws, possibly requiring a novel theoretical foundation.

### Open Question 2
How do identifiability metrics behave when the data-generating process involves stochastic encoders (e.g., variational distributions) or discrete latent factors, which are excluded from the current taxonomy? The paper’s limitation section notes: "The taxonomy does not cover stochastic encoders or discrete factors, all of which arise in practice." Unresolved because the taxonomy and metric definitions assume deterministic mappings and continuous factors. Stochasticity introduces sampling noise that may interact with metric estimation; discrete factors violate assumptions of correlation/regression-based measures. The extent to which identified failure modes persist under such settings is unknown and could critically affect applications like discrete representation learning or causal discovery with binary variables.

### Open Question 3
Do the metric failure modes identified with synthetic encoders (E1–E10) manifest similarly in representations learned by actual neural networks trained on real-world datasets, or are they artifacts of the synthetic control? The paper acknowledges: "Our analysis uses synthetic encoders by design, to isolate metric misspecification from optimisation artefacts." It also notes that "a systematic study of how metric failures manifest across different families of learned encoders would be a complementary direction," implying this is an open gap. Unresolved because synthetic encoders bypass training dynamics, hyperparameters, and approximate convergence. Real learned encoders may exhibit idiosyncratic biases that could amplify or mask the structural failures. Without empirical validation on diverse learned models, the practical severity of the misspecification remains uncertain.

## Limitations
- Exact definitions for some encoder geometries (e.g., E6) and the multi-factor constraint (D𝐹) are unspecified, possibly affecting reproduction fidelity.
- DCI-D relies on a nonlinear probe with free hyperparameters; results may vary with different settings or implementations.
- All experiments use synthetic data; generalizability to real-world representations remains unverified.
- The taxonomy assumes perfect knowledge of the data-generating process, which is often absent in practice.

## Confidence
- **MCC failure modes** (inflation with latent correlation and high \(m/n\) under null encoder): **High** confidence, backed by theory and extensive synthetic evidence.
- **DCI-D limitations** (fails to detect multi-factor redundancy and inflates for overcomplete linear entanglement): **High** confidence, though probe robustness requires further stress-testing.
- **R² robustness and blind spot** (stable across correlation and overcompleteness but fails on nonlinear constraints): **High** confidence, consistent with linear probe theory.
- **Taxonomy/checklist utility**: **Medium** confidence; a promising framework but needs empirical validation on real representations.

## Next Checks
1. **MCC null distribution verification**: Sample random encoders across a grid of \(m/n\), compute MCC, and compare the empirical false-positive floor to the predicted \(\sqrt{2\log m / n}\) scaling.
2. **DCI-D hyperparameter sensitivity**: In the multi-factor redundancy regime, vary probe hyperparameters (tree depth, number of trees, learning rate) and quantify variation in disentanglement scores; identify robust operating ranges.
3. **Nonlinear probe extension**: Replace the linear probe in R² with a flexible nonlinear model (e.g., kernel ridge regression) and test whether it restores detection of multi-factor constraints without spurious inflation; cross-check against the checklist’s validity conditions.
