# Tight Analysis of Decentralized SGD: A Markov Chain Perspective

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2601.07021  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07021  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** DSGD with constant step size converges to a stationary distribution; its bias decomposes into deterministic (graph spectral gap & client heterogeneity) and stochastic components; local‑parameter variance scales as O(1/m) independent of network topology at first order; Richardson‑Romberg extrapolation eliminates the first‑order decentralization bias.

## Executive Summary

Decentralized Stochastic Gradient Descent (DSGD) is a fundamental algorithm for distributed learning over networks, where each client updates its local model and exchanges updates with neighbors. This paper provides a rigorous analysis of DSGD with a constant step size by modeling its iterates as a time‑homogeneous Markov chain. Under standard assumptions—strong convexity, smoothness, i.i.d. zero‑mean noise, and a doubly stochastic communication matrix—the authors prove geometric ergodicity, showing convergence to a unique stationary distribution at a rate governed by the step size and problem conditioning. The stationary bias is decomposed into two first‑order terms: (i) a deterministic component proportional to the product of the step size, the graph’s spectral gap (captured by Λ), and the heterogeneity level (ζ_*); and (ii) a stochastic correction arising from nonlinear noise effects. The variance of local parameters, to leading order, is inversely proportional to the number of clients (∝ 1/m) and does not depend on the network topology. Moreover, the analysis motivates a Richardson‑Romberg scheme that removes the first‑order bias entirely, improving final accuracy without extra communication per iteration. These findings clarify how network structure, data heterogeneity, and stochastic noise jointly affect DSGD’s asymptotic performance and suggest practical modifications.

## Method Summary

The algorithm under scrutiny is Decentralized SGD (DSGD) in its “adapt‑then‑combine” form. Each client k maintains a parameter vector θ_k. In every round t:

1. **Adapt:** θ_k^{temp} = θ_k^t ‑ γ ∇f_k(θ_k^t; ξ_t^k), where γ is a constant step size and ∇f_k a stochastic gradient based on local data and randomness ξ_t^k.
2. **Combine (gossip):** θ_k^{t+1} = Σ_ℓ W_{kℓ} θ_ℓ^{temp}, where W is a symmetric doubly stochastic matrix encoding an undirected, connected graph (λ₂(W)<1).

The stacked vector Θ ∈ ℝ^{md} evolves according to a time‑homogeneous Markov kernel R_γ, making the process a Markov chain. The analysis proceeds by establishing contraction in the Wasserstein‑2 distance under a Lyapunov condition, which together with recurrence yields geometric ergodicity. The stationary distribution π_γ is then studied via Taylor expansions of the expected drift and conditional covariance, leading to explicit first‑order characterizations of its mean (bias) and covariance (variance).

## Key Results

- **Geometric ergodicity:** For step sizes γ satisfying 0 < γ < 2/L, the DSGD iterate is a geometrically ergodic Markov chain, converging to a unique stationary distribution π_γ at a linear rate in Wasserstein‑2 distance.
- **Bias decomposition:** The deterministic component of the stationary mean error is Θ_det ‑ θ⋆ = γ·G·∇F(θ⋆), where G = (I‑W)†W encodes the graph’s spectral gap; heterogeneity enters through ζ_* = ‖∇F(Θ_det)‖. A stochastic correction term (∝ γ/m) appears for general smooth losses, vanishing for quadratic objectives.
- **Topology‑independent variance:** The leading term of the stationary covariance of local parameters is (γ/m)(Ā⁻¹ ⊗ I) C(θ⋆)(Ā⁻¹ ⊗ I)^T, where Ā is the average Hessian and C(θ⋆) the noise covariance at the optimum. This first‑order term does not involve the graph matrix W, implying that the variance scales as 1/m regardless of network sparsity.
- **Richardson‑Romberg extrapolation:** Running DSGD with step sizes γ and γ/2 and appropriately combining the iterates eliminates the first‑order deterministic bias, reducing the error from O(γ) to O(γ²).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

The paper’s analysis relies on three interconnected mechanisms, each built on specific mathematical properties and assumptions.

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** DSGD iterates form a geometrically ergodic Markov chain that converges to a unique stationary distribution π_γ, with convergence rate governed by step size γ and problem conditioning.
- **Mechanism:** The "Adapt‑then‑Combine" update with constant γ and stochastic noise ε_t (A4) defines a time‑homogeneous Markov kernel on ℝ^{md}. The analysis proves contraction in Wasserstein‑2 distance per iteration: W_2²(ρ₀R_γ, ρ₁R_γ) ≤ (1 ‑ 2γμ(1 ‑ Lγ/2)) W_2²(ρ₀, ρ₁). This geometric decrease, combined with Lyapunov conditions (A1 strong convexity, A5 co‑coercivity), implies ergodicity and convergence to π_γ.
- **Core assumption:** A1 (strong convexity, smoothness), A4 (zero‑mean, i.i.d. noise), A5(2) (L‑co‑coercive noisy gradients).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “DSGD converges to a stationary distribution, with its bias, to first order, decomposable into two components: one due to decentralization… and one due to stochasticity.”  
  - Section 4.1, Proposition 4.1: explicit contraction inequality and proof sketch using co‑coercivity and conditional expectation.  
  - Corpus neighbor “Stochastic Gradient Descent under Markovian Sampling Schemes”: contextualizes Markov chain analysis for SGD variants.
- **Break condition:** If γ ≥ 2/L (violates A5(2) limit) or γ so large that 1 ‑ 2γμ(1 ‑ Lγ/2) ≤ 0, contraction fails. Non‑i.i.d. or biased noise (A4 violation) also breaks the Markov property and contraction.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The stationary bias Θ^{sto} ‑ θ⋆ decomposes additively: a first‑order term γ(I‑H)G∇F(θ⋆) from decentralization/heterogeneity (deterministic DGD bias), plus a first‑order stochastic correction ─γ/(2m)(Ā⁻¹ ⊗ I)∇³f(θ⋆) J C(θ⋆) due to noise nonlinearities. The topology (via G = (I‑W)†W) and heterogeneity (via H) only appear in the deterministic part; stochastic bias depends on noise covariance C(θ⋆) and average Hessian Ā.
- **Mechanism:** The stationary mean Θ^{sto} satisfies (I‑W)Θ^{sto} = ‑γ W E_{π_γ}[∇F(Θ)]. Taylor expansion of ∇F around θ⋆ and the variance expansion (B.4, B.9) yield the bias. For quadratics, stochasticity cancels (Proposition 4.4). For general smooth f_k, the O(γ) stochastic correction emerges from the third derivative interacting with the leading variance term γ/m · J C(θ⋆).
- **Core assumption:** A1 (4th derivative bounded via K₃), A6 (smooth noise covariance), A2 (λ₂(W)<1).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “bias… decomposable into two components: one due to decentralization (growing with the graph’s spectral gap and clients’ heterogeneity) and one due to stochasticity.”  
  - Proposition 3.6 & 4.5: explicit first‑order expansions for deterministic and stochastic bias, with residual bounds.  
  - Proposition 4.4: shows stochasticity does *not* add bias for quadratic objectives, isolating the source.
- **Break condition:** If f_k not 4‑times differentiable (A1(c) violation), Taylor expansion’s remainder is uncontrolled, breaking the bias decomposition. Non‑smooth C(θ) (A6 violation) also disrupts the variance expansion leading to bias.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The stationary variance of local parameters, to first order in γ, is Var(θ_k^{sto}) ≈ (γ/m)(Ā⁻¹ ⊗ I) C(θ⋆)(Ā⁻¹ ⊗ I)^T (Lemma B.9), independent of network topology (i.e., independent of λ₂(W) and G). This yields linear speed‑up O(1/m) in the number of clients m without explicit parameter averaging.
- **Mechanism:** Variance analysis leverages the expansion ∫ (θ ‑ θ^{det})^{⊗2} dπ_γ = γ(P ⊗ P)(···)† ∫ C dπ_γ + O(γ^{3/2}). The projection P ⊗ P onto the consensus space removes topology dependence at first order. The leading term only involves the average Hessian Ā and noise covariance C(θ⋆), which are client‑aggregate quantities, not graph‑dependent.
- **Core assumption:** A1 (smoothness), A2 (connected graph, λ₂(W)<1), A4 (noise), A5(4) (4‑th moment), A6 (smooth C).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “the variance of local parameters is, at the first‑order, inversely proportional to the number of clients, regardless of the network topology and even when clients’ iterates are not averaged at the end.”  
  - Proposition 4.2: statement and proof of the O(γ/m) variance, with residual O(γ^{3/2}).  
  - Lemma B.9: derivation showing cancellation of topology‑dependent terms in the first‑order coefficient.
- **Break condition:** If m is small relative to γΛ² (from Lemma 4.3’s bound), higher‑order topology terms dominate, breaking the “topology‑independent” claim. Also, if C(θ) not smooth (A6), the expansion’s remainder may not be O(γ^{3/2}).

## Foundational Learning

This section outlines key mathematical concepts used in the analysis, why they are needed, and quick sanity checks.

- **Concept: Markov chain convergence in Wasserstein metric**  
  *Why needed here:* The DSGD iterates form a Markov process; proving ergodicity (convergence to π_γ) via contraction in the Wasserstein‑2 distance justifies the steady‑state analysis (bias/variance expansions). The contraction coefficient μγ(1‑Lγ/2) directly influences the mixing rate.  
  *Quick check:* Why does co‑coercivity (A5) enable a contraction in W₂? How does it relate to strong convexity?  
  (Answer sketch: Co‑coercivity of the expected gradient step implies the expected squared distance shrinks conditionally; combined with noise variance bounds this yields a Wasserstein contraction.)

- **Concept: Spectral gap of communication matrix W**  
  *Why needed here:* λ₂(W) (or Λ = 2‖(I‑W)†W‖) quantifies graph connectivity. It appears in the deterministic bias magnitude (Proposition 3.6) and in higher‑order variance terms (Lemma 4.3). For well‑connected graphs (e.g., fully connected), Λ→0 and bias diminishes.  
  *Quick check:* For a ring network, λ₂(W) ≈ 1 ‑ O(1/m²). How does this affect the deterministic bias term γG∇F(θ⋆)?  
  (Answer sketch: Λ ≈ 2/m², so the bias shrinks quickly with m, but for sparse graphs Λ may be order 1, making bias significant.)

- **Concept: Bias‑variance trade‑off in constant‑step‑size SGD**  
  *Why needed here:* Constant γ induces a stationary distribution rather than a point; performance balances deterministic bias (decreases with smaller γ) and stochastic variance (decreases with larger γ). The paper’s expansions make this precise. Richardson‑Romberg (Section 5) then cancels first‑order bias.  
  *Quick check:* For quadratic f_k, why does stochasticity not add bias (Proposition 4.4)? What changes for non‑quadratic smooth f_k (Proposition 4.5)?  
  (Answer sketch: Quadratic objectives have linear gradients; the stochastic noise term’s expectation is zero and the variance contributes only to second‑order moments, not to the mean. Non‑quadratics introduce nonlinearity, and the variance interacts with curvature (third derivative) to produce a mean shift.)

## Architecture Onboarding

This section maps the DSGD algorithm into its functional components, identifies critical tuning choices, design trade‑offs, failure signatures, and proposes three initial experiments.

**Component map**  
Client k → ∇f_k(θ_k^t; ξ_t^k) → Adapt: θ_k^{temp} = θ_k^t ‑ γ∇f_k → Combine: θ_k^{t+1} = Σ_ℓ W_{kℓ} θ_ℓ^{temp} → Global state Θ^{t+1}

**Critical path**  
- Step size tuning: γ must satisfy 0 < γ < min(1/L, 1/(ΛL)) to keep first‑order expansions valid; too large breaks contraction (A5).  
- Graph topology: Sparse graphs (large Λ) increase deterministic bias linearly (γΛζ_*) but do not affect leading‑order variance. For linear speed‑up in m to dominate, need m ≫ γΛ².  
- Heterogeneity: ζ_* = ‖∇F(Θ̅)‖, where Θ̅ is the average stationary parameters; larger ζ_* amplifies deterministic bias.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Graph sparsity vs. bias: Sparse graphs reduce communication per round but increase deterministic bias through Λ. Richardson‑Romberg (Section 5) mitigates this without extra communication per iteration (only two runs with γ and γ/2).  
- Step size vs. variance: Var(θ_k) ∝ γ/m. Smaller γ reduces variance but slows convergence; larger γ may require averaging or RR to control bias.  
- Client number m: Linear speed‑up holds only while m ≫ γΛ². Adding clients yields diminishing returns on poorly connected graphs.

**Failure signatures**  
1. Exploding variance: γ too large → Lyapunov bound fails, W₂ contraction rate ≥1, chain not geometrically ergodic. Check eigenvalue of I ‑ 2γμ(1‑Lγ/2).  
2. Bias dominates: For heterogeneous data (large ζ_*) and sparse graph (large Λ), deterministic bias (γΛζ_*) exceeds stochastic floor √(γ/(m)), error plateaus.  
3. Non‑ergodicity: Graph disconnected (λ₂(W)=1) → multiple 1‑eigenvectors, consensus not enforced. Check λ₂(W)<1 (A2).  
4. Taylor expansion invalid: f_k not 4‑times differentiable (A1c fail) or γ large so ‖Θ^t ‑ θ⋆‖ not O(γ) (breaks Lemma 3.4 condition), leading to uncontrolled higher‑order terms.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. *Bias scaling with γ and Λ:* Run deterministic DGD (no noise) on a quadratic objective with fixed heterogeneity. Vary γ (log scale) and graph topologies (fully connected vs. ring). After convergence, plot ‖(1/m)Σ_k θ_k ‑ θ⋆‖² vs. γ to verify O(γΛζ_*) dependence. Repeat with Richardson‑Romberg to confirm O(γ²) reduction.  
2. *Variance scaling with m:* Use stochastic DSGD with fixed small γ and a graph having moderate Λ (e.g., grid). Vary m ∈ {4,16,64,256}. At a large time T, compute the empirical sample variance across clients: (1/m)Σ_k ‖θ_k^T ‑ θ^{det}‖². Confirm 1/m decay and independence of graph type (compare grid vs. fully connected) at leading order.  
3. *Richardson‑Romberg bias elimination:* On a ring graph (large Λ) under low noise, compare standard DSGD and RR‑DSGD (Algorithm 5). Plot distance to θ⋆ vs. iterations. Verify that RR reduces first‑order bias by a factor of roughly 2 when γ is halved, achieving lower steady‑state error when bias dominates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

The analysis opens several avenues for further research:

- **Open Question 1:** *What are the precise interactions between noise variance, step size, and graph connectivity (spectral gap) in determining the stationary distribution’s bias and variance for DSGD?*  
  *Basis:* The paper provides separate first‑order expansions for bias (decomposition into decentralization and stochasticity) and variance (∝ 1/m, topology‑independent at first order), but does not analyze how these components jointly scale with the noise level, step size, and spectral gap ρ.  
  *Why unresolved:* The expansions isolate first‑order dependencies but leave unquantified how these factors combined affect higher‑order terms and the overall stationary distribution shape. The analysis also assumes constant step size and smoothness, leaving open robustness.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* A refined expansion or empirical study quantifying interaction terms (e.g., σ²γ/(µρ)) and identifying regimes where one component dominates the other. Theoretical results could derive a unified expression for the mean‑squared error as a function of noise level, γ, and ρ, verified through experiments across variable noise and graph sparsity.

- **Open Question 2:** *Under what exact conditions on the noise variance, step size, and graph topology does Richardson‑Romberg extrapolation for DSGD provide a sample complexity improvement over standard DSGD?*  
  *Basis:* The authors propose Richardson‑Romberg to eliminate first‑order decentralization bias and note “it can be particularly beneficial in settings where noise is dominated by the bias due to decentralization.” However, they do not provide a threshold comparing the first‑order bias term O(γΛζ_*) and the first‑order variance term O(γτ²/m).  
  *Why unresolved:* The analysis shows RR reduces bias from O(γ) to O(γ²) but does not characterize when this reduction outweighs any potential increase in variance or higher‑order terms. The sample‑complexity comparison in Corollary 5.3 assumes conditions on γ but not a direct trade‑off between bias and variance magnitudes.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* A theorem deriving an inequality of the form: if τ²/m ≲ C·Λζ_*·γ (for some constant C), then RR‑DSGD achieves ϵ with fewer iterations. Empirically, plotting error vs. m for different noise levels and graph sparsity would illustrate the crossover where RR becomes advantageous.

- **Open Question 3:** *Can the dependence of the convergence rate on network topology (through the spectral gap ρ) be reduced from higher‑order terms to first‑order terms by modifying DSGD, without relying on averaging or gradient tracking?*  
  *Basis:* They show that for standard DSGD, “the network topology only impacts higher‑order terms” in the variance and bias, but note that for sparse graphs (ρ close to 1), “higher‑order error that depend on the graph prevent further speed‑up” as m grows. This suggests that for poorly connected networks, the linear speed‑up in m is lost at practical precisions.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper does not propose modifications to tighten the bounds or address the spectral‑gap dependence in higher orders. It mentions Richardson‑Romberg reduces the first‑order bias but does not solve the variance’s higher‑order topology dependence.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* An algorithmic change (e.g., modified combination matrix, adaptive step size) leading to a bound where the ρ‑dependent term appears at first order in γ, not γ² or higher. Theoretical proof would show a bound like O((1‑γµ)ᵗ + γρτ²/m) instead of the current O((1‑γµ)ᵗ + γτ²/m + γ²ρ²τ²). Numerical experiments with varying ρ would validate the improved scaling.

- **Open Question 4:** *Does the Markov chain analysis extend to decentralized algorithms with gradient tracking or time‑varying communication graphs, and how do the bias and variance expansions change?*  
  *Basis:* The authors state their framework “could readily be extended to more complex decentralized algorithms” and that they hope it “will serve as a basis for the development of decentralized algorithms tailored to handle stochasticity.” They also note Assumption A2 requires a symmetric doubly stochastic matrix, which excludes directed or time‑varying graphs.  
  *Why unresolved:* They do not apply the analysis to gradient tracking (which uses extra variables) or dynamic topologies. The current proof relies on constant W and the fixed‑point characterization; generalizing requires handling state augmentation or non‑homogeneous Markov chains.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Formal extension of the Markov chain approach to a gradient tracking variant (e.g., adding gradient difference terms) proving geometric ergodicity and deriving analogous first‑order expansions. For time‑varying graphs, one would need to average over graph distributions and show bound stability. Experimental verification comparing bias/variance under different graph models would support the theory.

- **Open Question 5:** *To what extent can the first‑order bias and variance expansions for DSGD be preserved under weaker regularity assumptions, such as non‑strongly convex objectives or bounded but non‑smooth gradients?*  
  *Basis:* The analysis strongly relies on Assumption A1 (µ‑strong convexity, L‑smoothness, bounded third derivative). The bounds in Lemma 3.4, Proposition 4.2, and Theorem 4.6 all depend on µ, L, and K₃. Relaxing these assumptions is not discussed, yet real‑world decentralized problems often involve non‑convex losses or heavy‑tailed noise.  
  *Why unresolved:* The contraction arguments (e.g., in Proposition 4.1) use strong convexity for the µγ factor. Without it, convergence to a stationary distribution may be slower or require diminishing step sizes. The Taylor expansions also need smoothness. The impact on the first‑order terms (γH G∇F(θ⋆) for bias, γC(θ⋆)/m for variance) is unknown.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Developing a non‑strongly convex analysis (e.g., using Moreau envelopes or convex‑concave formulations) to derive asymptotic bias/variance formulas. Empirical evaluation on objectives with weaker properties (e.g., logistic regression with separable data but not strongly convex) to test if the linear speed‑up and topology independence persist.

## Limitations

- **Strong regularity assumptions:** Requires µ‑strong convexity, L‑smoothness up to fourth derivatives (A1), i.i.d. zero‑mean noise (A4), and doubly stochastic communication matrix with λ₂(W)<1 (A2). Many real‑world decentralized settings violate these (non‑convex objectives, correlated noise, directed graphs).
- **Constant step size:** The Markov chain framework relies on a fixed γ; decaying or adaptive steps would break time‑homogeneity and invalidate the stationary distribution analysis.
- **Linear speed‑up condition:** The O(1/m) variance scaling holds only when m ≫ γΛ². For sparse graphs (large Λ) or large γ, topology‑dependent higher‑order terms dominate, limiting the benefit of adding clients.

## Confidence

- **Convergence to stationary distribution (geometric ergodicity):** High – rigorous Wasserstein‑2 contraction proof under A1, A5.
- **Bias decomposition (deterministic + stochastic first‑order terms):** Medium – depends on Taylor expansion and smooth noise; may not extend to non‑smooth or heavy‑tailed settings.
- **Variance scaling (O(γ/m), topology‑independent at leading order):** Medium – requires m ≫ γΛ²; higher‑order topology terms may be non‑negligible otherwise.
