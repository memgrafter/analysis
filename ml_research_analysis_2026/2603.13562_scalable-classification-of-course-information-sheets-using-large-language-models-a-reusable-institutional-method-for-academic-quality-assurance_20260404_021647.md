# Scalable Classification of Course Information Sheets Using Large Language Models: A Reusable Institutional Method for Academic Quality Assurance

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.13562
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13562
*   **Reference Count:** 26
*   **Primary Result:** Automated classification of 4,684 course information sheets achieved 87% agreement with expert labels; longitudinal comparison showed a reduction in "Clear" GenAI risk from 60.3% (Year 1) to 48.2% (Year 2), with personalized reports emailed to 91.4% of teaching teams.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a method for universities to audit large volumes of course documentation for risks related to student use of generative AI in assessments. Facing the need to review thousands of course information sheets, the authors developed a four-phase pipeline combining manual pilot sampling, iterative prompt engineering with multiple LLMs, full-scale production scanning using GPT-4o, and a longitudinal re-scan. The system not only classified courses into risk categories but also automatically generated and sent personalized reports to teaching teams. Results demonstrated high agreement with human experts and a notable shift toward lower-risk categories in the second year, suggesting the method's potential for scalable academic quality assurance and its transferability to other audit domains.

## Method Summary
The core method is a four-phase pipeline. Phase 0 involves a manual pilot sample to understand the data. Phase 1 uses this sample for iterative prompt engineering, comparing outputs from GPT-4o, o3-mini, and DeepSeek R1 to refine a system prompt that structures LLM output into risk categories and narratives. Phase 2 executes the finalized prompt on the entire corpus of Bachelor/Master course sheets (N=4,684) via the GPT-4o API. The pipeline handles multilingual sheets and multi-fiche courses. Phase 3 repeats the scan identically one year later (N=4,675) for longitudinal comparison. The system also automates personalized email report generation and distribution to teaching teams using university metadata.

## Key Results
*   Achieved 87% agreement with expert labels on the pilot sample after prompt refinement.
*   Year 1 classification distribution: 60.3% Clear risk, 15.2% Potential risk, 24.5% Low risk.
*   Year 2 re-scan showed a reduction in Clear risk to 48.2%, with the most significant improvements in practice-oriented programmes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by institutionalizing an iterative, validation-driven approach to LLM deployment for a complex, heterogeneous document classification task. The initial pilot phase surfaces edge cases (e.g., internships, practical components) that drive prompt refinement. Multi-model comparison during engineering builds robustness against model-specific quirks. Using a state-of-the-art model (GPT-4o) for production ensures high reasoning capacity on ambiguous natural language. The longitudinal design with an identical prompt allows for tracking net change, though it requires sensitivity analysis to separate curriculum evolution from measurement artefacts. Closing the loop with automated, personalized stakeholder communication transforms audit findings into actionable feedback at scale.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Heterogeneous Document Preprocessing:** Why needed: University course sheets are inconsistent HTML structures; multi-fiche courses require consolidation. Quick check: Verify that a single course's full assessment description is correctly extracted from all related pages.
*   **Iterative Prompt Engineering:** Why needed: High-stakes classification requires precision; initial prompts fail on edge cases. Quick check: Use a held-out validation set to measure agreement improvement after each prompt iteration.
*   **Longitudinal Audit Design:** Why needed: To measure true curricular change amidst pipeline and content drift. Quick check: Ensure the exact same prompt and model version are used for re-scans; analyze matched-course subsets.
*   **Stakeholder Communication Loop:** Why needed: Audit impact depends on driving pedagogical change, not just generating reports. Quick check: Track email delivery rates and correlate with subsequent classification shifts at the programme level.
*   **Domain Taxonomy Adaptation:** Why needed: The three-tier risk taxonomy is specific to GenAI; transfer requires rebuilding the classification logic. Quick check: Draft a new rubric for a different domain (e.g., accessibility) and test it on a sample.

## Architecture Onboarding
The pipeline is a sequential data processing and communication system. The component map is:
Raw HTML Course Sheets -> HTML Parser & Text Extractor -> LLM Classifier (with System Prompt) -> Output Parser (Risk Category, Narrative) -> Report Generator (Multilingual) -> Email Distributor (via University System)

**Critical Path:** The end-to-end latency is dominated by the LLM API call per course sheet. Preprocessing (parsing) and post-processing (report generation, email queuing) are relatively fast but must handle failures (e.g., un-parseable HTML) gracefully to avoid blocking the batch.

**Design Tradeoffs:** 
1.  **Accuracy vs. Cost/Throughput:** Using GPT-4o maximizes validity but is expensive and slower than smaller models; the tradeoff was justified for a one-time institutional audit.
2.  **Automation vs. Human Oversight:** Fully automated emails increase reach but risk propagating errors; the design relied on high pilot-phase agreement and a post-hoc validation plan.
3.  **Generalizability vs. Specificity:** The prompt is tuned for GenAI risk; transferring to a new domain requires starting the pilot/iteration phase anew, trading off time for domain fit.

**Failure Signatures:** 
1.  Sudden spike in "Clear risk" classifications for health/education programmes, indicating the model may overgeneralize "off-site" or "practical" as high-risk.
2.  Poor fluency or terminology in non-English/Dutch reports, suggesting language detection or prompt language handling failed.
3.  High variance in classifications for the same course across re-runs, pointing to uncontrolled LLM stochasticity (temperature).

**First 3 Experiments to Run:**
1.  **Pilot Validation Replication:** Run the final prompt on a new, manually labeled sample of 30-50 diverse courses to confirm the 87% agreement benchmark and identify any new failure modes not in the original pilot.
2.  **Model Sensitivity Check:** Re-process a subset of courses with different LLM temperatures or a comparable alternative model (e.g., Claude 3.5 Sonnet) to quantify output stability for the core classification task.
3.  **Report Quality Audit:** Have native-speaking educators review a random sample of generated reports in each language for accuracy, tone, and actionability, and correlate issues with the source document's language and length.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can institutions most effectively design longitudinal LLM-based analyses to distinguish genuine curricular change from measurement artefacts when both the course content and the analysis pipeline (prompts, models) may evolve?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper explicitly identifies "pipeline confounds" as a key methodological challenge (Section 1.2, 2.6, 5.3.2). It proposes solutions like version-controlled prompts, sensitivity analysis on matched subsets, and fixed validation subsets but notes these as protocols rather than validated best practices.
**Why unresolved:** The paper's own Year 2 comparison required sensitivity analysis to bound confounds and acknowledges that observed risk reductions could partly reflect course retirements/new courses rather than policy diffusion. The optimal mix of documentation, statistical adjustment, and human review for robust institutional tracking remains empirically undefined.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal studies across multiple institutional contexts comparing different confound-mitigation strategies (e.g., maintaining a static prompt vs. calibrating for drift, using anchor courses) with ground-truth audits of a subset of courses to measure true change versus detected change.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** What specific features of course documentation (beyond mere presence/absence of AI guidelines) most reliably predict assessment vulnerability to GenAI misuse, and how can LLMs be prompted to extract and reason on these features with higher validity?
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The classification relies heavily on high-level taxonomies (controlled vs. uncontrolled settings) and explicit policy mentions. The paper notes persistent errors with "sparse documentation" for internships and practical courses (Section 5.4), implying the system struggles with nuanced, implicit assessment design features not clearly stated.
**Why unresolved:** The 87% agreement on a pilot sample suggests inherent ambiguity in the task. Errors at scale (e.g., misclassifying practical courses) indicate the model may not capture subtleties like "grade contribution weighting" or "supervision structure" effectively from often-cryptic catalogue text. The boundary between what can be reliably inferred from documentation versus requiring human judgment is unclear.
**What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed error analysis correlating specific textual omissions/patterns (e.g., lack of supervision details, vague assessment verbs) with expert judgments on a large, diverse set of manually audited courses. This could inform a feature-based prompting strategy or identify a threshold of required documentation detail for automated classification.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Under what conditions does the direct, personalised email feedback loop successfully translate institutional audit findings into concrete course-level pedagogical change, and what factors mediate or moderate this effect?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper's core purpose includes "operationalising results through direct stakeholder communication" (Abstract) and "closing the loop" between policy and practice (Section 1.1). The Year 2 improvements in practice-oriented programmes (Section 5.3.1) are cited as suggestive of impact, but causality is not established.
**Why unresolved:** The observed risk reduction (Year 1 to Year 2) could be due to the emails, parallel institutional initiatives, natural curriculum review cycles, or selective course retirements. No direct measurement of teaching team engagement with the reports, subsequent actions taken, or perceived usefulness was reported. The mediating role of programme leadership (dashboards) vs. individual emails is also unknown.
**What evidence would resolve it:** A mixed-methods study tracking a cohort of teaching teams post-email: surveys/report analytics on engagement, interviews on perceived relevance and actions taken, and linking self-reported changes to subsequent Year 3 classifications. Experimental design (e.g., randomised delayed feedback) could isolate the email's causal impact.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** How transferable is the multi-phase pipeline (pilot iteration → production scan → longitudinal re-scan) to fundamentally different audit domains (e.g., sustainability, accessibility), and what domain-specific adaptations are non-negotiable for maintaining acceptable validity?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper strongly claims transferability (Abstract, Section 1.3, 6.3) but specifies that transfer "requires: (a) domain-appropriate taxonomy; (b) pilot sample with expert labels; (c) iteration to address domain-specific ambiguities; and (d) validation of error rates." This frames transferability as a process, not a guarantee.
**Why unresolved:** The paper only demonstrates transferability within the GenAI risk domain (Year 1 to Year 2). It does not pilot the pipeline in a substantively different domain (e.g., measuring sustainability learning outcomes). The nature and severity of "domain-specific ambiguities" in other contexts, and whether the same pilot size/iteration protocol suffices, are open questions. The acceptable error rate may also vary by domain stakes.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Action research implementing the full pipeline in at least one new domain (e.g., accessibility) at an institution, documenting the iteration process, failure modes specific to that domain, and final validity metrics compared to expert panels. Comparative analysis across domains would identify cross-cutting and unique challenges.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** What is the optimal protocol for assembling a pilot sample for prompt engineering in highly heterogeneous corpora (like thousands of unique course sheets) to maximise generalisation and minimise scale-emergent errors?
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper used a manually selected pilot of 30 courses (Section 3.3, 4.2) that was diverse by discipline and language but small. It explicitly states that two major error patterns (internships, physical/practical components) "surfaced only during the full production scan" because "the 30-course pilot could not fully represent the heterogeneity" (Section 4.3.2). This reveals a fundamental sampling problem.
**Why unresolved:** No systematic method is proposed for constructing a maximally informative pilot when full coverage is impossible. The paper implies the pilot was ad-hoc based on team knowledge. Questions remain: How large must a pilot be to capture rare edge cases? Should sampling be stratified by known high-variance features (e.g., presence of 'stage', ' practicum' in title)? Can automated clustering be used?
**What evidence would resolve it:** A methodological study using the full 4,684-course corpus as a "test bed": generate many random pilot samples of varying sizes and stratification schemes, apply the prompt iteration protocol to each, and measure how well the final prompt performs on the *unseen* remainder. This would yield data-driven guidelines for pilot design in similar large-scale, heterogeneous document classification tasks.

## Limitations
*   **Undisclosed Pilot Composition:** The exact makeup and labeling rationale of the 30-course pilot sample are not detailed, preventing full replication of the prompt engineering process and assessment of its representativeness.
*   **Black-Box Data Preprocessing:** The specific scraping and parsing code for handling HTML heterogeneity and multi-fiche consolidation is not provided, creating a critical gap in the reproduction chain.
*   **Unreported LLM Parameters:** The LLM temperature and other generation parameters are not specified, introducing potential stochasticity that could affect output consistency and the reported agreement metrics.

## Confidence
*   **High Confidence:** The procedural description of automated email generation and distribution is clear and verifiable.
*   **Medium Confidence:** The reported 87% expert agreement on the pilot sample is plausible given the described iterative process but lacks full transparency on the expert labeling protocol and the pilot's diversity.
*   **Low Confidence:** The claim of a genuine reduction in "Clear" risk from Year 1 to Year 2 is suggestive but low confidence due to potential unaddressed confounders (e.g., actual curriculum changes, undocumented pipeline drift, sampling differences between scans).

## Next Checks
1.  **Re-run production pipeline on a fresh, manually annotated sample (n≥100)** spanning all three risk categories and diverse programs to verify the 87% agreement benchmark, explicitly documenting label rationale for disagreements.
2.  **Audit non-English/Dutch output quality** by having a native-speaking educator review a random sample of generated narratives for terminology accuracy and fluency, correlating issues with the language detection logic.
3.  **Correlate classification outcomes with concrete documentation metrics** (e.g., assessment description length, presence of specific keywords like "onsite" or "portfolio") to diagnose whether classifications are driven by sparse/ambiguous data versus intended policy interpretation.
