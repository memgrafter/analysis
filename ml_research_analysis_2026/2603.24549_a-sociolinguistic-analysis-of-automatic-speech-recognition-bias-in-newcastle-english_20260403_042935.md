# A Sociolinguistic Analysis of Automatic Speech Recognition Bias in Newcastle English

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24549
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24549
- **Reference count:** 25
- **Primary result:** ASR systems exhibit biased performance for regional dialects like Newcastle English, with error patterns systematically linked to linguistic features (phonological, lexical) and speaker demographics (gender, age).

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a sociolinguistic analysis of bias in a state-of-the-art commercial automatic speech recognition (ASR) system (Rev AI) when processing Newcastle English speech from the DECTE corpus. The authors manually analyzed 3,005 transcription errors, coding them by linguistic domain (phonological, lexical, morphosyntactic) and linking them to speaker demographics. Phonological errors were the most prevalent (60.8%), driven by dialect-specific vowel quality (e.g., monophthongisation of FACE and GOAT) and glottalisation. Lexical errors (19.6%) frequently involved local vocabulary (e.g., “aye”). Social analysis revealed higher error rates for male speakers and individuals at age extremes (16–20 and 61–90). An acoustic case study confirmed that speakers with more monophthongal realizations of FACE and GOAT vowels incurred more errors. The results demonstrate that ASR errors are not random but are systematically patterned by linguistic and social factors, underscoring the need for more inclusive ASR training and evaluation.

## Method Summary
The study used the DECTE corpus, containing 72 hours of spontaneous speech from 160 speakers of Newcastle English. After comparing four ASR systems on a 10% subset, the authors selected Rev AI (UK English configuration) as the best performer (WER ~31.95%). They processed 48 recordings (83 speakers) with Rev AI and computed word error rates (WER) against the human transcriptions. Errors were aligned at the word level and manually coded by two sociophoneticians according to a protocol linking misrecognitions to specific Newcastle linguistic features. Statistical analysis employed negative binomial generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with error count as the dependent variable and speaker ID as a random effect. An acoustic case study focused on vowel monophthongisation: for speakers with high vs. low error rates, the authors used the Montreal Forced Aligner to extract F1/F2 trajectories for FACE and GOAT tokens, then applied discrete cosine transform (DCT) coefficients to quantify glide reduction and correlated these with error counts.

## Key Results
- **Phonological errors dominate (60.8%)**, primarily due to dialect-specific vowel quality (39.5% of phonological errors) and glottalisation (22.7%). Examples include “like” → “lake” (FACE monophthongisation) and “forgot to tell” → “forgot tell” (t-glottalisation).
- **Lexical errors (19.6%)** often involve local vocabulary and toponyms (e.g., “aye”, “Sunderland”, “Gateshead”), reflecting standardisation bias.
- **Social disparities**: Male speakers accounted for 57.4% of errors; the youngest (16–20) and oldest (61–90) age groups showed higher error shares (26.3%, 31.4%) than working adults. A GLMM showed a significant main effect of gender within lexical errors ( males: µ̂=9.12, females: µ̂=5.25, p=.008). Acoustic analysis confirmed that speakers with more monophthongal FACE/GOAT vowels had significantly higher error counts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** ASR acoustic models underfit regional vowel quality and glottalisation patterns present in Newcastle spontaneous speech, leading to systematic phonological errors.
- **Mechanism:** The acoustic model (likely a DNN/HMM hybrid or end-to-end system) was trained on a distribution of speech that under-represents the specific phonetic realisations of Newcastle English (e.g., monophthongal FACE/GOAT, centering diphthongs, near-glottalisation). When presented with these out-of-distribution acoustic patterns, the model's state-tying or tokenization fails to correctly map them to the intended phoneme sequence, resulting in substitutions that often reflect the nearest acoustically plausible Standard Southern British English (SSBE) equivalent.
- **Core assumption:** The ASR's acoustic model relies on a relatively homogeneous, mainstream accent representation and does not adequately model the continuous phonetic parameter space (e.g., formant trajectories, voice onset time) associated with regional variants.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "Phonological errors were predominant (60.8%), driven by dialect-specific vowel quality and glottalisation."
  - [section 4.1.3]: Details that vowel quality errors (39.5% of phonological errors) and glottalisation/glottal stop errors (22.7%) are the largest subcategories. Specific examples link errors like "like"→"lake" (FACE monophthongisation) and "forgot to tell"→"forgot tell" (t-glottalisation) to Newcastle features.
  - [corpus]: The DECTE corpus provides spontaneous speech with verified transcriptions, confirming the ground-truth presence of these features. The error analysis directly links misrecognitions to these features. *Assumption:* Rev AI's training data, being proprietary and optimized for general "UK English," lacks sufficient exemplars of these specific Newcastle variants.
- **Break condition:** If a controlled experiment introducing only the specific Newcastle vowel or glottalisation tokens (from DECTE) into a clean, noise-free test set produces no significant increase in error rate compared to SSBE tokens, then the mechanism is likely not acoustic model underfitting but perhaps a language model or vocabulary issue.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The ASR language model's vocabulary and decoding probabilities are biased towards standard lexicon and grammatical constructions, causing lexical standardisation and morphosyntactic "corrections" of dialectal forms.
- **Mechanism:** The decoder's language model (n-gram, neural LM) assigns higher probability to sequences composed of standard vocabulary (e.g., "television" over "telly") and standard morphosyntax (e.g., "my" over "me" for possessive, "know" over "knaa"). When the acoustic model generates an ambiguous or regionally-accented lattice, the decoder resolves the ambiguity by selecting the high-probability standard path, effectively "correcting" the dialectal input. For low-frequency local lexical items (e.g., "aye", "bairn", place names like "Sunderland"), they may be absent from the vocabulary or have such low prior probability that the decoder substitutes a phonetically similar standard word.
- **Core assumption:** The language model was trained on text corpora (e.g., web data, news) that under-represents or excludes regional vocabulary, non-standard grammar, and local toponyms, reinforcing a standard language norm.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "Lexical errors were secondary (19.6%), often involving local vocabulary (e.g., 'aye')." and mentions "standardisation errors."
  - [section 4.1.5]: Lexical errors are dominated by toponyms (Sunderland, Gateshead) and local vocabulary ("aye", "Geordie", "bloke", "canny", "nowt", "bairn"). The analysis notes these are "strong markers of regional identity."
  - [section 4.1.6]: Standardisation errors (9.8%) primarily involve pronouns (56.5%), conjunctions, and vocabulary, where dialect forms ("me"→"my", "wor"→"our", "telly"→"television") are replaced with standard equivalents.
  - [corpus]: The DECTE transcriptions contain these dialectal forms, proving their natural occurrence. The ASR's consistent replacement indicates a LM bias, not mere acoustic confusion.
- **Break condition:** If the ASR system's log probabilities or beam search outputs can be inspected, and for a set of dialectal utterances, the dialectal word sequence is never in the top-N hypotheses despite a clean acoustic signal, then the LM is the primary bottleneck. Alternatively, if fine-tuning the LM on a small corpus of DECTE transcripts dramatically reduces these specific errors without changing the acoustic model, it confirms the LM's role.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Socially conditioned linguistic variation (gender, age) interacts with dialectal feature usage, creating performance disparities where speakers who employ more non-standard, salient regional forms (disproportionately men and young/old adults) incur higher error rates.
- **Mechanism:** Sociolinguistic patterns dictate that certain social groups use more vernacular, regionally-marked linguistic features. In this Newcastle data, men and speakers at age extremes (16-20, 61-90) produced more errors. The mechanism is that these groups exhibit a higher *density* or *salience* of the dialectal features (like specific lexical items, vowel monophthongisation, or 'g'-dropping) that the ASR system already struggles with (Mechanisms 1 & 2). It is not a direct "bias against men/age" in the model, but an indirect bias *mediated* by linguistic behaviour. The ASR fails more on the speech patterns that happen to be more frequent in these social groups within this community.
- **Core assumption:** Error rate is a function of linguistic feature realisation, and the distribution of those features correlates with social variables (gender, age) due to well-documented sociolinguistic patterning (e.g., women using more standard forms, age-grading).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "Social analysis revealed higher error rates for male speakers and individuals at age extremes (16-20, 61-90)."
  - [section 4.1.1]: Descriptive stats show males account for 57.4% of errors; youngest (16-20) and oldest (61-90) groups have higher error shares (26.3%, 31.4%) than working adults.
  - [section 4.1.2]: GLMM shows a significant main effect of gender *within the lexical error type* (males: ˆµ=9.12, females: ˆµ=5.25, p=.008). The interaction term for Standardisation×Female was significant, but the key lexical finding supports the claim.
  - [section 5 (Discussion)]: Links higher male errors to men using "more non-standard forms" and discusses age-grading patterns where young/old use more vernacular forms.
  - [corpus]: The DECTE corpus is balanced by gender and age, allowing this correlation to be measured. The manual error coding links each error to a specific linguistic feature, which can then be aggregated by speaker demographics.
- **Break condition:** If, after statistically controlling for the *quantity* and *type* of dialectal features used by a speaker (e.g., number of lexical dialect items, degree of vowel monophthongisation), the social variables (gender, age) no longer significantly predict error counts, then the social disparity is fully explained by linguistic behaviour (supporting Mechanism 3). If social variables remain significant predictors, there may be an additional, unmeasured model bias.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Sociolinguistic Salience & Indexicality
- **Why needed here:** To understand *why* certain features (e.g., "aye", monophthongal GOAT) cause ASR failures, one must know they are not random but are intentional, socially meaningful markers of local identity. An engineer must learn to identify which linguistic variants are likely to be under-represented in training data because they are regionally or socially marked.
- **Quick check question:** Given a list of Newcastle English features (e.g., clear /l/, 'g'-dropping, "wor" pronoun), which are most likely to be high-salience identity markers and thus least represented in mainstream ASR training corpora?

### Concept: Acoustic-Phonetic Parameter Space vs. Categorical Units
- **Why needed here:** ASR acoustic models often discretize continuous speech signals into phones or subword units. The failure on monophthongisation (Section 4.2) shows the system struggles with gradient variation along a continuum (diphthong vs. monophthong). An engineer needs to grasp that model performance can collapse when faced with phonetic realities that don't fit neatly into its discrete decision boundaries.
- **Quick check question:** How might the formant trajectory (F1/F2 movement) of a speaker's GOAT vowel with a centered, monophthongal quality differ from the expected SSBE diphthong trajectory, and why would this confuse an acoustic model trained primarily on the latter?

### Concept: Demographic Performance Skews as Proxy Measures
- **Why needed here:** Gender/age/WER correlations (Section 4.1.1) are not root causes but proxy signals for underlying linguistic variation. An engineer must avoid treating demographics as causal and instead use them as a heuristic to identify subgroups whose speech likely contains higher concentrations of the problematic linguistic features.
- **Quick check question:** In the Newcastle data, why might the higher error rate for male speakers be an indicator of linguistic (not demographic) bias, and what specific linguistic evidence from the paper supports this?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Audio → Feature Extraction → Acoustic Model → Language Model (Decoder) → Transcript

### Critical Path
The error chain for a dialectal utterance:  
`Regionally-accented audio` → `Feature extraction` → `AM emits low-probability/ambiguous phone sequence due to unseen acoustic patterns` → `LM resolves ambiguity by selecting high-probability standard words/phrases` → `Output contains phonological/lexical standardisation errors`.

### Design Tradeoffs
- *Training Data Breadth vs. Depth:* A model trained on vast but acoustically homogeneous "general" speech (breadth) will have poorer performance on specific dialects vs. a model trained on a smaller but deliberately diverse set (depth). This study indicates Rev AI prioritizes breadth.
- *Vocabulary Size vs. Dialect Coverage:* A large vocabulary reduces OOV rates for standard terms but may still exclude low-resource dialectal lexicon unless explicitly targeted. LM smoothing may back off to common words.
- *Black-box vs. Fine-tunable:* Commercial systems offer ease of use but no insight or adaptation. Research systems allow fine-tuning on dialect data (as cited: Torgbi et al., 2025; Sanabria et al., 2023) but require resources.

### Failure Signatures
- *Phonological Cluster:* High WER on specific phones/diphthongs (e.g., FACE, GOAT) across many words, especially when acoustic analysis shows consistent non-SSBE formant patterns.
- *Lexical OOV/Substitution:* Consistent replacement of a set of known local words ("aye", place names) with specific standard substitutes or deletion.
- *Social Disparity Pattern:* Error rate distributions that correlate with social variables (gender, age) in a way that matches known sociolinguistic hierarchies of language use (e.g., more non-standard form usage).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Feature-Specific Error Rate:** For a subset of DECTE, compute WER *only* on words containing known target Newcastle phonological features (e.g., words with /t/ in T-to-R environments, FACE/GOAT vowels). Compare to WER on matched control words without these features. Expect significantly higher WER on feature-bearing words.
2. **Lexicon Recall Test:** Extract all unique word types from DECTE transcripts. Query the ASR's output vocabulary (if accessible via API logs or by probing with clean, single-word utterances) to see which local lexical items ("bairn", "canny", "Sunderland") are OOV or have very low recognition confidence compared to standard synonyms.
3. **Social Group Controlled Comparison:** Re-run the GLMM (Section 3.4) but add a covariate for the *speaker-level frequency* of the top 10 most error-inducing linguistic features (e.g., count of 'g'-dropping tokens, count of "aye" tokens). Test if the gender/age effects diminish or disappear when controlling for this individual linguistic behaviour. This tests Mechanism 3 directly.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. To what extent do ASR systems misrecognise dialectal features of Newcastle English, and which specific phonological, lexical, and morphosyntactic features are most affected? The study answers this for one commercial ASR system and one dialect; it remains unknown whether other ASR architectures exhibit similar bias profiles or whether the identified problematic features generalize to other regional varieties.
2. How do ASR error rates vary across social variables such as gender, age, and socioeconomic status within a single regional speech community? The study found descriptive trends but statistical power was limited; larger, balanced samples are needed to clarify independent and joint contributions, and replication in other communities is required.
3. How can sociolinguistic, community-informed analysis identify the potential causes and patterns of bias in speech technologies? The paper demonstrates one approach but does not offer a scalable or standardized methodology; it remains unclear how to systematically integrate acoustic measures, handle compound errors, and translate analyses into actionable improvements.
4. What is the appropriate granularity for error classification in ASR bias studies—should errors be categorized by their linguistic root cause (e.g., phonology, lexicon) or by surface output patterns that may combine multiple dialectal features? The authors separated by root cause for analytical clarity but note that many failures involve simultaneous dialectal cues; no empirical evidence exists on which scheme best captures perceptual reality or guides mitigation.

## Limitations
- The exact list of the 83 speakers and 48 recordings used from the DECTE corpus is not specified, preventing exact replication of the dataset.
- The internal details of the Rev AI ASR system (architecture, training data, vocabulary) are proprietary and undisclosed, limiting insight into the source of bias.
- Inter-annotator agreement metrics and the final coding rulebook for error classification are not provided, creating uncertainty in the manual error coding procedure.
- The analysis is restricted to a single ASR system (Rev AI); while other systems were compared on a subset, the detailed error pattern analysis was not performed for them, limiting generalizability of the bias findings to other ASR architectures.

## Confidence
- **ASR bias exists for Newcastle English** → **High** (direct error analysis with systematic coding).
- **Phonological errors are the most common error type** → **High** (quantified as 60.8% with detailed breakdown).
- **Lexical errors involve local vocabulary and toponyms** → **High** (explicit examples and counts; standardisation patterns clear).
- **Male speakers have higher error rates** → **Medium** (significance only within lexical errors; may be confounded by linguistic behavior).
- **Age extremes have higher error rates** → **Medium** (descriptive trends; no statistical test reported).
- **Monophthongal vowel realizations increase errors** → **High** (acoustic correlation study with DCT measures).
- **Errors are systematic, not random** → **High** (patterned across linguistic and social dimensions).

## Next Checks
1. **Feature-specific error rate validation:** Compute WER separately on words containing known Newcastle phonological features (e.g., FACE/GOAT monophthongisation) and on matched control words. A significant difference confirms phonological bias.
2. **Lexicon recall assessment:** Determine which local lexical items (e.g., “bairn”, “canny”, place names) are out-of-vocabulary or have low confidence in the ASR system by probing with clean single-word utterances or analyzing API logs.
3. **Social mediation test:** Extend the GLMM by adding speaker-level frequencies of key dialectal features as covariates. If gender/age effects vanish, the social disparity is fully mediated by linguistic behavior.
