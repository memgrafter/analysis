# A Few-Shot LLM Framework for Extreme Day Classification in Electricity Markets

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16735
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16735
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: With only two months of training data, the LLM framework achieves an F1-score of 35.29%, substantially outperforming SVM (4.65%) and XGBoost (4.8%).

## Executive Summary
Predicting next-day extreme electricity price spikes is challenging due to rare events and volatile conditions. This study introduces a few-shot framework that transforms system data (demand, renewables, weather, prices) into textual prompts for a large language model (GPT-4.1), enabling spike classification without model training. Using Texas market data, the LLM classifier performs comparably to supervised SVM and XGBoost models when trained on three years of data. Critically, with only two months of training data, the LLM markedly outperforms both baselines, demonstrating its effectiveness as a data-efficient tool for spike classification in scarce-data scenarios.

## Method Summary
The framework consists of three components: a Data Preprocessor that collects raw market and weather data, engineers statistical features (e.g., means, standard deviations, lags), and labels spike days based on the 95th percentile of intraday price standard deviation; a Prompt Generator that converts each day’s feature vector into a natural-language description, computes embeddings, builds a FAISS index of historical days, retrieves similar past days via maximal marginal relevance (MMR), and assembles a prompt with system instructions, examples, and the query; and a Spike Predictor that sends the prompt to GPT-4.1, parses the binary output ("Yes"/"No") and confidence score, and makes a prediction. The approach avoids any model training, relying instead on the LLM’s in-context learning from retrieved examples.

## Key Results
- With a full three-year training period (2021–2023), the LLM’s classification performance is comparable to supervised SVM and XGBoost models on the 2024 test set.
- Under a limited two-month training regime, the LLM achieves an F1-score of 35.29%, far surpassing SVM (4.65%) and XGBoost (4.8%) when using precision-recall optimal thresholds.
- The LLM’s confidence threshold for optimal predictions is substantially higher (~0.9) than those of the baselines (~0.04–0.3), indicating a more conservative but precise spike identification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The framework’s success hinges on three synergistic mechanisms. First, converting structured electricity market features into natural language allows an off-the-shelf LLM to interpret system conditions without numerical training; the model’s pre-trained knowledge maps linguistic patterns (e.g., “high net demand forecast mean and low reserve margin”) to spike likelihood. Second, retrieval of historically similar days via FAISS and MMR provides concrete few-shot examples, enabling analogical reasoning that compensates for the lack of supervised training on labeled spikes. Third, the LLM leverages its vast pre-trained understanding of general market dynamics, weather impacts, and grid operations to reason beyond the immediate examples, filling gaps when historical data is sparse. Together, these mechanisms allow the LLM to function as a data-efficient classifier, especially under limited training data.

## Foundational Learning
- **Feature Engineering for Rare Events**: Tailored statistics (momentum, lags, min/max) are needed to capture precursors to volatility rather than just high price levels. Quick check: Does your feature set distinguish between normal high-load days and conditions that historically preceded price *volatility*?
- **In-Context Learning (Few-Shot Prompting)**: The LLM learns the classification task from examples embedded in the prompt. Prompt construction, example selection (via MMR), and ordering are critical. Quick check: How does the choice of *k* (number of examples) and their relevance trade off against prompt length limits and example redundancy?
- **Similarity Search for Analogical Reasoning**: The quality of the FAISS index and embedding model must capture similarity in the textual description space that the LLM uses. Quick check: Is your embedding model effectively mapping feature vectors to semantically meaningful text for similarity search?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Preprocessor -> Prompt Generator -> Spike Predictor.  
**Critical path:** Data integrity → Feature correctness → Textual prompt clarity → Effective example retrieval → LLM API response parsing.  
**Design tradeoffs:** Prompt length vs. example richness (more *k* improves context but risks noise/limit exceedance); retrieval specificity (searching for similar spike days ensures positive examples but may miss analogies from normal days); static vs. dynamic thresholds (validation-set optimization adapts to LLM calibration but may overfit).  
**Failure signatures:** Parsing failure (malformed LLM output); homogeneous predictions (LLM outputs nearly identical confidences); degradation with more data (poor similarity metric); excessively high optimal threshold (e.g., >0.95, making predictor always “No”).  
**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Validate text-to-feature fidelity by inspecting generated prompts and querying the LLM for summaries.  
2. Benchmark retrieval relevance by manually assessing top-*k* examples for known spike queries and calculating precision@*k*.  
3. Isolate LLM vs. retrieval by comparing performance with *k=0* (zero-shot) versus *k=3*.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does the spike day definition (threshold percentile) affect classification performance? The paper uses the top 5% by intraday price standard deviation but does not test sensitivity.
2. Does the framework maintain its data-efficiency advantage in other electricity markets (e.g., CAISO, PJM, EPEX) with different characteristics? Generalizability is untested.
3. What aspects of the textual prompt design (feature ordering, phrasing) most influence accuracy? The conclusion suggests expanding the prompt format, implying current design is not optimized.
4. Are the LLM’s confidence scores well-calibrated? No calibration analysis (e.g., reliability diagrams) is provided.

## Limitations
- **Prompt design sensitivity:** Performance depends on precise wording and feature ordering; robustness is unexplored.
- **Embedding-text misalignment:** FAISS search operates on textual embeddings, which may not align with the LLM’s internal reasoning about spike causality, potentially retrieving irrelevant analogies.
- **Static LLM knowledge:** The model’s knowledge is frozen at its training cut-off (likely 2023); market changes in 2024 could invalidate latent knowledge.
- **Threshold calibration overfitting:** Using validation-set optimized PR thresholds may overfit to the specific test year; threshold stability is unknown.

## Confidence
- **LLM as a data-efficient spike classifier (vs. baselines with 2mo data): High** (substantial F1 gap directly measured).
- **Few-shot retrieval (MMR+FAISS) as the key enabler: Medium** (mechanism sound but quantitative contribution inferred; no explicit ablation shown).
- **Textualization of features preserves predictive signal: Low** (core assumption untested; no isolation of textual format vs. direct numerical prompts).

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt Perturbation Test:** Systematically rephrase prompts (synonyms, structure, ordering) and measure F1-score variance to quantify robustness.
2. **Retrieval Sanity Check:** For random query days, manually inspect top-*k* examples, calculate precision@*k* (are retrieved days actual spikes?), and compare raw feature vector similarity.
3. **Zero-Shot vs. Few-Shot Ablation:** Run framework with *k=0* on two-month data regime and report F1 delta versus *k=3* to isolate retrieval’s marginal contribution.
