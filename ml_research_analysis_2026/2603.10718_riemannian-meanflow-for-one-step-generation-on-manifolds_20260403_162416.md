# Riemannian MeanFlow for One-Step Generation on Manifolds
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.10718
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10718
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: RMF enables one-step generative modeling on Riemannian manifolds by learning an average velocity field, achieving competitive MMD scores with just 1 NFE, dramatically reducing sampling cost compared to ODE-based baselines.

## Executive Summary
Generative models on Riemannian manifolds typically require solving ordinary differential equations (ODEs) for sampling, which is computationally expensive. This work introduces Riemannian MeanFlow (RMF), a method that learns an average velocity field to map noise directly to data in a single step without ODE integration. RMF leverages parallel transport to define a time-averaged velocity and derives a MeanFlow identity that connects this average to the instantaneous velocity field via directional derivatives. The training avoids complex geometric computations by operating in a common tangent space using logarithmic maps and Jacobian-vector products. The objective decomposes into two terms; conflict-aware multi-task learning (PCGrad) mitigates gradient interference. RMF supports conditional generation with classifier-free guidance. Experiments on spheres ($S^2$), tori ($T^n$), and the rotation group $\text{SO}(3)$ show that RMF obtains lower MMD than baselines like RFM, RCT, and G-LSD at 1 NFE, demonstrating a superior quality-efficiency trade-off.

## Method Summary
RMF learns a neural network $u_\theta(x_t, r, t)$ that predicts the average velocity from $x_r$ to $x_t$. For a given pair of points $x_1$ (data) and $x_0$ (noise) and times $r<t$, the interpolation point $x_t$ is obtained via geodesic interpolation: $x_t = \text{Exp}_{x_1}(\kappa(t)\text{Log}_{x_1}(x_0))$ with $\kappa(t)=1-t$. The instantaneous velocity is $\dot{x}_t = \frac{1}{1-t}\text{Log}_{x_t}(x_1)$. The average velocity $u_\text{gt}$ is defined by parallel-transporting $\dot{x}_t$ from $x_t$ to $x_r$ (or vice versa) and averaging; the resulting MeanFlow identity (Prop. 3.1) expresses $u_\text{gt}$ as $\dot{x}_t - (t-r)\xi_t$, where $\xi_t$ is a directional derivative of $u_\theta$ computed automatically via Jacobian-vector products (JVP). Training minimizes a loss decomposed into $L_1 = \|u_\theta - \dot{x}_t\|_g^2$ and $L_2 = 2\langle u_\theta, (t-r)\xi_t\rangle_g$, with gradients potentially conflicting. PCGrad projects conflicting gradient components before summing. At inference, sample $x_0\sim p_0$, compute $u_\theta(x_0,0,1)$, then set $x_1^\text{gen} = \text{Exp}_{x_0}(u_\theta(x_0,0,1))$. Conditional generation uses classifier-free guidance by training a conditional model and combining conditional and unconditional outputs at sampling.

## Key Results
- **One-step quality:** RMF achieves lower Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) than Riemannian Flow Matching (RFM), Riemannian Conservation Laws (RCT), and G-streamlined Diffusion (G-LSD) when using 1 Number of Function Evaluations (NFE) on $S^2$, $\mathbb{T}^2$, $\mathbb{T}^7$, and $\text{SO}(3)$.
- **Efficiency:** Sampling requires a single network evaluation and an exponential map, eliminating ODE integration overhead.
- **Stability:** Conflict-aware PCGrad improves training convergence, particularly on the Earth disaster dataset where gradient conflicts are frequent.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** RMF enables one-step generation on manifolds by learning an average-velocity field that directly maps noise to data without ODE integration.  
**Mechanism:** Defines the average velocity over an interval by parallel-transporting instantaneous velocities to the current tangent space, then derives a MeanFlow identity linking this average to the instantaneous velocity via a covariant derivative.  
**Core assumption:** Parallel transport preserves geometric structure, making the time-averaged velocity well-defined and meaningful across point-dependent tangent spaces.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "RMF defines an average-velocity field via parallel transport and derives a Riemannian MeanFlow identity that links average and instantaneous velocities for intrinsic supervision." Section 3.1: Eq. (5) and Prop. 3.1 formalize the definition and identity. Related MeanFlow work on Euclidean spaces (e.g., Geng et al., 2025a) provides context, but the Riemannian extension via parallel transport is novel.  
**Break condition:** If parallel transport is numerically unstable or the manifold lacks well-behaved geodesics (e.g., incomplete manifolds), the averaging may not reflect the true mean direction, degrading one-step accuracy.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Practical training avoids expensive geometric computations by operating in a common tangent space using logarithmic maps.  
**Mechanism:** Represents nearby points via the Log map in $T_{x_t}M$, converting manifold operations into Euclidean vector operations; computes directional derivatives via Jacobian-vector products (JVPs) instead of Christoffel symbols.  
**Core assumption:** All points involved ($x_0$, $x_1$, $x_t$) lie within a normal neighborhood of $x_t$, where the Log map is single-valued, smooth, and differentiable.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "We make this identity practical in a log-map tangent representation, avoiding trajectory simulation and heavy geometric computations." Section 3.1: Eq. (10) shows the implemented target using Log maps and JVPs. Geomstats (Miolane et al., 2020a) provides standard implementations of Exp/Log, but the specific use for MeanFlow supervision is from this paper.  
**Break condition:** Near cut loci (e.g., antipodal points on a sphere), the Log map becomes multi-valued or discontinuous, causing instabilities in velocity estimation and gradient computation.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Conflict-aware multi-task learning (PCGrad) stabilizes optimization by mitigating gradient interference between the decomposed RMF loss terms.  
**Mechanism:** Decomposes the RMF objective into $L_1$ (regression to instantaneous velocity) and $L_2$ (regularization via directional derivative); when their gradients have negative cosine similarity, PCGrad projects each onto the orthogonal complement of the other before summing.  
**Core assumption:** Negative gradient cosine similarity indicates conflicting objectives that harm convergence; suppressing the conflicting components improves joint optimization without manual weighting schedules.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "We decompose the RMF objective into two terms and apply conflict-aware multi-task learning to mitigate gradient interference." Section 3.2: Prop. 3.2 gives the decomposition; Algorithm 1 and Eq. (15) detail PCGrad application. Section 5.1: Figure 2 empirically shows frequent negative cosine similarities on the Earth dataset, and RMF-MT improves over RMF where conflicts are stronger.  
**Break condition:** If the gradient conflicts are actually beneficial (e.g., implicit regularization) or PCGrad projections remove useful signal due to noisy gradient estimates, convergence may slow or performance degrade.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data sampler -> Network $u_\theta$ -> JVP module -> Loss calculator ($L_1$, $L_2$) -> PCGrad module -> Optimizer.

**Critical path**: During training: (1) Sample $x_1 \sim p_1$, $x_0 \sim p_0$, and times $(r,t)$; (2) Compute $x_t$ via geodesic interpolation and $\dot{x}_t$; (3) Forward $u_\theta(x_t,r,t)$; (4) Compute $\xi_t$ via JVP with direction $(\dot{x}_t,0,1)$ and stop-gradient; (5) Compute $L_1$ and $L_2$; (6) Backpropagate to get gradients $g_1, g_2$; (7) Apply PCGrad; (8) Update $\theta$. During inference: sample $x_0$, compute $u_\theta(x_0,0,1)$, then $x_1^\text{gen}=\text{Exp}_{x_0}(u_\theta)$.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Log map vs. Christoffel symbols: Using Log avoids manual coordinate derivatives but restricts to normal neighborhoods; Christoffel-based covariant derivatives work globally but require basis management.
- PCGrad vs. fixed weighting: PCGrad adapts to gradient conflicts without schedule tuning but may over-project if gradient estimates are noisy.
- One-step vs. multi-step: RMF trades some sample quality for massive speedup; residual errors from the MeanFlow identity approximation may limit fidelity on complex manifolds.

**Failure signatures**:
- High MMD at 1 NFE compared to multi-step baselines, indicating poor one-step approximation.
- Persistent negative cosine similarity between $g_1$ and $g_2$ despite PCGrad, suggesting optimization instability or inappropriate projection.
- NaNs during training near manifold singularities (e.g., antipodal on sphere) due to Log map breakdown.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Earth disaster datasets on $S^2$: Tests multi-class spherical distributions; evaluates whether RMF captures non-uniform data on a curved manifold.
2. Protein torsion angles on $\mathbb{T}^2$ and $\mathbb{T}^7$: Tests periodic manifolds with high-dimensional wrap-around structure; assesses scalability to multiple toroidal dimensions.
3. Synthetic datasets on $\text{SO}(3)$: Tests non-Euclidean Lie group geometry; verifies handling of non-commutative rotations and bi-invariant metric.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: How can RMF be extended to Riemannian manifolds that lack closed-form exponential and logarithm maps?  
*Basis*: The paper states: "Future work includes extending RMF to broader geometric settings where closed-form geodesics are unavailable." (Conclusion)  
*Why unresolved*: Current training relies on explicit Exp/Log to construct interpolation paths and compute velocities; without them, the log-tangent representation becomes intractable.  
*Resolving evidence*: Develop efficient numerical approximations or alternative representations that avoid direct geodesic computations while preserving geometric consistency; validate on manifolds like hyperbolic spaces or Stiefel manifolds.

**Open Question 2**: Under what theoretical conditions does RMF's one-step generation converge to the data distribution?  
*Basis*: The paper provides empirical results but no convergence analysis; the MeanFlow identity is derived but the approximation error of the learned average-velocity field remains unquantified.  
*Why unresolved*: Without theoretical guarantees, it is unclear when RMF will succeed or fail, particularly on high-dimensional or complex manifolds.  
*Resolving evidence*: Prove that minimizing the decomposed RMF objective yields a vector field whose one-step integration approximates the data distribution under smoothness and geometry assumptions; derive sample complexity bounds.

**Open Question 3**: What causes gradient conflicts between the two decomposed RMF losses, and under what data regimes does PCGrad fail to improve or degrade performance?  
*Basis*: Section 5.1 reports frequent negative cosine similarities; Table 1 shows RMF-MT degrades on the Volcano dataset (small size).  
*Why unresolved*: The paper uses PCGrad as a heuristic without analyzing the root causes of conflict or its interaction with dataset size and manifold geometry.  
*Resolving evidence*: Investigate the geometry of the loss landscape; identify properties that lead to gradient misalignment; design adaptive or theoretically grounded multi-task optimization methods that provably improve convergence.

## Limitations
- **Time distribution and hyperparameters** (e.g., joint distribution of $(r,t)$, condition dropout rate) are unspecified; default assumptions may bias results and hinder reproduction.
- **Network architecture details** (activation functions, normalization, exact initialization) are missing; replication relies on heuristics.
- **Singularity handling** in manifold operations (e.g., antipodal points on $S^2$, ambiguous logarithm on $\text{SO}(3)$) is not described; may cause numerical failures or require ad-hoc fixes.

## Confidence
- **MeanFlow identity (Prop. 3.1)**: High – mathematically sound under Riemannian assumptions.
- **One-step generation via average velocity**: Medium – empirical results are promising, but theoretical conditions (no cut loci, sufficient capacity) may not always hold.
- **Practical training (log‑map + PCGrad)**: Medium – ablations show benefit, but sensitivity to singularities and gradient noise remains.

## Next Checks
1. **Time distribution sensitivity**: Train RMF with alternative distributions over $(r,t)$ (e.g., uniform over triangle, beta-distributed) and measure the impact on MMD and training stability.
2. **Singularity mitigation**: Introduce jitter for antipodal points on $S^2$ and angle clipping on $\text{SO}(3)$; compare stability, convergence, and final MMD.
3. **PCGrad ablation**: Remove PCGrad and monitor gradient cosine similarities; if conflicts persist, compare performance with other conflict-resolution methods (e.g., gradient surgery, magnitude-based weighting).
