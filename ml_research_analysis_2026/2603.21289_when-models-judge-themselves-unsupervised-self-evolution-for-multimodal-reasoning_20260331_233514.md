# When Models Judge Themselves: Unsupervised Self-Evolution for Multimodal Reasoning

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID**: 2603.21289  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21289  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Unsupervised self-evolution improves multimodal reasoning accuracy by up to +5.9% on MathVision and averages +3.3% across five benchmarks, matching supervised RL performance with greater training stability.

## Executive Summary

Multimodal reasoning systems typically require expensive human annotations or teacher model distillation to improve. This paper introduces an unsupervised self‑evolution framework: an Actor model generates multiple reasoning trajectories per input, while a frozen Judge (identical copy of the Actor) provides a bounded quality modulation. Final rewards combine self‑consistency frequency with calibrated Judge scores, and policy updates use group‑relative advantages via GRPO. No ground‑truth labels are needed. On the MathVision benchmark, training on Geo3K lifts accuracy from 25.0% to 30.9% (+5.9 absolute). Across five mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MathVision, MathVerse, WeMath, LogicVista, DynaMath) the method achieves an average improvement of +3.3% over the base Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B. It outperforms prior unsupervised approaches (MM‑UPT, VisionZero) and reaches performance comparable to supervised RL. Training remains stable, exhibiting healthier entropy and reduced response length collapse. The benefits generalize to ChartQA and MMVP and hold across model scales from 2B to 32B parameters.

## Method Summary

The core framework consists of an Actor (policy πθ) and a frozen Judge (Jϕ, a snapshot of the Actor). For each unlabeled image–question pair (I, q), the Actor samples a group of G = 8 reasoning trajectories τi. The final answer of each trajectory is extracted using a parser that looks for the last \boxed{} expression; missing or multiple answers are marked invalid. Self‑consistency computes the empirical answer distribution p̂(y) over the group; the reward rSCi is the frequency of τi’s answer within the group. The Judge scores each (I, q, τi) on correctness, reasoning quality, and visual grounding, producing a raw score si. A calibration function g(si) maps si to a bounded range [0.8, 1.2] via piecewise adjustments with thresholds th = 0.95, tl = 0.40 and λ+ = λ− = 0.2. The total reward is Ri = rSCi * g(si) − λfmt * δi, where δi = 1 for invalid format and λfmt = 0.5. Group‑relative advantages are then computed: Ai = αRi − log(∑j exp(αRj)), with α = 1. The Actor is updated by minimizing the GRPO loss (clip ε = 0.2, KL coefficient β = 0.01) using the old policy πθold as reference.

Training runs for 20 epochs on unlabeled geometry/math datasets (Geo3K, GeoQA, MMR1) with learning rate 1e‑6, AdamW optimizer, weight decay 1e‑2. The Judge remains frozen throughout. The group size G = 8 stabilizes the self‑consistency distribution. During training, entropy and response length are monitored to ensure stability.

## Key Results

- MathVision accuracy increases by +5.9% (25.0% → 30.9%) when training on Geo3K; average improvement of +3.3% across five mathematical reasoning benchmarks versus the base model.
- The method surpasses prior unsupervised approaches (MM‑UPT, VisionZero) and attains performance on par with supervised RL.
- Training exhibits stability: higher entropy and reduced response length collapse; benefits generalize to ChartQA and MMVP and persist across model scales from 2B to 32B parameters.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

The framework succeeds by exploiting the Actor’s own diversity: multiple sampled trajectories yield an answer distribution whose peak (self‑consistency) correlates strongly with correctness. The frozen Judge, initially identical to the Actor, evaluates each trajectory’s reasoning quality and visual grounding; its scores are calibrated to a bounded range to prevent over‑confident but incorrect answers from dominating the reward. The group‑relative advantage computation in GRPO normalizes rewards within each batch, reducing variance and guarding against reward hacking. This combination provides a dense, intrinsic supervision signal that guides policy updates without any external labels. The frozen Judge ensures a consistent evaluation standard, while the GRPO update maintains training stability, as evidenced by healthier entropy and less response length collapse.

## Foundational Learning

- **Self‑Consistency**: An ensemble method that selects the final answer by majority vote over multiple sampled reasoning paths. *Why needed*: It supplies an unsupervised estimate of answer correctness from the model’s own outputs. *Quick check*: For a batch, compute the majority answer for each input and verify it correlates with true accuracy on a held‑out set.
- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**: A variant of PPO that uses group‑relative advantages (normalized within each sampled group). *Why needed*: It stabilizes policy gradient updates by reducing variance and preventing extreme reward scaling. *Quick check*: Compute advantages as αRi − log(∑j exp(αRj)); check that their mean is near zero and variance is reasonable.
- **Reward Calibration**: A function g(s) that bounds the Judge’s raw score s to a narrow range around 1.0. *Why needed*: It prevents the Judge’s overconfidence from overwhelming the self‑consistency signal and encourages exploration. *Quick check*: Plot histograms of raw si and calibrated g(si); after calibration most values should lie between 0.8 and 1.2.
- **Frozen Judge**: The Judge model is a static copy of the Actor and receives no gradient updates. *Why needed*: Avoids reward hacking (the Actor can’t game a mutable judge) and ensures a consistent evaluation policy throughout training. *Quick check*: Verify that the Judge’s parameters are not included in the optimizer’s parameter list.
- **Unsupervised Reinforcement Learning**: Learning without external rewards or annotations, relying solely on intrinsic signals. *Why needed*: Removes the cost and bottleneck of human labeling, enabling scaling to large unlabeled datasets. *Quick check*: Confirm that the training loop does not access any ground‑truth answer labels.
- **Multimodal Reasoning**: Tasks that require integrating visual and textual information, such as solving math problems with diagrams. *Why needed*: The paper targets this challenging domain where labeled data is especially scarce. *Quick check*: Check that each training input contains both an image and a text question.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Input (I, q) → Actor (πθ) samples G trajectories τ₁…τ_G → For each τ: Answer Extractor yields aᵢ → Self‑Consistency module builds p̂(y) and sets rSCᵢ = p̂(aᵢ) → Judge Jϕ (frozen) scores (I, q, τᵢ) → Calibration g(sᵢ) → Reward Rᵢ = rSCᵢ·g(sᵢ) − λ_fmt·δᵢ → Group‑relative advantage Aᵢ = αRᵢ − log(∑ⱼ exp(αRⱼ)) → GRPO loss updates Actor.

**Critical path**  
The asynchronous pipeline that must execute for every training step: sample trajectories → extract answers → compute self‑consistency → run Judge → calibrate scores → add format penalty → compute group advantages → calculate GRPO loss → update Actor parameters. If any step fails (e.g., answer extraction breaks), the entire update is compromised.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Frozen vs. trainable Judge*: Frozen ensures stability but may limit supervision as the Actor improves; a slowly updated judge could adapt but risks reward hacking.  
- *Group size G*: Larger G yields a more reliable self‑consistency distribution but increases computational cost; G=8 was chosen as a sweet spot.  
- *Calibration thresholds*: th and tl determine how aggressively the Judge’s score is clipped; too narrow and the signal is dampened, too wide and over‑confident scores dominate.  
- *λ_fmt*: Penalizes invalid answer formats; too high may discourage creative reasoning, too low may ignore formatting discipline.  
- *GRPO hyperparameters*: Clip ε and KL coefficient β balance safety (preventing large policy jumps) and learning speed.

**Failure signatures**  
- Judge scores collapse to extremes (g(s) ≈ 1.2 or 0.8) after calibration, indicating uninformative modulation.  
- Advantage values exhibit very high variance, leading to loss spikes or KL divergence.  
- Training loss becomes NaN or grows uncontrollably.  
- Validation accuracy does not improve relative to the base model.  
- Average response length drifts dramatically (either exploding or collapsing), suggesting unstable policy.

**First experiments**  
1. **Judge Score Distribution Check** – Run the frozen Judge on a fixed set of trajectories; plot calibrated scores. Expect a roughly Gaussian distribution centered at 1.0 with standard deviation <0.2.  
2. **Ablation of Judge Modulation** – Train a variant with g(s)=1 (i.e., only self‑consistency reward) and compare final validation accuracy to the full method to quantify the judge’s contribution.  
3. **Training Dynamics Monitoring** – Log per‑epoch entropy of πθ and average response length; ensure entropy stays above 0.5 (nats) and length varies by less than 10% after the first few epochs.

## Limitations

- The exact Judge implementation (prompt format, whether only the  full trajectory is scored) is unspecified, potentially compromising reward fidelity.
- The answer extractor’s regex for parsing \boxed{} is ambiguous; variations may lead to inconsistent self‑consistency counts and format penalties.
- Training hyperparameters (learning rate schedule, batch size, optimizer steps per batch) are not fully disclosed, hindering precise replication.
- The method relies on a frozen Judge that is a copy of the initial Actor; if the base model is weak, the Judge may provide noisy or biased scores, capping the attainable performance.
