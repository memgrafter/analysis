# Benchmarks Are Not That Out of Distribution: Word Overlap Predicts Performance

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10657  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10657  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Lower word unigram cross-entropy between pre-training corpora and evaluation benchmarks robustly predicts higher zero-shot performance, indicating benchmarks are only weakly out-of-distribution.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether standard NLP benchmarks are out-of-distribution relative to pre-training data by measuring tokenizer-agnostic word-level overlap. Using unigram cross-entropy and word frequency statistics as metrics, the authors run controlled experiments across multiple pre-training datasets (8.5B–60B tokens), model sizes (400M–3B parameters), and ten zero-shot benchmarks. They find a consistent inverse correlation between word-level cross-entropy and benchmark scores: lower cross-entropy (closer word distribution match) reliably predicts better performance. Even with cross-entropy held constant, larger pre-training subsets improve scores, suggesting word frequency statistics provide an additional signal. The results imply that many benchmarks share substantial lexical overlap with common pre-training corpora, challenging the assumption that they are strongly out-of-distribution.

## Method Summary
The authors compute two simple, tokenizer-agnostic metrics from pre-training corpora and benchmark text: (1) word unigram cross-entropy, measuring the surprise of seeing benchmark words under the corpus word distribution, and (2) word frequency statistics (e.g., top-k word coverage). They then correlate these metrics with zero-shot benchmark scores across four diverse pre-training datasets and three model sizes (400M, 1B, 3B). Controlled subsets of pre-training data are used to isolate the effect of data quantity while approximately holding cross-entropy constant. This design allows them to test whether lexical distributional alignment alone predicts performance, or if other data properties (e.g., volume, diversity) matter beyond overlap.

## Key Results
- Word unigram cross-entropy is a robust inverse predictor of zero-shot benchmark performance across all tested datasets and model scales.
- With cross-entropy roughly matched, larger pre-training data subsets yield higher scores, indicating word frequency statistics (e.g., coverage of common words) explain residual performance variation.
- Standard benchmarks exhibit only weak distributional shift from typical pre-training corpora when measured at the word level.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Lower word unigram cross-entropy means the benchmark’s word distribution is less surprising under the pre-training corpus distribution. This lexical alignment reduces the model’s effective in-context learning burden: common words and patterns in the benchmark have been seen frequently during pre-training, so the model assigns them higher probability and requires less adaptation. The mechanism is essentially a reduction in distributional mismatch at the word level, which simplifies the mapping from pre-training objectives to benchmark tasks. Word frequency statistics further help because even with similar cross-entropy, datasets with higher coverage of high-frequency words provide more reinforcement on core vocabulary, aiding generalization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Unigram cross-entropy:** Measures distributional similarity between two text corpora at the word type level; lower values indicate closer alignment. *Why needed:* Provides a tokenizer-agnostic, interpretable metric for OOD analysis. *Quick check:* Would shuffling sentences preserve this metric? Yes, because it depends only on word frequencies.
- **Tokenizer-agnostic metrics:** Metrics computed on raw words, bypassing subword tokenization effects. *Why needed:* Avoids confounds from tokenizer choices that vary across models. *Quick check:* How would BPE entropy differ? It would capture subword distributional shifts not visible at word level.
- **Zero-shot evaluation:** Testing models without task-specific fine-tuning. *Why needed:* Isolates knowledge and capabilities acquired during pre-training. *Quick check:* Would fine-tuning likely weaken the observed correlation? Probably, as it adapts to benchmark specifics.
- **Distributional shift:** The difference between training and test data distributions. *Why needed:* Central concept in understanding model generalization and benchmark validity. *Quick check:* Is word-level shift sufficient to capture all relevant shifts? Likely not, as syntax and semantics may diverge.
- **Word frequency statistics:** Summary measures of how common words are distributed (e.g., top-k coverage). *Why needed:* Provide complementary signal to cross-entropy about data quality and coverage. *Quick check:* Do rare words matter more for cross-entropy? Yes, they contribute heavily when underestimated.
- **Pre-training data quality:** Properties of the corpus that affect model performance beyond mere volume. *Why needed:* Guides curation of better datasets. *Quick check:* Is lexical overlap a proxy for factual accuracy or diversity? Possibly, but not directly measured.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Pre-training Corpora (multiple sources) -> Word Frequency Estimator -> Unigram Cross-Entropy Calculator -> Benchmark Text Analyzer -> Correlation Analyzer -> Model Performance Metrics

**Critical Path:**  
1. Extract word unigram distributions from each pre-training corpus.  
2. Compute cross-entropy of each benchmark’s word distribution under each corpus distribution.  
3. Collect zero-shot benchmark scores for models trained on each corpus.  
4. Correlate cross-entropy values with scores across conditions.  
5. Control for cross-entropy by selecting corpus subsets with similar values, then compare performance with different data volumes.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- **Simplicity vs. coverage:** Word-level metrics are interpretable and tokenizer-agnostic but miss subword, syntactic, and semantic shifts that could affect performance.  
- **Controlled vs. realistic:** Using matched subsets isolates variables but reduces ecological validity; real-world pre-training mixes diverse sources.  
- **Correlation vs. causation:** The design shows predictive power but cannot prove that reducing cross-entropy causes improvement; other data qualities may co-vary.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Correlation breaks for non-English languages or morphologically rich languages where word forms vary more.  
- No correlation when using subword-based metrics, indicating word-level analysis is insufficient.  
- High variance in correlation for very small (<400M) or very large (>10B) models not tested.

**First Three Experiments:**  
1. Replicate analysis on multilingual benchmarks (e.g., XGLUE) to test language generality.  
2. Replace word unigrams with BPE token unigrams to assess impact of subword statistics.  
3. Train a model on a corpus where sentences are shuffled (preserving word frequencies) and test if performance drops despite matched cross-entropy.

## Limitations
- Word-level metrics may overlook critical subword, syntactic, or semantic distributional differences that influence model behavior.
- Findings are constrained to English, zero-shot inference, and model scales between 400M and 3B parameters; extrapolation to other settings is uncertain.
- The observed correlation, while robust, does not establish that word overlap is the causal driver; it may proxy for other unmeasured data quality dimensions.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** Lower word unigram cross-entropy robustly predicts higher benchmark scores. **Confidence: High** (consistent inverse correlation across multiple controlled experiments).
- **Claim:** Standard benchmarks are only weakly out-of-distribution relative to pre-training corpora. **Confidence: Medium** (inferred from word-overlap metrics; depends on whether word-level stats adequately capture "distribution").
- **Claim:** Word frequency statistics explain performance gains beyond cross-entropy. **Confidence: Medium** (empirically shown in constant-cross-entropy experiments, but underlying mechanism not fully isolated).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the analysis using subword-based metrics (e.g., BPE entropy) and across multilingual benchmarks to test metric and language generality.
2. Conduct an ablation where pre-training data is artificially manipulated to decouple word overlap from other quality signals (e.g., by shuffling sentences while preserving unigram frequencies).
3. Extend experiments to larger models (≥10B parameters) and fine-tuned evaluation to assess scalability of the predictions.
