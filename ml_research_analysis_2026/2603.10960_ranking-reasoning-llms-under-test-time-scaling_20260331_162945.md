# Ranking Reasoning LLMs under Test-Time Scaling

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.10960
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10960
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** A comprehensive study of 72 statistical ranking methods for evaluating LLMs under test-time scaling, finding most converge to a Bayesian accuracy gold standard with sufficient trials, while greedy-based priors reduce variance in low-budget regimes but risk bias.

## Executive Summary
This paper systematically investigates how to rank Large Language Models (LLMs) when multiple outputs are sampled per prompt (test-time scaling). It introduces Scorio, a library implementing 72 diverse statistical ranking methods that consume a binary response tensor. Evaluated on 20 models across four Olympiad benchmarks with up to 80 trials per question, the study finds that with sufficient samples, nearly all methods agree with a Bayesian accuracy gold standard. However, in the low-budget (single-sample) regime, method stability varies significantly. A key technique—using a single greedy decode as an empirical prior in Bayesian estimation—substantially reduces variance but can introduce bias if greedy and stochastic sampling orderings diverge, particularly on harder problems. The work provides practical guidance for selecting ranking methods based on test-time budget and warns that auxiliary signals (e.g., verifiers) may improve stability at the cost of deviating from correctness-based orderings.

## Method Summary
The core task is to produce a model ranking from a three-dimensional binary response tensor `R` of shape (L models, M questions, N trials), where each entry indicates if a sampled answer was correct. The authors implement 72 ranking methods within the open-source **Scorio** library. These methods aggregate `R` in fundamentally different ways: **pointwise** (e.g., average accuracy `avg`, Pass@k), **Bayesian** (e.g., `BayesU@N` using a uniform Dirichlet prior, `BayesR0@N` incorporating a greedy prior), **paired-comparison** (e.g., Bradley-Terry, Elo, which model win probabilities between model pairs), **voting rules** (e.g., Borda, Copeland, which treat each question as a vote), **IRT** (e.g., Rasch, which model latent ability and question difficulty), and **graph/spectral** methods (e.g., PageRank, HodgeRank, α-Rank, which construct graphs from win relationships). The evaluation measures each method's ranking against a gold standard (Bayesian posterior mean with uniform prior on all 80 trials, `BayesU@80`) and its own stability across different single-trial samples.

## Key Results
*   With the full trial budget (N=80), most ranking methods achieve near-perfect agreement (mean Kendall's τ_b = 0.93–0.95) with the `BayesU@80` gold standard, and 19–34 methods recover exactly the same model ordering.
*   In the low-budget regime (N=1), the top methods still reach τ_b ≈ 0.86 against the gold standard. Using a greedy decode as an empirical prior (`BayesR0@N`) reduces variance by 16–52% at N=1 but can bias the ranking when greedy and stochastic sample orderings disagree, a risk that increases on harder benchmarks.
*   High self-consistency (a method's stability across different single-trial samples) does not guarantee agreement with the correctness-based gold standard. The greedy prior acts as a shrinkage estimator toward the greedy ordering and should only be used after verifying strong alignment between greedy and stochastic performances.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Bayesian averaging (`BayesU@N`) yields a robust, interpretable gold-standard ranking that closely matches most statistical ranking methods when many trials are available.
*   **Mechanism:** It treats each model's per-question correctness as a Binomial draw from a Bernoulli process with fixed probability `p`. A uniform Dirichlet prior (Beta(1,1)) is applied, and the posterior mean `(successes+1)/(trials+2)` is averaged across questions to produce a model score. The ranking is then ordered by this score.
*   **Core assumption:** Trials are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Bernoulli samples for a given model-question pair.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   Abstract: "most full-trial rankings agree closely with the Bayesian gold standard BayesU@80 (mean Kendall's τ_b = 0.93–0.95)".
    *   Section 2.1: Explains that `BayesU@N` with a uniform prior is order-equivalent to the mean accuracy `avg@N`, chosen for its minimal assumptions and interpretability.
    *   Corpus: The development of Scorio.jl and adoption of Bayesian approaches in other test-time scaling literature support its use as a stable target.
*   **Break condition:** The equivalence to `avg@N` holds only for binary outcomes under a uniform prior. For multi-category outcomes or non-uniform priors, the ordering may differ. Strong non-i.i.d. structure at the question level (e.g., clusters of correlated difficulty) could also make simple averaging suboptimal.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Using greedy decoding as an empirical prior in Bayesian ranking (`BayesR0@N`) reduces variance in low-budget regimes but may introduce bias when greedy and stochastic orderings diverge.
*   **Mechanism:** The prior adds pseudo-counts from one greedy decode (`R0`) per question. For the binary case, this effectively adds one "success" if greedy was correct and one "failure" if not, shrinking the posterior mean toward the greedy outcome. It combines prior information (the single greedy sample) with observed stochastic trials.
*   **Core assumption:** Greedy performance provides a signal correlated with the expected ordering under stochastic sampling. The prior strength (one pseudo-count per question) is fixed regardless of N.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   Abstract: "Using greedy decoding as an empirical prior (BayesR0@N) reduces variance at N=1 by 16–52%, but can bias rankings when greedy and stochastic sampling disagree."
    *   Section 3.4: Quantifies variance reduction and defines greedy–sampling alignment τ_G-S (Kendall's τ between greedy and full-sample stochastic rankings). Higher τ_G-S correlates with greater benefit from the prior.
    *   Corpus: Greedy decoding is a common baseline in test-time scaling studies, making its use as a prior a natural consideration.
*   **Break condition:** When greedy performance poorly estimates the stochastic average (e.g., greedy under-explores on harder problems), the shrinkage distorts the true ranking. The paper recommends checking τ_G-S on a pilot sample before applying the prior.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Different ranking methods converge to distinct asymptotic orderings as the number of trials grows, reflecting their inherent modeling biases; no single method is universally superior.
*   **Mechanism:** As M, N → ∞ under i.i.d. sampling, pointwise methods (like `avg`) converge to marginal model success probabilities `p_ℓ`. Paired-comparison methods (e.g., Bradley–Terry) converge to latent strengths `π` that best explain the observed win-rate matrix. These are different functionals of the underlying data distribution and need not be monotone transformations of each other. The paper provides a constructed counterexample where `avg` and Bradley-Terry produce opposite orderings.
*   **Core assumption:** The data are generated from an underlying distribution `P` over `{0,1}^L` for each question. Each ranking method estimates a different summary statistic of `P`.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   Abstract: "High self-consistency does not imply gold-standard agreement" indicates a method can be stable yet differ from accuracy-based evaluation.
    *   Appendix C.2: Provides the formal counterexample and asymptotic analysis proving non-equivalence of `avg` and Bradley-Terry.
    *   Corpus: The deliberate implementation of 72 diverse methods in Scorio acknowledges that the "best" ranking depends on the evaluation target (accuracy vs. pairwise dominance).
*   **Break condition:** If the underlying data generation process truly follows the assumptions of a specific ranking model (e.g., a Bradley-Terry generative process for win outcomes), then that method's ordering will be more accurate for that target. Absent such guarantees, method choice must align with the desired evaluation goal.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Test-time scaling**
    *   **Why needed:** The paper studies evaluation when multiple outputs are generated per prompt (N>1), which is the essence of test-time scaling. This explains the three-dimensional response tensor structure and the need for ranking methods that aggregate over trials.
    *   **Quick check:** In test-time scaling, what does increasing N vertically change in the tensor R? (Answer: It adds more independent samples per model-question pair, reducing the variance of performance estimates.)
*   **Concept: Response tensor transformations**
    *   **Why needed:** All ranking methods consume the same tensor `R` but transform it differently—into pointwise means, pairwise win matrices, or setwise events. Recognizing these representations clarifies connections between methods and explains divergent rankings.
    *   **Quick check:** How would you compute the win matrix `W` from `R`? (Answer: For each model pair (i,j) and each question m, count trial instances where R[i,m,n]=1 and R[j,m,n]=0, then sum over all m and n.)
*   **Concept: Bayesian estimation with Dirichlet-multinomial**
    *   **Why needed:** The Bayesian estimators `BayesU@N` and `BayesR0@N` are central to the analysis of low-budget stability and variance reduction. The Dirichlet prior adds pseudo-counts; the posterior mean shrinks raw proportions.
    *   **Quick check:** With N=3 trials, 2 successes for a model on a question, and a uniform Dirichlet prior (α₀=α₁=1), what is the posterior mean? (Answer: (2+1)/(3+2)=3/5=0.6.)
*   **Concept: Kendall's τ_b**
    *   **Why needed:** It is the primary metric for comparing rankings, accounting for ties. Understanding its adjustment for ties is crucial for interpreting agreement scores, especially on hard benchmarks with many ties.
    *   **Quick check:** How does τ_b differ from τ_a in the presence of ties? (Answer: τ_b adjusts the denominator by the number of tied pairs in both rankings, while τ_a does not, making τ_b more conservative when ties are frequent.)
*   **Concept: Self-consistency**
    *   **Why needed:** It measures a method's stability under low-budget sampling (agreement between its N=1 and N=80 rankings). High self-consistency does not guarantee correctness, a key paper insight.
    *   **Quick check:** If a method has high self-consistency but low agreement with `BayesU@80`, what does this imply? (Answer: The method is stable but targets a different objective than accuracy-based ranking, e.g., pairwise dominance or reliance on auxiliary signals.)

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Input R (and optional R0) -> Transformation Layer (reduces to pointwise, pairwise, or setwise summaries) -> Ranking Engine (family-specific algorithms: metrics, Bayesian, paired-comparison, IRT, graph/spectral) -> Output Rankings & Scores.
*   **Critical path:** 1. Load binary response tensor `R` (L×M×N). 2. Select ranking method, which internally performs its specific transformation (e.g., `avg` computes mean over N; `bradley_terry` computes win matrix and optimizes likelihood). 3. Return integer ranking array (1=best) and raw score array.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Statistical vs. computational:** Exact methods (e.g., Kemeny-Young) are NP-hard; library uses MILP for small L but may fallback to heuristics. Bayesian MCMC is costly.
    *   **Interpretability:** Simple metrics (`avg`) are transparent; graph methods (PageRank) are less so.
    *   **Stability vs. bias:** Methods like `BayesR0@N` reduce variance but may bias if greedy prior is misaligned.
    *   **Tie handling:** Policies (ignore, half-count, full) affect ordering, especially on hard benchmarks with many ties.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   High discrepancy between a method's full-trial ranking and `BayesU@80` (τ_b well below 1) signals the method's target differs from accuracy.
    *   High variance in τ_b across single-trial subsamples indicates low-budget instability.
    *   Using an empirical prior when τ_G-S (greedy–sampling alignment) is low tends to degrade gold-standard agreement.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Gold standard agreement:** Compute each method's ranking using all N=80 trials and measure Kendall's τ_b against `BayesU@80`. (Section 3.1, Table 1).
    2.  **Low-budget stability:** For N=1, subsample 80 single-trial datasets, compute rankings, and measure (a) agreement with `BayesU@80` and (b) self-consistency (agreement with the method's own N=80 ranking). (Section 3.2, Tables 2, 18, 19).
    3.  **Empirical prior analysis:** Compare `BayesU@N` vs. `BayesR0@N` across N=1–80, compute mean Δτ and standard deviation reduction, and correlate with greedy–sampling alignment τ_G-S. (Section 3.4, Figures 2, 6, 7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question:** How to adapt ranking methods for partial-credit and rubric-based evaluations under test-time scaling?
    *   **Basis:** Explicitly listed as a natural next step in the Conclusion. Only binary outcomes were studied; finer outcome categories could affect ranking stability and convergence.
    *   **What evidence would resolve:** Deploy existing multi-category Bayesian and IRT estimators on partial-credit benchmarks, measuring low-budget stability and agreement with human consensus or aggregated scores.
*   **Question:** How can informative priors (e.g., from greedy decoding or verifiers) be safely incorporated into Bayesian ranking to avoid systematic bias, and how to determine when they are beneficial?
    *   **Basis:** Section 3.4 shows the greedy prior (`BayesR0@N`) reduces variance at N=1 but biases rankings when greedy and stochastic disagree; Limitations warn that auxiliary priors can introduce bias if misaligned.
    *   **What evidence would resolve:** Systematically vary prior strength and sources across multiple benchmarks, measuring the trade-off between variance reduction and bias (e.g., via alignment metrics like τ_G-S), to derive safe-use guidelines.
*   **Question:** Why do auxiliary-signal categorical ranking schemes improve self-consistency yet deviate from correctness-based orderings, especially on harder benchmarks, and can we design schemes that achieve both stability and correctness fidelity?
    *   **Basis:** Section 3.5 reports that Verifier-only and similar schemes have high self-consistency but lower gold-standard agreement, with further degradation on HMMT'25 and BrUMO'25; a negative correlation between self-consistency and gold agreement is observed.
    *   **What evidence would resolve:** Ablation experiments isolating each auxiliary signal and weight combination, combined with modeling of signal–correctness relationships, to discover weighting strategies that jointly optimize stability and correctness alignment.

## Limitations
*   **Exact evaluation details unspecified:** The precise prompts, model-serving configurations (e.g., vLLM parameters), and answer extraction logic for generating the response tensor `R` are not provided, blocking exact reproduction.
*   **Method hyperparameters ambiguous:** Key parameters for complex methods (Elo K-factor, IRT prior variances, α-Rank settings) are not explicitly listed; reliance on library defaults may cause deviation from reported results.
*   **Tie-handling policy undefined:** The convention for handling ties in rankings and Kendall's τ_b (e.g., τ_b vs τ_a) is not specified, which can significantly affect reported agreements on benchmarks with many tied model performances.

## Confidence
*   **Mechanism 1 (BayesU@80 gold standard):** Medium confidence. Strong empirical support and theoretical basis (equivalence to `avg@N`), but depends on i.i.d. trial assumption and uniform prior choice.
*   **Mechanism 2 (Greedy prior variance reduction):** Medium-High confidence. Concrete variance reductions and alignment metric provide clear evidence; bias risk on hard benchmarks is well-demonstrated.
*   **Mechanism 3 (Divergent asymptotic orderings):** Medium confidence. Theoretical counterexample is solid, but real-world data may not perfectly match the constructed separation; practical impact depends on benchmark difficulty and model distribution.
