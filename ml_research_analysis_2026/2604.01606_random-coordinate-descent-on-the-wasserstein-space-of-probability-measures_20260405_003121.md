# Random Coordinate Descent on the Wasserstein Space of Probability Measures

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.01606
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01606
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Introduces Random Wasserstein Coordinate Descent (RWCD/RWCP) whose iteration complexity scales with the sum of coordinate-wise smoothness constants $L_{\text{sum}}$ instead of the global $d\cdot L$, yielding significant speedups on anisotropic problems.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the high computational cost of optimizing functionals over probability measures with Wasserstein-2 geometry, particularly in high-dimensional or ill-conditioned settings. It proposes Random Wasserstein Coordinate Descent (RWCD) for smooth objectives and Random Wasserstein Coordinate Proximal-Gradient (RWCP) for composite problems. These methods perform per-iteration updates restricted to a single randomly selected coordinate, realized via pushforward maps that modify only the chosen marginal while preserving others. Theoretical guarantees show convergence rates mirroring Euclidean coordinate descent: iteration complexity scales with $L_{\text{sum}}$ rather than $d\cdot L$, leading to $O(L_{\text{sum}}/\epsilon)$ for non-convex and geodesically convex settings, and $O(L_{\text{sum}}/m\ln(1/\epsilon))$ under Polyak-Łojasiewicz or strong convexity. Numerical experiments on ill-conditioned quadratic, MMD, and neural network functionals demonstrate substantial reductions in computational work measured in coordinate-gradient evaluations compared to full-gradient Wasserstein methods.

## Method Summary
The RWCD method for smooth functionals samples a coordinate $i$ with probability $p_i = L_i / L_{\text{sum}}$, where $L_i$ are coordinate-wise smoothness constants. For each particle $x$, the update is $x_i \gets x_i - \gamma_i (\partial_i E[\mu](x))$ with $\gamma_i = 1/L_i$. This corresponds to a pushforward map that alters only the $i$-th marginal. RWCP for composite functionals $E=F+\psi$ samples $i$ with $p_i \propto \eta_i = L_i + \sqrt{H_i^2 + L_i^2}$, where $H_i$ pertains to the regularizer’s curvature. For each particle, it solves a one-dimensional proximal subproblem along direction $e_i$ to obtain a step $s$, then updates $x_i \gets x_i + s$. Baselines are full Wasserstein gradient descent (WGD) and full Wasserstein proximal gradient (WPG), which update all coordinates per iteration. Computational work is compared in terms of coordinate-gradient evaluations: one RWCD/RWCP iteration costs $N$ evaluations, while one WGD/WPG costs $N\cdot d$ for dimension $d$ and $N$ particles.

## Key Results
- Iteration complexity scales with $L_{\text{sum}}$ rather than $d\cdot L$, enabling speedups on anisotropic landscapes.
- Convergence rates: $O(L_{\text{sum}}/\epsilon)$ for non-convex and geodesically convex cases; $O(L_{\text{sum}}/m\ln(1/\epsilon))$ under Polyak-Łojasiewicz or strong convexity.
- Empirical results on quadratic, MMD, and neural network functionals show significant reductions in computational work compared to full-gradient methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The proposed algorithms succeed due to three key mechanisms:

1. **Coordinate-wise smoothness exploitation**: The iteration complexity scales with $L_{\text{sum}} = \sum_{i=1}^d L_i$ instead of the global constant $dL$. The algorithm sets sampling probabilities $p_i \propto L_i$ and step sizes $\gamma_i = 1/L_i$, which adapts to anisotropic curvature. This makes the coordinate updates unbiased for the full gradient in expectation while allowing larger steps on stiff coordinates. (Core assumption: $E$ is $L_i$-smooth along each coordinate; see Definition 6 and Section 3.)

2. **Marginal-preserving pushforward updates**: The update $\mu_{k+1} = T\#\mu_k$ with $T(x) = x - \gamma_i U_i \nabla WE[\mu_k](x)$ modifies only the $i$-th marginal because the transport map applies pointwise to the $i$-th component, leaving other coordinates unchanged. This is the exact Wasserstein analogue of Euclidean coordinate descent. (Proposition 7 and Algorithm 1.)

3. **Descent lemma and convergence analysis**: Using coordinate-wise smoothness, the expected one-step decrease satisfies $\mathbb{E}[E[\mu_{k+1}]|\mu_k] \le E[\mu_k] - \frac{1}{2L_{\text{sum}}} \|\nabla WE[\mu_k]\|_{L^2(\mu_k)}^2$. This yields rates $O(L_{\text{sum}}/\epsilon)$ for non-convex/geodesically convex cases and $O(L_{\text{sum}}/m \ln(1/\epsilon))$ under Polyak-Łojasiewicz or strong convexity. The proof adapts Euclidean RCD techniques to the Wasserstein metric, relying on the non-linear Wasserstein inner product and displacement interpolants. (Theorem 12 and Section A.1.)

Break conditions: If any coordinate lacks $L_i$-smoothness or the landscape is isotropic ($L_{\text{sum}} \approx dL$), the advantage vanishes. Incorrect $p_i,\gamma_i$ or inaccurate subproblem solutions can cause divergence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Wasserstein-2 geometry and gradient**: Why needed: The entire algorithm operates on the metric space $(\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d), W_2)$; the gradient $\nabla WE$ is defined via first variation along displacement interpolants. Quick check: What is the relationship between $\nabla WE[\mu](x)$ and the Euclidean gradient of the first variation? (Answer: $\nabla WE[\mu](x) = \nabla \frac{\delta E}{\delta\mu}(x)$.)
- **Pushforward measure**: Why needed: Updates are expressed as pushforwards $\mu_{k+1}=T\#\mu_k$. Quick check: If $X\sim\mu$ and $T:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}^d$ is measurable, what is the law of $T(X)$? (Answer: It is the pushforward measure.)
- **Coordinate-wise smoothness vs global smoothness**: Why needed: Improved bounds rely on $L_{\text{sum}}$ rather than $dL$. Quick check: For an anisotropic quadratic $f(x) = \frac12 x^\top P x$, compute $L_i = P_{ii}$ and compare $L_{\text{sum}}$ to $d\cdot \max_i P_{ii}$.
- **Geodesic convexity & Polyak-Łojasiewicz**: Why needed: Different convergence rates depend on the geometry of the objective. Quick check: Write the geodesic convexity inequality (14) and explain how it differs from Euclidean convexity.
- **Random coordinate descent (Euclidean)**: Why needed: The Wasserstein version directly mirrors the Euclidean method. Quick check: What are the optimal $p_i$ and $\gamma_i$ for an $L_i$-smooth coordinate-wise objective? (Answer: $p_i = L_i / L_{\text{sum}}$, $\gamma_i = 1/L_i$.)

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component map
Empirical measure representation → Coordinate sampler (draw $i_k \sim p_i$) → Gradient evaluator (compute $\nabla WE[\mu_k]$ at each particle) → Update operator (apply RWCD or RWCP update) → Loop.

#### Critical path
Accurate gradient computation → correct coordinate sampling ($p_i = L_i/L_{\text{sum}}$) → proper marginal-preserving update (using $U_i$ projection). Any error breaks theoretical guarantees.

#### Design tradeoffs
- **Sampling vs accuracy**: Importance sampling ($p_i \propto L_i$) reduces iteration count but requires precomputed $L_i$; uniform sampling loses adaptivity.
- **Particle number $N$**: Larger $N$ reduces discretization error but increases per-iteration cost.
- **Step size tuning**: Using $\gamma_i = 1/L_i$ is optimal under smoothness; in practice, one might need to adapt if $L_i$ are estimated.

#### Failure signatures
- Non-monotone energy increase (violates descent lemma).
- Energy plateau after many iterations (possible $L_i$ misestimation).
- Particle collapse or explosion (step size too large or gradient miscalculation).
- Loss of marginal preservation in diagnostics (track marginal histograms for untouched coordinates).

#### First 3 experiments
1. **2D anisotropic quadratic**: Verify staircase behavior, fast collapse of stiff coordinate. Check that $L_{\text{sum}}$ predicts iteration speed.
2. **50D anisotropic quadratic**: Quantify speedup vs WGD at same coordinate-gradient budget; ensure parallel scaling with dimension.
3. **MMD-type functional**: Test in non-convex setting; monitor gradient norm decay; confirm narrow band of RWCD vs WGD.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can block coordinate descent (BCD) be formulated and analyzed in the Wasserstein space, and what convergence rates can be achieved for block-based pushforward updates?
2. Can the coordinate descent framework be adapted to kernel-based or grid-based discretizations, and how do their computational costs and convergence properties compare to particle methods?
3. What are the convergence guarantees for RWCP when the variational subproblems are solved only approximately (inexactly), and what level of subproblem accuracy suffices to maintain the stated rates?
4. Can the sampling probabilities and step sizes be adapted online without prior knowledge of $L_i$, while preserving convergence rates?

## Limitations
- **Baseline specification gaps**: WPG baseline lacks explicit $\eta$ and subproblem solver details, making fair comparisons difficult.
- **Parameter uncertainty for complex functionals**: Exact $L_i$ values for neural networks are not provided; estimation via Monte Carlo introduces variance.
- **Sensitivity to problem anisotropy**: Acceleration relies on $L_{\text{sum}} \ll d\cdot L$; isotropic problems yield minimal improvement, and theoretical assumptions may not always hold in practice.

## Confidence
- **Complexity scaling with $L_{\text{sum}}$**: High; proofs rigorously adapt Euclidean analysis to Wasserstein geometry.
- **Computational work reduction**: Medium; experiments support it but reproduction uncertainties and implementation subtleties temper confidence.
- **Marginal-preserving updates**: High; the pushforward construction analytically guarantees it.
- **Convergence rates**: Medium–High; derived under standard assumptions but may be sensitive to step size tuning in non-convex settings.
- **Effectiveness on neural networks**: Low–Medium; exact $L_i$ verification is challenging, and discretization errors may affect results.

## Next Checks
- **Descent validation**: Run RWCD on a 2D anisotropic quadratic; verify monotonic energy decrease and compare empirical convergence rate to theoretical $1/L_{\text{sum}}$ scaling.
- **L_i robustness**: Deliberately misestimate $L_i$ (e.g., multiply by 0.5, 2.0) and measure impact on iteration count and stability; identify safe margin for practical tuning.
- **Neural network replication**: Approximate $L_i$ via finite differences on a small particle set; run RWCD and compare energy trajectory to the paper’s Figure 5; if mismatch, investigate sensitivity to particle count and step size.
