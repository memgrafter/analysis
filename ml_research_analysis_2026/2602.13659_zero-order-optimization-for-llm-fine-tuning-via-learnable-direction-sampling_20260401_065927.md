# Zero-Order Optimization for LLM Fine-Tuning via Learnable Direction Sampling

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13659
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13659
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Proposes ZO-LDSD, a learnable direction sampling module for zero‑order optimization, which improves memory‑efficient LLM fine‑tuning, achieves dimension‑independent convergence, and raises test accuracy on SST-2 (e.g., OPT‑1.3B: 0.928 → 0.935).

## Executive Summary
Fine‑tuning large language models (LLMs) demands considerable memory for gradients and optimizer states, motivating zero‑order (ZO) methods that estimate gradients via function evaluations. Classical ZO suffers from high variance and a convergence slowdown proportional to parameter dimension d, limiting scalability. This paper introduces ZO‑LDSD, a framework that treats the perturbation distribution as a learnable policy, updating its mean to align with the gradient direction. The resulting algorithm yields theoretical convergence guarantees that are independent of d, and empirically demonstrates consistent accuracy improvements on the SST-2 sentiment classification task when applied to strong ZO baselines (e.g., ZO‑SGD) with RoBERTa‑Large and OPT‑1.3B. With only a modest increase in forward passes (K=5), ZO‑LDSD delivers a 0.7% absolute gain for OPT‑1.3B, establishing a practical and memory‑efficient fine‑tuning approach.

## Method Summary
ZO‑LDSD operates as a plug‑and‑play module around any base ZO optimizer. At each iteration, it samples K perturbation directions \(\{v_i\}_{i=1}^K\) from a Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \varepsilon^2 I)\). It evaluates the loss at \(x + \tau v_i\) for all samples, then selects \(v^* = \arg\min_i f(x + \tau v_i)\) via greedy selection. A two‑point directional derivative is computed using an additional evaluation at \(x - \tau v^*\), yielding \(d = (f(x+\tau v^*) - f(x-\tau v^*))/(2\tau)\). This gives a gradient estimate \(g_x = d \cdot v^*\) for the parameter update \(x \leftarrow x - \gamma_x g_x\). Concurrently, ZO‑LDSD updates the policy mean \(\mu\) with a REINFORCE‑style estimator: for each sample, an advantage \(A_i\) is computed using a leave‑one‑out baseline, and \(\nabla_\mu \mathbb{E}[A_i] \approx \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^K A_i \frac{v_i - \mu}{\varepsilon^2}\). The mean is then moved as \(\mu \leftarrow \mu + \gamma_\mu g_\mu\). This adaptive sampling aligns the distribution with the gradient, reducing estimator variance. The method adds only \(O(d)\) memory for \(\mu\) and requires \(K+1\) forward passes per step. Effective hyperparameters in practice include \(K=5\), \(\varepsilon=1\), \(\gamma_\mu=10^{-3}\), and a small \(\tau\) (e.g., \(10^{-3}\)), while \(\gamma_x\) is inherited from the base ZO optimizer.

## Key Results
- ZO‑LDSD consistently improves SST‑2 test accuracy across multiple ZO baselines; for OPT‑1.3B fine‑tuned with ZO‑SGD, accuracy increases from 0.928 to 0.935.
- Theoretical analysis shows convergence bounds that do not explicitly depend on parameter dimension d, addressing the O(d) slowdown of classical ZO.
- The approach incurs only a modest computational overhead (e.g., \(K=5\) → 6 forward passes per iteration) while delivering significant gains.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Updating the mean of the perturbation distribution to align with the gradient reduces variance of zero‑order gradient estimates.
- **Mechanism:** The mean µ is treated as a policy. After drawing directions v ∼ N(µ, ε²I), the algorithm computes the expected alignment E[Ct|Ft₋₁] and its gradient ∇µ via a REINFORCE‑style estimator (using a baseline). A gradient ascent step on µ (learning rate γµ) steers the sampling toward high‑alignment directions.
- **Core assumption:** The Gaussian policy yields a differentiable alignment objective; Monte‑Carlo with K samples approximates ∇µ unbiasedly enough for practical updates.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “treats the sampling distribution over perturbation directions as a learnable policy and updates it to reduce the variance of directional estimates.”  
  - [section 3.3] Algorithm 1 lines 5–6 implement the policy update.  
  - [section 3.4] Lemma 1 links alignment Ct to variance.  
  - [corpus] ConMeZO (arXiv:2511.02757) and Prior‑Informed ZO (arXiv:2601.04710) also use adaptive direction sampling for ZO.
- **Break condition:** If ε or γµ are too large, or if the gradient direction changes faster than the policy can track (large γₓ), the alignment may degrade. Theoretically ensured by ε ≤ O(d⁻³ᐟ² cos β(1−cos β)‖µ‖) and γµ ≤ O(‖µ‖²) (Theorem 1).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The learned direction sampling eliminates the explicit dimension d from convergence bounds, removing the O(d) slowdown of classical ZO.
- **Mechanism:** Standard ZO has E[Ct|Ft₋₁] ≈ 1/d because random directions are nearly orthogonal to the gradient. The policy learning increases E[Ct|Ft₋₁] to Θ(1) by aligning µ with the gradient. Lemma 1 shows the convergence rate is inversely proportional to this alignment; thus the bound becomes independent of d.
- **Core assumption:** The algorithm maintains a non‑trivial alignment cos βₜ ≥ δ (δ > 0). This holds almost surely for random initialization or can be enforced by setting µ₀ ∥⏐∇f(x₀).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “Theoretically, the analysis yields convergence guarantees without explicit d dependence.”  
  - [section 3.5] Lemma 3 gives an O(1) bound, while Corollary 1 recovers the d‑dependent classic result.  
  - [section 3.2] Discusses the 1/d scaling of E[Ct] for i.i.d. Gaussian directions.  
  - [corpus] CurvZO (arXiv:2603.21725) and ZO‑SAM (arXiv:2603.13115) also aim to improve ZO convergence, indirectly supporting the role of alignment.
- **Break condition:** If the policy enters a degenerate state (βₜ near 0 or π/2), ∇µE[Ct|Ft₋₁] vanishes and alignment stops improving, reverting to the O(d) behavior. Theorem 1 requires βₜ not too extreme for monotonic growth.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** ZO‑LDSD functions as a plug‑and‑play module that can enhance any existing ZO optimizer without changing its core hyperparameters.
- **Mechanism:** The adaptive sampling and policy update are wrapped around a base ZO update. The only change is that the parameter update (Algorithm 2, line 7) uses a direction v* selected from a learned distribution; the update rule itself (e.g., ZO‑SGD, ZO‑AdaMM) can be swapped in. The module adds only O(d) memory for µ and K forward passes.
- **Core assumption:** The base ZO estimator provides a directional derivative that is an unbiased (or low‑bias) estimate of the true gradient projection; the greedy selection of v* is compatible with that estimator.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “The resulting ZO‑LDSD algorithm serves as a plug‑and‑play module compatible with existing ZO optimizers.”  
  - [section 4] “It is important to note that this framework is not a standalone optimizer but a plug‑in module.”  
  - [section 5.2] Table 1 shows consistent gains when the module is added to three baselines (ZO‑SGD, ZO‑AdaMM, JAGUAR).  
  - [corpus] Several neighboring works (ConMeZO, CurvZO) also propose modular improvements, confirming the design pattern.
- **Break condition:** Incompatibility may arise if the base optimizer relies on gradient history (e.g., momentum) and the bias from greedy v* selection violates stability; also, small K increases variance of the policy gradient, destabilizing learning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Zero‑order (gradient‑free) optimization**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s entire premise is to avoid backpropagation; understanding ZO estimators (e.g., Eq. 2) is essential to see how memory savings are achieved and why variance is high.  
  *Quick check:* “How does the variance of the standard ZO gradient estimator scale with parameter dimension d, and why?”

  *Why needed:* The core mechanism for updating µ is a policy gradient step; the score‑function estimator and baseline are used to reduce variance.  
  *Quick check:* “What is the role of the baseline bₜ in the REINFORCE update for µ, and how is it computed here?”
- **Non‑convex convergence analysis for ZO**  
  *Why needed:* The theoretical contribution (dimension‑independent bound) hinges on linking expected gradient alignment to the convergence rate. Understanding Lemma 1 and the breakdown of E[Ct|Ft₋₁] explains the O(d) issue.  
  *Quick check:* “In Lemma 1, what is the quantity Ct, and why does its conditional expectation control the convergence speed?”

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - Input: model parameters x, policy mean µ.
  - DirectionSampler: draws K vectors vᵢ ∼ N(µ, ε²I).
  - FunctionEvaluator: computes f(x + τvᵢ) for all i (and later f(x − τv*) if needed).
  - GreedySelector: picks best direction v* = argminᵢ f(x + τvᵢ).
  - ZOEstimator: computes parameter update gₓ using v*.
  - PolicyGradient: computes g_µ using all K samples with a baseline.
  - Updaters: x ← x − γₓgₓ; µ ← µ + γ_µ g_µ.

- **Critical path (per iteration):** Sample K directions → evaluate f(x + τvᵢ) → select v* → compute f(x − τv*) (extra forward) → form gₓ → update x → form g_µ (using all samples) → update µ.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Memory: only µ (extra O(d)) vs. storing optimizer states/activations in backprop; huge savings.
  - Compute: K + 1 forward passes per iteration; larger K improves estimate quality but costs more.
  - Hyperparameters: ε must scale with ‖µ‖ as per Theorem 1; γₓ similar to base ZO steps; γ_µ controls policy learning rate.
  - Variance vs. bias: Greedy selection and REINFORCE both introduce bias but reduce variance; tuning K and baseline mitigates.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Stagnant alignment: cos βₜ ≈ 0 (policy not learning). Check: monitor (µ·∇f)/(‖µ‖‖∇f‖) if accessible, or test accuracy plateau.
  - Diverging loss: γₓ too large or τ inappropriate.
  - Exploding µ norm: violates theoretical ε condition; may need clipping.
  - Noisy policy updates: high variance in g_µ when K is small; increase K or improve baseline.

- **First 3 experiments (from paper):**
  1. **Toy linear regression** (a9a) – compares gradient alignment and norm of LDSD vs. baseline ZO, showing faster alignment growth.
  2. **Main LLM fine‑tuning** (SST‑2, RoBERTa‑Large & OPT‑1.3B) – evaluates ZO‑LDSD on top of ZO‑SGD, ZO‑AdaMM, JAGUAR under two forward‑pass budgets (6 forwards/iteration vs. more iterations with 2 forwards), reporting consistent accuracy lifts.
  3. **Ablation study** (SST‑2 RoBERTa‑Large LoRA) – varies K, γ_µ, ε, identifying practical operating ranges (e.g., K=5 often best).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Does constraining the norm of the learnable mean vector µ (e.g., ∥µ∥=1) improve stability, convergence, or hyperparameter sensitivity of ZO‑LDSD while preserving its theoretical guarantees?  
*Basis in paper:* Section 3 suggests the sampling distribution is invariant under simultaneous rescaling of µ and ε², making this a natural design direction.  
*Why unresolved:* No empirical or theoretical exploration of this constraint.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Theoretical analysis under ∥µ∥=1 and experiments comparing constrained vs. unconstrained versions.

### Open Question 2
What is the actual scaling relationship between ε and d required for ZO‑LDSD? Theorem 1 requires ε = O(d^{-3/2}), yet experiments fix ε = 1 even for models up to 1.3B parameters.  
*Basis in paper:* The condition appears loose; Appendix A.2 uses constant ε.  
*Why unresolved:* No systematic study varying ε with d; the tightness of the bound remains unknown.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Experiments with ε scaled as d^{-α} for various α, plus theoretical refinement.

### Open Question 3
How does ZO‑LDSD integrate with other zero‑order optimizers beyond the three tested (ZO‑SGD, ZO‑AdaMM, JAGUAR), particularly those with curvature awareness?  
*Why unresolved:* Compatibility with advanced ZO methods (e.g., HiZOO, ZoAR, LOREN) is not examined.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Experimental validation on a broader set of ZO optimizers, measuring whether gains persist, degrade, or require adaptation.

### Open Question 4
What is the practical impact of the initialization of µ on convergence? Theory distinguishes random µ₀ (weighted‑average bound) from µ₀ aligned with ∇f(x₀) (standard O(1/T) bound), but gradients are unavailable at initialization in ZO settings.  
*Why unresolved:* The paper does not specify µ₀ initialization; the relevance of the two regimes is unclear.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Compare different µ₀ strategies (random, small random, heuristic from early ZO steps) and track alignment evolution.

### Open Question 5
How should hyperparameters γµ, ε, and K be scheduled or adapted during training, and how do they interact with the base optimizer’s step size γx?  
*Why unresolved:* The paper uses fixed values; no adaptive schedule or comprehensive interaction study exists.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic hyperparameter search with dynamic schedules (e.g., decaying γµ, alignment‑based ε) across tasks and model sizes; theoretical robustness analysis.

## Limitations
- Unspecified training details: iteration/epoch count, batch size, stopping criterion, and baseline ZO‑optimizer forward‑pass budgets impede exact replication.
- Missing hyperparameters: perturbation scale τ and initialization µ₀ are not reported, requiring guesswork or external benchmarks.
- LoRA configuration (rank, α, target modules) used in the ablation study is not detailed, limiting reproducibility of the LoRA experiments.
- Theoretical reliance on a maintained alignment (cos βₜ ≥ δ) may not hold in practice; the policy mean can stagnate or diverge.
- High variance in the policy gradient estimator with small K means results may be sensitive to random seeds; robustness is not thoroughly validated.

## Confidence
- **Improved performance (SST‑2):** Medium – gains are consistent across baselines on one dataset but require broader validation.
- **Dimension‑independent convergence:** Medium – proof assumes persistent alignment; empirical evidence for this condition is sparse.
- **Variance reduction via alignment:** Medium – Lemma 1 connects alignment to variance, but direct variance measurements are absent.
- **Plug‑and‑play compatibility:** High – experiments demonstrate improvement when added to three distinct ZO optimizers.

## Next Checks
1. **Alignment verification:** Track cos(μₜ, ∇f(xₜ)) throughout training to confirm the policy reliably increases alignment.
2. **Scaling test:** Train models of varying parameter dimensions (different LLM sizes or synthetic tasks) to empirically verify the removal of the O(d) slowdown.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Perform a systematic sweep over K, γ_μ, ε, and τ to validate the robustness of reported gains and identify optimal ranges.
