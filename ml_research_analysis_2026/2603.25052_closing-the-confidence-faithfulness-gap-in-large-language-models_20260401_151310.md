# Closing the Confidence-Faithfulness Gap in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.25052
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25052
- Reference count: 35
- Primary result: A two-stage adaptive pipeline reduces Expected Calibration Error (ECE) by 4–7× relative to unsteered verbalized confidence.

## Executive Summary
The paper reveals that large language models encode internal accuracy estimates and verbalized confidence in nearly orthogonal linear directions within the residual stream, explaining why expressed confidence often diverges from actual performance. This representational dissociation persists across multiple 7–8B models. Moreover, prompting the model to reason and rate confidence jointly introduces a "Reasoning Contamination Effect" that inverts the geometric relationship between these signals. To close the gap, the authors propose a two-stage adaptive pipeline: first, a linear probe reads the model’s internal accuracy estimate; second, contrastive activation addition steers the verbalized confidence to match that estimate. This intervention yields substantial improvements in calibration, drastically lowering ECE and outperforming prior baselines.

## Method Summary
The method leverages the orthogonal encoding insight. **Stage 1 (Probe):** Train ridge regression probes on activations from prompts that elicit pure correctness (e.g., chain-of-thought without confidence) to predict empirical accuracy (fraction correct over 50 samples). The trained probe captures the internal accuracy direction. **Stage 2 (Steering):** Build a contrastive activation addition (CAA) vector from pure confidence prompts by contrasting high-confidence and low-confidence responses. For each test question, use the probe to obtain a target confidence value. Using a held-out validation set, learn an inverse mapping from target confidence to steering strength α via monotonic interpolation. At test time, compute α* for the question and generate the confidence under a pure confidence prompt while injecting the scaled CAA vector (α*·v). The steered verbalized confidence aligns with the internal accuracy signal and is much better calibrated.

## Key Results
- Accuracy and confidence directions are nearly orthogonal (cosine similarity < 0.04) across all layers and three model families.
- Joint solve-and-rate prompting causes a "Reasoning Contamination Effect", flipping the cosine similarity from +0.26 to -0.63.
- Adaptive steering reduces Expected Calibration Error (ECE) by 4–7× compared to unsteered verbalized confidence and outperforms the SteerConf baseline on all evaluated models and datasets (MATH, MMLU, TriviaQA, TruthfulQA).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Orthogonal Encoding
**Claim:** Gold calibration (empirical accuracy) and verbalized confidence occupy nearly orthogonal linear directions in the residual stream, explaining why models fail to verbalize faithful confidence despite internally knowing their accuracy.  
**Mechanism:** Train separate ridge regression probes on activations from pure correctness prompts (to predict empirical accuracy) and pure confidence prompts (to predict verbalized confidence). The probe weight vectors approximate the underlying encoding directions and exhibit very low cosine similarity (< 0.04) across all layers.  
**Core assumption:** The most predictive linear probe direction is a good approximation of the true representational direction; cosine similarity between probe vectors reflects the geometric relationship between the underlying encodings.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: “well-calibrated accuracy signals and verbalized confidence occupy nearly orthogonal linear directions (cosine similarity < 0.04) in the residual stream”  
- Section 3.3: Figure 3c and explicit statement that similarity remains below 0.04 across layers.  
- Related work (Marks & Tegmark, 2024; Burns et al., 2024) shows linear structure for truth/falsehood; this paper shows analogous orthogonal structure for confidence vs. accuracy.  
**Break condition:** The orthogonality finding could fail for significantly larger models (70B+), different architectures, or if the probe method is biased (e.g., regularization forces orthogonality).

### Mechanism 2: Reasoning Contamination Effect
**Claim:** Prompting the model to reason and rate confidence jointly contaminates the confidence representation, inverting its geometric relationship with accuracy (cosine similarity drops from +0.26 to −0.63).  
**Mechanism:** Construct contrastive activation addition (CAA) vectors separately under pure confidence and joint solve-and-rate conditions. The confidence and accuracy directions under the pure prompt are weakly aligned; under the joint prompt they become strongly anti-correlated. This suggests the reasoning process interferes with the confidence readout.  
**Core assumption:** The CAA approach isolates the component of activations that varies with expressed confidence while controlling for question content; the change in directional cosine is caused by the joint reasoning+rating task itself.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: “prompting the model to reason and rate confidence jointly causes a ‘Reasoning Contamination Effect,’ inverting the relationship…”  
- Section 3.4: Figure 3d shows the layer-wise cosine similarity for both conditions; the gap is the contamination effect.  
- Concurrent work (Kumaran et al., 2026; Seo et al., 2026) finds confidence is cached and can be disturbed; this paper identifies a specific geometric inversion.  
**Break condition:** The contamination effect might diminish with better instruction tuning or if the model is explicitly trained to separate reasoning from confidence assessment.

### Mechanism 3: Adaptive Steering Pipeline
**Claim:** A two-stage adaptive pipeline—using a probe to read the model’s internal accuracy estimate and CAA to steer verbalized confidence to match it—substantially improves calibration (reducing ECE by 4–7×).  
**Mechanism:** Stage 1: Train ridge probe on pure correctness activations to predict empirical accuracy; calibrate outputs via isotonic regression. Stage 2: Build CAA steering vector from pure confidence prompts. For each test question, use the probe’s predicted accuracy as a target confidence, then invert a mapping from steering strength α to verbalized confidence (obtained on validation) to compute a per‑question α that pushes the verbalized confidence toward the target. Apply α during generation under a pure confidence prompt.  
**Core assumption:** The probe provides an accurate, question-specific target; CAA steering can shift verbalized confidence in a controlled, generalizable way without degrading the underlying correctness signal.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: “introduce a two-stage adaptive pipeline… This intervention substantially improves calibration, reducing Expected Calibration Error (ECE) by 4–7×”  
- Section 3.8: Table 4 shows results across three models; adaptive steering yields much lower ECE/Brier/MAE than unsteered verbalized confidence and SteerConf.  
- SteerConf (Zhou et al., 2025) is a prior prompting baseline; adaptive steering outperforms it.  
**Break condition:** If the probe’s accuracy predictions are themselves miscalibrated, or if the steering vector does not transfer well to new distributions, the adaptive pipeline may overshoot or undershoot targets.

## Foundational Learning

### Linear Probing & Representation Geometry
- **Why needed:** The analysis relies on training linear probes to extract direction vectors for accuracy and confidence and comparing their geometric relationship (cosine similarity, principal angles).  
- **Quick check:** If two concepts are encoded in orthogonal subspaces, what would you expect the cosine similarity between their top probe directions to be? What about the mean principal angle between 10‑dimensional predictive subspaces?  
  *Answer: Cosine near 0; mean principal angle near 90°.*

### Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) Steering
- **Why needed:** CAA is the tool used to causally shift verbalized confidence and to construct the steering vectors that define the confidence direction.  
- **Quick check:** How does CAA differ from simply adding a fixed vector to all activations? Why is the contrastive computation (high‑conf minus low‑conf) important for isolating the desired direction?  
  *Answer: CAA isolates the direction associated with confidence variation while controlling for question content; adding a fixed vector would not necessarily align with the confidence signal and could affect other aspects.*

### Calibration Metrics (ECE, Brier Score, MAE)
- **Why needed:** The paper’s main outcome is improved calibration; understanding these metrics is necessary to interpret Table 4 and the claimed improvements.  
- **Quick check:** If a model always says 80% confidence and is correct 80% of the time, what is its Expected Calibration Error (ECE)? What if it says 95% confidence but is only correct 60% of the time?  
  *Answer: First case ECE = 0 (perfect calibration); second case positive ECE (|0.95 – 0.60| weighted by bin proportion).*

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Probe trainer (pure correctness activations) → Gold calibration probe  
CAA builder (pure confidence activations) → Steering vector v  
Adaptive α controller (validation mapping) → α mapping function  
Generation engine (steering injection) → Steered confidence outputs  

### Critical Path
1. Train gold probe on training split.
2. Build CAA steering vectors on training split.
3. On validation, sweep α to map α → verbalized confidence; fit inverse function.
4. On test: (a) use probe to get target c*, (b) compute α* via inverse, (c) generate confidence with steering at α*, (d) evaluate calibration.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Layer selection: Steering can be applied at different layers; paper finds answer‑token position best, but prompt‑completion activations are more practical.
- Steering strength range: Must be chosen per model; too small no effect, too large distorts outputs.
- Separate passes vs. joint: Confidence elicitation in separate forward pass avoids reasoning contamination but costs compute.
- Probe complexity: Ridge regression balances expressivity and overfitting; deflation for subspaces adds robustness.

### Failure Signatures
- Probe test R² < 0.3 → no linearly decodable accuracy signal.
- CAA vector derived from MATH fails to shift confidence on MMLU → lack of cross‑dataset generalization.
- Adaptive steering lowers verbalized confidence but answer accuracy also drops → contamination or mis‑specified target.
- Inverse mapping is noisy → calibration becomes unstable.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Linear encodability: Train ridge probes on pure correctness/pure confidence activations; report train/test R² across layers.
2. Directional orthogonality: Compute cosine similarity between the two probe weight vectors; also compute principal angles and CCA for subspaces.
3. Reasoning contamination: Under pure vs. joint prompts, compute contrastive confidence and accuracy directions and their cosine similarity; observe the gap.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the representational dissociation between accuracy and verbalized confidence persist in larger language models (e.g., >70B parameters)? The study used only 7–8B models; scale may alter the geometry.
2. What specific computational mechanism within the transformer architecture causes the "Reasoning Contamination Effect" where joint solve-and-rate prompting inverts the correlation between accuracy and confidence directions? The paper documents the effect but not its circuit-level cause.
3. Why does the effectiveness of base-model-derived steering vectors vary significantly when transferred to instruct-tuned variants (e.g., weaker effect on Mistral-7B-Instruct vs. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct)? The variability suggests differences in post‑training procedures but is not explained.
4. Can the two‑stage adaptive steering pipeline be extended to open‑ended generation tasks where "accuracy" is not easily defined? Extending beyond QA requires rethinking how the probe target is constructed.
5. What is the precise functional role of the nearly orthogonal accuracy and confidence subspaces during autoregressive generation? Is the miscalibration a readout failure, or a byproduct of optimization for other objectives (e.g., helpfulness)? The training dynamics that led to this geometry remain unclear.

## Limitations
- Confidence parsing method not detailed; free‑form responses could bias CAA vectors and calibration metrics.
- Steering injection protocol (layer, tokens) unspecified; may alter steering effectiveness and monotonicity.
- Linear probing may oversimplify; true representations could be non‑linear, questioning orthogonality measure.
- Adaptive pipeline’s inverse mapping derived from validation; may overfit and not generalize.

## Confidence
- Orthogonal encoding of accuracy and confidence: High
- Reasoning contamination effect: Medium
- Adaptive steering improvements: Medium
