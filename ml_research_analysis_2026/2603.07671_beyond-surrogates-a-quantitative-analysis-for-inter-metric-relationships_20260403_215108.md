# Beyond Surrogates: A Quantitative Analysis for Inter-Metric Relationships
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.07671
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07671
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: The paper proves a strict hierarchical relationship among metric groups and shows asymmetric regret transfer: optimizing Listwise metrics yields stable guarantees for Pairwise metrics with logarithmic scaling, while optimizing Pairwise for Listwise objectives risks polynomial error amplification, explaining offline-online metric mismatch.

## Executive Summary
"Metric Mismatch"—the phenomenon where offline validation improvements do not translate to online gains—is a major challenge in production machine learning. This paper provides a theoretical foundation to quantify direct relationships between evaluation metrics. By modeling metrics through a unified risk functional with weight functions, the authors identify three structural families: Pointwise (e.g., Accuracy), Pairwise (e.g., AUC), and Listwise (e.g., NDCG). They establish that Bayes-optimal ranking solutions automatically achieve optimal classification, but not conversely, and reveal a striking asymmetry in regret transfer: moving from Listwise to Pairwise metrics incurs only logarithmic growth in list size, whereas moving from Pairwise to Listwise leads to polynomial amplification. These results explain why offline AUC improvements often vanish in online top-heavy objectives. Empirical validation on synthetic data and MovieLens corroborates the theory and offers practical guidance for metric selection.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a unified risk framework for evaluation metrics, expressed as a weighted sum of instance-level utilities. This formulation cleanly separates metrics into three structural groups: Pointwise (weights independent of rank), Pairwise (weights depend on relative order), and Listwise (weights decay with position). They define Bayes-optimal sets for each metric and introduce the Regret Transfer function Ψ_A→B(ε), which upper bounds the regret on metric B given a bounded regret on metric A. Theoretical results include: (1) a strict inclusion hierarchy where the Bayes-optimal set for ranking metrics is contained within that for classification; (2) asymmetric transfer rates: from Listwise to Pairwise the worst-case coefficient grows as O(log n) or O(log² n), while from Pairwise to Listwise it grows as O(n) or O(n log n). The theory is evaluated via simulation—generating predictors with metric-specific error patterns—and real experiments on the MovieLens-1M dataset with BCE, BPR, and ListNet models. Results confirm the predicted relationships and the practical impact of metric mismatch.

## Key Results
- The paper introduces a unified risk framework that categorizes metrics into Pointwise, Pairwise, and Listwise based on their evaluation unit.
- It proves a strict Bayes-optimal set hierarchy: optimal ranking (Pairwise/Listwise) inherently satisfies optimal classification, but not vice versa.
- Regret transfer between metrics is highly asymmetric: optimizing Listwise metrics yields a logarithmic guarantee on Pairwise metrics, while optimizing Pairwise for Listwise objectives results in polynomial error amplification, explaining the prevalence of offline-online metric mismatch.

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Evaluation metrics are structurally categorized into Pointwise, Pairwise, and Listwise groups based on their evaluation unit (independent items, pairs, or full lists).
- Mechanism: Using a unified risk framework (Eq. 3) with weight function w(i) and utility ϕ(y), groups are distinguished: Pointwise metrics treat instances independently (w(i) constant or truncated), Pairwise metrics aggregate pairwise comparisons (w(i) depends on relative ranks), and Listwise metrics assign position-sensitive weights (w(i) decreasing with rank).
- Core assumption: Metrics fit the generalized form in Eq. (3) and are defined under binary relevance for clarity.
- Evidence anchors:

  - [corpus]: Corpus neighbors discuss metric evaluation but do not directly evidence this specific categorization; evidence here is weak.
- Break condition: Metrics that are non-decomposable or have complex dependencies beyond the weight function (e.g., metrics involving set-level properties or non-linear aggregates not captured by Eq. 3).

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The Bayes-optimal set for Listwise and Pairwise metrics is a subset of that for Pointwise metrics (ML ⪯B MP, MR ⪯B MP), meaning optimal ranking inherently satisfies optimal classification.
- Mechanism: For binary relevance, ranking optimality requires f to be a monotonic transformation of η(x), which implies correct classification relative to threshold τ=0.5. Classification optimality only requires correct side-of-threshold assignment, allowing arbitrary intra-class ordering.
- Core assumption: Binary labels y∈{0,1} and continuous conditional expectation η(x); hypothesis space includes all measurable functions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: States "optimal ranking inherently satisfies classification optimality."
  - [section 4.2]: Theorem 4.3 formally proves ML ⪯B MP and ML ≡B MR via set inclusions.
  - [corpus]: No direct corpus evidence; neighboring papers focus on metric mismatch but not thisBayes-optimal hierarchy.
- Break condition: Multi-level relevance scores or non-binary settings where classification thresholding is ambiguous; also if η(x) has mass exactly at 0.5, the inclusion may not be strict.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Regret transfer between Listwise and Pairwise metrics is asymmetric: optimizing Listwise metrics (e.g., NDCG) provides a stable guarantee for Pairwise metrics (AUC) with logarithmic scaling, while optimizing AUC for Listwise objectives leads to severe error amplification with polynomial scaling.
- Mechanism: Through the Regret Transfer Function ΨA→B(ε), coefficients derived from weight differentials (∆max, ∆min) and list size n show that CR→L ∝ O(n log n) or O(n) depending on label balance, while CL→R ∝ O(log n) or O(log² n), as proved in Theorem 4.6 and 4.7.
- Core assumption: Decreasing weight function w(r) (e.g., w(r)=1/log(1+r)), large list size n, and fixed label distribution during training.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: Summarizes "regret transfer is highly asymmetric" and "optimizing Listwise metrics provides a stable, scaled guarantee for Pairwise metrics."
  - [section 4.3.2]: Theorems 4.6 and 4.7 provide explicit transfer bounds and asymptotic rates.
  - [corpus]: Corpus neighbors address offline-online alignment but do not quantify this asymmetry; evidence is limited.
- Break condition: Non-decreasing weight functions, very small list sizes where asymptotic rates don't apply, or dynamic label distributions that change scale during optimization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can the framework be extended to multi‑graded relevance and metrics like ERR or RBP?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors restrict to binary relevance: “they are well‑defined and free from the ambiguity encountered in multi‑level settings” (Section 3) and assume binary labels throughout.
- Why unresolved: Multi‑graded utilities are non‑linear in relevance, and optimal ranking may not reduce to sorting by η(x); this could alter the inclusion hierarchy (⪯B, ≡B) and regret transfer coefficients.
- What evidence would resolve it: A generalized Bayes‑optimal set definition for graded relevance (e.g., via the Probability Ranking Principle), corresponding theorems, and empirical validation on datasets with graded labels (e.g., TREC).

### Open Question 2
- Question: How tight are the theoretical worst‑case regret transfer bounds under realistic data distributions and model classes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that the extreme constructions used to show transfer failure or asymmetry are “of limited practical reference” (Section 4.3) and that “the fixed environment of the simulation may not fully demonstrate the scaling divergence” (Section 5.3), implying the bounds may be loose in practice.
- Why unresolved: The bounds are supremum over all predictors, including pathological ones unlikely to arise from standard optimization. Real predictors obey smoothness, margin, or other inductive biases that restrict the regret manifold, so actual transfer could be significantly smaller.
- What evidence would resolve it: Empirical estimation of Ψ(ε) from training trajectories of common losses (e.g., cross‑entropy, BPR, ListNet) on diverse datasets, comparing observed ratios to worst‑case coefficients; theoretically, incorporating distributional assumptions (e.g., low noise, margin conditions) to derive tighter, data‑dependent bounds.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does model capacity and inductive bias affect the inclusion relationships and regret transfer asymmetry?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The analysis assumes a sufficiently rich hypothesis class F (all measurable functions); capacity limitations are never discussed, yet they are intrinsic to practical learning.
- Why unresolved: Restrictions on F (e.g., linear models, finite‑width neural nets) change the attainable Bayes‑optimal sets. For instance, linear models cannot perfectly order points by η(x) if the relationship is non‑monotonic, potentially altering both subsumption relations and actual regret transfer during training.
- What evidence would resolve it: Theoretical characterization of F*_A under capacity constraints (e.g., for linear models) and experiments that compare regret transfer across model families while holding the training objective constant.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How do distribution shifts between training and deployment impact the metric relationships and transfer bounds?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper explicitly assumes a single fixed distribution P (Section 3) and acknowledges in the introduction that domain‑shift guarantees “implicitly rely on the assumption that this structural consistency persists across domains,” which is not addressed here.
- Why unresolved: Under shift, the Bayes‑optimal predictor and the deterministic inclusion relations (e.g., ML ⊆ MP) may no longer hold; the regret transfer coefficients become distribution‑dependent, complicating offline metric selection for online objectives.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extend the framework to two distributions P (train) and P′ (deploy), using divergence measures to bound the change in regret; conduct experiments with controlled covariate or label shifts to test the robustness of the theoretical asymmetry.

## Limitations
- The framework assumes binary relevance and idealized Bayes-optimal predictors, limiting applicability to multi-level relevance scenarios.
- Empirical validation is restricted to a single dataset (MovieLens-1M) and synthetic simulations, raising concerns about generalizability.
- Key experimental details (hyperparameters for baseline models, precise error injection procedures) are not provided, hindering faithful reproduction.
