# MentalBench: A Benchmark for Evaluating Psychiatric Diagnostic Capability of Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12871
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12871
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** MentalBench introduces a psychiatrist-validated knowledge graph (MentalKG) and 24,750 synthetic clinical cases to evaluate psychiatric diagnostic reasoning; LLMs achieve >94% accuracy on complete structured cases but drop to 62–88% with incomplete information and show critical calibration failures (under-diagnosis in ambiguous cases, over-diagnosis in unambiguous ones).

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the lack of clinically grounded benchmarks for evaluating large language models (LLMs) in psychiatric diagnosis, which has traditionally relied on social media data. It presents MentalBench, built on MentalKG—a knowledge graph encoding DSM-5 criteria for 23 disorders, validated by psychiatrists. Using this graph, the authors generate 24,750 synthetic clinical cases that systematically vary in information completeness (medical chart vs. patient self-report) and diagnostic complexity (single vs. differential diagnosis). Evaluations of state-of-the-art LLMs reveal high accuracy on structured single-disorder queries (>94%) but significant performance drops with incomplete information (62–88%) and in differential diagnosis. Models exhibit calibration failures: proprietary models under-diagnose in ambiguous scenarios, while open-source models over-diagnose in unambiguous ones. MentaLLaMA, trained on social media, underperforms (~20%), and errors are pronounced for under-represented disorders. The work highlights the need for LLMs to self-regulate diagnostic cardinality and handle information sparsity—key challenges for real-world clinical deployment.

## Method Summary
MentalBench comprises two core components: MentalKG, a psychiatrist-validated knowledge graph that structures DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, symptom hierarchies, and differential diagnosis rules; and a synthetic case generation pipeline that produces diverse clinical narratives. Symptom profiles are sampled from MentalKG to satisfy specific diagnostic constraints (e.g., full criteria, partial presentations, ambiguous overlaps). These profiles are instantiated with demographic attributes (age, gender, occupation, marital status) and then transformed into natural language narratives via LLMs, mimicking either a structured medical chart or a patient’s self-report. The resulting dataset of 24,750 cases is evaluated zero-shot on various LLMs using prompts that specify single or multiple answer constraints. Model predictions are compared against exact-match ground truth, and errors are categorized as over-diagnosis, under-diagnosis, or incorrect, enabling fine-grained analysis of diagnostic reasoning capabilities.

## Key Results
- LLMs achieve high accuracy on Type 1 cases (complete medical chart, single disorder) with some models exceeding 94%, but performance declines significantly on Type 2 (incomplete self-report) to a range of 62–88%.
- Proprietary models show low recall (58–69%) in Type 3 ambiguous differential diagnosis (under-diagnosis), while open-source models show low precision (50–57%) in Type 4 unambiguous cases (over-diagnosis).
- MentaLLaMA, which was trained on social media text, performs poorly overall (~20% accuracy), and models consistently struggle with under-represented disorders (e.g., schizophrenia spectrum accuracy below 40% in Type 2).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
#### Mechanism 1: Synthetic cases from a validated knowledge graph provide a low-noise logical backbone
MentalKG structurally encodes DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, symptom thresholds, and differential diagnosis rules. Symptom profiles are sampled from this graph to ensure clinical validity, then augmented with demographics and linguistically naturalized via LLMs. This decouples diagnostic logic from narrative noise, enabling controlled evaluation that systematically varies information completeness and complexity. The graph acts as a "golden-standard logical backbone" ensuring that each case’s diagnostic answer is unambiguous and derived from explicit criteria.

#### Mechanism 2: Diagnostic cardinality is a separable skill that LLMs lack without explicit constraints
Models are tested on ambiguous (Type 3: multiple correct diagnoses) and unambiguous (Type 4: single correct diagnosis) differential cases. Proprietary models exhibit high precision but low recall in Type 3 (tending to under-diagnose), while open-source models show high recall but low precision in Type 4 (tending to over-diagnose). When prompts explicitly constrain the number of diagnoses (single vs. multiple), performance improves dramatically, indicating that models possess latent knowledge but cannot autonomously infer the appropriate diagnostic cardinality from clinical evidence alone.

#### Mechanism 3: Information incompleteness reveals a critical sensitivity to data sparsity
The contrast between Type 1 (complete structured chart) and Type 2 (incomplete self-report) cases demonstrates that models struggle to map fragmented, subjective narratives to diagnostic criteria. This drop in performance (e.g., from >94% to 62–88%) mirrors a key real-world challenge: clinicians often must diagnose with incomplete information. The result underscores that LLMs’ diagnostic competence is brittle when facing the inherent sparsity of typical patient reports.

## Foundational Learning
- **DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria Structure**  
  *Why needed:* MentalBench’s design relies on formally representing DSM-5 rules (symptom counts, duration, exclusion criteria). Understanding these structured definitions is essential to grasp what the models are applying.  
  *Quick check:* What are the minimum symptom count and duration requirements for a Major Depressive Episode, and how does this differ from Persistent Depressive Disorder?

- **Differential Diagnosis as Directed Relationships**  
  *Why needed:* The knowledge graph models directional differential relationships (e.g., MDD vs. Bipolar II) with specific discriminating features, turning classification into nuanced boundary-setting.  
  *Quick check:* What is the key differentiating factor between Major Depressive Disorder with Psychotic Features and Schizoaffective Disorder, and how would this be encoded as a directed edge?

- **Evaluation Calibration vs. Raw Accuracy**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s core finding is mis-calibrated decision thresholds, not just low accuracy. Precision/recall trade-offs in ambiguous vs. unambiguous settings reveal the nature of failure.  
  *Quick check:* Why is high recall but low precision in Type 4 (unambiguous) cases more indicative of a cardinality control problem than a knowledge gap?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
MentalKG -> Symptom Profile Sampler -> Patient Profile Instantiator -> Clinical Case Generator -> Evaluation Harness

**Critical path:**  
1. Knowledge graph construction with psychiatrist validation → ensures clinical validity  
2. Symptom profile generation per disorder → creates base cases  
3. Injection of differential diagnosis logic → creates Type 3/4 cases  
4. Patient profile instantiation → adds demographic variability  
5. Narrative generation via LLMs → creates natural language scenarios (chart vs. self-report)  
6. Model inference with constrained prompts → measures performance  
7. Error categorization (over/under-diagnosis) → diagnoses failure modes

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Synthetic vs. real data: Chose synthetic for control and interpretability, sacrificing some ecological validity.  
- Graph expressivity: Used explicit differential nodes with triggering conditions and discriminating rules rather than simple disorder-symptom edges to capture nuanced boundaries.  
- Stylistic variation: Generated narratives with multiple LLMs (GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Qwen3) to mitigate model-specific biases, adding complexity but improving robustness.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Type 2 performance collapse: Model fails when information is incomplete (patient self-report).  
- Type 3 low recall: Model under-diagnoses in ambiguous cases (mostly proprietary).  
- Type 4 low precision: Model over-diagnoses in unambiguous cases (mostly open-source).  
- Disease-specific accuracy <40% for schizophrenia spectrum in Type 2: Indicates data/knowledge gaps for under-represented disorders.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Main evaluation: Measure accuracy across all models and 4 case types to establish performance hierarchy and identify cardinality calibration issues.  
2. Disease-level heatmap analysis: Identify specific disorders (e.g., ADHD, schizophrenia spectrum) where models consistently fail, linking to gaps in prior benchmarks.  
3. Error type distribution: Categorize errors in Type 3/4 as Over-diagnosis, Under-diagnosis, or Incorrect to confirm calibration failure patterns diverging by model family.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Interactive diagnostics:** How can psychiatric diagnostic benchmarks be extended to capture the interactive nature of real-world clinical dialogues rather than static case narratives? Current cases are descriptive; future work aims to simulate doctor–patient interactions using the knowledge graph.
2. **Multilingual and cultural generalization:** Can MentalBench be generalized to multiple languages and cultural contexts? All cases are in English, limiting generalizability where symptom expression differs.
3. **Intrinsic cardinality awareness:** Do LLMs possess an intrinsic awareness of diagnostic cardinality (when to report a single vs. multiple diagnoses) without explicit prompt engineering? Models only succeed when cardinality is explicitly constrained.
4. **Reasoning process alignment:** How can we evaluate whether an LLM’s internal reasoning conforms to DSM-5 criteria, rather than only its final decision? The current evaluation does not examine step-by-step alignment with diagnostic logic.

## Limitations
- Synthetic cases may not fully capture the complexity, bias, or rare comorbidity patterns of real clinical records.
- MentalKG’s coverage of 23 disorders and DSM-5 encoding might miss nuanced diagnostic boundaries, especially for less common conditions.
- LLM-generated narratives can introduce stylistic artifacts or oversimplify symptom expression; the generation process depends on specific LLMs that may bias the benchmark.
- Evaluation is zero-shot; fine-tuned or specialized models could behave differently.
- Metrics focus on exact-match accuracy and calibration, without assessing clinical safety implications (e.g., risks of false negatives).

## Confidence
- Synthetic-case low-noise evaluation: **Medium–High** (psychiatrist validation, but ecological validity uncertain)
- Cardinality calibration failure: **High** (consistent across model families, supported by error-type breakdown)
- Information incompleteness drop: **High** (robust Type 1 vs. Type 2 gap)
- MentaLLaMA underperformance: **Medium** (single-model observation; requires broader replication)
- Under‑represented disorder errors: **Medium** (specific low accuracies may reflect dataset imbalance)

## Next Checks

2. Perform sensitivity analysis by varying DSM‑5 criterion thresholds (e.g., symptom count, duration) in MentalKG and re‑evaluating models to test boundary robustness.
3. Augment the benchmark with a subset of real de‑identified clinical notes to compare model performance on synthetic versus real-world narratives.
