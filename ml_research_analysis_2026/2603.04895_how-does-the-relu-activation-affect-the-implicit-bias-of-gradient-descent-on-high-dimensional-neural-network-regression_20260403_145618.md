# How Does the ReLU Activation Affect the Implicit Bias of Gradient Descent on High-dimensional Neural Network Regression?

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.04895
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04895
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** For shallow ReLU networks with high‑dimensional random data, gradient descent with small initialization converges to a solution that approximates the minimum‑ℓ2‑norm solution with a gap of order Θ(√(n/d)), and for a single positive neuron it exactly matches the minimum‑ℓ2‑norm solution restricted to positive examples.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies the implicit bias of gradient descent (GD) on shallow ReLU networks with squared loss under high‑dimensional random data. The authors develop a primal‑dual analysis that tracks predictions and data-span coefficients, showing the ReLU activation pattern stabilizes with high probability. For a single positive neuron, with sufficiently small initialization, GD fits all positive labels exactly and outputs zero on negatives, coinciding with the minimum‑ℓ2‑norm solution restricted to positive examples. For two neurons (one positive, one negative), the dynamics decouple and each neuron fits one sign group; the overall solution approximates the global minimum‑ℓ2‑norm but with a Euclidean gap of order Θ(√(n/d)), supported by matching upper and lower bounds. The results interpolate between worst‑case and orthogonal regimes under explicit high‑dimensional assumptions on the data spectrum.

## Method Summary
The analysis re‑expresses the network weights in terms of dual coefficients α via the relation w = Xᵀα, where X is the data matrix. This primal‑dual reformulation converts the non‑convex GD updates into a sign‑controlled linear iteration involving the predictions β = Xw and a diagonal mask D(β) that encodes which examples are active (β_i > 0). Under high‑dimensional assumptions (d ≫ n) and isotropic random data, the Gram matrix XXᵀ concentrates around its mean ∥λ∥₁I, which ensures that once the activation pattern stabilizes, each neuron’s dynamics become equivalent to ordinary least squares on its assigned subset of examples. The theory is complemented by simulations that verify the predicted scaling of the gap between the GD solution and the minimum‑ℓ2‑norm solution.

## Key Results
- For a single positive neuron, with sufficiently small initialization, gradient descent fits all positive labels exactly and outputs zero on negatives, matching the minimum‑ℓ2‑norm solution restricted to positive examples.
- For two neurons (one positive, one negative), the dynamics decouple: each neuron fits one sign group, and the combined solution approximates the global minimum‑ℓ2‑norm solution with a Euclidean gap of order Θ(√(n/d)).
- The results hold under high‑dimensional assumptions on the data spectrum, specifically effective dimensions d₂ and d_∞ satisfying d₂ ≥ C₀² n² (y_max/y_min)² and d_∞ ≥ C₀ n^{1.5} (y_max/y_min), which guarantee the Gram matrix concentration needed for activation stabilization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Primal‑Dual Representation
The primal‑dual reformulation transforms the non‑convex GD dynamics into a sign‑controlled linear iteration on β = Xw and α = (XXᵀ)⁻¹β. By restricting w to the row span of X (w = Xᵀα), the updates become β_{t+1} = β_t - η s_k XXᵀ D(β_t)(β_t - y) and α_{t+1} = α_t - η D(β_t)(β_t - y). The diagonal mask D(β) directly encodes which examples have β_i > 0 (active), separating the ReLU nonlinearity into a sign condition. Core assumptions: w always lies in span(X) (the orthogonal component never updates), XXᵀ invertible (rank(X)=n), and the step size is small enough to preserve the representation. Break condition: If XXᵀ becomes ill‑conditioned (e.g., d not much larger than n, small eigenvalues), the mapping between w and α breaks down and the sign‑preservation lemmas no longer hold.

### Mechanism 2: High‑Dimensional Concentration
In high dimensions, concentration of XXᵀ around ∥λ∥₁ I ensures that self‑interaction dominates cross‑talk. Using non‑asymptotic Hanson‑Wright bounds, with probability ≥1−2e^{-n/C_g}, ∥XXᵀ - ∥λ∥₁ I∥ ≤ C∥λ∥₁ max(√(n/d₂), n/d_∞). Substituting into the β‑update shows β_{t+1,i} ≥ β_{t,i} - η∥λ∥₁(β_{t,i}-y_i) - η·(small), preserving positivity for active positives. For negatives, β_j = ∥λ∥₁ α_j + cross; a sufficiently negative α_j with bounded ∥α∥ forces β_j ≤ 0 and stops α updates. Core assumptions: Assumption 2 requires d₂ ≥ C₀² n² (y_max/y_min)² and d_∞ ≥ C₀ n^{1.5} (y_max/y_min), and labels are bounded and non‑zero. Break condition: When d is moderate (n ~ d), concentration degrades and cross‑terms remain significant; the activation pattern may not stabilize.

### Mechanism 3: Small Initialization Forces Stabilization
A prescribed small all‑positive initialization forces the system into the stable regime after one GD step. For a single neuron, w(0)=Xᵀ(XXᵀ)⁻¹ε with 0<ε_i ≤ 1/(C_α y_min) gives β(0)=ε>0. Lemma 5 keeps positives active; Lemma 6 drives negatives’ α_j below –y_min/(C_α∥λ∥₁) freezing them. For two neurons, both start with small positive ε, and the model h = σ(β_⊕)−σ(β_⊖) causes the first update to separate sign groups; Lemma 10 then holds for t≥1. Subsequent updates are equivalent to linear regression on S₊ (for ⊕) and S₋ (for ⊖). Core assumptions: small initialization (ε tiny), step size η ∈ [1/(C_g∥λ∥₁), 1/(C_g∥λ∥₁)], and high‑dimensional Assumption 2. Break condition: Too large ε prevents α from becoming negative enough for negatives, leaving them active; random Gaussian init often starts some positives with β_i≤0, causing those examples never to be fit.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Primal‑Dual Representation in Mirror Descent**  
- *Why needed:* It rewrites w as Xᵀα, turning the ReLU breakpoint into a simple sign(β) test. This enables tracking of which examples receive gradient updates.  
- *Quick check:* Given β = Xw and α = (XXᵀ)⁻¹β, derive the update for α_{t+1} in terms of D(β_t) and (β_t - y). If β_{t,i}>0, how does α_{t,i} change?

**Concept: High‑Dimensional Random Matrix Concentration**  
- *Why needed:* The proof hinges on tight spectral bounds for XXᵀ to argue that the self‑term (∥λ∥₁ I) dominates the cross‑terms in the updates. This is the engine behind Lemmas 5 and 6.  
- *Quick check:* What does Assumption 2 guarantee about λ_min(XXᵀ) and λ_max(XXᵀ) relative to ∥λ∥₁? Plug the bounds into Lemma 7.

**Concept: Minimum‑ℓ2‑Norm Interpolation and Implicit Bias**  
- *Why needed:* The implicit bias is expressed as the distance between the GD limit and the minimum‑norm solution over all feasible interpolants. For single ReLU this leads to a convex program (7) whose solution is a linear interpolant on a data‑dependent subset.  
- *Quick check:* For single ReLU, why can the minimum‑norm solution w* sometimes include negative‑labeled examples in its support (with zero prediction), whereas the GD solution ignores all negatives? What KKT condition explains the difference?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** (X, y) → Shallow ReLU network (m neurons, signs s_k, weights w_k) → Squared loss → Gradient descent. Analysis tools: primal β_k = Xw_k, dual α_k = (XXᵀ)⁻¹β_k, diagonal mask D(β_k).

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Write primal‑dual updates.  
  2. Prove Lemmas 5 & 6 (sign preservation under high‑d concentration).  
  3. Use small init to enter the good regime after one step.  
  4. Show activation pattern fixed (Lemmas 9/10) ⇒ dynamics become per‑neuron linear regression.  
  5. Identify the limit as minimum‑norm on the active subset.  
  6. Compare to the constrained ReLU minimum‑norm solution via KKT and spectral bounds to obtain Θ(√(n/d)) gap.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  • More neurons (m > 2) increase expressivity but require strong disjoint‑activation assumptions (Appendix D); otherwise neurons compete.  
  • High effective dimension (d ≫ n) is essential for spectral concentration; moderate d destroys the separation.  
  • Step size must lie in a narrow window [1/(C_g∥λ∥₁), 1/(C_g∥λ∥₁)]; too large destabilizes the primal.  
  • Initialization variance must be O(1/y_min²) to ensure one‑step alignment; larger init breaks the mechanism.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  • Activation pattern drift: In moderate d, β_i signs may oscillate (Appendix F, Figure 6).  
  • Inactive positives: A positive example with β_i(0)≤0 never gets updated → local minimum (Figure 5).  
  • Gram matrix near‑singular: d not large enough violates Lemma 7; cross‑terms can dominate.  
  • Multi‑neuron without disjoint init: Neurons compete, leading to entangled β fields (Figure 7).

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Stabilization test:** Simulate single ReLU (n=10, d=2000) with tiny positive init. Plot β_i over iterations; verify after t=1 all positives stay >0 and all negatives ≤0. Final w should match linear regression on positives.  
  2. **Gap scaling:** Vary d (fix n=10). For each d, compute w(∞) by GD and w* via convex program (7). Plot ||w(∞)-w*|| vs √(n/d); expect linear trend (Theorem 2).  
  3. **Two‑neuron decoupling:** Use init from Theorem 3. Track β_⊕ and β_⊖. Check positive examples active only in β_⊕ (negative in β_⊖) and vice versa. Compute combined norm gap against the relaxed two‑neuron min‑norm solution (Lemma 4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** Characterize the implicit bias for shallow ReLU networks with more than two neurons (m > 2) under general random initializations, without the current disjoint activation assumption.  
*Basis:* The authors provide only partial extensions for m>2 that require a “disjoint” initialization.  
*Why unresolved:* The interaction among multiple neurons complicates the primal‑dual dynamics, and existing techniques do not easily extend to overlapping active sets.

**Open Question 2:** Understand the implicit bias when the feature dimension d is high but not extremely high relative to n (i.e., d > n but d not ≫ n), where concentration effects weaken and cross‑example interactions become significant.  
*Basis:* The current analysis relies heavily on near‑orthogonality of random high‑dimensional features; moderate dimensions break the concentration bounds.  
*Why unresolved:* The primal‑dual variables become tightly coupled across examples, making the current approach inapplicable; new tools are needed.

**Open Question 3:** Characterize the implicit bias for more realistic initialization schemes (e.g., standard Gaussian small initialization) that do not ensure a disjoint or all‑positive primal start, and determine whether gradient descent still converges to a global minimum with a well‑defined bias.  
*Basis:* The authors assume a carefully chosen initialization; simulations show random initialization can lead to local minima.  
*Why unresolved:* Standard initializations may cause some positive‑labeled examples to start inactive and remain so; the theory for such settings is lacking.

## Limitations
- Exact constants (C_g, C_α, C) in spectral concentration and step‑size bounds are unknown, affecting the required minimal d and initialization scale.
- The precise high‑dimensional regime threshold (how large d must be relative to n) remains unspecified due to the hidden constant C₀ in Assumption 2.
- The analysis for m > 2 neurons relies on strong disjoint activation assumptions that may not hold in practice; generalization beyond this is open.
