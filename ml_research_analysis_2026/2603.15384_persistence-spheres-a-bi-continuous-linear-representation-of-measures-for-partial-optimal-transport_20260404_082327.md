# Persistence Spheres: a Bi-continuous Linear Representation of Measures for Partial Optimal Transport
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.15384
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15384
- **Reference count**: 13
- **Primary result**: Provides a linear representation of persistence diagrams that is stable under partial optimal transport (POT1) and bi-continuous for compactly supported measures, with strong empirical performance across diverse tasks.

## Executive Summary
Persistence diagrams are widely used in topological data analysis, but existing vectorization methods often lack stability under the partial optimal transport (POT1) metric or invertibility. The paper introduces *persistence spheres*, a novel linear representation that maps an integrable measure on the upper half-plane to a continuous function on the unit sphere. The construction uses two key ingredients: a signed diagonal augmentation μᵃᵘᵍ = μ − (π_Δ)#μ, which encodes POT1 deletions, and the lift‑zonoid transform Λ(σ)(v) = ∫ ReLU(⟨v,(1,p)⟩) dσ(p), evaluated on the sphere. Theoretically, the map S(μ) = Λ(μᵃᵘᵍ)|_{S²} is Lipschitz under POT1 (∥S(μ)−S(ν)∥∞ ≤ 2√2 POT₁(μ,ν)) and, for compactly supported targets, the inverse is continuous (uniform convergence of sphere functions implies POT1 convergence). For growth‑controlled measure classes Mc(A,B) this yields a bi‑continuous embedding into L²(S²). Extensive experiments on functional, temporal, graph, mesh, and point cloud data show that persistence spheres achieve state‑of‑the‑art or competitive performance compared to persistence images, landscapes, splines, and sliced Wasserstein baselines across clustering, regression, and classification tasks.

## Method Summary
The core of persistence spheres is the transformation of an integrable measure μ on the persistence diagram space X = {(x,y): x<y} into a function on S². First, compute the signed augmentation μᵃᵘᵍ = μ − (π_Δ)#μ, where π_Δ(p) = ((x+y)/2, (x+y)/2) projects a point onto the diagonal. This signed measure effectively subtracts mass that would be deleted under partial transport. Second, apply the lift‑zonoid transform Λ(σ)(v) = ∫ ReLU(⟨v,(1,p)⟩) dσ(p) for each direction v ∈ S²; for positive measures, Λ(σ) is the support function of the lift zonoid Z_σ ⊂ ℝ³. The persistence sphere S(μ) is the restriction of Λ(μᵃᵘᵍ) to S². For a discrete diagram μ = Σ wᵢ δ_{pᵢ}, the evaluation on a grid of directions is a simple weighted sum of ReLU differences: S(μ)(v) = Σ wᵢ [ReLU(⟨v,(1,pᵢ)⟩) - ReLU(⟨v,(1,π_Δ(pᵢ)⟩)]. The representation is linear in μ and parameter‑free at the measure level; only numerical discretization (grid resolution) is needed. Optionally, the sphere function can be expanded in spherical harmonics to obtain a fixed‑dimensional feature vector for machine learning.

## Key Results
- **Stability under POT1**: ∥S(μ)−S(ν)∥∞ ≤ 2√2·POT₁(μ,ν) (Theorem 2).
- **Bi‑continuity**: If μ has compact support in X and ∥S(μₙ)−S(μ)∥∞ → 0, then POT₁(μₙ,μ) → 0 (Theorem 4). Thus S is continuous and its inverse is continuous on its image for compactly supported targets.
- **Bi‑continuous embedding**: For growth‑controlled measure classes Mc(A,B) (satisfying a compatibility condition), S embeds Mc(A,B) into L²(S²) as a homeomorphism onto its image (Theorem 7).
- **Empirical performance**: Across a diverse suite of datasets (functional data, time series, graphs, meshes, point clouds), persistence spheres achieve competitive or superior results compared to persistence images, landscapes, splines, and sliced Wasserstein kernels in clustering, regression, and classification tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Persistence spheres achieve their theoretical and empirical properties through three interconnected mechanisms.

**Mechanism 1: Signed diagonal augmentation directly encodes POT₁ deletions.**  
The subtraction of the diagonal pushforward means that any mass not matched in the transport between two measures appears as a signed difference of augmented measures: μᵃᵘᵍ − νᵃᵘᵍ = (μ ⊕ Δ ν) − (ν ⊕ Δ μ). This expression involves only positive cross‑augmented measures, allowing the application of Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality to derive the stability bound (Theorem 2). The mechanism requires μ to have finite first moment so that μᵃᵘᵍ is well‑defined; if first moments diverge, the augmentation and stability may fail.

**Mechanism 2: Lift‑zonoid transform via ReLU yields a linear continuous map.**  
For a positive measure σ, Λ(σ) is the support function of the lift zonoid Z_σ, a compact convex set in ℝ³. The transform Λ extends by linearity to signed measures with ∫‖(1,p)‖₂ d|σ|(p) < ∞. Convergence results for zonoids (Proposition 4) state that Hausdorff convergence of Z_σₙ is equivalent to weak convergence of σₙ plus uniform integrability. Hence Λ(σₙ) → Λ(σ) uniformly on S² under these conditions. This continuity property carries over to the signed augmentation used in persistence spheres.

**Mechanism 3: Bi‑continuity via local Hölder bound and tail control.**  
For a compactly supported reference μ, uniform convergence S(μₙ)→S(μ) on S² implies POT₁‑convergence μₙ→μ (Theorem 4). The forward direction (POT₁→uniform) follows from the Lipschitz property of ReLU and duality (Theorem 2). The inverse direction (uniform→POT₁) is proven by controlling mass outside a compact core using lemmas on vanishing tails (Lemmas 7–9). On the core, a local Hölder bound (Theorem 3) is derived by approximating arbitrary 1‑Lipschitz test functions using ReLU ridge families, relying on approximation theory from Mao et al. (2024). This yields POT₁ ≤ C·∥S(μₙ)−S(μ)∥∞^{2/5} on the core, with tail errors vanishing. The combination gives continuity of the inverse.

## Foundational Learning
- **Partial Optimal Transport (POT₁)**: Generalizes optimal transport by allowing unmatched mass to be deleted to the diagonal at cost proportional to persistence. *Why needed*: Provides the natural metric for comparing persistence diagrams. *Quick check*: For μ,ν on X, POT₁(μ,ν) = inf_γ ∫‖p−q‖ dγ(p,q) + ∫‖p−π_Δ(p)‖ d(μ−(π₁)#γ) + ∫‖q−π_Δ(q)‖ d(ν−(π₂)#γ).
- **Lift Zonoids**: For a positive measure σ, the lift zonoid Z_σ = { ∫₀¹ (1, p) t dσ(p) : p ∈ X } ⊂ ℝ³. *Why needed*: Serves as the geometric object whose support function equals Λ(σ). This link provides convex geometry intuition and yields convergence results. *Quick check*: For discrete σ = Σ cᵢ δ_{pᵢ}, Z_σ = Σ cᵢ [0, (1,pᵢ)], the Minkowski sum of line segments.
- **ReLU Ridge Approximation Theory**: ReLU features of the form ReLU(⟨v,(1,p)⟩) form a family of ridge functions. *Why needed*: The local Hölder bound (Theorem 3) depends on the ability to approximate 1‑Lipschitz test functions by such ridge functions in Sobolev spaces. Recent results (Mao et al., 2024) guarantee approximation with controlled complexity. *Quick check*: Given a 1‑Lipschitz f on a compact subset of ℝ², there exists a signed measure α on S¹×ℝ with bounded total variation such that f(x) ≈ ∫ ReLU(⟨ω,x⟩+b) dα(ω,b).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Input: integrable measure μ (e.g., a persistence diagram as a weighted set of points).  
  → Step 1: Compute signed augmentation μᵃᵘᵍ = μ − (π_Δ)#μ (subtract mass projected onto diagonal).  
  → Step 2: Evaluate S(μ)(v) = Λ(μᵃᵘᵍ)(v) = ∫ ReLU(⟨v,(1,p)⟩) dμᵃᵘᵍ(p) for each v on a sphere grid.  
  → Step 3 (optional): Expand S(μ) in spherical harmonics to produce a fixed‑length feature vector.
- **Critical path**:  
  The main computational bottleneck is Step 2, which requires O(|μ|·|grid|) operations. For large diagrams (thousands of points) and fine grids (e.g., 100×200), this can become expensive. Step 1 involves a simple projection for each atom.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - *Grid resolution*: Higher resolution yields a better approximation of C(S²) but increases feature dimension (∼ Nθ²/2 for spherical harmonics bandlimit Nθ) and runtime.  
  - *Measure type*: Discrete measures allow direct summation; continuous intensities require sampling or analytic integration.  
  - *Parameter‑free*: Unlike persistence images, no persistence‑dependent weighting is needed; only numerical choices (grid size) affect the representation.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - *Numerical overflow*: Very large coordinates produce huge ReLU arguments; scaling by first moment may be needed.  
  - *Non‑integrable measures*: If ∫‖(1,p)‖₂ dμ(p) diverges, the transform is undefined; indicates unsuitable diagram (e.g., infinitely many points with linear growth).  
  - *High cost*: O(|diagram|·|grid|) may be prohibitive; consider sparse or stochastic grids.  
  - *Inverse continuity failure*: If the reference measure is not compactly supported, uniform convergence does not guarantee POT1 convergence (Proposition 8).
- **First 3 experiments** (reproducibility checks):  
  1. *One‑point diagonal drift*: For μₖ = δ_{(k,k)} (or shifted), observe S(μₖ)(v) asymptotes to 1_{s(v)>0}·Pers(μ₀) (Corollary 1).  
  2. *Stability bound*: For random small diagrams, compute POT₁ (via LP) and ∥S(μ)−S(ν)∥∞; verify ∥S diff∥∞ ≤ 2√2·POT₁ (Theorem 2).  
  3. *Inverse continuity on compact support*: Choose compactly supported μ; add far‑away point with small mass ε. Check that POT₁(μ,μ+εδ) and ∥S(μ+εδ)−S(μ)∥∞ both vanish as ε→0. Then fix ε and let the point drift to infinity; verify uniform convergence fails.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension beyond ReLU features**: Can the signed augmentation principle be applied with other feature families (e.g., polynomials, Fourier) to obtain stable, bi‑continuous linear summaries under POT₁? What approximation‑theoretic conditions are necessary? The current analysis leverages ReLU ridge approximation theory; extending to other bases remains open.
2. **Constructive inversion**: Given a function f ∈ C(S²) (e.g., from a learned sphere representation), how can one efficiently reconstruct a persistence measure μ such that S(μ) ≈ f? Inverse continuity does not provide an algorithm, and the non‑convex optimization over atoms and weights lacks theoretical guarantees.
3. **Scope of growth‑controlled classes**: The bi‑continuous embedding holds for Mc(A,B), defined by a compatibility condition (44). This class is somewhat technical; it is unclear whether common statistical models of diagrams (e.g., from Poisson point processes) satisfy it, and whether the condition can be replaced by a more intuitive, finitely‑checkable criterion.
4. **Predicting summary bias**: The paper shows that different summaries (persistence spheres, landscapes, images) have distinct deformation behaviors (e.g., diagonal drift, high‑persistence amplification). However, we lack a systematic theory linking geometric properties of the diagram distribution to which summary’s bias is advantageous for a given downstream task. A meta‑analysis across diverse generative models could provide practical guidance.

## Limitations
- Exact train/test splits and random seeds are not disclosed; reported empirical numbers cannot be precisely replicated.
- Baseline hyperparameters (e.g., for persistence images, landscapes, splines) are incompletely specified; some baseline results are cited from external work, making direct comparison uncertain.
- Spherical harmonics expansion details (truncation degree, normalization) are ambiguous; different choices affect feature dimensionality and downstream performance.
- Theoretical guarantees assume compact support or growth‑controlled measures; many real‑world diagrams may violate these assumptions, potentially weakening stability in practice.
- Computational cost scales with diagram size and grid resolution; no analysis of memory/time limits for large‑scale applications.

## Confidence
- **Theorems 2, 3, 4 (stability, local Hölder, inverse continuity)**: High – mathematically proven under clear assumptions.
- **Theorem 7 (bi‑continuous embedding for Mc(A,B))**: Medium – proof is sound but the applicability of Mc(A,B) to realistic unbounded diagrams is not fully established.
- **Empirical performance claims (e.g., specific R² values, ranking over baselines)**: Low – due to unreported splits, incomplete baseline specifications, and reliance on external benchmark numbers.
