# On Randomness in Agentic Evals

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07150
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07150
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Single-run pass@1 scores vary significantly (2.2–6.0 percentage points) across independent runs, with trajectories diverging extremely early, challenging the reliability of agentic evaluations.

## Executive Summary
Agentic systems are commonly evaluated using single-run pass@1 scores, assuming that they are reliable indicators of performance. This paper challenges that assumption by collecting 60,000 agent trajectories on the SWE-Bench-Verified benchmark across three models and two scaffolds. The analysis reveals substantial variance in single-run success rates (standard deviations >1.5 percentage points even at temperature 0) and extremely early divergence between independent runs—often within the first 1% of generated tokens. Furthermore, the gap between optimistic (pass@k) and pessimistic (pass^k) bounds reaches up to 24.9 percentage points, indicating that performance is highly dependent on stochastic choices. These findings imply that previously reported improvements of only a few points may be due to evaluation noise rather than genuine progress. The authors recommend multi-run estimation, statistical power analysis for determining required runs, and reporting both bounds to characterize the performance envelope. Overall, the study establishes randomness as a fundamental factor in agentic evaluations, necessitating methodological changes for reliable assessment.

## Method Summary
The authors executed a large-scale multi-run evaluation on the SWE-Bench-Verified benchmark, which consists of 500 issue-resolution tasks. For each of three models (Qwen3-32B, DeepSWE-preview, Devstral-2) and two agent scaffolds (nano-agent and R2E-Gym), they performed 10 independent runs at two temperature settings (0 and 1.0), totaling approximately 60,000 trajectories. Each run followed the same procedure: the scaffold constructs a conversation context from the task description, the model generates tokens (including reasoning and tool calls), the scaffold parses and executes those calls in a controlled environment, and the loop continues until a termination condition (e.g., passing tests or exhausting a maximum number of tool calls). Success was determined by executing the unit tests associated with each task. The recorded outcomes were used to compute per-run resolution rates (pass@1), their means and standard deviations, as well as optimistic (pass@k) and pessimistic (pass^k) bounds for k up to 5. Additionally, token-level divergence analysis was conducted to identify the earliest point at which two independent runs on the same task differ.

## Key Results
- Single-run pass@1 scores exhibit significant variance: across configurations, the range (max – min) across 10 runs spans 2.2–6.0 percentage points, with standard deviations exceeding 1.5 pp even at temperature 0.
- Token-level trajectory divergence occurs extremely early; the median first divergence position is within the first 1% of generated tokens (e.g., token 5 for DeepSWE-preview at temperature 1.0). Small initial differences cascade into entirely different solution strategies.
- The gap between pass@k and pass^k is substantial, reaching up to 24.9 percentage points, demonstrating that agent success relies heavily on stochastic exploration and that successful solutions are not consistently reproducible.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Early divergence cascades
In autoregressive agent systems, a single token difference early in the trajectory forks the path. As the agent continues generating tokens and interacting with the environment, this small difference amplifies, leading to fundamentally different solution strategies or outcomes. Evidence from token-level analysis shows that trajectories diverge within the first 1% of tokens (median token 5 for DeepSWE-preview at temperature 1.0), and a case study demonstrates how a difference at token 94 produced opposite success/failure results. This cascading effect is sustained throughout long-horizon coding tasks.

### Mechanism 2: Persistent non-sampling variance
Even when using greedy decoding (temperature 0), modern inference engines introduce non-determinism due to floating-point precision, parallelization, hardware optimizations, and batching strategies. Similarly, tool execution environments may have timing or other stochastic aspects. These low-level sources cause different outcomes across repeated runs independent of sampling randomness. Evidence: Standard deviations >1.5% are observed at temperature 0 across multiple model-scaffold pairs, and variance does not consistently decrease with greedy decoding, contrary to expectations.

### Mechanism 3: Dependency on stochastic exploration
Agent performance on complex tasks is strongly dependent on the ability to explore a vast space of potential solution trajectories. A single run may stumble upon a viable path by chance, while multiple attempts increase that chance (pass@k > pass@1). Conversely, low pass^k indicates the same agent often fails to replicate its success, meaning successful paths are fragile. Evidence: For DeepSWE-preview, pass@1=34.4%, pass@5=52.9% (gap +18.5 pp), pass^5=15.5% (gap -18.9 pp from pass@1); similar patterns hold for other configurations.

## Foundational Learning

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Task (SWE-Bench-Verified) → Scaffold (nano-agent or R2E-Gym) → Model (Qwen3-32B, DeepSWE-preview, or Devstral-2) → Environment (tool execution) → Success determination

**Critical path**  
1. Load task definition (issue description, repository snapshot).  
2. Scaffold builds initial conversation context (system + user message with task).  
3. Model generates next tokens (may include reasoning and tool calls).  
4. Scaffold parses tool calls, executes them in the environment, captures output.  
5. Output is appended to context; loop to step 3 until termination condition (tests passed or max tool calls reached).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Run multiplicity vs. computational cost: More runs improve variance estimates but linearly increase resource usage; power analysis helps find a balance.  
- Deterministic inference (temp 0) vs. exploration: Lower temperature reduces sampling variability but does not eliminate non-deterministic sources and may limit creative solutions; variance often does not decrease as expected.  
- Scaffold independence: Using two distinct scaffolds helps separate model-related from environment-related variance, but mixing models and scaffolds can confound results if not balanced.

**Failure signatures**  
- High standard deviation in single-run pass@1 across 10 runs (>1%) indicates substantial evaluation noise.  
- Large pass@k – pass^k gaps (>10 pp) point to strong dependence on stochastic choices and low consistency.  
- Early median divergence (<1% of tokens) validated by token-level comparisons.  
- Non-zero variance at temperature 0, signaling non-sampling sources.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Baseline variance measurement**: Run each model-scaffold pair 10 times on the full benchmark; compute per-run pass@1, mean and standard deviation.  
2. **Divergence timing**: For every pair of runs on the same task, compute the first token position where trajectories differ; aggregate across tasks to obtain distribution of divergence positions.  
3. **Bounds estimation**: Using the 10 runs per task, calculate pass@k and pass^k for k=2–5; plot curves and quantify gaps between optimistic and pessimistic bounds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How do dynamic context management strategies (e.g., compaction, summarization) affect evaluation variance in agentic systems?** The study only examined append-only contexts; compaction's impact on trajectory divergence and variance remains unknown. Resolving this would require multi-run experiments with varied compaction policies on SWE-Bench and comparing variance metrics.
2. **Does longer task horizon amplify evaluation variance and trajectory divergence due to cascading effects?** The current analysis used moderate-length coding tasks; longer-horizon tasks may exhibit stronger variance but this is untested. Evidence could come from replicating the protocol on benchmarks like WebArena or AgentBench and comparing normalized first-divergence positions and variance metrics across trajectory lengths.
3. **Are the observed variance patterns generalizable to other agentic domains beyond software engineering?** The study is confined to SWE-Bench-Verified; variance may vary with action spaces and task structures (e.g., reasoning, robotics). This could be addressed by running similar multi-run experiments on diverse benchmarks such as BIG-Bench Hard and AlfWorld.

## Limitations
- Generalizability is limited to coding tasks with three models and two scaffolds; other domains (e.g., reasoning, robotics) may exhibit different variance patterns.
- The exact cause of non-sampling variance at temperature 0 is inferred but not isolated; it could involve hardware, software, or prompt stochasticity.
- Token-level divergence analysis might miss semantic differences that appear earlier than the first token mismatch.

## Confidence
- Observed single-run pass@1 variance (std dev >1.5 pp) and pass@k/pass^k gaps: **High**
- Early divergence (median <1% tokens) and its cascading effect: **Medium**
- Attribution of temperature-0 variance to non-sampling sources: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-benchmark replication**: Run the same evaluation protocol on at least two additional agentic benchmarks (e.g., GAIA, AgentBench) to test domain transfer of variance patterns.
2. **Deterministic inference isolation**: Execute agents with fully deterministic settings (fixed seeds, deterministic kernels, no batching) at temperature 0 to quantify residual non-sampling variance.
3. **Semantic divergence analysis**: For a stratified sample of tasks, compare trajectories using tool-call sequences and code edit metrics to identify when early divergence leads to outcome differences.
