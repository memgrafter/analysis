# Do What I Say: A Spoken Prompt Dataset for Instruction-Following

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09881
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09881
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Text prompts consistently outperform spoken prompts for text-output tasks (e.g., ASR, MT), but spoken prompts perform on par or better for speech-output tasks (TTS, S2ST); informal/short prompts underperform across tasks, and performance varies with speaker gender.

## Executive Summary
This study introduces DOWIS, the first human-recorded parallel spoken-text prompt dataset designed to evaluate Speech Large Language Models (SLLMs) on instruction-following. DOWIS covers 9 tasks, 11 languages, and 5 prompt styles, enabling paired evaluation alongside existing text-based benchmarks. Testing on two SLLMs (Phi-4, Qwen2.5-Omni) reveals a consistent modality gap: for text-output tasks like ASR and machine translation, text prompts yield significantly better performance than spoken prompts, with larger gaps in low-resource and cross-lingual settings. In contrast, for speech-output tasks (TTS, S2ST), spoken prompts match or exceed text prompts. Informal and short prompts degrade performance universally, and results indicate sensitivity to speaker gender. These findings challenge the validity of text-only evaluation for SLLMs and demonstrate the necessity of speech-based prompting for accurate assessment.

## Method Summary
The authors construct DOWIS by recording human speakers (balanced gender) delivering prompts in 11 languages across 5 styles: basic, formal, detailed, informal, and short. Prompts are designed to pair with existing downstream benchmarks: FLEURS (ASR, MT, ST, S2ST), MCIF (TSUM, SSUM, SQA), and YTSeg (ACHAP). For each task-language pair, 10 prompt variants (5 styles × 2 modalities) are used. Two SLLMs—Qwen2.5-Omni-7B and Phi-4-multimodal-instruct—are evaluated in zero- or few-shot settings using default inference parameters. Performance is measured via task-specific metrics (e.g., WER for ASR, COMET for MT, BERTScore for text summarization). The core analysis compares scores between text and spoken prompts, and across prompt styles and speaker genders.

## Key Results
- Text prompts outperform spoken prompts on all text-output tasks: ASR (text WER 31.21 vs. speech 35.96), MT (text COMET 81.41 vs. speech 70.97), and ST.
- For speech-output tasks (TTS, S2ST), spoken prompts perform on par or better than text prompts.
- Informal and short prompts are consistently the most challenging across all tasks, underperforming basic, formal, and detailed prompts.
- The text-speech performance gap is larger for low-resource languages (e.g., Czech, Dutch) and cross-lingual settings.
- Performance varies with speaker gender: Qwen2.5-Omni prefers male prompts for summarization tasks but female prompts for translation and S2ST; Phi-4 shows less consistent patterns.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Modality-Induced Processing Gap
**Claim:** Spoken prompts introduce acoustic and prosodic variability that increases cognitive load on the SLLM's instruction parser, leading to degraded performance on text-output tasks.
**Mechanism:** The model must first transcode the continuous speech signal into a latent representation before instruction following. Acoustic distortions, disfluencies, and speaker variability create a noisier signal than clean text tokens. For precise text-generation tasks, this initial representation error cascades into the output.
**Evidence:** Consistent performance drops on ASR (ΔWER +4.75) and MT (ΔCOMET -10.44) with spoken prompts; gaps widen in low-resource/cross-lingual settings where speech representations are presumably weaker.
**Break condition:** If the speech encoder produces text-like latent representations (e.g., via better pre-training), the modality gap may close.

### Prompt Style Ambiguity Hypothesis
**Claim:** Informal and short prompts underperform because they lack explicit structural cues, forcing the model to infer implicit intent, which it is not robustly trained to do.
**Mechanism:** Informal prompts use colloquial grammar and ellipsis. The model's instruction parser, tuned on more explicit prompts, may fail to map these to the correct task schema, especially under speech variability.
**Evidence:** Informal and short prompts achieve the lowest scores across all nine tasks, regardless of modality.
**Break condition:** Instruction-tuning data that includes a balanced distribution of spontaneous, conversational speech commands could mitigate this.

### Speaker Representation Bias
**Claim:** Performance variation across speaker gender may stem from systematic biases in the model's acoustic or linguistic representations, not just acoustic signal quality.
**Mechanism:** The model's speech encoder may have been exposed to imbalanced speaker demographics during training, leading to differential feature extraction or alignment for male vs. female voices.
**Evidence:** Performance correlated with speaker gender for multiple tasks (e.g., Qwen2.5-Omni: male better for TSUM/SSUM, female better for TTS/MT/S2ST). Prompt intelligibility (WER) did not correlate with performance, ruling out simple acoustic quality as the sole driver.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Speech-Text Alignment in SLLMs**
  - **Why needed here:** DOWIS evaluates SLLMs on spoken instructions, requiring the model to map continuous speech to the same latent instruction space as text tokens. The modality gap suggests alignment is non-trivial.
  - **Quick check:** If an SLLM uses a separate speech encoder and text LLM, what failure modes might occur when the encoder produces a representation that doesn't perfectly align with the text-trained LLM's expected token distribution?

- **Concept: Multilingual Low-Resource Generalization**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper finds the text-speech gap is larger for low-resource languages (e.g., Czech, Dutch). This implicates the model's cross-lingual speech understanding capabilities.
  - **Quick check:** Why might a model that handles English spoken prompts well fail on a low-resource language spoken prompt, even if its text prompt performance is acceptable?

- **Concept: Prompt Engineering vs. Prompt Naturalness**
  - **Why needed here:** DOWIS varies prompt style (formal, informal, etc.). The core task is instruction following, but style introduces linguistic variation that tests robustness beyond mere task semantics.
  - **Quick check:** Is a model's failure on an informal spoken prompt due to not understanding the *task* or not recognizing the *linguistic form* as a valid instruction?

## Architecture Onboarding
Input Interface → Speech Encoder → Speech-Text Adapter/Projector → LLM Core → Output Head

**Critical path:** For a spoken prompt, the signal flows: audio → speech encoder → adapter → LLM core → output. The adapter is a known bottleneck; if poorly trained, the LLM receives a degraded instruction signal.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Shared vs. Separate Encoders:** A single encoder for all languages simplifies but may hurt low-resource performance; separate language-specific adapters could help but increase parameters.
- **Adapter Capacity:** A linear projector is lightweight but may be insufficient for complex speech-text mapping; a small neural network adds capacity but risks overfitting.
- **Training Data Mix:** Pre-training on massive text-only instruction data vs. fine-tuning on limited speech-instruction pairs creates tension between general instruction-following and speech robustness.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Catastrophic spoken prompt failure:** Very high WER/very low BERTScore on spoken but not text prompts (as seen with Phi on ASR). Indicates adapter or speech encoder integration/training issues.
- **Gender/language skew:** Performance correlates with speaker gender or language family in DOWIS, but not in text prompts. Suggests encoder bias or unrepresentative fine-tuning data.
- **Prompt style sensitivity:** Large delta between formal/detailed and informal/short prompts. Model may be overfitted to a narrow instruction style.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Modality Gap Baseline:** Run your SLLM on DOWIS for ASR (text-output) and TTS (speech-output) using both text and spoken prompts. Expect text prompts to win on ASR, parity on TTS. This confirms the core phenomenon and establishes a reference delta.
2. **Adapter Ablation:** If your model uses a trainable adapter, freeze it and evaluate on spoken prompts. Performance should drop significantly, confirming the adapter's role in bridging the modality gap.
3. **Prompt Style Stress Test:** Evaluate only on DOWIS's informal and short prompts across all languages. Compare to formal/detailed prompts. A large drop indicates the model's instruction parser is brittle to natural language variation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What explains the performance gap between text and spoken prompts for text-output tasks? The study did not isolate underlying factors such as acoustic variability, prosody, or lack of spoken instruction exposure during training. Evidence required: Controlled experiments using synthetic speech that manipulates acoustic and prosodic features while keeping linguistic content constant.
- **Open Question 2:** Why do informal and short prompts lead to underperformance across tasks? The paper does not analyze which specific linguistic properties (e.g., ambiguity, colloquialisms) make these prompts harder for SLLMs. Evidence required: Linguistic annotation of prompt corpora to identify distinguishing features, followed by ablation studies where these features are introduced into clear prompts.
- **Open Question 3:** Are the observed gender performance differences caused by model biases or acoustic differences in recordings? Without further testing, it is unclear whether models are biased towards certain voice characteristics or if other acoustic factors (e.g., pitch, speaking rate) are responsible. Evidence required: Experiments with synthetic voices that control for acoustic parameters while varying only speaker gender, or analysis of model training data for gender representation.
- **Open Question 4:** Do the findings on prompt modality and style generalize to other Speech LLMs beyond Phi-4 and Qwen2.5-Omni? Only two SLLMs were evaluated; the generalizability of the observed gaps to other architectures or model scales remains unknown. Evidence required: Replicating the DOWIS benchmark across a diverse set of SLLMs and comparing the pattern of results.

## Limitations
- The study demonstrates correlations but not causation; confounding factors (acoustic signal quality, speaker identity, linguistic complexity) are not fully isolated.
- DOWIS has limited speaker diversity (unknown demographic distribution) and may not capture full real-world command variance.
- Critical reproduction details (prompt concatenation logic, inference hyperparameters) are underspecified.
- Evaluation uses zero-/few-shot settings; it remains unknown whether supervised fine-tuning on DOWIS-style data could close the gaps.

## Confidence
- **[High]** Text prompts outperform spoken prompts for text-output tasks.
- **[Medium]** Informal and short prompts underperform across tasks.
- **[Medium]** The modality gap is larger in low-resource/cross-lingual settings.
- **[Low to Medium]** Performance varies with speaker gender.

## Next Checks
1. **Controlled acoustic perturbation experiment:** Systematically degrade spoken prompt quality (add noise, simulate channel distortion) while keeping text prompts pristine. If performance drop correlates with signal-to-noise ratio, it strengthens the modality-induced noise hypothesis over a fundamental representation mismatch.
2. **Speech encoder representation probing:** Extract latent vectors for matched text/spoken prompts from the SLLM's speech adapter or early LLM layers. Apply linear probes to predict task type or prompt style. Low predictability would indicate the encoder fails to disentangle acoustic variation from instruction semantics.
3. **Prompt formulation ablation with human judges:** Recruit annotators to paraphrase each DOWIS prompt into multiple written variants (e.g., informal → formal). Compare model performance on these text-only variants against the original spoken prompts. If formalized text versions recover performance, it isolates the linguistic ambiguity factor from the speech modality factor.
