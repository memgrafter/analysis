# Extending $μ$P: Spectral Conditions for Feature Learning Across Optimizers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.20937
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20937
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: A spectral conditions framework derives maximal update parameterization ( µP ) rules for multiple optimizers, enabling zero-shot transfer of optimal learning rates across model widths.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the hyperparameter tuning bottleneck for large language models by introducing a novel framework based on spectral scaling conditions. This approach simplifies the derivation of maximal update parameterization ( µP ) for a broad class of optimizers—including AdamW, ADOPT, LAMB, Sophia, Shampoo, and Muon—without relying on complex tensor programs. The derived µP rules ensure stable feature learning as model width increases, a claim empirically validated on NanoGPT and Llama2 benchmarks. Results demonstrate that optimal learning rates transfer zero-shot from smaller to larger widths, confirming the theory and enabling efficient hyperparameter tuning using small proxy models.

## Method Summary
The method replaces tensor programming with spectral norm conditions on weight matrices and their updates (C.2.). For a given optimizer’s update function Ψ, they compute the required learning rate scaling by matching the spectral norm of the update to Θ(√(n_l/n_{l-1})). This yields optimizer-specific scaling exponents c₁, c₂ such that η ∝ n_l^{-c₁} n_{l-1}^{-c₂}. Weight initialization is scaled accordingly via σₗ. The framework assumes a linear MLP with batch size 1 and relies on predictable scaling of spectral norms with width. Implementation involves applying per-layer learning rate multipliers (e.g., base_lr divided by fan_in for AdamW) and scaled initialization on benchmark models. Validation includes coordinate checks (feature norms stable), learning rate sweeps (optimal LR constant across widths), and wider-is-better tests.

## Key Results
- Derived µP scaling laws for AdamW, ADOPT, LAMB, Sophia, Shampoo, and Muon using spectral conditions.
- Empirically validated zero-shot learning rate transfer across increasing model widths on NanoGPT and Llama2.
- Confirmed stable feature learning (via coordinate checks) and monotonic loss improvement with increasing width.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper’s success hinges on three interconnected mechanisms. First, spectral conditions provide a tractable alternative to tensor programs: by requiring weight matrices and their updates to maintain specific spectral norm scalings (C.2.), the derivation reduces to order-of-magnitude matching, leveraging well-understood norm properties. This relies on assumptions that random weight spectral norms scale predictably (e.g., Θ(√(n_l/n_{l-1}))) and that activations preserve norms (Assumption 2). Second, the unified framework (equation 5) isolates the dominant term in each optimizer’s update, solves for the learning rate scaling exponents c₁, c₂, and ensures the update magnitude aligns with weight growth. For batch size 1, the rank-one gradient structure simplifies the spectral norm of the gradient, making the analysis feasible. Third, empirical tests on NanoGPT and Llama2 confirm the theory: coordinate checks show feature norms remain stable across widths, and optimal learning rates are invariant to width for all derived optimizers. Break conditions include deviations from spectral norm scaling (e.g., non-standard initialization) or violation of the fixed batch size assumption.

## Foundational Learning
- **Maximal Update Parameterization (μP)**: Defines scaling rules to decouple hyperparameters from model size; why needed: enables transfer of optimal hyperparameters from small to large models. Quick check: "What two spectral conditions (C.2.) characterize maximal and stable feature learning?"
- **Spectral Norm Scaling**: Provides a concrete, order-of-magnitude handle for µP derivations; why needed: avoids complex tensor programs. Quick check: "If a weight matrix is initialized with variance 1/n_{l-1}, what is the expected spectral norm as n_l, n_{l-1} grow?" (Θ(√(n_l/n_{l-1})))
- **Rank-One Gradient Structure**: Simplifies gradient norm analysis for batch size 1; why needed: makes order-of-magnitude calculations tractable. Quick check: "How does the spectral norm of ∇WₗL relate to the norms of the upstream gradient and the previous feature vector in a linear MLP with batch size 1?" (‖∇WₗL‖* = ‖∇hₗL‖₂ ‖h_{l-1}‖₂)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: W_l (weight matrix) -> σ_l (initialization scale) -> feature norms; Ψ (optimizer update) -> η (learning rate with scaling n_l^{-c₁} n_{l-1}^{-c₂}) -> ΔW_l; C.2. (spectral norm targets) guide σ_l and η.
- **Critical path**: 1. Isolate gradient-related term in optimizer update. 2. Compute its spectral norm using rank-one gradient and C.1. 3. Solve η n_l^{-c₁} n_{l-1}^{-c₂} ‖Ψ‖* = Θ(√(n_l/n_{l-1})) for c₁, c₂. 4. Derive σ_l from ‖W_l‖* = Θ(√(n_l/n_{l-1})). 5. Implement scaled initialization and per-layer LR.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Framework assumes linear MLP with batch size 1; transformers with layernorm/attention may alter scaling. Assumption 3 fixes batch size; scaling for width-dependent batches is heuristic. Optimizer interactions: some (Muon) already include width-dependent scalings. Depth-scaling remains unresolved.
- **Failure signatures**: Feature means diverge/vanish (coordinate check failure); optimal LR varies with width; training instability (loss spikes); Shampoo/Muon divergence if factors missing; wider-is-worse trend.
- **First 3 experiments**: 1. Coordinate check: Train proxy model at increasing widths under µP; verify feature norms scale as Θ(√n_l). 2. Zero-shot LR sweep: Sweep base LR on small and large widths; optimal base LR should align. 3. Wider-is-better: At µP-scaled optimal LR, compare loss across widths; expect monotonic improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Depth-scaling theory**: How to derive µP rules that ensure stable feature learning and zero-shot hyperparameter transfer when model depth varies? The paper provides only empirical insights, not a theoretical framework.
- **Relaxing fixed batch assumption**: Can µP accommodate batch sizes that scale with model width while maintaining zero-shot transfer? Currently, Assumption 3 requires fixed batch size; real-world training often uses larger or width-dependent batches.
- **Generalizability to other optimizers**: Does the spectral conditions approach systematically extend to optimizers like RMSprop, AdaDelta, or NovoGrad? The paper covers a broad class but leaves these cases unanalyzed.

## Limitations
- Depth-scaling rules are absent; theory for width-depth interaction is incomplete, with mixed empirical results.
- Batch size is fixed to 1 in the theoretical development; scaling for larger batches is unexplored.
- Critical optimizer hyperparameters (e.g., Sophia’s γ, Shampoo’s δ, Muon’s μ) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Theoretical assumptions (linear MLP, no cancellation, activation norm preservation) may not hold in transformer architectures, potentially invalidating scaling predictions.

## Confidence
- Spectral framework derivation: High (theoretically grounded, uses well-understood norm scaling).
- Optimizer-specific LR scaling laws: Medium-High (empirical validation on benchmarks, but relies on unspecified HPs and fixed batch size).
- Zero-shot LR transfer across widths: Medium (consistent in reported experiments, but depth/batch effects unknown).
- Generalizations to depth-varying or large-batch regimes: Low (lacks theoretical derivation and shows contradictory depth results).

## Next Checks
- Coordinate check: Train models of increasing width with µP; measure feature vector norms across batches. Expect ‖h_l‖ = Θ(√n_l) and stability throughout training.
- Zero-shot LR transfer: For each optimizer, sweep base learning rate on a small width (e.g., 256) and a large width (e.g., 2048). The optimal base LR should be approximately constant across widths.
- Wider-is-better: Train models of varying widths at the µP-scaled optimal LR. Final training/validation loss should decrease monotonically with increasing width, indicating effective feature learning.
