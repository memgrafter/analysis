# When Do Language Models Endorse Limitations on Human Rights Principles?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04217
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04217
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs' endorsements of human rights limitations are highly sensitive to evaluation format, language, rights category, emergency framing, and persona prompts.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) navigate trade‑offs involving human rights principles. Using 1 152 synthetic scenarios based on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights across eight languages, the authors evaluate eleven LLMs via dual response formats (Likert‑scale and open‑ended) under different persona steerings. Key findings reveal significant format dependence, cross‑linguistic variation (higher endorsement in Chinese and Hindi), a categorical bias favoring limitations on economic, social, and cultural rights over political and civil rights, increased acceptance under natural disaster framing, and high steerability. These results challenge assumptions of stable alignment and highlight the fragility of LLM value expressions.

## Method Summary
The study generated 144 unique English scenarios covering UDHR articles, severity levels, and emergency contexts, which were translated into eight languages (Arabic, Chinese, Romanian, Russian, Spanish, Hindi, Zulu, plus English). Each scenario was presented to eleven LLMs under three system persona prompts: default, “champion of individual rights”, and “champion of government authority”. Models responded in two formats: Likert‑scale (1–5) via token log‑prob extraction or sampling, and open‑ended paragraphs. Open‑ended responses were scored by GPT‑4.1 after back‑translation to English. The analysis computed mean endorsement scores, Jensen‑Shannon divergence between the two response distributions, and steerability deltas. Statistical comparisons used Wilcoxon signed‑rank, Mann‑Whitney U, and Bonferroni correction.

## Key Results
- **Format dependence:** Likert and open‑ended responses diverge significantly, as measured by Jensen‑Shannon divergence, with models like Llama 3.3 70B showing poor consistency and Qwen 2.5 72B showing strong consistency.
- **Cross‑linguistic & categorical biases:** Endorsement rates are elevated in Chinese and Hindi compared to English or Romanian. Across all models, there is a statistically significant bias to endorse limitations on economic, social, and cultural rights (ESC) over political and civil rights (PC).
- **Contextual & steerability effects:** Natural disaster framing increases acceptance of rights restrictions more than civil unrest or baseline. Persona prompts cause substantial shifts in endorsement scores (steerability delta), demonstrating high susceptibility to steering.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The observed patterns arise from three non‑exclusive mechanisms:

**1. Format‑dependent value articulation.** Likert queries directly probe token‑level probability distributions over discrete choices, while open‑ended prompts invoke different contextual associations and reasoning chains. LLMs lack a single, context‑invariant preference; output modes tap distinct parts of the training distribution, leading to divergent responses.

**2. Language‑specific cultural framing.** Languages embed culturally inflected value systems (e.g., collective welfare in Chinese and Hindi contexts). Prompting in these languages activates latent associations from training corpora that prioritize social stability or community over individual liberties, yielding higher endorsement of rights limitations.

**3. Implicit negative‑rights bias in training.** RLHF/DPO and next‑token objectives often weight harms from active interference (PC rights violations) more heavily than omissions (ESC rights violations). Consequently, models inherit a hierarchy that protects PC rights more strongly and makes ESC rights more amenable to limitation.

## Foundational Learning
Key concepts that underpin this research:

- **Synthetic Scenario Evaluation**  
  *Why needed:* Enables systematic manipulation of rights‑trade‑off variables (right type, severity, emergency) but raises questions about ecological validity.  
  *Quick check:* What are two risks of using synthetic scenarios for human‑rights alignment testing?

- **UDHR Rights Taxonomy (PC vs. ESC)**  
  *Why needed:* Distinguishes negative rights (freedoms from interference) from positive rights (entitlements to resources), critical for interpreting the categorical bias finding.  
  *Quick check:* Why is property (Article 17) classified as PC while adequate living standard (Article 25) is ESC? What philosophical distinction does this reflect?

- **Prompt Steerability & Persona Framing**  
  *Why needed:* High sensitivity to persona prompts indicates brittle alignment; role‑play fluency may masquerade as value stability.  
  *Quick check:* If a model’s endorsement score shifts by ≥2 points under persona steering, does that indicate unstable values or strong role‑play capability?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Scenario Generator (GPT‑4.1) → Translation Module (Googletrans/Google Translate) → Dual‑Format Evaluator (Likert log‑prob extraction & open‑ended sampling) → LLM Judge (GPT‑4.1) → Steering Injector (persona prepending) → Metrics Engine (mean endorsement, JS divergence, steerability delta) → Statistical Analysis (Wilcoxon, Mann‑Whitney, Bonferroni)

**Critical path:**  
UDHR article selection → scenario generation (with severity/emergency tags) → translation → model querying under 3 persona conditions → response collection → open‑ended judging → computation of metrics and statistical testing.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Synthetic vs. real data: control vs. ecological validity.  
- Log‑prob vs. sampling: deterministic distribution vs. capturing variability.  
- Back‑translation + English judge: enables cross‑lingual comparison but introduces semantic distortion risk, especially for low‑resource languages.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Back‑translation similarity below thresholds (ROUGE‑1 < 0.8 high‑resource, < 0.6 low‑resource) indicates translation failure.  
- LLM judge agreement with humans below κ=0.4 or frequent >2‑point misclassifications requires prompt refinement.  
- Token extraction mismatches (e.g., “ 1” vs “1”) due to tokenizer idiosyncrasies need adjustment.

**First three experiments to replicate:**  
1. Replicate format dependence on a new model family (e.g., Cohere Command) to test generalizability.  
2. Vary the actor from “government” to “corporation” to probe actor‑specificity of the ESC>PC bias.  
3. Test emergency framing with “public health emergency” vs. natural disaster to isolate why natural disasters elevate endorsement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Alignment with non‑UDHR frameworks.** How do LLMs behave under regional human‑rights instruments (e.g., African Charter, ASEAN Declaration)? The study only used UDHR/ECHR, leaving open whether biases generalize to other cultural‑legal traditions.

2. **Causal basis of observed biases.** What aspects of training data distribution or alignment procedures (RLHF/DPO) produce the cross‑linguistic variation and ESC/PC hierarchy? The paper identifies patterns but does not dissect their origins.

3. **Robust alignment interventions.** What architectural or training modifications can yield LLMs that maintain consistent human‑rights alignment across formats, languages, and prompt framings? The findings of brittleness call for methods that stabilize value expression.

## Limitations
- Synthetic scenarios lack real‑world complexity; findings may not generalize to genuine human‑rights dilemmas.
- Machine translation quality varies, particularly for low‑resource languages (e.g., Zulu), risking semantic drift that could affect endorsement patterns.
- LLM judge reliability unvalidated; open‑ended responses scored by GPT‑4.1 without human agreement metrics.

## Confidence
Evidence confidence for the main claim clusters:
- Format dependence: **High** – robustly quantified across 11 models via Jensen‑Shannon divergence.
- Cross‑linguistic variation: **Medium** – consistent elevation in Chinese/Hindi but may conflate cultural framing with translation artifacts.
- Categorical bias (ESC > PC): **Medium** – statistically significant but mechanistic explanation remains speculative.
- Emergency framing: **High** – clear experimental manipulation with effect sizes reported.
- Steerability: **High** – directly measured persona deltas demonstrate pronounced instability.

## Next Checks
1. **Human‑LLM judge concordance:** Recruit domain experts to score 500 open‑ended responses (stratified by language and right type). Target ≥95% within 1‑point agreement; if below κ=0.7, refine judge prompt or use multiple judges.
2. **Translation stress test:** Repeat the pipeline for three high‑risk languages (Chinese, Hindi, Zulu) using professional human translation. Compare endorsement distributions to rule out translation‑induced artifacts.
3. **Actor‑generalization probe:** Generate scenarios where the limiting actor is a corporation or community leader. Test whether the ESC > PC bias persists; if it vanishes, the bias may be state‑specific rather than categorical.
