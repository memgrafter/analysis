# Direct Learning of Calibration-Aware Uncertainty for Neural PDE Surrogates

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11090  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11090  
- Reference count: 17  
- Primary result: XReg achieves significantly lower ECE (0.02–0.06) than MC dropout (0.11–0.37) and ensembles (0.11–0.30) for observed fractions 0.4–1.0, with adaptive uncertainty scaling.

## Executive Summary
Neural PDE surrogates are increasingly used for scientific modeling, but their reliability hinges on well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, especially when data are scarce or observations are only partially available. Existing uncertainty quantification methods—such as MC dropout, ensembles, or post-hoc calibration—lack a direct mechanism to optimize uncertainty against train–test distribution mismatch. This paper introduces cross-regularized uncertainty learning (XReg), which partitions model parameters into a predictor (θ, ψ) and generalization‑noise controls (ρ). XReg updates the predictor on the standard training split \(D_{\text{train}}\) while routing gradient updates for ρ through a held‑out regularization split \(D_{\text{reg}}\). This separation directly tunes the scale of uncertainty to match held‑out data without altering the predictor. Instantiated within Fourier Neural Operators (FNO) with multiplicative Gaussian noise injected either at the output head or internal layers, XReg is evaluated on APEBench under varying observed fractions and training set sizes. Results show consistently lower held‑out Gaussian‑mixture ECE across all regimes, competitive NLL, spatial localization of uncertainty to high‑error regions, and adaptive scaling of uncertainty inversely with data availability.

## Method Summary
XReg learns uncertainty by decoupling predictive fitting from generalization‑noise adaptation. The predictor parameters (θ: FNO backbone; ψ: predictive noise scale) are trained via a mixture negative log‑likelihood on \(D_{\text{train}}\) to capture the conditional distribution of the target. The generalization‑noise controls ρ modulate a multiplicative Gaussian perturbation (either at the output head or within selected internal feature blocks). Every \(k_{\text{reg}}\) steps, ρ is updated using gradients from a held‑out regularization set \(D_{\text{reg}}\) to minimize a regularization objective—either mixture NLL or a moment‑matched surrogate—thereby scaling the uncertainty to match \(D_{\text{reg}}\). At inference, the predictive distribution combines both noise sources, yielding a total variance that is adaptive to the train‑test mismatch. The instantiation uses a 4‑layer FNO with width 8 and 12 Fourier modes; training alternates between the two updates, increasing cost by roughly 20% (\(k_{\text{reg}}=5\)). This generic approach can be applied to other neural operator architectures and noise injection schemes.

## Key Results
- XReg attains held‑out Gaussian‑mixture ECE of 0.02–0.06 for observed fractions 0.4–1.0, far below MC dropout (0.11–0.37) and 3‑ensemble (0.11–0.30).
- One‑step test NLL remains competitive, indicating predictive accuracy is not compromised.
- Uncertainty fields localize spatially to regions with larger one‑step errors, and the learned generalization uncertainty magnitude adapts inversely to both observed fraction and training‑set size.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1**  
**Claim:** Gradient routing through a held‑out regularization split separates predictive fit from generalization‑noise learning, reducing train–test mismatch in uncertainty estimates.  
**Mechanism:** Parameters are partitioned into predictor (θ, ψ) and generalization‑noise controls (ρ). Predictor updates use gradients from \(D_{\text{train}}\) to minimize prediction loss. Generalization‑noise updates use gradients from \(D_{\text{reg}}\) to minimize a regularization objective (e.g., mixture NLL), directly tuning uncertainty scale to match held‑out data without altering the predictor.  
**Core assumption:** The regularization split approximates the test distribution; optimizing ρ on it adjusts uncertainty to compensate for overfitting to \(D_{\text{train}}\).  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.1 formalize the train/reg split and alternating updates (Eqs. 1–2). Corpus neighbors focus on epistemic uncertainty for operators but do not address gradient‑routing splits.  
**Break condition:** If \(D_{\text{reg}}\) is not representative of test shift, ρ may learn noise scales that are maladaptive to the true deployment distribution.

**Mechanism 2**  
**Claim:** Learned generalization‑noise magnitude adapts inversely to data availability (observed fraction and training‑set size), yielding regime‑appropriate uncertainty without manual tuning.  
**Mechanism:** The regularization objective penalizes mismatch between predictive distribution and \(D_{\text{reg}}\). When observations are sparse (low observed fraction) or training data scarce, \(D_{\text{reg}}\) error increases, driving \(\nabla_{\rho} L_{\text{reg}}\) to increase σ_gen. Conversely, abundant data reduces mismatch, lowering σ_gen.  
**Core assumption:** Train–reg mismatch correlates with regime difficulty; gradient direction on ρ correctly identifies whether to inflate or deflate uncertainty.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 6.6 shows increasing mask fraction (less observed signal) produces larger learned generalization uncertainty; increasing train‑set size reduces it. Section 6.5/Table 1 confirm ECE improvements across observed‑fraction and train‑size sweeps.  
**Break condition:** If \(L_{\text{reg}}\) is poorly matched to true test loss (e.g., using moment‑matched NLL when test metric differs), adaptation may be misaligned.

**Mechanism 3**  
**Claim:** Uncertainty fields localize spatially to regions of high one‑step prediction error, indicating selective rather than uniform variance allocation.  
**Mechanism:** The mixture likelihood objective allows noise parameters (ψ, ρ) to vary across input space. Gradients from \(D_{\text{reg}}\) drive higher σ_gen in areas where residuals are large, because those regions contribute more to \(L_{\text{reg}}\). Internal‑noise injection amplifies this spatial selectivity via feature‑wise perturbations.  
**Core assumption:** Residual magnitude is a proxy for local model unreliability; optimizing a location‑wise likelihood encourages uncertainty to track it.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 6.3/Figure 4: uncertainty field varies spatially, concentrating in regions with larger residuals. Section 6.4/Figure 5 (car‑pressure experiment) shows spatial alignment between error and uncertainty “hot spots.”  
**Break condition:** If the model has insufficient capacity to assign location‑wise noise (e.g., only head‑level scalar σ_gen), spatial selectivity may be limited.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cross‑regularization (XReg) principle**  
  *Why needed:* Foundation for splitting gradient flows; without it, there is no mechanism to directly learn uncertainty from held‑out mismatch.  
  *Quick check:* If you mistakenly update (θ, ψ, ρ) all on \(D_{\text{train}}\), what happens to the adaptive scaling claim?

- **Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) architecture**  
  *Why needed:* The instantiation backbone; understanding Fourier layers and output heads is necessary to place noise injection correctly.  
  *Quick check:* How does FNO achieve resolution invariance, and why might that affect uncertainty propagation?

- **Calibration metrics (Gaussian‑mixture ECE, NLL)**  
  *Why needed:* Quantifies the primary outcome; ECE measures alignment of predictive confidence with empirical accuracy, NLL assesses distribution fit.  
  *Quick check:* Why use a mixture ECE instead of single‑Gaussian ECE for a multi‑sample predictive distribution?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Predictor branch: FNO backbone (θ) + output‑head predictive σ_pred (ψ) → updated on \(D_{\text{train}}\).  
Generalization branch: low‑dimensional ρ controls multiplicative noise either at output head (σ_gen) or internal feature blocks → updated on \(D_{\text{reg}}\).  
Data splits: \(D_{\text{train}}\) (fit), \(D_{\text{reg}}\) (regularize ρ), \(D_{\text{test}}\) (report). Fixed, non‑overlapping.

**Critical path**  
1. Prepare APEBench one‑step pairs and define observed‑fraction masks.  
2. Implement three‑way split; ensure masks are fixed per regime.  
3. Code dual‑loop: inner loop updates (θ, ψ) on \(D_{\text{train}}\); every \(k_{\text{reg}}=5\) steps, sample \(D_{\text{reg}}\) and update only ρ via \(\nabla_{\rho} L_{\text{reg}}\).  
4. At evaluation, separate \(U_{\text{pred}} = \sigma_{\text{pred}}^2\) and \(U_{\text{gen}} = \text{Var}_{\omega}[\mu]\) (or \(\sigma_{\text{gen}}^2\) for head‑only).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *k_reg frequency:* Smaller \(k_{\text{reg}}\) (more frequent ρ updates) improves adaptation but increases overhead (~\(1 + 1/k_{\text{reg}} \cdot C_{\text{reg}}/C_{\text{train}}\)). Default \(k_{\text{reg}}=5\) gives ~1.2× cost.  
- *Noise placement:* Head‑only is simpler but may lack spatial selectivity; internal‑layer noise enables spatial variation but requires careful initialization to avoid degenerate features.  
- *Regularization objective:* Mixture NLL vs. moment‑matched NLL; former matches test ECE computation but is noisier; latter is smoother but may bias variance estimates.

**Failure signatures**  
- σ_gen collapses to zero (ρ not learning; check \(L_{\text{reg}}\) gradient magnitude or learning rate).  
- Train‑reg loss gap remains large (ρ updates ineffective; try increasing \(k_{\text{reg}}\) or adjusting \(\eta_{\rho}\)).  
- Uncertainty uniform across space (noise placed only at head; switch to internal‑layer injection).  
- Test ECE worse than MC dropout despite lower reg ECE (overfitting to \(D_{\text{reg}}\); ensure \(D_{\text{reg}}\) is truly held‑out and not too small).

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Reproduce APEBench sweep: run observed‑fraction (0.4–1.0) and train‑size (20–70) sweeps with default config (Table 1). Verify XReg ECE consistently below baselines.  
2. Ablate noise injection site: compare head‑only vs. internal‑noise variants under same regime. Check spatial localization (Figure 4) and layerwise σ_gen evolution (Figure 3, right).  
3. k_reg overhead test: increase \(k_{\text{reg}}\) to 10 and 20; measure train time and final test ECE. Expect small ECE trade‑off for near‑standard training cost.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Long‑horizon uncertainty:** How can XReg be extended to provide well‑calibrated uncertainty in autoregressive rollouts, where errors compound?  
2. **Richer internal noise:** Can XReg instantiate correlated or multimodal internal predictive noise to better capture complex uncertainty structures?  
3. **D_reg sensitivity:** How sensitive is XReg’s learned uncertainty to the size and representativeness of the held‑out regularization set \(D_{\text{reg}}\)?  
4. **Generalization across PDEs and architectures:** Does XReg’s calibration advantage generalize across diverse PDE families (e.g., chaotic systems) and neural operator architectures beyond FNO/OTNO?

## Limitations
- Incomplete experimental reporting: exact APEBench data partition, observed‑fraction mask generation, and internal‑layer noise injection details are missing, hindering exact replication.
- D_reg representativeness assumption: the mechanism relies on the held‑out regularization split reflecting the test distribution; no sensitivity analysis confirms robustness.
- Limited evaluation scope: all experiments are on 1D APEBench; generalization to higher‑dimensional PDEs or real‑world datasets remains unverified.

## Confidence
