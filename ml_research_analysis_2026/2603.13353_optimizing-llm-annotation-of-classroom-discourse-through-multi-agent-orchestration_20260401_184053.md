# Optimizing LLM Annotation of Classroom Discourse through Multi-Agent Orchestration

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13353
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13353
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** Multi-stage orchestration (verification + adjudication) raises F1 scores for complex pedagogical categories (e.g., Revoicing from ~0.30 to 0.52–0.54) and enables non-reasoning models to match reasoning models at 20–25% lower token cost.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the unreliability of single-pass LLM annotation for nuanced pedagogical constructs in classroom discourse. The authors propose a hierarchical, three-stage orchestration framework: initial annotation, model self-verification against rubric definitions, and adjudication by an independent model on disagreements. Evaluated on 800 teacher utterances from 63 transcripts using the Talk Moves scheme and six LLM variants, the method yields consistent F1 improvements over single-pass baselines, with particularly strong gains on the challenging Revoicing category. Notably, non-reasoning models with adjudication nearly match reasoning-enabled models while reducing token costs by 20–25%, highlighting the cost-aware benefits of process design over model scale.

## Method Summary
The core method is a zero-shot, multi-agent annotation pipeline. First, multiple LLMs generate initial labels for each utterance with justifications. Second, each model audits its own output against the official rubric, revising labels if inconsistencies are detected (verification). Third, if verified labels disagree, an independent adjudicator model examines all labels and justifications to select a final label aligned with the rubric. The authors compare this orchestrated approach to single-pass baselines across six LLM families (reasoning and non-reasoning) on a dataset of expert-annotated classroom discourse. Token usage is tracked to evaluate cost-efficiency.

## Key Results
- Adjudication increased average F1 for Revoicing from ~0.30 (single-pass) to 0.52–0.54, a 70–80% relative gain.
- Non-reasoning models with adjudication achieved near-identical performance (~0.60 F1) to reasoning-enabled adjudication (~0.61 F1) at 20–25% lower token cost.
- The three-stage pipeline consistently outperformed single-pass annotations across all model variants and multiple Talk Moves categories.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Self-Verification as Error Detection Loop
- **Claim:** The self-verification stage improves reliability by having each model audit its initial label against rubric definitions before proceeding.
- **Mechanism:** After an initial annotation, the model is prompted to compare its output to the formal rubric criteria. If inconsistencies are found (e.g., labeling an utterance as "Revoicing" without evidence of exact restatement), the model revises its label. This forces explicit alignment between free-text generation and discrete rubric categories, reducing overconfidence in surface-level pattern matching.
- **Core assumption:** Models can perform accurate self-introspection on their own categorical compliance when given structured rubrics. *Assumption: The model's self-critique capability is sufficient to catch a meaningful fraction of its own errors, which is an inference from outcome improvement rather than a proven capability.*
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Describes the stage explicitly; [section] Results show systematic F1 gains; [corpus] Neighbor papers explore similar verification.
- **Break condition:** If verification prompts rarely result in label revisions across the dataset, the audit loop may be superficial or the model overconfident in its initial pass.

### Mechanism 2: Disagreement-Driven Adjudication
- **Claim:** The adjudication stage resolves ambiguous or contested cases by having an independent model synthesize verified labels and justifications, yielding superior final decisions.
- **Mechanism:** When initial (verified) models disagree on a label, an adjudicator model examines all outputs and their textual justifications. It is instructed to select the label best supported by the rubric and context. This aggregates multiple "perspectives" and leverages the adjudicator's broader context window to weigh evidence, mimicking expert consensus resolution.
- **Core assumption:** Disagreement among verified models signals genuine ambiguity that benefits from a fresh, integrative evaluation. *Assumption: The pattern of disagreement is a valid signal of annotation difficulty rather than random noise, and the adjudicator is not itself susceptible to the same systematic biases.*
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Defines the stage; [section] Table 1 and Fig. 1 show adjudication consistently delivers highest F1 scores; [corpus] Adjacent research patterns.
- **Break condition:** If adjudication performance is no better than a randomly selected verified label among disagreers, the process adds no value and may inject noise.

### Mechanism 3: Orchestration Structure > Pure Model Reasoning
- **Claim:** Hierarchical orchestration (verification + adjudication) enables non-reasoning models to achieve near-parity with reasoning models at lower cost, making process design the dominant factor for reliability.
- **Mechanism:** Reasoning models start with higher-quality single-pass outputs. However, verification and adjudication stages systematically correct errors from both model types. The adjudication stage, in particular, appears to "level the playing field" by resolving residual errors from any single model, making the initial quality gap less consequential.
- **Core assumption:** The marginal benefit of a reasoning model's internal deliberation is largely subsumed by external verification/adjudication processes. *Assumption: The token-heavy "thinking" process in reasoning models yields diminishing returns once a multi-stage external audit pipeline is present.*
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] States non-reasoning models with adjudication achieved near-identical performance at lower cost; [section] Figure 2 shows Pareto frontier; [corpus] Weak direct evidence; related work on reasoning for confidence calibration.
- **Break condition:** If a gap persists between reasoning and non-reasoning models even after full adjudication, then reasoning capability would remain a necessary, non-substitutable input.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Annotator Module:** Takes a target utterance + dialogue context, applies a rubric prompt, outputs a label + short justification.
  2. **Verifier Module:** Takes the original context, the Annotator's label/justification, and the full rubric. Outputs: (a) revised label/justification if inconsistency found, (b) confirmation flag if not.
  3. **Adjudicator Module:** Triggered only if *verified* labels from multiple annotator-verifier pairs disagree. Takes all labels/justifications + full rubric, outputs final label.
  4. **Orchestrator:** Manages flow, aggregates results, tracks token counts per stage.
- **Critical path:**
  Input -> [Annotator x N] -> [Verifier x N] -> IF all verified labels agree THEN output; ELSE -> [Adjudicator] -> Output.
  *Note: The "x N" indicates parallel runs with different model instances/families.*
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Token Cost vs. Reliability:** Adding adjudication increases cost significantly; the tradeoff depends on the criticality of the "Revoicing"-type categories for your research question.
  - **Adjudicator Model Choice:** Using a reasoning-enabled model for adjudication gives a small absolute gain (~0.01 F1) per Fig. 2. For large datasets, the cost may not justify this marginal gain.
  - **Disagreement Threshold:** The paper implies adjudication triggers on *any* disagreement. Could a threshold (e.g., only adjudicate if >1 model disagrees) save cost with minimal accuracy loss?
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **High Verification Revision Rate:** If >~50-70% of labels are revised in verification, it suggests the initial annotation prompt is poorly specified or models are consistently making a specific error type.
  - **Low Disagreement Rate:** If verified labels almost always agree, the multi-model setup provides little value; a single high-quality model with verification may suffice.
  - **Adjudicator Mirrors Majority:** If the adjudicator's output almost always matches the most common verified label, it may be acting as a simple voter rather than a true integrator of justifications.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline Replication:** Run the single-pass annotation strategy on 50-100 sampled utterances. Compute per-category F1 against gold labels. Establish the base reliability gap this work aims to close.
  2. **Add Verification Layer:** Implement the self-verification prompt. Run the full annotated+verified pipeline on the same sample. Measure (a) change in per-category F1, (b) proportion of labels revised by verification.
  3. **Add Adjudication & Measure Tradeoffs:** Implement the full adjudication pipeline (using 3 model families as in the paper). For the sample, (a) compare final F1 to single-pass and verified-only, (b) log total tokens per utterance at each stage, (c) identify which categories drive the largest adjudication gains. ## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What fundamental limitations of LLMs or the Talk Moves rubric cause certain pedagogical constructs (e.g., Revoicing) to achieve lower absolute F1 scores (max ~0.54) even after full adjudication, compared to higher-scoring categories like Restating?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper shows consistent relative gains from adjudication across all categories but reports absolute F1 scores in Table 1 where Revoicing remains substantially lower than other categories. The authors do not analyze why the ceiling for some categories is lower, focusing instead on relative improvements.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper attributes performance differences to "conceptual and pedagogical complexity" but does not investigate whether the limitations stem from rubric ambiguity, data sparsity, model capacity for specific reasoning, or inherent subjectivity in the construct.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed error analysis comparing model outputs (including justifications) for high- vs. low-performing categories, potentially involving human evaluation of failure modes (e.g., contextual mis interpretation, rubric misalignment). Manipulating rubric specificity or data sampling for low-scoring categories could isolate the cause.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can dynamic orchestration—where models selectively skip verification or adjudication based on confidence or uncertainty estimates—preserve most reliability gains while reducing token costs beyond the fixed 20–25% savings seen when switching from reasoning to non-reasoning adjudication?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The framework is a fixed three-stage pipeline. The paper emphasizes cost-aware tradeoffs between reasoning/non-reasoning models but does not explore whether stages can be conditionally omitted (e.g., if single-pass confidence is high) to optimize the cost-accuracy Pareto frontier further.
- **Why unresolved:** The study compares discrete orchestration strategies (single-pass, verified, adjudicated) but treats the process as sequential and mandatory. It does not test adaptive control flow based on intermediate uncertainty, which could be crucial for large-scale, resource-constrained deployments.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An experiment where models assign confidence scores at each stage (e.g., via self-assessment or consistency checks), and a policy (learned or heuristic) decides whether to proceed to subsequent stages. Token usage and final F1 would be compared against the fixed pipeline baseline across multiple models.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent do the formulation and specificity of the Talk Moves rubric definitions themselves limit LLM annotation reliability, and could rubric refinement (e.g., adding examples, clarifying edge cases) improve performance, especially for complex categories, within the same orchestration framework?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study assumes the existing rubric (with high human inter-rater reliability) is directly suitable for LLMs. The self-verification and adjudication stages audit outputs against these definitions, but the paper does not consider that the definitions might be ambiguous or insufficiently operationalized for LLMs.
- **Why unresolved:** The framework audits consistency with given definitions but does not question whether the definitions are optimal for LLM interpretation. The persistent gap for categories like Revoicing may partly arise from rubric vagueness rather than model reasoning deficits.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A variant experiment where rubrics are iteratively refined (e.g., with exemplars, negative examples, or disambiguating rules) guided by error analysis, then re-run the full orchestration pipeline. Performance changes would indicate the extent to which rubric design—not just orchestration—is a bottleneck.

## Limitations
- Exact prompt wording, rubric text, and context serialization are unspecified.
- Adjudication protocol details (inputs, decision rule) are unclear.
- Model inference settings (temperature, max_tokens) and possible few-shot examples are not reported.

## Confidence
- Hierarchical orchestration improves F1 over single-pass: **High**
- Self-verification acts as an effective error-detection loop: **Medium**
- Non-reasoning + adjudication achieves near parity with reasoning adjudication: **High**
- The orchestration structure is the primary driver of gains (vs. model reasoning): **Medium**
