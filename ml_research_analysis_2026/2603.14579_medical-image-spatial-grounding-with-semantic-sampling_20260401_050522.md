# Medical Image Spatial Grounding with Semantic Sampling
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.14579
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14579
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** MIS-SemSam improves Qwen3-VL-32B's spatial grounding accuracy by 13.06 percentage points to 66.5% on the MIS-Ground benchmark.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of 3D spatial grounding in medical images for vision-language models (VLMs). The authors introduce MIS-Ground, a comprehensive benchmark exposing vulnerabilities across modality, slice direction, coordinate systems, and terminology. They propose MIS-SemSam, an inference-time optimization that uses semantic sampling to aggregate probabilities over token neighborhoods. On MIS-Ground, MIS-SemSam yields a significant accuracy gain for Qwen3-VL-32B, demonstrating a viable approach to mitigate language brittleness and anatomical prior reliance in medical VLM grounding.

## Method Summary
MIS-SemSam operates at inference time without model retraining. It processes the VLM's output token probabilities for spatial coordinates, then aggregates these probabilities across a semantic neighborhood of tokens (e.g., synonyms or related terms) instead of selecting the single top token. This smoothing mechanism reduces sensitivity to exact wording and anchors predictions in broader semantic consistency, improving robustness to linguistic variation and ambiguous spatial references in medical contexts.

## Key Results
- MIS-SemSam increases Qwen3-VL-32B accuracy on MIS-Ground by 13.06 percentage points, reaching 66.5%.
- The model achieves 77.6% accuracy on slice direction grounding tasks.
- Text-only anatomical relationship accuracy stands at 69.3%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
VLMs exhibit brittleness in medical spatial grounding due to over-reliance on exact terminology matches and strong anatomical priors (e.g., assuming standard organ placement). These factors cause failures with synonymous terms, unusual orientations, or atypical anatomies. MIS-SemSam's semantic sampling aggregates predictions across token neighborhoods, diluting the impact of any single poorly chosen token and capturing broader semantic intent, thereby mitigating language brittleness and reducing prior-induced errors.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Medical Image Spatial Grounding**  
   *Why needed:* Core capability for clinical decision support, requiring precise localization from textual queries.  
   *Quick check:* Query a VLM with "point to the right kidney" in a CT scan; success requires accurate 3D spatial reasoning.

2. **Language Brittleness in VLMs**  
   *Why needed:* Medical terminology is not monolithic; brittleness to synonyms or phrasing causes inconsistent performance.  
   *Quick check:* Swap "superior" with "upper" in identical queries; measure accuracy drop.

3. **Semantic Sampling**  
   *Why needed:* Single-token selection amplifies noise; neighborhood aggregation smooths predictions.  
   *Quick check:* Compare top-1 token selection vs. averaging top-k semantically similar tokens on a probing set.

4. **3D Medical Imaging Slices**  
   *Why needed:* Grounding must handle axial, sagittal, and coronal views, each with distinct spatial semantics.  
   *Quick check:* Test grounding accuracy separately on each slice orientation from the same 3D volume.

5. **Anatomical Priors**  
   *Why needed:* VLMs internalize common anatomical assumptions that fail on pathologies or variants.  
   *Quick check:* Use images with reversed organ placement (e.g., situs inversus) and evaluate grounding consistency.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Medical 3D image + text query → VLM feature encoder → token embedding layer → semantic sampling module (neighborhood definition + probability aggregation) → spatial coordinate output.

**Critical path:** Image and text encoding → cross-modal token alignment → extraction of spatial token probabilities → construction of token neighborhoods based on embedding similarity → aggregation of neighborhood probabilities → final coordinate prediction.

**Design tradeoffs:** Semantic sampling adds computational overhead during inference but avoids model retraining; effectiveness heavily depends on the quality of the VLM's token embedding space and the neighborhood definition heuristic.

**Failure signatures:** Sharp accuracy declines on non-standard slice directions (e.g., oblique planes); high variance when introducing controlled synonym swaps; overconfidence in predictions when anatomical priors are violated (e.g., missing organs).

**First experiments:** 1) Replicate MIS-SemSam on LLaVA-Med and MedVL-SAM2 to test generalizability across VLM architectures. 2) Evaluate on MIS-Ground's slice-direction-specific subsets to quantify orientation sensitivity. 3) Introduce systematic terminology noise (e.g., replacing standard terms with layman synonyms) and measure robustness compared to baseline.

## Limitations
- Findings are tied to Qwen3-VL-32B; generalizability to other VLMs remains unverified.
- MIS-Ground's design choices (e.g., specific slice directions, terminology sets) may embed unintended biases not fully analyzed.
- Absence of comparisons with recent medical grounding SOTA (e.g., MedGround, MedVL-SAM2) limits contextualization of the reported 66.5% accuracy.

## Confidence
- **Benchmark design & vulnerability testing:** High (structured evaluation across clear, orthogonal axes).
- **MIS-SemSam efficacy on Qwen3-VL-32B:** Medium (strong single-model result but lacking multi-model evidence).
- **Generalization claims & SOTA superiority:** Low (no cross-model or cross-benchmark validation provided).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-model replication:** Apply MIS-SemSam to at least three other VLMs (e.g., LLaVA-Med, MedVL-SAM2, BLIP-Med) using MIS-Ground to test generalizability.
2. **Benchmark comparative analysis:** Evaluate leading models from MedGround and VividMed on MIS-Ground to contextualize the 66.5% accuracy claim.
3. **Error stratification analysis:** Disaggregate failure cases by anatomy (e.g., cardiac vs. neuro), orientation (axial/sagittal/coronal), and terminology variants to identify persistent brittleness patterns.
