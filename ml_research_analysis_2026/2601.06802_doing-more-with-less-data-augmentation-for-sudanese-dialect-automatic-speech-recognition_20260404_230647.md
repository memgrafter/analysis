# Doing More with Less: Data Augmentation for Sudanese Dialect Automatic Speech Recognition

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06802  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06802  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Best model (Whisper-Medium with combined self‑training and TTS augmentation) achieves WER of 57.1% in‑domain and 51.6% out‑of‑domain, a ~45% relative improvement over the zero‑shot multilingual Whisper baseline (78.8% WER).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of building Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems for low‑resource dialects, using Sudanese Arabic as a case study. With only ~4 hours of labeled speech, the authors employ two data augmentation techniques: (1) self‑training with pseudo‑labels from 45 hours of unlabeled in‑domain speech, and (2) text‑to‑speech (TTS) synthesis from a morphological corpus. The best model—a Whisper‑Medium fine‑tuned on the combined augmented dataset (28.4 hours total)—substanti‑ ally reduces Word Error Rate (WER) compared to zero‑shot baselines and demonstrates that targeted augmentation can overcome resource limitations for marginalized language varieties.

## Method Summary
The method centers on augmenting a small gold‑standard Sudanese speech corpus (~4 h) via two complementary streams. First, a teacher model (Whisper fine‑tuned on the gold data) generates pseudo‑labels for a large unlabeled audio corpus (45 h); high‑confidence pseudo‑labels are filtered by a threshold. Second, a TTS system (Klaam FastSpeech2) converts a morphologically annotated text corpus into synthetic speech (~4.6 h), despite being trained on a different (Levantine) accent. A student Whisper model is then fine‑tuned on the union of gold data, filtered pseudo‑labeled data, and synthetic speech. The combined dataset totals ~28.4 hours and yields the best results, with confidence threshold choice and model size affecting performance.

## Key Results
- Whisper‑Medium with combined self‑training (confidence 0.7) and TTS augmentation achieves **57.1% WER** on the in‑domain OOOK‑Eval set and **51.6% WER** on an out‑of‑domain holdout set.
- This represents a **~45% relative improvement** over the zero‑shot multilingual Whisper baseline (78.8% WER).
- Self‑training alone yields 54.1% holdout WER for Medium; TTS alone performs poorly (71.6%), but their combination is synergistic for Medium, while smaller models sometimes degrade.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Self‑training improves ASR performance for a low‑resource dialect by leveraging unlabeled in‑domain speech to expand acoustic‑linguistic coverage.
- **Mechanism:** A teacher model, initially fine‑tuned on a small set of gold‑standard transcriptions, generates pseudo‑labels for a large corpus of unlabeled audio. A student model is then trained on the union of gold‑standard and pseudo‑labeled data. The student’s improved acoustic model can generate better pseudo‑labels in iterative rounds, but the paper shows a single round.
- **Core assumption:** The pseudo‑labels, even if noisy, provide a useful training signal that captures the dialect’s acoustic variability and unseen vocabulary, and the initial gold‑standard data anchors the model to prevent catastrophic divergence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Explicitly states the use of "self‑training with pseudo‑labels generated from unlabeled speech" and that the best model used "combined self‑training and TTS augmentation (28.4 hours)."
  - [section 4.3] Details implementation: "Fine‑tuning a teacher model on a cleaned version of the Sudanese dialect Speech dataset, then using this teacher model to generate pseudo‑labels for 17,973 unlabeled OOOK recordings... training a student model on the combined Sudanese dialect dataset and the newly generated pseudo‑labeled OOOK dataset."
- **Break condition:** Degrades if confidence filtering is too aggressive (threshold 0.9 reduces data diversity) or too permissive (threshold 0.7 introduces more noise). Also fails if unlabeled data is acoustically mismatched or if the initial teacher model is too weak.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Text‑to‑Speech (TTS) data augmentation improves ASR performance by synthetically expanding the textual and lexical diversity of the training set, even if acoustic realism is limited.
- **Mechanism:** A TTS system (Klaam, FastSpeech2‑based) converts a morphologically annotated text corpus (Lisan‑Sudanese, 52K tokens) into synthetic speech (4.6 hours). This audio‑text pair is added to the training mix. The value is hypothesized to come from novel sentence structures and vocabulary not present in the 4‑hour gold‑standard speech dataset.
- **Core assumption:** The synthetic speech, despite potential acoustic mismatch (single‑speaker, non‑dialectal accent), provides a beneficial lexical and linguistic regularization effect when combined with real speech. Its primary role is complementary, not standalone.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Notes "TTS‑based augmentation using synthetic speech from the Klaam TTS system" and that the best model used "combined self‑training and TTS augmentation."
  - [section 3.3 & 4.4] Describes TTS dataset construction from a morphological token corpus and its use: "Fine‑tuning Whisper on this dataset... and also performing fine‑tuning on only Lisan‑Sudanese TTS. Additional fine‑tuning experiments combined both pseudo‑labeled and TTS‑generated speech." Section 5.4 shows that TTS‑only training yields poor results (WER 71.6% for Medium) but combination helps.
  - [section 5.4] Explicitly states: "synthetic data is linguistically rich (Labels) but failed due to acoustic distance between TTS output and domain dialect... The primary value of TTS augmentation appears to lie in its effect when combined with other techniques rather than as a standalone solution."
- **Break condition:** Fails if TTS system’s acoustic characteristics (speaker, prosody, recording environment) are too distant from the target domain speech, as evidenced by "prohibitively high 150% WER" for Small model trained only on TTS. Also fails if the text corpus lacks relevant dialect‑specific morphology or vocabulary.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The synergistic combination of self‑training and TTS augmentation with a medium‑sized Whisper model yields the most robust generalization by addressing complementary weaknesses: acoustic coverage (self‑training) and lexical/textual coverage (TTS).
- **Mechanism:** The student model is trained on a tripartite dataset: (1) gold‑standard clean speech (provides high‑quality anchor), (2) pseudo‑labeled unlabeled speech (provides natural acoustic diversity and speaker variation), and (3) TTS‑generated speech (provides novel textual constructs and vocabulary). This combination is hypothesized to create a more comprehensive representation of the dialect than any single source.
- **Core assumption:** The weaknesses of self‑training (limited to vocabulary/transcriptions in unlabeled audio) and TTS (limited acoustic realism) are non‑overlapping, so their combination provides additive or multiplicative benefits without compounding errors catastrophically, especially when anchored by gold data.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] States the best model is "Whisper‑Medium fine‑tuned with combined self‑training and TTS augmentation (28.4 hours)" achieving "WER of 57.1% on the evaluation set and 51.6% on an out‑of‑domain holdout set."
  - [section 5.5] Reports: "The combination of both self‑training and TTS data‑augmentation alongside gold‑standard produced the most compelling results... with SDN WhisperMedium fine‑tuned on SDN‑clean and pseudo‑labeled(con=0.7) and TTS data... achieving 51.6% WER on the holdout dataset, a relative improvement of approximately 45% over the zero‑shot baseline."
  - [section 5.5] Notes this combined approach resulted in "the best overall generalization across all experimental conditions."
- **Break condition:** Synergy breaks if combination amplifies a specific error mode. Paper shows that for the Small model, the combination sometimes hurt performance compared to self‑training alone (e.g., Small with SDN‑clean+Pseudo(con=0.9) holdout WER 52.1% vs. combined 53.2% or 64.5%). Suggests model capacity is critical—Medium benefits, but smaller model may be overwhelmed by increased data complexity and noise.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Pretrained Model Fine‑Tuning (Whisper)
- **Why needed here:** The entire work builds on fine‑tuning OpenAI’s Whisper models (Small, Medium, Large‑V2). These are encoder‑decoder models pre‑trained on 680k hours of weakly labeled multilingual audio. The paper’s hypothesis is that this massive pre‑training provides a universal speech representation that can be efficiently adapted to a low‑resource dialect with minimal additional data.
- **Quick check question:** What is the likely risk of fine‑tuning a large pre‑trained model like Whisper on only 4 hours of in‑domain data without any augmentation?

### Concept: Semi‑Supervised Learning via Self‑Training
- **Why needed here:** Self‑training is the first augmentation pillar. It requires understanding the teacher‑student paradigm: a model trained on labeled data generates pseudo‑labels for unlabeled data, which is then used to train a new model. Critical nuances include confidence thresholding to filter noise and the risk of error reinforcement.
- **Quick check question:** How does the choice of confidence threshold (e.g., 0.7 vs. 0.9) represent a fundamental trade‑off in self‑training, and what does the paper’s result for the Medium model on the holdout set imply about this trade‑off?

### Concept: Synthetic Data & Domain Mismatch
- **Why needed here:** TTS augmentation is the second pillar, but it fails acoustically. This requires understanding that synthetic speech from a TTS system (especially one trained on a different accent, like Levantine) has a systematic acoustic distribution shift from natural, spontaneous dialect speech. The value is in the text, not the sound.
- **Quick check question:** Based on the results in Table 2 (e.g., TTS‑only WERs of 150% and 71.6%), what is the primary failure mode of TTS augmentation when used alone for this dialect?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Gold Speech (SDN‑clean) → Teacher Model (Whisper fine‑tuned) → Pseudo‑Label Generator (for OOOK‑unlabeled) → Confidence Filter (threshold 0.7/0.9)  
Lisan‑Sudanese Text Corpus → TTS System (Klaam FastSpeech2) → Synthetic Speech (4.6h)  
Gold Speech + Filtered Pseudo‑Labels + Synthetic Speech → Training Pipeline (HuggingFace Trainer) → Student Model (Whisper fine‑tuned) → Evaluation (OOOK‑Eval, Holdout)

### Critical path
1. Acquire & prepare gold Sudanese speech (4h) and clean to 3.93h.
2. Train teacher Whisper (e.g., Medium) on cleaned gold data for 5k steps.
3. Transcribe unlabeled OOOK corpus (45.3h) with teacher; compute per‑utterance confidence.
4. Filter pseudo‑labels by confidence threshold (e.g., ≥0.7 yields ~19.83h audio).
5. Generate TTS speech: filter Lisan‑Sudanese tokens (≥25% diacritics → 1,878 sentences), synthesize with Klaam FastSpeech2 trained on Arabic Speech Corpus (~4.6h audio).
6. Combine datasets: gold (~3.93h) + filtered pseudo + TTS (~28.4h total).
7. Fine‑tune a fresh Whisper‑Medium on the combined set for 5k steps, LR 1e‑5, warm‑up 500.
8. Evaluate on OOOK‑Eval and holdout set; compute WER/CER.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Gold vs. Pseudo vs. Synthetic:** Gold is high‑quality but scarce. Pseudo provides acoustic diversity but risks noise. Synthetic provides lexical diversity but has acoustic mismatch. Balance by fixing gold proportion and tuning mix of others.
- **Confidence threshold:** Low (0.7) → more pseudo‑data, more noise, potentially more diversity. High (0.9) → less data, less noise, may discard useful examples. Medium model benefits from lower threshold (0.7) for holdout, suggesting diversity helps generalization.
- **Model scale vs. data:** With very limited data (~7.25h), Medium sometimes outperforms Large on holdout (57.5% vs. 59.7% WER), possibly due to lower overfitting risk. With larger augmented set (28h), Medium still wins, implying capacity not the bottleneck.
- **TTS source corpus:** Lisan‑Sudanese text filtered for diacritic density aims for written‑like quality but may not match spontaneous speech register, risking unnatural sentences.

### Failure signatures
1. **Language detection failure:** Model outputs MSA or another dialect, or empty output. Seen in zero‑shot OpenAI and ARBML models.
2. **Hallucination:** Model generates plausible‑sounding but contextually wrong words, especially in ARBML‑CV‑Ar fine‑tuned on Common Voice.
3. **Systematic character substitution:** Confusion between similar phonemes (e.g., qaf ↔ kaf, jim ↔ sheen) across many samples, indicating phoneme mapping mismatch.
4. **Degradation on holdout vs. in‑domain:** Large WER gap between OOOK‑Eval and holdout set; indicates overfitting to broadcast conditions in OOOK‑Eval.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Zero‑shot baseline:** Evaluate pre‑trained Whisper (Small/Medium/Large‑v2) and ARBML Arabic‑adapted models on OOOK‑Eval and holdout. Goal: establish performance gap; expected: Large‑V2 ~78.8% WER, Arabic‑fine‑tuned models degrade severely on holdout.
2. **Full fine‑tuning (no augmentation):** Fine‑tune each Whisper size on combined gold Sudanese + Arabic Speech (7.25h total) for 5k steps. Goal: measure raw benefit of target‑domain fine‑tuning; expected: all improve over zero‑shot; Medium may rival Large on holdout; WER ~60–65%.
3. **Self‑training (no TTS):** (a) Train teacher on SDN‑clean (3.93h). (b) Generate pseudo‑labels for OOOK‑unlabeled at confidence 0.7. (c) Train student on SDN‑clean + pseudo. Goal: isolate value of unlabeled in‑domain data; expected: Medium student improves over full fine‑tuning (e.g., 57.1% vs. 64.1%), Small shows smaller gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Increasing unlabeled data volume/diversity:** How does scaling unlabeled speech affect self‑training effectiveness for Sudanese ASR? (Future work: explore larger corpora.)
- **Adapting TTS to Sudanese accent:** Can a Sudanese‑accented TTS reduce acoustic gap and improve standalone TTS augmentation? (Limitation: current TTS uses Levantine accent.)
- **Gender and geographic diversity impact:** To what extent do gender and geographic diversity in training data affect robustness across speaker groups? (Limitation: dataset lacks gender balance and is geographically restricted to Central Sudanese.)
- **Cross‑dialect transfer learning:** Does transfer from closely related dialects (e.g., Egyptian, Levantine) improve low‑resource Sudanese ASR compared to MSA or scratch training? (Future work: explore cross‑dialect transfer.)

## Limitations
- Gold data cleaning criteria (4h → 3.93h) unspecified.
- Pseudo‑label confidence calculation method (e.g., token‑average vs. minimum) not detailed.
- TTS training configuration for Klaam on 3.8h Arabic Speech Corpus omitted; synthetic quality may vary.
- Findings restricted to one dialect and TTS system; generalizability unknown.

## Confidence
- **Self‑training effectiveness:** Medium – improvement consistent, but threshold sensitivity and teacher robustness not fully mapped.
- **TTS augmentation benefit:** Low – standalone TTS fails (WER >70%); lexical diversity effect inferred indirectly from combination results.
- **Synergistic combination:** Medium – best outcomes with both augmentations, but small‑model degradation suggests conditional synergy dependent on capacity and data mix.

## Next Checks
1. **Confidence metric ablation:** Reimplement self‑training with alternative confidence scores (e.g., utterance entropy, token minimum) and compare pseudo‑label error rates and final student WER.
2. **In‑domain vs. out‑of‑domain TTS:** Train a TTS system on a small in‑domain Sudanese speech sample (if accessible) and compare augmentation impact against the out‑of‑domain (Levantine) TTS used; correlate acoustic feature distances with WER changes.
3. **Lexical/acoustic disentanglement:** Replace transcripts in pseudo‑labeled audio with TTS‑generated text (keeping original acoustics) to isolate the contribution of lexical diversity from acoustic diversity in the combined augmentation.
