# TimeTox: An LLM-Based Pipeline for Automated Extraction of Time Toxicity from Clinical Trial Protocols

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.21335
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21335
- Reference count: 9
- Primary result: LLM pipeline achieved high stability (82% perfect) on real-world oncology protocols, establishing stability as the key production metric.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces TimeTox, an automated pipeline for extracting “time toxicity” — cumulative healthcare contact days — from clinical trial Schedule of Assessments (SoA) tables. Using Google’s Gemini models, the pipeline first condenses multi-page PDFs into focused summaries, then applies a single‑pass “vanilla” extraction model three times per protocol, and finally aggregates results via a position‑based consensus mechanism that matches arms by relative burden rather than name. Evaluated on 644 real‑world oncology protocols (1,288 arms), the production pipeline achieved 95.3% clinically acceptable accuracy (IQR ≤ 3 days) and 82.0% perfect stability (IQR = 0). The authors conclude that extraction stability on heterogeneous real‑world protocols, not accuracy on synthetic benchmarks, is the decisive metric for deployment, favoring the vanilla architecture over a more accurate but brittle two‑stage alternative.

## Method Summary
TimeTox comprises three stages. First, a summarization model (Gemini 2.5 Flash) scans each full protocol PDF (100–400 pages) and extracts only the pages containing the SoA table and its immediate context (legends, visit definitions), producing a condensed summary PDF (typically 5–15 pages). This reduces cost and avoids distraction from irrelevant content. Second, a vanilla extraction model (Gemini 3.0 Flash, temperature = 0.1) runs three independent times on the summary PDF with a structured prompt that defines time toxicity, specifies six cumulative windows (screening, 1, 3, 6, 9, 12 months), enforces rules for expanding grouped columns (e.g., “C4–C12”) and cycle handling, and demands strict JSON output per arm. Third, a consensus engine groups all extracted arms by (protocol, intervention_type), sorts each group by the 12‑month contact day count, assigns a positional index (0, 1, 2,…), and takes the median of each timepoint across runs that share the same index. This tolerates variations in arm names and minor fluctuations while preserving relative rankings.

## Key Results
- On 644 real‑world oncology protocols (1,288 arms), the 3‑run consensus achieved 95.3% clinically acceptable accuracy (IQR ≤ 3 days) and 82.0% perfect stability (IQR = 0).
- The single‑pass “vanilla” pipeline was selected over a more accurate two‑stage pipeline due to dramatically better real‑world stability, despite lower synthetic benchmark scores.
- Position‑based consensus reduced the median 3‑run IQR from 2 days (raw) to 0 days for the majority of arms; only 3 of 24 multi‑arm groups showed any positional swaps.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The pipeline’s success rests on three design principles that prioritize stability for downstream comparative analysis.

**Mechanism 1: Stability over synthetic accuracy** – Real‑world protocols are heterogeneous; a pipeline producing consistent outputs across runs, even if systematically offset, enables valid cross‑protocol comparisons. High inter‑run variability corrupts relative rankings regardless of any single run’s absolute accuracy. The vanilla architecture sacrificed synthetic performance to gain remarkable stability, while the two‑stage pipeline, despite perfect synthetic scores, failed due to cascading errors from structural extraction variations.

**Mechanism 2: Position‑based arm matching** – Arm names are prone to LLM hallucination and inconsistency. Instead of matching on error‑prone strings, the system assumes the ordinal ranking of arms by 12‑month contact days is stable. Within each (protocol, intervention_type) group, arms are sorted by that count and assigned a positional index; consensus aggregates medians across runs sharing the index. This tolerates name variations as long as the relative ordering holds. The approach proved effective, though arms with nearly identical contact days can swap positions.

**Mechanism 3: SoA‑focused pre‑processing** – Full protocols span hundreds of pages, but only 5–15 contain relevant SoA data. A preliminary Gemini 2.5 Flash call extracts these key pages into a summary PDF, reducing input size, cost, and distraction. This stage succeeded for 99.5% of protocols and improved both accuracy and stability of the subsequent extraction.

## Foundational Learning
- **Clinical Trial Protocol Structure**: The Schedule of Assessments table encodes visit schedules (e.g., “C1D1” for Cycle 1 Day 1) and arm‑specific markers. Understanding this format is essential to map to calendar days. Quick check: For a 21‑day cycle listing “C1D1, C1D8, C2D1”, the calendar days are 1, 8, and 22.
- **LLM Prompt Engineering & Output Structuring**: The extraction prompt forces multi‑step reasoning (cycle expansion, unique day counting) and strict JSON output via temperature = 0.1 and schema enforcement. Quick check: Why use temperature = 0.1 instead of 0.0? To introduce minimal randomness that avoids deterministic “lazy” outputs while maintaining high consistency.
- **Interquartile Range (IQR) as a Stability Metric**: IQR (Q3 − Q1) measures dispersion across three runs; ≤ 3 days is clinically acceptable. Quick check: For outputs [30, 32, 35], Q1 = 30, Q3 = 35, IQR = 5 > 3 → not acceptable; perfect stability requires IQR = 0.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Raw PDF → Summarizer (Gemini 2.5 Flash) → Summary PDF → Vanilla Runner (Gemini 3.0 Flash) ×3 → Raw JSON outputs → Position‑based Consensus Engine → Final Consensus JSON

**Critical path**: Failure occurs if summarization misses SoA pages (all subsequent steps starve) or if any vanilla run emits malformed JSON (retry logic mitigates transient issues). Position‑based matching requires stable sorting by 12‑month count; ties risk positional swaps that cross‑contaminate consensus.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Vanilla vs. two‑stage: Vanilla avoids brittle structural decomposition, trading synthetic accuracy for real‑world stability.
- 3 vs. 5 consensus runs: 3 runs chosen after pilot showed diminishing returns; 5 runs would improve stability by ~2% but increase cost/latency by 67%.
- Position‑based vs. name‑based matching: Position‑based tolerates arm name variation but assumes stable ordinal ranking; may fail if arms have similar contact days.

**Failure signatures**:
- High IQR (>3) on 3‑run consensus → unstable arm (flag for review).
- Zero arms extracted → summary failure or complete parsing errors.
- Unexpected arm count (e.g., summary found 3 but consensus yields 2) → likely position swap or run dropout.
- Impossible counts (e.g., screening days > 12‑month total) → catastrophic reasoning error.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Synthetic sanity check**: Run full pipeline on 20 synthetic schedules; verify consensus matches programmatic ground truth (MAE ~0, IQR = 0).
2. **Real‑world stability pilot**: Process 20–50 diverse real protocols with 5 vanilla runs; compute IQR distribution; expect ~75–80% arms with IQR = 0.
3. **Position‑swap stress test**: On 100‑protocol pilot data, identify groups with ≥2 arms of same intervention type; check if 12‑month counts differ by ≤3 days; if swap rate >20%, refine consensus logic.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Specific failure modes in the two‑stage pipeline** – What precise structural extraction errors (e.g., ambiguous column headers, footnote parsing) cause high variability on real protocols despite perfect synthetic accuracy?
2. **Bias from position swaps** – For protocols with multiple arms of similar burden, how much does cross‑arm contamination via position matching bias final time‑toxicity estimates? The paper reports a 12.5% swap rate but did not measure directional bias.
3. **Generalizability across models and domains** – Are the stability findings and vanilla architecture’s superiority specific to Gemini models or oncology SoA tables? Comparative evaluation with other LLMs (GPT‑4, Claude) and document types was not conducted.
4. **True absolute accuracy and bias** – Without a reliable real‑world gold standard (manual extraction deemed too subjective), the pipeline’s absolute error and any systematic bias remain unknown.

## Limitations
- **Prompt completeness**: Full prompts for summary and vanilla stages are not disclosed; minor wording changes could alter behavior.
- **Dataset access**: The 644 real‑world oncology protocols used for evaluation are not publicly available.
- **Generalizability**: The pipeline’s performance may not extend to non‑oncology trials or protocols with highly fragmented SoA tables.

## Confidence
- **High**: Production deployment prioritizes extraction stability over synthetic accuracy (strongly supported by pilot comparisons between vanilla and two‑stage pipelines).
- **Medium**: Position‑based consensus tolerates arm name variation and yields stable results (evidence shows low swap rates but vulnerability with similar‑value arms).
- **Low**: Pre‑processing via SoA‑focused summarization preserves all necessary context (99.5% success rate reported, but no detailed audit of omitted critical content).

## Next Checks
1. **Stability replication**: Run the vanilla pipeline (3 runs) on 50 diverse oncology protocols outside the paper’s set. Compute IQR distribution; expect ≥75% arms with IQR ≤ 3 days.
2. **Position‑swap stress test**: On protocols with ≥2 arms of same intervention type, identify those with 12‑month counts within ±3 days. Measure positional swap frequency across 5 runs; if >15%, median consensus may mix arms.
3. **Summary extraction audit**: Manually review 20 summary PDFs for missing SoA table pages or critical footnote context; target omissions <1%.
