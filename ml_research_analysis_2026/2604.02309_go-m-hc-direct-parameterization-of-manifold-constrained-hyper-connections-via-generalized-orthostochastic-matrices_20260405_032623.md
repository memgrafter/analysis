# go-$m$HC: Direct Parameterization of Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections via Generalized Orthostochastic Matrices

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2604.02309
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02309
*   **Reference count:** 40
*   **Primary result:** go-mHC provides an exact, O(d³) parameterization of doubly stochastic matrices for learned residual mixing, achieving theoretical minimum loss on synthetic tasks and up to 10× faster convergence than prior mHC methods.

## Executive Summary
go-mHC introduces a novel, exact parameterization of the Birkhoff polytope (the set of doubly stochastic matrices) for manifold-constrained hyper-connections. It replaces iterative approximations (Sinkhorn-Knopp) or factorial searches with a smooth, closed-form construction using generalized orthostochastic matrices. A learned skew-symmetric matrix is mapped to an orthogonal matrix via the Cayley transform; a block-wise Frobenius norm projection then yields a valid doubly stochastic mixing matrix. A single hyperparameter `s` controls the trade-off between computational cost (O(d³)) and expressivity, ranging from the orthostochastic boundary (s=1) to an arbitrarily dense approximation of the full polytope (s→∞). This yields faster convergence, exact constraints by design, and scalable model capacity.

## Method Summary
The core of go-mHC is a deterministic function Φ_{d,s} that maps an unconstrained parameter set to the Birkhoff polytope B_d. Given a hyperparameter `s`, the method learns a `ds × ds` skew-symmetric matrix `X`. It computes an orthogonal matrix `Q` via the Cayley transform: `Q = (I - X)(I + X)⁻¹`. `Q` is partitioned into `d×d` blocks of size `s×s`. The final mixing matrix `H_res` is formed by taking the average squared Frobenius norm of each block: `(H_res)_{ij} = (1/s) * ||Q_{ij}||_F²`. This construction guarantees `H_res ∈ B_d` exactly, with parameter count O(ds²) and computational complexity O(s³d³). The `s` hyperparameter governs expressivity: `s=1` restricts outputs to orthostochastic matrices, while larger `s` allows denser coverage of the polytope's interior.

## Key Results
*   **Theoretical & Empirical Optimality:** Attains the theoretical minimum loss on synthetic stream-mixing tasks and converges up to 10× faster than mHC-lite and KromHC baselines.
*   **Scalable Expressivity:** Spectral analysis shows that increasing `s` expands coverage toward the full Karpelevič region of the Birkhoff polytope; with `s=2`, it achieves near-complete spectral reach.
*   **Practical Parity:** In a 30M-parameter GPT model, go-mHC (`s=2`) achieves top scores in grammar (6.63) and consistency (5.22) on GPT-4.1 evaluation, matching or exceeding baselines with acceptable FLOP overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Exact Parameterization via Orthostochastic Bridge
The Cayley transform provides a smooth, surjective map from the space of `ds×ds` skew-symmetric matrices to the special orthogonal group `SO(ds)`. The subsequent block-wise Frobenius norm projection, `Φ_{d,s}(Q)`, is mathematically guaranteed to yield a doubly stochastic matrix. This creates an exact, closed-form link from an unconstrained O(ds²)-sized parameter space to the Birkhoff polytope, eliminating the need for iterative projection (mHC) or factorial enumeration (mHC-lite). The coverage of `B_d` improves with `s`, converging to the full polytope as `s→∞`.

*Evidence Anchors:* Formal definition and proof in [section 3.2]; complete pipeline in [section 3.3]; theoretical foundation from Nechita et al. on generalized orthostochastic matrices.

*Break Condition:* Guarantees require numerical stability of `(I+X)⁻¹`. For small `s` (e.g., `s=1`), expressivity is limited to the orthostochastic subset.

### Mechanism 2: Accelerated Convergence via Smooth Orthogonal Parameterization
Optimization occurs on the Stiefel manifold (orthogonal matrices) via the Cayley parametrization, which is a smooth, diffeomorphic transform. This avoids the zig-zagging and saturation inherent in iterative methods like Sinkhorn-Knopp (which uses exponentials) and the combinatorial landscape of permutation-based methods (mHC-lite). Gradients flow through a well-conditioned composite function (linear skew map → Cayley → block-projection), maintaining a non-vanishing Jacobian and a more favorable loss landscape geometry.

*Evidence Anchors:* [section 3.2] on norm-preserving stability; [section 5.1] empirical 10× speedup; [section B.9] ablation implicating softmax as bottleneck.

*Break Condition:* The gradient advantage presumes the target mixing matrices are well-represented by the `Φ_{d,s}` mapping. For extreme boundary points poorly covered by small `s`, convergence may degrade.

### Mechanism 3: Tunable Expressivity via Block Size `s`
` s ` acts as a continuous knob between efficiency and expressivity. Larger `s` produces larger orthogonal matrices `Q`, whose block projection `Φ_{d,s}(Q)` more finely samples the convex hull of `B_d`. Theorem 2.8 (Nechita et al.) ensures that as `s→∞`, the union over `s` of `Φ_{d,s}(SO(ds))` is dense in `B_d`. Spectrally, increasing `s` expands the reach into the Karpelevič region, the true boundary of achievable eigenvalues for doubly stochastic matrices.

*Evidence Anchors:* Theorem 2.8 in [section 3.2]; spectral histograms in [section 4] and Figure 3; discussion in [section 6].

*Break Condition:* The O(s³d³) FLOP and parameter cost grow with `s`. For very large `d`, even `s=2` is costlier than KromHC's O(d²). Very small `s` (e.g., `s=1` for d>4) leads to severe expressivity gaps.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Birkhoff Polytope (B_d):** The convex hull of all `d×d` permutation matrices; all matrices with non-negative entries and row/column sums equal to 1.
    *Why needed:* The target space for constrained residual mixing matrices; guarantees identity-like stability (preserving averages) in deep network residual branches.
    *Quick check:* Why does a doubly stochastic matrix preserve the arithmetic mean of a vector, and how does this property stabilize gradient flow in deep networks?

*   **Manifold Constraint:** Enforcing a solution lies exactly on a specific geometric manifold (here, `B_d`) during optimization, rather than projecting onto it after each step.
    *Why needed:* To achieve exact doubly stochasticity by design, avoiding the overhead, approximation error, or non-differentiability of iterative post-hoc projection methods.
    *Quick check:* What is the fundamental trade-off between parameter efficiency and exact manifold membership when comparing a Birkhoff-von Neumann sum (convex combination of permutations) to a smooth manifold map like `Φ_{d,s}`?

*   **Cayley Transform:** A smooth, invertible map from the space of skew-symmetric matrices to the orthogonal group: `Q = (I - X)(I + X)⁻¹`.
    *Why needed:* Provides a compact, differentiable, and surjective parameterization of orthogonal matrices without the need for expensive re-normalization steps (e.g., QR decomposition) during training.
    *Quick check:* For any real skew-symmetric matrix `X`, why is the matrix `(I + X)` guaranteed to be invertible, ensuring the Cayley transform is always defined?

## Architecture Onboarding
Input -> Skew-symmetrization -> Cayley Transform -> Block Projection -> Integration

*   **Critical Path:** The key substitution is replacing the iterative `P = Sinkhorn-Knopp(softmax(...))` or combinatorial search in mHC-lite with the direct computation `P = block_projection(Cayley(skew(...)))`. Correct batched matrix inversion and block partitioning are essential.

*   **Design Tradeoffs:**
    *   `s` selection: `s=1` is fastest but only reaches orthostochastic matrices; `s=2` is the recommended default; `s≥3` increases cost for marginal expressivity gain. Selection depends on `d`, model depth, and task sensitivity to mixing matrix complexity.
    *   vs. KromHC: go-mHC (O(d³)) is more expressive but more costly than KromHC (O(d²)). They are composable: go-mHC can parameterize each Kronecker factor in KromHC to regain expressivity at quadratic cost.
    *   Initialization: The learned skew-symmetric `X` should be initialized with small values (e.g., near-zero Gaussian) to start near the barycenter `(1/d)J_d` for stable training. Large initial norms risk `(I+X)` near-singularity.

*   **Failure Signatures:**
    *   **Numerical instability:** NaNs during `(I+X)⁻¹`. *Diagnostic:* Check max absolute row/column sum deviation of `H_res` from 1; if >1e-5, clip `X` norms or use higher precision.
    *   **Under-expressivity:** Performance plateaus below baseline on tasks requiring complex, non-cyclic mixing. *Diagnostic:* Plot eigenvalue histogram of sampled `H_res`; large gaps near the Karpelevič boundary indicate `s` is too small for the chosen `d`.
    *   **Memory blow-up:** Parameter count much larger than O(ds²). *Diagnostic:* Ensure the learned parameter vector size is `ds(ds-1)/2`, not `d²s²`.

*   **First 3 Experiments:**
    1.  **Exactness Stress Test:** For random `X` with increasing norm (d=3,4; s=1,2,3), compute `H_res` and verify row/column sums equal 1 within 1e-6. Log failure rate vs. condition number of `(I+X)`.
    2.  **Convergence Benchmark:** Replicate the synthetic stream-mixing task (Section 5.1). With identical optimizer/lr, compare steps for go-mHC(s=2) vs. mHC-lite and KromHC to reach the theoretical minimum loss (ε²/3).
    3.  **Spectral & FLOP Profiling:** For d=4, s=1–4, sample 5000 `H_res` matrices. Plot eigenvalue histograms against the Karpelevič boundary. Measure wall-clock time and profile FLOPs to confirm the O(s³d³) scaling trend.

## Limitations
*   **Unspecified Initialization:** Optimal initialization for `X` to balance stability and convergence speed is not detailed.
*   **`s` Selection Heuristic:** While `s=2` is a default, no practical guidance exists for choosing `s` given a specific `d`, model depth, or task, nor a formal bound on the expressivity error for finite `s`.
*   **FLOP Claim Verification:** The stated O(s³d³) complexity is asserted but not empirically broken down by operation (Cayley inversion vs. block norms) for validation.

## Confidence
*   **Exactness:** High. Theoretical guarantee via Nechita et al.; practical success depends on numerical conditioning.
*   **Convergence Speed:** Medium. Strong empirical evidence on synthetic tasks; broader validation across diverse architectures/languages is needed.
*   **Expressivity Tuning:** High. Theorem + spectral evidence confirm the `s→∞` limit fills `B_d`; small `s` limits are clearly demonstrated.
*   **Practical Utility (LM Performance):** Medium. Par with baselines on a single 30M-parameter model; scale and domain generality require more testing.
