# Stochastic Resetting Accelerates Policy Convergence in Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16842
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16842
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Stochastic resetting—randomly resetting the agent to a start state during training—accelerates policy convergence in reinforcement learning.

## Executive Summary
Stochastic resetting is a simple training-time intervention that randomly resets the agent to a designated start state with probability \( r \) at each step, while preserving the learned value function. This paper demonstrates across tabular and deep reinforcement learning tasks that such resetting can significantly speed up policy convergence, particularly in environments with sparse rewards and difficult exploration. The approach contrasts with common techniques like discounting, which alter the optimal policy itself; resetting instead modifies the distribution of training trajectories to improve learning efficiency.

The authors identify three primary mechanisms: (1) truncating long, uninformative trajectories to shorten the chains over which temporal-difference updates propagate, (2) increasing the frequency of goal encounters in sparse-reward settings, and (3) preserving the optimal policy unlike discount factor adjustments. These are validated through experiments in GridWorld, WindyCliff, and MountainCar, showing faster convergence and higher sample efficiency.

## Method Summary
The study employs Q-learning in both tabular and deep (DQN) forms. During training, at each step the agent is reset to the start state with probability \( r \) before action selection; the value function (Q-table or network) is retained across resets. Three environments are used: deterministic square GridWorld (sizes \( N=60,120 \)) with a distant goal, WindyCliff—a cliff-like grid with stochastic downward wind—and MountainCar with an extended left boundary to create a hard-exploration sparse-reward scenario. The primary evaluation metric is the length of a greedy evaluation episode (number of steps to reach the goal), measured periodically during training. For tabular tasks, learning rate \( \alpha \) and exploration \( \varepsilon \) are annealed linearly to \( 10^{-3} \); for DQN, standard hyperparameters (Adam, replay buffer, target network) are used. Reset rates \( r \) are varied to assess impact on convergence speed and final performance.

The methodology explicitly isolates resetting as an offline modification to trajectory generation, ensuring that TD targets are computed from the underlying environment dynamics (i.e., the reset transition is not stored or used for updates). This allows a clean comparison with baselines and ablations, including variations of the discount factor \( \gamma \) and reward structures.

## Key Results
- In GridWorld, non-zero reset rates reduce training steps to convergence and shorten evaluation episodes even though they worsen the median first-passage time of a random walker, indicating a learning benefit beyond mere search efficiency.
- In WindyCliff, stochastic resetting accelerates convergence without changing the optimal policy (final episode length matches the dynamic programming optimum), whereas varying the discount factor \( \gamma \) alters the optimal policy itself.
- In MountainCar with sparse rewards and an extended boundary, intermediate reset rates substantially increase the number of goal encounters during training and lead to earlier achievement of ≤200-step evaluation performance, demonstrating a marked improvement in sample efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Truncating Long Trajectories to Accelerate Value Propagation
**Claim:** Stochastic resetting accelerates policy convergence by shortening the effective chains of states over which temporal-difference (TD) updates propagate reward information backward from the goal.  
**Mechanism:** In on-policy TD control (e.g., Q-learning), value updates spread one step at a time from reward states. Resetting interrupts long, meandering exploration paths, replacing them with shorter sub-trajectories from the start state to the goal. This concentrates TD updates along more direct routes, speeding the propagation of accurate Q-values to earlier states.  
**Core assumption:** The primary bottleneck is the speed of value propagation across the state space, not the initial discovery of rewarding states.  
**Evidence anchors:** In GridWorld (\(N=60\)), resetting worsens the median first-passage time of a random walker but still yields faster policy convergence at moderate exploration rates (\(\varepsilon=0.1, 0.5\)). The final contiguous path from the last reset to the goal is shorter under higher reset rates. Q-table snapshots show value propagating along progressively more direct routes as reset rate increases.  
**Break condition:** When exploration is highly random (high \(\varepsilon\), e.g., 0.9), the agent behaves like a memoryless random walker; the detrimental effect on search efficiency dominates and resetting slows convergence.

### Mechanism 2: Increasing the Rate of Goal Encounters in Sparse-Reward, Hard-Exploration Settings
**Claim:** In environments with sparse rewards and difficult initial exploration, stochastic resetting increases the frequency at which the agent encounters the goal, providing more learning signals per training step.  
**Mechanism:** Resetting prevents the agent from spending excessive time in unproductive regions (e.g., deep traps in MountainCar). By frequently returning the agent to the start, it increases the number of attempts per unit time to discover the goal, thereby accelerating learning when the main bottleneck is rare reward observation.  
**Core assumption:** The learning bottleneck is the insufficient number of goal-reaching transitions to learn a good policy, not the credit assignment problem across long trajectories.  
**Evidence anchors:** In the extended MountainCar with sparse reward, intermediate reset rates significantly increase the cumulative number of goals reached during training and lead to earlier achievement of ≤200-step evaluation performance. Under dense step penalties, the benefit disappears.  
**Break condition:** When a dense reward signal provides frequent feedback, the bottleneck shifts from reward discovery to credit assignment, and resetting offers no benefit or can impair performance.

### Mechanism 3: Preserving the Optimal Policy While Accelerating Convergence (Unlike Discounting)
**Claim:** Stochastic resetting is distinct from the discount factor \( \gamma \): it leaves the optimal policy unchanged while accelerating convergence, whereas \( \gamma \) reshapes the optimal policy itself by changing the effective value landscape.  
**Mechanism:** Resetting modifies the distribution of training trajectories but does not alter the MDP's transition probabilities or rewards. Therefore, the optimal policy \( \pi^* \) remains invariant. Discounting directly changes the Bellman equation, favoring policies that prioritize short-term rewards and altering the optimal solution.  
**Core assumption:** The optimal policy is well-defined and unique for the given MDP; resetting is an external intervention on the learning process only.  
**Evidence anchors:** In WindyCliff, varying the reset rate \( r \) converges to the same final evaluation episode length (matching the dynamic-programming optimal path length \( L^* \)), while varying \( \gamma \) leads to different final path lengths. The DP-optimal paths themselves change with \( \gamma \).  
**Break condition:** The distinction holds for TD-based methods that converge to the fixed point of the Bellman equation; it may not directly apply to policy gradient methods where resetting alters gradient estimates.

## Foundational Learning
### Temporal-Difference (TD) Learning
- **Why needed here:** The paper's core mechanism (value propagation acceleration) relies on how TD updates spread reward information backward through state visitation chains. Understanding one-step TD(0) is necessary to see why shorter chains propagate faster.
- **Quick check:** If an agent reaches a reward state in 5 steps vs. 20 steps, how does this affect the number of state updates needed for the start state's Q-value to reflect that reward under Q-learning?

### Markov Decision Process (MDP) & Optimal Policy
- **Why needed here:** The resetting intervention operates within a standard MDP framework. The claim that resetting preserves the optimal policy depends on understanding that the policy is a solution to the Bellman equation for a fixed MDP.
- **Quick check:** Does adding a probabilistic reset to every transition change the underlying MDP's transition probabilities and rewards? Why or why not?

### Exploration-Exploitation Tradeoff (ε-greedy)
- **Why needed here:** Resetting interacts directly with the exploration mechanism (ε-greedy). The net effect depends on the relative strength of the exploration rate \( \varepsilon \) and reset rate \( r \).
- **Quick check:** In a large GridWorld, why might a very high exploration rate (\(\varepsilon\to 1\)) cause resetting to reduce performance even if it truncates trajectories?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
The architecture consists of:
1. **Agent:** Tabular Q-learner or Deep Q-Network (DQN) with experience replay.
2. **Environment:** The task (GridWorld, WindyCliff, MountainCar) providing states, actions, rewards.
3. **Reset Controller:** Module that, at each training step with probability \( r \), teleports the agent to the designated start state before action selection. No update is applied to the reset transition; learned parameters (Q-table/network) persist across resets.
4. **Evaluation Loop:** A separate environment instance runs a greedy policy (no reset, no exploration) every fixed number of training steps to measure policy quality.

**Critical path**  
Reset probability \( r \) → alters distribution of training trajectories (more start-to-goal segments) → changes frequency and directness of state visits → increases efficiency of TD updates along relevant paths → faster reduction in evaluation episode length.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Reset rate \( r \):* Low \( r \) provides minimal benefit; high \( r \) fragments trajectories, preventing the agent from learning long-horizon dependencies (e.g., building momentum in MountainCar). Optimal \( r \) is environment- and reward-dependent.
- *Reward structure:* Benefit requires sparse rewards where goal encounter is the bottleneck. Dense feedback (step penalty) reduces or eliminates the benefit.
- *Exploration method:* Works with ε-greedy; effect magnitude depends on \( \varepsilon \). Potentially incompatible with directed exploration bonuses that already encourage structured exploration.

**Failure signatures**  
- In environments with deceptive rewards or local optima, truncating trajectories may prevent the agent from discovering a necessary but long detour.
- In partially observable settings, resetting could destroy crucial memory states needed for effective behavior.
- Training instability if reset rate is so high that the agent rarely experiences transitions that do not involve the reset state, leading to poor coverage.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **GridWorld Size & ε Sweep:** For \( N=60 \) and \( N=120 \), sweep \( r \) across {0, 0.0001, 0.0002, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.003} and \( \varepsilon \) across {0.1, 0.5, 0.9}. Measure median evaluation episode length and final path length. Expected: For \( N=60 \), moderate \( \varepsilon \) (0.1,0.5) show faster convergence for \( r>0 \) despite worse random-walk FPT; high \( \varepsilon \) (0.9) shows no benefit or slowdown.
2. **WindyCliff: γ vs r Comparison:** Fix \( \gamma \) (e.g., 0.98) and sweep \( r \). Then fix \( r=0 \) and sweep \( \gamma \). Compare final evaluation episode lengths. Expected: Varying \( r \) changes speed but not final performance (same optimal path length); varying \( \gamma \) changes final optimal path length.
3. **MountainCar DQN: Reward & Boundary Sweep:** Use extended boundary (-1.7) with sparse reward. Sweep \( r \) (e.g., 0, 0.0003, 0.003). Metric: fraction of trials reaching ≤200 steps by 50k training steps and cumulative goals reached. Also run with step penalty and standard boundary (-1.2) as controls. Expected: Sparse reward + deep trap shows clear optimum at intermediate \( r \); other conditions show no benefit or impairment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: How does stochastic resetting interact with policy-gradient methods?
- **Basis in paper:** The Discussion states: "In policy-gradient methods, where updates depend on entire trajectory returns rather than single-step Bellman backups, resetting would alter the variance and bias of gradient estimates in ways yet to be characterized."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only evaluates value-based methods (Q-learning, DQN). Policy gradients use different update rules, so resetting could change gradient variance by affecting trajectory length/distribution, potentially introducing bias or harming performance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical experiments comparing learning curves, gradient variance, and final performance with/without resetting across standard policy-gradient benchmarks (e.g., MuJoCo, Atari). Theoretical analysis of how resetting modifies the expected return estimator’s statistics.

### Open Question 2: What is the comparative benefit of deterministic versus stochastic resetting schedules?
- **Basis in paper:** The Discussion notes: "Throughout this work, we study the memoryless stochastic resetting protocol. Its deterministic limit is fixed-interval restarting... A systematic empirical comparison between stochastic and deterministic restart schedules is an important direction for future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper uses only memoryless stochastic resetting. Deterministic schedules (e.g., reset every \( k \) steps) are common in practice (episode truncation), but their interaction with learning dynamics differs in periodicity and state-visitation patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments in GridWorld, MountainCar, and other domains comparing both schedules across reset frequencies, measuring convergence speed, final performance, and robustness. Analysis of how schedule regularity affects value propagation and exploration.

### Open Question 3: Under what precise conditions does resetting fail or harm learning, particularly with deceptive rewards or partial observability?
- **Basis in paper:** The Discussion states: "When progress relies on identifying informative behaviors rather than abandoning unproductive trajectories, such as in environments with deceptive rewards or partial observability, richer exploration methods remain necessary." Also, in MountainCar with high reset rates, performance degraded.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper shows resetting helps when long unproductive trajectories are the bottleneck, but does not systematically characterize environments where it is detrimental. The hypothesized failure modes (deceptive rewards, partial observability) are not tested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Designing environments that specifically require persistent exploration through uninformative states to reach sparse rewards (e.g., chain problems with misleading local optima) or using partially observable variants of GridWorld/MountainCar. Measure resetting’s impact on success rate and compare against exploration bonuses.

### Open Question 4: What are the optimal reset rate schedules that adapt during training?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred: The paper uses a fixed reset rate throughout training. Results show intermediate \( r \) values are best, suggesting a need to balance momentum-building vs. trajectory truncation. However, early training might benefit from higher \( r \) to encourage frequent restarts, while later stages might need lower \( r \) to allow longer trajectories for fine-tuning. This adaptive tuning is not explored.
- **Why unresolved:** Fixed \( r \) is a simple default, but learning dynamics change over time (value function improves, exploration decays). An optimal schedule could further accelerate convergence by aligning reset frequency with learning stage.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement and test adaptive schedules (e.g., decay \( r \) linearly with training steps, or set \( r \) based on TD error magnitude or visit counts). Compare against fixed-\( r \) baselines in terms of sample efficiency and final performance.

### Open Question 5: Does stochastic resetting provide benefits in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) settings?
- **Basis in paper:** The Discussion mentions: "In multi-agent settings (see, e.g., Ref. [32]), shorter effective trajectories could reduce the exploration burden in combinatorially large state-action spaces."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper focuses on single-agent RL. In MARL, joint action spaces grow exponentially, making exploration harder. Resetting could shorten individual agent trajectories and reduce credit assignment complexity, but might also disrupt inter-agent coordination if resets are not synchronized or if agents have differing roles.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement stochastic resetting in standard MARL benchmarks (e.g., cooperative navigation, predator-prey) with independent learners (e.g., IQL) and centralized critics (e.g., MADDPG). Measure sample efficiency, emergent coordination, and scalability with number of agents. Analyze whether resetting should be agent-specific or global.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter and training detail ambiguities:** Initial learning rate \( \alpha \) and initial exploration \( \varepsilon \) for annealed tabular runs are unspecified, and no fixed convergence criterion or total training steps are stated, hindering exact replication.
- **Reward specification inconsistency:** In WindyCliff, the step penalty used in the main learning experiments is unclear (different values appear in supplementary figures).
- **Scope limited to value-based methods:** All experiments use Q-learning or DQN; generalizability to policy gradients, partially observable environments, or settings with deceptive long-term dependencies remains untested.
