# Quantifying and extending the coverage of spatial categorization data sets

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09373  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09373  
- **Reference count:** 7  
- **Primary result:** An LLM-guided extension (LCXRK) achieves a coverage score of 0.964, significantly outperforming prior TRPS extensions (~0.918).

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the limited linguistic and scene diversity in the standard TRPS spatial relation dataset. The core innovation is a scalable method that uses an LLM (Gemini 1.5 Flash) to generate spatial labels across many languages, then quantifies dataset “coverage” via a similarity-based metric. By greedily selecting new scenes and languages that maximize this coverage, the authors construct a 42-scene extension (LCXRK) that substantially broadens the representativeness of the dataset. The approach demonstrates strong alignment between LLM and human labels for high-resource languages and provides a data-driven path toward globally diverse spatial semantics benchmarks.

## Method Summary
The method proceeds in four stages: (1) Acquire all stimulus images (220 scenes from TRPS, Zhang, LJSP, LCXRK) and define reference spatial labels (e.g., TRPS modal English, LCXRK intended terms). (2) Use Gemini 1.5 Flash to generate a single spatial term for every scene in 23 candidate languages, given the scene PDF and a reference-language label list. (3) Build a scene similarity matrix where similarity is the proportion of languages in which two scenes elicit the same LLM-generated label. (4) Define coverage of a subset S (e.g., TRPS) over a universe U (all 220 scenes) as the average, over all u in U, of the maximum similarity between u and any s in S. Greedily add candidate scenes (or languages) that yield the largest marginal coverage increase. Validate by comparing coverage scores and Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) visualizations against prior extensions and, where available, human-labeled language distance data.

## Key Results
- LLM-generated labels align well with human data for most high-resource languages (binary scores >0.9), validating their use as a scalable proxy.
- The proposed 42-scene LCXRK extension achieves a coverage score of 0.964, a significant improvement over prior TRPS+Zhang (~0.918) and TRPS+LJSP (~0.918) sets.
- The coverage-driven selection identifies specific scenes and languages (e.g., Portuguese, Romanian) that most effectively increase dataset representativeness, outperforming ad-hoc or theory-driven prior expansions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** LLM-generated spatial labels for high-resource languages align sufficiently with human labels to serve as a proxy for dataset expansion.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM is prompted with scene descriptions and reference-language labels, then generates a spatial term for each image in the target language. Alignment is measured via binary (match with any human) and graded (match proportion) scores.
- **Core assumption:** For languages with substantial digital presence, the LLM’s internal representation of spatial semantics mirrors that of native speakers for the TRPS stimulus set.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states binary scores >0.9 for most languages. Figure 2 shows mean binary scores of 0.91 (image-based) and 0.90 (text-based) across 7 languages, and ~0.80 across 21 languages.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A similarity-based coverage metric, computed from LLM labels, can quantify how representative a subset of scenes is of a larger universe of possible scenes.
- **Mechanism:** Coverage(S) = (1/|U|) Σ_{u∈U} max_{s∈S} sim(s,u). Scene similarity is 1 if two scenes elicit the same label in a given language, 0 otherwise; overall similarity averages across languages.
- **Core assumption:** Scenes that elicit the same spatial term across many languages are semantically proximate, and covering these proximities ensures the subset represents the diversity of the universe.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports LCXRK coverage of 0.964 vs. ~0.918 for prior sets. Table 1 shows quantified improvements. MDS visualizations (Figure 3) show LCXRK’s broader distribution.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Prioritizing scenes and languages that maximize the coverage metric leads to extensions that improve dataset representativeness more effectively than prior ad-hoc or theory-driven extensions.
- **Mechanism:** Candidate scenes/languages are scored by the increase in overall coverage they provide when added to the existing matrix; the top-scoring additions are selected for human labeling.
- **Core assumption:** Maximizing this coverage metric (based on LLM labels) correlates with maximizing human-perceived diversity of spatial relations across languages.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes the approach identifies key languages/scenes. Figure 3 MDS shows LCXRK’s broader distribution. A 0.49 correlation between LLM-based and human-based language distance rankings validates language prioritization.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Coverage Metric (Equation 1)
- **Why needed:** Provides a quantitative, scalable objective for dataset extension, moving beyond intuitive or theory-limited additions.
- **Quick check:** If a new scene is identical to an existing TRPS scene in all 23 LLM languages, its maximum similarity contribution is 1, offering zero new coverage.

### Concept: LLM-Human Alignment Evaluation (Binary vs. Graded Scores)
- **Why needed:** Justifies using LLMs as proxies; binary scores check for *any* human match, graded scores check for *typicality*.
- **Quick check:** Why might an LLM achieve a high binary score but a lower graded score? (It matches a minority human response but not the majority.)

### Concept: Scene & Language Similarity via Label Matching
- **Why needed:** Forms the basis of the coverage metric; defines what “similar” means in the semantic space.
- **Quick check:** How is the overall similarity between two scenes computed across languages? (Average of 1 (match)/0 (mismatch) across all languages for those two scenes.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
LLM Label Generator -> Similarity Matrix Builder -> Coverage Calculator -> Greedy Selector -> Validator

Critical path: Generate LLM labels for all (universe scenes × candidate languages) -> Build full similarity matrix -> For current subset S, compute coverage of U -> For each candidate c not in S, compute coverage if S∪{c} -> Select c with max increase -> Repeat.

Design tradeoffs:
- **LLM vs. Human Labels:** Use LLMs for scale/initial screening, but final validation requires human data. Tradeoff: speed/cost vs. ecological validity.
- **Scene Universe Definition:** Using 220 existing scenes limits diversity. Feature-based generation of thousands of scenes would increase breadth but also noise and cost.
- **Similarity Measure:** Simple label matching is binary and coarse. Embedding similarity could capture gradients but loses interpretability.

Failure signatures:
- LLM label evaluation shows systematic low alignment for a candidate language (e.g., <0.6 binary score).
- Coverage scores for new sets do not significantly exceed prior sets in bootstrapped confidence intervals.
- MDS visualization shows new scenes clustering tightly with old ones (low marginal coverage), contrary to metric’s prediction.
- Human validation of “modal response” for added scenes shows low alignment with target terms.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Replicate LLM-Human Alignment:** Run the image-based and text-based LLM labeling conditions for the 71 TRPS scenes across the 7 Carstensen et al. languages. Confirm the ~0.9 binary score benchmark.
2. **Compute Coverage Baselines:** Using the 220-scene LLM label matrix, calculate coverage scores for TRPS alone, TRPS+Zhang, TRPS+LJSP as in Table 1. Validate the MDS 2D/3D stress plots.
3. **Test Scene Prioritization:** Using the coverage metric, rank all 42 LCXRK scenes by their minimum similarity to the TRPS. Manually inspect the top-5 and bottom-5 to see if they intuitively represent “most different” relations (e.g., cardinal directions, “outside”).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the LLM-based coverage approach be effectively combined with a systematic feature-based stimulus generation method to ensure both broad and even coverage of the spatial relation space?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state that combining their LLM-based approach with the feature-based approach of Carstensen et al. (2015) is "a high priority for future work" because the feature-based method can enumerate all logically possible feature configurations.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper uses a term-based method to fill gaps, which does not guarantee systematic coverage. A hybrid method is proposed but not implemented or evaluated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A study that generates a large pool of candidate scenes via a feature-based enumeration, then uses the LLM-derived coverage metric to select a final subset, comparing its coverage against the LCXRK set and analyzing linguistic representativeness.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent does the coverage of the TRPS and its extensions, as measured by the LLM-augmented method, change when LLM-supported languages are replaced by a truly typologically diverse sample that includes low-resource languages?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors explicitly propose using their coverage measure to compare the existing dataset (likely LLM-supported) against a set including only high-resource languages, to quantify diversity missed by LLM-based approaches.
- **Why unresolved:** The current analysis is limited to the ~23 high-resource languages supported by Google Translate/Gemini. The dataset of Levinson et al. (2003) includes languages not supported by Google Translate, but its coverage relative to an LLM-based set is not computed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compute the coverage scores for the Levinson et al. (2003) human-labeled dataset (90 languages, some low-resource) and compare them to a coverage score computed over an LLM-labeled set restricted to only the high-resource languages from that sample. The difference would quantify the coverage gap due to language resource bias.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do LLMs generate labels with nearly identical accuracy when provided with image stimuli versus text-only descriptions of the same scenes?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors ran a text-based control (images stripped, only focal/background object specs provided) and found mean binary and graded scores were "virtually identical" to the image-based condition. This surprising result is reported but not explained.
- **Why unresolved:** The core method relies on LLMs labeling images. If visual analysis contributes little, the methodological foundation shifts. The paper does not investigate *why* the LLM performs similarly.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A follow-up experiment where textual descriptions are systematically varied (e.g., adding/removing geometric descriptors, relational context) to see which components drive LLM label consistency with humans. Alternatively, probing the LLM's internal representations for image-based vs. text-only inputs.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What specific factors cause the large variation in LLM labeling accuracy across languages (e.g., high scores for Chinese/Japanese vs. lower for Korean, Spanish, French in some comparisons)?
- **Basis in paper:** The evaluation results show significant language-specific score variation (e.g., Korean binary score is lower; Spanish LLM overuses 'en'). The paper notes cases of idiosyncratic human responses but does not systematically analyze why LLM performance varies by language beyond suggesting resource availability.
- **Why unresolved:** Understanding this variation is critical for applying the method to new languages. The paper does not test if errors correlate with linguistic typology, LLM training data size, or script differences.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed error analysis correlating LLM-human label mismatch rates per language with metadata like: (1) size of the language in LLM training corpora, (2) typological features (e.g., from WALS/Glottolog), (3) complexity of the language's spatial lexicon.

## Limitations
- LLM alignment varies; binary scores are >0.9 for 7 high-resource languages but drop to ~0.80 across 21 languages, limiting reliability for some languages.
- Reference labels for Zhang, LJSP, and LCXRK scenes are not fully specified in the paper, risking prompt misalignment and reproduction errors.
- The scene universe is restricted to 220 existing images, possibly omitting broader spatial configurations and limiting generalizability.
- The coverage metric lacks direct validation against human diversity judgments; its correlation with perceived representativeness is inferred, not proven.

## Confidence
- **LLM-human alignment for high-resource languages:** High (evidenced by >0.9 binary scores in controlled conditions).
- **LLM-human alignment across all 23 languages:** Medium (scores drop to ~0.80; performance is inconsistent).
- **Coverage metric validity:** Medium (supported by MDS visualizations and correlation with human language distances, but not directly validated with human diversity ratings).
- **Extension effectiveness (LCXRK vs. prior sets):** Medium (coverage scores are higher, but human validation revealed several added scenes where LLM labels diverged from native speaker intuitions).

## Next Checks
1. **Full alignment audit:** For each of the 23 target languages, gather human spatial labels for the TRPS scenes and compute binary/graded agreement with Gemini; confirm that only languages meeting a strict threshold (e.g., binary >0.85) are used for LLM-driven expansion.
2. **Human coverage judgment study:** Recruit participants to view scene subsets (TRPS, Zhang, LJSP, LCXRK) and rate their perceived coverage of spatial relations; analyze correlation between these ratings and the proposed coverage metric.
3. **Scalability stress test:** Expand the universe to >500 scenes (e.g., generated via text-to-image models and LLM-proposed spatial descriptors), then re-run the greedy selection to verify that the metric still yields substantial coverage improvements over random baselines.
