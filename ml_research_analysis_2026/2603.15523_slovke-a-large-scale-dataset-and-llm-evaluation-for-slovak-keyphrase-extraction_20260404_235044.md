# SlovKE: A Large-Scale Dataset and LLM Evaluation for Slovak Keyphrase Extraction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15523
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15523
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Constructed SlovKE (227k abstracts), found extractive methods fail on Slovak morphology (11.6% exact vs 51.5% partial F1@6); KeyLLM narrows gap to 15.2% vs 49.1%.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the lack of evaluation resources for keyphrase extraction in morphologically rich, low-resource languages by introducing **SlovKE**, a large-scale dataset of 227,432 cleaned Slovak scientific abstracts with author-assigned keyphrases. Evaluating standard extractive models (YAKE, TextRank, KeyBERT) and a generative approach (KeyLLM with GPT-3.5-turbo), the study reveals a severe performance collapse for extractive methods on exact-match metrics (max 11.6% F1@6) versus partial-match (up to 51.5%), caused by inflectional morphology. The generative model partially overcomes this by producing canonical keyphrase forms, narrowing the gap. The work highlights morphological mismatch as a core failure mode for extractive methods in inflected languages and releases the dataset and code to spur further research.

## Method Summary
The authors built **SlovKE** by scraping and cleaning the Slovak Central Register of Theses (CRZP), removing duplicates, recovering keyphrases from abstract text, filtering non-Slovak/malformed entries, and normalizing keyphrases via POS-aware lemmatization. The final dataset splits into train (182,745), validation (22,343), and test (22,344). Baselines included unsupervised extractive methods (YAKE, TextRank, KeyBERT) configured for Slovak. The generative **KeyLLM** used prompted GPT-3.5-turbo, with an optional clustering step to reduce API cost. All models were evaluated on *F1@6* using both **exact-match** (strict string equality after lemmatization) and **partial-match** (token overlap) metrics. A manual evaluation on 100 documents measured relevance beyond exact matches.

## Key Results
- SlovKE provides 227,432 Slovak abstracts—a 25× scale-up over prior Slovak KPE datasets.
- Unsupervised extractive baselines achieve at most **11.6% exact-match F1@6**, with a large gap to **partial-match F1@6 (up to 51.5%)**.
- KeyLLM achieves **~15.2% exact-match F1@6** and **~49.1% partial-match**, narrowing the gap by generating canonical forms.
- Manual evaluation (κ=0.61) confirms KeyLLM identifies relevant concepts missed by exact matching, pinpointing morphological mismatch as the primary extractive failure.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Standard extractive keyphrase extraction models fail on morphologically rich languages because they cannot match inflected surface forms in the text to the canonical keyphrases assigned by authors.
- **Mechanism:** Extractive models (YAKE, TextRank, KeyBERT) select n-grams directly from the input text. In Slovak, nouns/adjectives inflect for case, number, and gender. An author-assigned keyphrase like "Rozvojový potenciál" (nominative) may appear in the abstract only as "rozvojového potenciálu" (genitive). Since extractive methods output the surface form they extract, an exact string match with the canonical keyphrase fails unless the exact same inflection appears in the gold list.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "...large gap to partial-match F1@6 (up to 51.5%), due to morphological mismatch between inflected text and canonical keyphrases." [section 6.1] "The gap between exact and partial matching is striking: YAKE’s exact F1@6 of 11.6 vs. partial F1@6 of 40.6... reflects the difficulty of matching inflected surface forms to author-assigned keyphrases."
- **Break condition:** If the language has minimal inflection (e.g., English) or if gold keyphrases are provided in the exact inflected forms found in the text.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Generative LLM-based keyphrase extraction (KeyLLM) narrows the exact-partial matching gap because it can generate canonical forms, decoupling keyphrase selection from surface form presence.
- **Mechanism:** KeyLLM uses a prompted LLM (GPT-3.5-turbo) to generate keyphrases from the document's semantic content. The model is not constrained to copy surface tokens; it can produce the lemma or standard citation form (e.g., "Rozvojový potenciál") even if the text only contains an inflected variant (e.g., "rozvojového potenciálu"). This aligns the output form more closely with the canonical author-assigned keyphrases.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "KeyLLM achieved ~15.2% exact-match F1@6 and ~49.1% partial-match, narrowing this gap by producing canonical forms." [section 6.2] "KeyLLM’s exact-match F1@6 (~15.2) is substantially higher than the best extractive baseline (YAKE, 11.6)... This pattern provides direct evidence that generative models are more robust to morphological inflection..."
- **Break condition:** If the LLM lacks adequate coverage of the language's morphology or domain terminology, or if the prompt does not instruct form normalization.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The exact-partial matching gap is a diagnostic metric for evaluating keyphrase systems on morphologically rich languages, as it quantifies the penalty exact matching incurs from inflectional variation.
- **Mechanism:** Exact matching requires string identity after any preprocessing (e.g., lemmatization). Partial matching accepts any token overlap. The difference between the two scores approximates the proportion of correctly identified concepts that are penalized solely due to form mismatch. A large gap indicates the evaluation is systematically underestimating extractive model quality due to morphology.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Unsupervised baselines achieved at most 11.6% exact-match F1@6, with a large gap to partial-match F1@6 (up to 51.5%), due to morphological mismatch..." [section 6.1] "We argue that this exact–partial gap is a diagnostic metric... it quantifies the degree to which morphological inflection distorts automated evaluation."
- **Break condition:** If the language has negligible morphology, the gap should be minimal. If gold keyphrases are already in inflected forms matching the text, the gap would vanish.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Source (CRZP) -> Cleaning Pipeline -> Dataset (SlovKE) -> Baseline Models (YAKE, TextRank, KeyBERT) -> KeyLLM System (GPT-3.5-turbo + optional clustering) -> Evaluation Harness (Exact/Partial F1@6) -> Manual Evaluation Protocol.
- **Critical path:** Raw CRZP scrape → Multi-stage cleaning → Dataset release → Model inference (baselines + KeyLLM) → Automated metrics → Sub-sample manual eval → Analysis of exact/partial gap & failure modes.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Exact vs. Partial Matching: Exact is strict but fair for canonical form; partial is lenient but may reward fragments. Both used to diagnose morphology issues.
  - Embedding-based Clustering for KeyLLM: Reduces API cost but risks losing topic-specific keyphrases if threshold is too low. Optimal at high threshold (0.90) or no clustering.
  - N-gram range for extractive models: Larger range increases recall risk but introduces noise (wrong granularity). Paper uses (1,2) for main eval.
  - Prompt language: Slovak vs. English prompt showed no significant difference, allowing use of cheaper English prompt.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Extractive models (YAKE/TextRank/KeyBERT): High exact-partial gap. Often extract correct concept but in wrong inflected form (morphological mismatch). Also: semantic redundancy (multiple surface variants), wrong granularity (irrelevant trigrams).
  - KeyLLM: Lower exact-partial gap, but primary failure: "unmotivated adjective extraction" (standalone adjectives lacking topical specificity). Precision drops at higher k.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate baseline gap: Run YAKE, TextRank, KeyBERT on SlovKE's Test22K. Compute Exact F1@6 and Partial F1@6. Verify the ~40-51 point gap.
  2. Run KeyLLM with/without clustering: Execute KeyLLM prompt with GPT-3.5-turbo on a 1k sample. Compare scores using no clustering vs. clustering with SlovakBERT (threshold 0.90). Measure cost and F1.
  3. Manual spot-check of mismatch cases: Randomly sample 20 documents from Test22K where an extractive model's top prediction had high partial match but zero exact match. Annotate semantic equivalence of predicted phrase to a gold keyphrase (just inflected).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How should evaluation metrics for keyphrase extraction be redesigned to account for morphological variation in inflected languages like Slovak?
  - **Basis:** The paper identifies the large gap between exact-match (11.6% F1@6) and partial-match (51.5% F1@6) F1 scores as a fundamental evaluation challenge caused by morphological inflection. It explicitly states that "developing morphology-aware evaluation protocols is an important direction for the field."
  - **Why unresolved:** Current metrics either penalize correct concepts expressed in different inflected forms (exact match) or over-reward partial token overlap (partial match), leading to systematic underestimation of model quality. No alternative metrics are proposed or tested.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** New metrics that combine lemmatization, semantic similarity (e.g., embedding-based matching), or morphological normalization could be developed and validated against human judgments on a diverse set of inflected languages.
- **Question 2:** Can supervised fine-tuning of Slovak-specific language models (e.g., SlovakBERT) on the SlovKE training set significantly surpass the performance of both unsupervised baselines and the prompting-based KeyLLM approach?
  - **Basis:** The limitations section explicitly states that "supervised fine-tuning of sequence labeling or generative models... is the most direct path to improved performance" and notes that the dataset provides over 180,000 training documents for this purpose.
  - **Why unresolved:** The study only evaluated unsupervised extractive methods and a prompting-based LLM approach. No supervised models were trained, leaving the upper bound of performance on this dataset unknown.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Fine-tuning SlovakBERT (or similar) for sequence labeling or sequence-to-sequence keyphrase generation on the SlovKE training split, then evaluating exact-match and partial-match F1@6 on the test set compared to the reported baselines.
- **Question 3:** To what extent do the observed failure modes (morphological mismatch, wrong granularity) and the effectiveness of KeyLLM generalize beyond Slovak theses to other document types (e.g., journal articles, conference papers) and other Slavic languages?
  - **Basis:** The limitations section explicitly notes that the dataset "draws exclusively from theses and dissertations" and may not "capture the full diversity of Slovak academic writing." It also states that the morphological mismatch finding is "relevant to other inflected languages."
  - **Why unresolved:** The dataset's exclusive source (thesis registry) and single-language focus (Slovak) limit claims about generalizability. Domain-specific genre conventions and morphological complexity variations across languages could alter results.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Constructing or evaluating on comparable datasets for other Slavic languages (e.g., Czech, Polish) and other academic genres (e.g., journal abstracts) would test the portability of both the failure analysis and the KeyLLM advantage.
- **Question 4:** How reliable are manual keyphrase relevance judgments in morphologically rich languages, and what is the minimum scale of manual evaluation needed to produce stable, generalizable insights about model performance?
  - **Basis:** The paper conducted manual evaluation on only 100 documents with two annotators (κ=0.61). The limitations section explicitly states that "scaling this evaluation—potentially through crowdsourcing or active-learning-based annotation—would strengthen the generalizability of our findings."
  - **Why unresolved:** The small sample size and single-language, single-domain focus raise questions about the robustness of the observed patterns (e.g., KeyLLM capturing additional relevant concepts). Inter-annotator agreement, while substantial, leaves room for subjectivity in judging morphological variants and conceptual adequacy.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** A larger-scale manual evaluation (e.g., thousands of documents) across multiple domains and with multiple annotators per document, measuring how evaluation metrics stabilize with increased sample size and how judgments correlate with automated metrics.

## Limitations
- **Unknown baseline hyperparameters:** Full configuration details (e.g., YAKE’s `deduplication_threshold`, TextRank’s window size) are not documented, risking deviation from reported results.
- **Lemmatization implementation ambiguity:** The exact pipeline (Stanza model variant, case handling) for normalizing predictions and gold keyphrases is unspecified, critically affecting exact-match scores.
- **Dataset noise:** Residual issues include mixed-language abstracts, appended keyphrases, and institutional variability in missing keyphrases, which may skew model performance.
- **LLM linguistic coverage assumption:** KeyLLM’s advantage assumes GPT-3.5-turbo has sufficient Slovak morphological knowledge; this may not hold for low-resource dialects or niche terminology.

## Confidence
- **High:** Extractive models fail due to morphological mismatch (exact–partial gap). Supported by consistent score divergences and manual analysis.
- **Medium:** Generative LLMs (KeyLLM) mitigate this by producing canonical forms. Evidence is strong but tied to a single LLM (GPT-3.5-turbo) and prompt design.
- **High:** The exact–partial gap is a valid diagnostic for morphological richness. Theoretically sound and empirically demonstrated.
