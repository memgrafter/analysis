# Learning to Assist: Physics-Grounded Human-Human Control via Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.11346
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11346
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: First method to successfully track close-contact assistive human-human interactions in physics simulation, achieving up to 85.8% success on HHI-Assist and robust performance under unseen recipient dynamics.

## Executive Summary
Assistive close-contact motions like lifting and supporting involve forceful physical exchanges that are challenging for kinematic motion imitation, which ignores reactive forces. This paper introduces AssistMimic, a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) framework that jointly trains a Supporter and a Recipient within a physics simulator. The system initializes both agents from a single-person motion prior (PHC), uses dynamic reference retargeting to adapt the Supporter’s hand goals to the Recipient’s current pose, and employs contact-promoting rewards to prioritize physical support over strict trajectory tracking. As a result, AssistMimic achieves state-of-the-art success rates on two assistive interaction benchmarks (Inter-X and HHI-Assist) and maintains robustness when the Recipient’s physical parameters change. Ablations confirm that all three design components are critical for performance.

## Method Summary
AssistMimic formulates assistive interaction as a partially observable Markov game where the Supporter and Recipient are co-trained via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). The Recipient is artificially impaired by reducing its PD gains and torque limits, simulating a need for assistance. Both agents receive proprioceptive states and an assistive state that includes partner observations, contact flags, and estimated forces. The Supporter’s reference hand trajectories are dynamically retargeted: when the agents are within a distance threshold, the Supporter’s goal is offset from the Recipient’s current joint positions rather than fixed global positions, ensuring the hands move to support the Recipient’s real-time pose. Additionally, a contact-promoting reward replaces the strict tracking penalty when the Supporter’s hands are near the Recipient, encouraging application of force and ignoring noisy reference trajectories. Policies are initialized from a pre-trained single-human tracking controller (PHC) by zero-padding the assistive inputs, which stabilizes exploration. Training proceeds jointly, with the Supporter’s reward partially coupled to the Recipient’s success, promoting bidirectional adaptation. After training specialists on motion clips, a generalist policy is distilled via DAgger.

## Key Results
- Specialist policies achieve success rates of 74.9% on Inter-X and 85.8% on HHI-Assist, significantly outperforming sequential training baselines.
- The method remains robust under altered Recipient dynamics: e.g., 57.9% success when Recipient mass increased by 50% on Inter-X.
- Ablations show necessity of all key components: without weight initialization training fails entirely (0% SR), without dynamic retargeting alignment fails (high MPJPE), and without contact reward robustness drops sharply.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Joint multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) enables reactive coordination where both agents co-adapt during physically entangled interactions.
- **Mechanism:** The Supporter and Recipient policies are optimized simultaneously in a shared physics simulator. The Recipient’s dynamics are artificially weakened (reduced PD gains, torque limits) to simulate impairment, forcing the Supporter to learn supportive behaviors. The Supporter’s reward is explicitly coupled to the Recipient’s success, encouraging bidirectional adaptation.
- **Core assumption:** The Recipient cannot perform the assistive motion independently due to physical constraints, so its policy must learn to receive support rather than replaying a fixed trajectory.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract statement on joint training; Section 3.1 defines the MARL MDP with asymmetric dynamics (κ); Section 4.1 shows sequential training underperforms (62.4% vs 74.9% SR on Inter‑X).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Initializing policies from a single‑person motion prior (PHC) stabilizes RL exploration by transferring essential locomotion skills.
- **Mechanism:** Weights from a pre‑trained single‑agent tracking controller are copied for the proprioceptive input branch, while the new assistive‑state inputs are zero‑padded. This preserves the prior’s locomotion behavior while allowing gradual adaptation to interaction cues.
- **Core assumption:** Basic whole‑body locomotion skills are transferable from single‑person to assistive contexts and provide a sufficient bootstrap for MARL.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions prior transfer improves exploration; Section 3.2 details zero‑padding initialization; Table 2 ablation shows 0% success without initialization on Inter‑X.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Dynamic reference retargeting and contact‑promoting rewards together ensure physically meaningful support under noisy, occluded reference trajectories and Recipient deviations.
- **Mechanism:** (a) Retargeting: When agents are proximal, the Supporter’s hand reference positions are offset from the Recipient’s current body joints (rather than fixed global targets). (b) Contact reward: When hands are near the Recipient, strict tracking penalties are replaced with a term that incentivizes measured contact forces, ignoring noisy hand trajectories.
- **Core assumption:** Kinematic motion capture of close‑contact interactions is noisy/occluded; fixed reference tracking causes misalignment and unstable contacts.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract describes dynamic retargeting; Sections 3.3/3.4 formalize retargeting (Eq. 9) and contact reward (Eq. 11); Ablations in Table 3 show performance drop without retargeting on HHI‑Assist and without contact reward under dynamics changes.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Proprioceptive and assistive state inputs → Actor/Critic MLP (for each agent) → PD target joint angles.  
  Modules: Weight initialization (copy PHC weights, zero‑pad), Dynamic reference retargeting (gate by root distance, adjust hand goals), Contact-promoting reward (online during rollouts).
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Load pre‑trained PHC weights → zero‑pad for assistive inputs.  
  2. Collect rollouts with Physical State Initialization (PSI) to avoid penetrations.  
  3. Compute retargeted hand goals (if proximal) and contact rewards.  
  4. Perform PPO update (both agents’ critic and actor) with coupled rewards.  
  5. Repeat for 2k iterations (specialist) → distill generalist via DAgger.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Joint MARL vs sequential: Joint enables co‑adaptation but heavier compute.  
  - Retargeting threshold τ_dist: Lower values increase stability but reduce adaptation range.  
  - Contact reward scaling (α, β): Balances force application vs. tracking fidelity; may require per‑dataset tuning (e.g., HHI‑Assist uses α_torque=0.5, Inter‑X=0.0).
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - 0% success rate without weight initialization (training collapse).  
  - High MPJPE when retargeting disabled on bed/chair tasks (misaligned support).  
  - Early terminations without contact reward (unstable contacts).  
  - Poor generalization if Recipient motion range exceeds retargeting’s relative‑offset assumption.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Specialist evaluation:** Train/test on subject‑clustered motion clips from Inter‑X/HHI‑Assist; measure success rate and MPJPE.  
  2. **Ablation study:** Remove weight initialization, retargeting, or contact rewards individually; quantify impact.  
  3. **Robustness test:** Alter Recipient dynamics at test time (mass ×1.2/1.5, PD ×0.5, hip torque ×0.5) and measure success rate drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can we achieve tighter integration between motion planning and tracking control for assistive human‑human interactions to enable adaptive, real‑time coordination?  
**Basis in paper:** Explicitly listed in the limitations section.  
**Why unresolved:** The current approach separates high‑level motion planning (e.g., from diffusion models or reference trajectories) from low‑level tracking, limiting dynamic adjustment based on physical feedback.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A unified architecture that jointly optimizes planning and control, tested on benchmark assistive tasks with improved success rates, robustness, and natural adaptation to partner dynamics.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How can visual observations of the interaction partner be incorporated into the control policy to reduce reliance on ground‑truth state information?  
**Basis in paper:** Mentioned as a key challenge in the limitations.  
**Why unresolved:** The method assumes access to precise partner state (joint positions, velocities), which is unavailable in real‑world deployment; using camera input would make the system applicable to actual human‑robot assistance.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Integration of a vision encoder (e.g., CNN or pose estimator) into the policy, demonstrating comparable performance to the state‑based version using only visual input, possibly with domain adaptation to handle perception noise.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** How can the learned assistive policies be successfully transferred from simulation to real humanoid robots?  
**Basis in paper:** Identified as a major challenge in the limitations.  
**Why unresolved:** Simulation abstracts real‑world factors (actuator latency, unmodeled contact, sensor noise), causing a sim‑to‑real gap.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Successful deployment on a real humanoid platform performing assistive tasks, using sim‑to‑real techniques (domain randomization, system identification, residual control), with metrics showing comparable success rates to simulation.

## Limitations
- Missing simulator and contact force extraction details (e.g., finger‑joint aggregation thresholds, smoothing) hinder faithful reproduction of the contact‑promoting reward.
- Omitted critical hyperparameters and architectural details (network sizes, activation functions, PPO clip range, AMP update frequency, PSI window) block exact replication.
- Generalization to assistive interactions beyond lifting/supporting (e.g., carrying, guiding) remains unproven; evaluation is limited to two datasets.

## Confidence
- **Joint MARL for reactive coordination:** Medium confidence (sequential training ablation shows gap, but hyperparameter sensitivity unknown; break condition untested).
- **Weight initialization from PHC prior:** High confidence (ablation yields 0% success without initialization; procedure clearly described).
- **Dynamic retargeting + contact reward:** High confidence (ablations show catastrophic failure without each component, though threshold tuning may be dataset‑specific).

## Next Checks
1. Validate contact extraction: Record ground‑truth contact forces and actual hand‑Recipient distances during rollouts; compare against extracted forces and binary flags to verify correctness and threshold sensitivity.
2. Hyperparameter sensitivity sweep: Systematically vary contact reward weights (α, β) and retargeting threshold τ_dist on a subset of Inter‑X; measure success rate and MPJPE to determine robust ranges.
3. Break condition for MARL assumption: Remove Recipient impairment (restore full PD gains and torque limits) and retrain/evaluate. If joint training no longer outperforms sequential, it confirms impairment is necessary for co‑adaptation; otherwise the claim is weakened.
