# Automatic Simplification of Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures Descriptions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11982
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11982
- Reference count: 20
- Primary result: LLMs demonstrate a trade-off where GPT-4o reduces textual complexity but sacrifices meaning preservation, while GemmaAgent preserves meaning but increases complexity, and automatic metrics fail to align with human judgments.

## Executive Summary
This study explores using Large Language Models (LLMs) for Automatic Text Simplification (ATS) of Common Vulnerability and Exposure (CVE) descriptions to make them accessible to non-experts. Evaluated on a manually cleaned dataset of 40 CVEs, GPT-4o significantly lowered readability complexity but poorly preserved meaning, whereas the custom GemmaAgent system preserved semantic fidelity but increased complexity. Human evaluations by cybersecurity experts revealed frequent failures in meaning preservation and high disagreement, and automatic evaluation metrics did not consistently reflect human simplicity judgments, underscoring the challenge of automating CVE simplification.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed a cleaned dataset of 40 CVE descriptions. Two simplification approaches were tested: zero-shot prompting with GPT-4o and a custom GemmaAgent system that integrates term extraction, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and a 4-parameter Gemma model. Outputs were assessed using automatic metrics (Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, D-SARI, BERTScore, SBERT, MeaningBERT) and manual review by cybersecurity experts who rated simplicity, meaning preservation, and required modifications.

## Key Results
- GPT-4o reduced complexity (FKGL: 12.45 → 9.49) but exhibited poor meaning preservation (D-SARI: 0.09, BERTScore: 0.58).
- GemmaAgent preserved meaning (MeaningBERT: 76.94, SBERT: 0.92) but increased complexity (FKGL: 14.20).
- Human evaluation found that 35 of 40 simplifications required modification, primarily due to meaning distortion, and automatic metrics showed no correlation with human simplicity judgments.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not explicitly articulate a mechanism; however, the GemmaAgent’s design likely leverages term extraction to flag technical jargon, RAG to retrieve accessible explanations from a knowledge base, and the generative Gemma model to produce simplified outputs grounded in retrieved context. This aims to preserve meaning by anchoring generation to authoritative sources but may inadvertently raise complexity through verbose or retained technical terms.

## Foundational Learning
- **Automatic Text Simplification (ATS)**: Needed to reduce linguistic barriers for non-experts. Quick check: Does output have lower readability scores without omitting critical information?
- **Large Language Models (LLMs)**: Provide flexible paraphrasing and generation. Quick check: Can the model handle domain-specific terminology accurately?
- **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**: Supplies external knowledge to maintain factual accuracy. Quick check: Does retrieval improve semantic similarity to the original?
- **Term Extraction**: Identifies key technical terms for targeted processing. Quick check: Are extracted terms central to the vulnerability?
- **Meaning Preservation Metrics**: Quantify semantic fidelity post-simplification. Quick check: Do BERTScore or SBERT align with expert assessments?
- **Human Evaluation in Cybersecurity**: Validates usability for the target audience. Quick check: Do experts converge on quality ratings?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input CVE → Term Extractor → RAG Retriever (with Knowledge Base) → Gemma Model → Simplified Output  
**Critical path**: Term extraction identifies technical terms; RAG fetches explanatory snippets from a knowledge base; Gemma model integrates these to generate simplified text.  
**Design tradeoffs**: The small Gemma 4B model reduces computational cost but may limit fluency; RAG improves accuracy but adds latency; term extraction focuses simplification but may miss contextual nuances.  
**Failure signatures**: Meaning distortion (incorrect technical details), increased complexity (higher FKGL), retrieval of irrelevant context, and expert disagreement on quality.  
**First experiments**:  
1. Replace rule-based term extraction with a neural tagger to improve term coverage.  
2. Vary the RAG knowledge base size to measure impact on meaning-complexity trade-off.  
3. Test alternative prompting strategies (e.g., few-shot examples) to control output complexity.

## Limitations
- Small, single-source dataset (40 CVEs) limits robustness and generalizability across vulnerability types.
- High expert disagreement in human evaluation undermines reliability of ground-truth simplification quality.
- Automatic complexity and meaning metrics fail to correlate with human simplicity judgments.
- GemmaAgent increased complexity, violating the core simplification objective.
- Absence of non-LLM baselines prevents assessment of net benefit.
- Unclear extendability to longer CVEs, other technical domains, or broader non-expert audiences.

## Confidence
- Trade-off exists (GPT-4o: complexity ↓, meaning ↓): High
- GemmaAgent increases complexity: High
- Automatic metrics ≠ human judgment: High
- Human eval reveals meaning preservation failures: High
- Findings generalize to broader CVE population: Medium
- LLMs are suitable for CVE simplification: Low

## Next Checks
1. Scale evaluation to a larger (n>500), stratified CVE corpus covering multiple products, severities, and description lengths.
2. Establish a robust human evaluation protocol with 5+ experts per item, adjudication to resolve disagreements, and a derived gold-standard simplification label.
3. Include non-LLM text simplification baselines (e.g., rule-based, supervised seq2seq) and measure computational cost/performance trade-offs.
