# When Do Multi-Agent Systems Outperform? Analysing the Learning Efficiency of Agentic Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.08272
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08272
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: MARL can achieve up to a 1/K sample complexity improvement over SARL for independent subtasks but suffers a K² penalty for dependent subtasks; a task alignment factor α determines the regime where MARL remains advantageous.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the conditions under which Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) outperforms Single-Agent RL (SARL) in the context of training large language models (LLMs). Using a PAC learning framework, the authors derive sample complexity bounds for MARL under two extreme task decomposition scenarios: independent subtasks, where MARL’s complexity is dominated by the hardest agent (yielding up to a 1/K speedup), and fully dependent subtasks, where error propagation incurs a K² penalty. To bridge theory and practice, they introduce a task alignment metric α that quantifies the mismatch between SARL and MARL reward decompositions. The resulting condition κ_d κ_ℓ ≤ (1-2α/ε)² characterizes when MARL retains a sample efficiency advantage. The theoretical findings are validated on synthetic tasks and the GSM8K mathematical reasoning benchmark, clarifying the deployment criteria for MARL systems.

## Method Summary
The authors adopt a Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) learning approach to derive uniform sample complexity bounds for both SARL and MARL settings. They consider a task decomposed into K subtasks, each handled by an individual agent, and analyze two structural assumptions: (1) independent subtask rewards, where overall performance is the minimum of individual rewards, and (2) fully dependent subtasks, where errors propagate sequentially. They define task alignment α as the maximum discrepancy between the SARL reward and the sum of per-agent rewards under independence, capturing decomposition fidelity. The bounds involve factors κ_d (ratio of per-agent horizon to total horizon) and κ_ℓ (logarithmic term capturing discount and accuracy). The theoretical results are complemented by experiments on synthetic environments and the GSM8K dataset, where decomposition granularity and alignment are varied.

## Key Results
- **Independent subtasks**: MARL sample complexity scales with the hardest agent, yielding up to a 1/K improvement over SARL.
- **Dependent subtasks**: MARL suffers a quadratic K² penalty due to error propagation across agents.
- **Task alignment condition**: MARL retains advantage if κ_d κ_ℓ ≤ (1-2α/ε)², where α measures reward decomposition mismatch.
- Experimental results on synthetic tasks and GSM8K confirm the predicted trade-offs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The derived PAC bounds reveal that the primary benefit of MARL—parallelization—reduces sample complexity by a factor of K when subtasks are independent and the hardest subtask dictates the overall difficulty. However, when subtasks are dependent, errors from one agent propagate to subsequent ones, effectively requiring each agent to succeed with higher probability and introducing a K² multiplicative penalty. The task alignment factor α captures the degree to which the independent assumption is violated; as α grows, the bound tightens and the advantage shrinks. The condition κ_d κ_ℓ ≤ (1-2α/ε)² thus balances the potential parallelization gain against misalignment and dependency costs, explaining when MARL is preferable.

## Architecture Onboarding
The methodological pipeline for deciding between MARL and SARL consists of the following components:

- **Task Decomposition**: Split the original RL task into K subtasks and assign each to an agent.
- **Alignment Estimation**: Compute or estimate α by comparing SARL reward with the sum of per-agent rewards under independence assumptions (e.g., via Monte Carlo rollouts).
- **Bounding Parameters**: Derive κ_d (based on horizon partition) and κ_ℓ (based on discount γ, number of agents K, and desired accuracy ε).
- **Condition Check**: Evaluate whether κ_d κ_ℓ ≤ (1-2α/ε)².
- **Training Mode Selection**: If condition holds, proceed with MARL; otherwise, use SARL.

**Component Map**:
Task Decomposition -> Alignment Estimation -> κ_d/κ_ℓ Computation -> Condition Check -> Training Mode Selection

**Critical Path**: The sequence from decomposition to condition check is essential; alignment estimation often dominates practical effort because α must be approximated without full training.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- Fine-grained decomposition (large K) increases parallelization potential but also risks larger α (misalignment) and larger κ_d (if subtask horizons are uneven).
- Assuming independence simplifies bounds but may underestimate α; assuming dependence yields pessimistic K² penalty.
- Accurate α estimation requires many rollouts, which may offset sample complexity gains.

**Failure Signatures**:
- α > ε/2, making (1-2α/ε)² negative or zero → MARL impossible.
- κ_d κ_ℓ too large (e.g., due to long total horizon or small ε) → condition fails even with low α.
- Empirical sample complexity of MARL far exceeds SARL despite condition predicting advantage → bound looseness or unmodeled dependencies.

**First Three Experiments**:
1. **Independent Synthetic Task**: Construct a task where each agent’s reward is independent and identical; vary K and measure sample complexity; expect 1/K scaling.
2. **Dependent Synthetic Task**: Create a chain of subtasks where earlier agent failure halts later ones; vary K; expect K² penalty.
3. **GSM8K Decomposition Study**: Apply MARL with different decompositions (e.g., by problem-solving stages); estimate α and test whether the condition κ_d κ_ℓ ≤ (1-2α/ε)² predicts the observed sample efficiency relative to SARL.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Under what conditions does Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) achieve consistent sample efficiency advantages over Single-Agent RL (SARL) in LLM training?
- **Basis**: The paper explicitly states in the introduction: "Under what conditions, if any, does MARL provide a clear and consistent advantage over SARL?" and derives PAC bounds showing independence (Proposition 4.4) vs dependence (Proposition 4.5) and alignment effects (Proposition 4.7).
- **Why unresolved**: The analysis assumes either fully independent or fully dependent subtasks and i.i.d. data; real-world tasks often involve partial dependencies and interactive settings with non-i.i.d. trajectories, making the conditions too idealized.
- **What evidence**: Extensive empirical validation across diverse LLM-RL tasks measuring sample complexity while varying decomposition granularity and alignment. Theoretical extension to non-i.i.d. (e.g., mixing conditions) and a continuum of dependency strengths would clarify applicability.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How can the task alignment factor α be accurately estimated and optimized in practice for MARL deployments?
- **Basis**: The paper defines α as the max reward discrepancy between SARL and independent MARL decomposition (Eq.4.1) and notes its impact on PAC bounds (Theorem 4.6). It briefly mentions Monte Carlo and gradient-based estimation but leaves practical validation open.
- **Why unresolved**: Without reliable estimation methods, the condition in Proposition 4.7 cannot be used to decide when MARL is beneficial. The trade-off between decomposition gains and misalignment costs remains untested.
- **What evidence**: Develop scalable estimation procedures for α within LLM rollout pipelines; conduct controlled ablations varying the decomposition (e.g., number of agents, segment boundaries) to measure α and correlate with sample efficiency outcomes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How do specific policy-gradient algorithms (PPO, GRPO) alter the sample complexity bounds derived in the learning-agnostic PAC analysis?
- **Basis**: The paper acknowledges its framework abstracts algorithm details and suggests integrating specific methods for more precise insights (Limitations: "Future investigations could integrate specific policy-gradient algorithms").
- **Why unresolved**: The derived bounds are uniform convergence based and ignore algorithmic variance, trust region effects, or optimization dynamics that significantly impact real LLM training; constants and scaling may differ from learning-agnostic predictions.
- **What evidence**: Derive PAC bounds tailored to PPO/GRPO in MARL/SARL settings; run experiments comparing empirical learning curves against theoretical predictions under identical decompositions and alignment.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What are the PAC sample complexity bounds for MARL when subtasks exhibit partial (rather than full) dependencies?
- **Basis**: The paper analyzes only two extreme cases: independent (Theorem 4.3) and dependent (Theorem 4.2). It explicitly states in Limitations: "We focused primarily on idealized task decomposition scenarios... exploring intermediate scenarios involving partially dependent subtasks..."
- **Why unresolved**: Real LLM tasks often have graded interdependencies (e.g., some segments loosely coupled); the K² penalty in dependent case may be overly pessimistic, while independent assumptions may be violated; a theory for intermediate regimes is missing.
- **What evidence**: Extend the PAC analysis to model dependencies via a tunable parameter (e.g., via a correlation metric between segment rewards) and derive bounds that interpolate between the known extremes, validated on synthetic tasks with controllable dependency strength.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes idealized subtask decompositions and i.i.d. data; real LLM multi-agent systems often have overlapping, non-stationary, or poorly defined subtasks and non-i.i.d. trajectories.
- The task alignment α and parameters κ_d, κ_ℓ lack clear practical estimation methods; their values in real deployments are unknown.
- Experimental validation is limited to synthetic tasks and a single real-world benchmark (GSM8K), which may not represent the diversity of LLM applications (e.g., code, creative writing).

## Confidence
- **Theoretical sample complexity bounds (independent vs dependent subtasks)**: High (derived via standard PAC framework; proofs sound under assumptions).
- **Advantage condition with task alignment α**: High (mathematically follows from the bounds; utility hinges on accurate α estimation).
- **Experimental validation on synthetic and GSM8K tasks**: Medium (results align with theory but limited task diversity; generalizability unproven).
- **Practical estimation of α**: Low/Medium (no concrete method proposed; remains an open challenge).
