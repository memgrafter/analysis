# Don't Eliminate Cut: Exponential Separations in LLM-Based Theorem Proving

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10512
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10512
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A cut-aware hierarchical learner provably requires exponentially less data than a flat (cut-free) learner, with sample complexity ratio \(N_{\text{flat}}/N_{\text{hier}} \gtrsim (\Lambda/\lambda)^{D/(p\gamma)}\), where \(\lambda \ll \Lambda\) are cut-elimination blow-up factors, \(D\) proof depth, \(p\) margin exponent, and \(\gamma\) learning curve exponent.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses why subgoal decomposition (e.g., using cuts/lemmas) yields significant empirical gains in LLM-guided theorem proving despite worst-case hardness. It models tactic proposal as a Lipschitz policy in a finite-horizon deterministic MDP with compact state/action spaces. A latent proof DAG distribution captures reusable cut/lemma/sketch structure. Analysis under Tsybakov margin conditions and sequential Rademacher complexity shows that a hierarchical learner—which infers and leverages the latent DAG—requires exponentially less data than a flat learner that searches over cut-free proof trees. The separation hinges on the blow-up factor \(\Lambda/\lambda\) from cut elimination: when a depth-\(D\) DAG of size \(\sim\lambda^D\) expands to a cut-free tree of size \(\sim\Lambda^D\) with \(\lambda \ll \Lambda\), the sample complexity ratio scales as \((\Lambda/\lambda)^{D/(p\gamma)}\). This provides a principled justification for subgoal decomposition in agentic theorem provers.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a theoretical framework where theorem proving is a Markov decision process (MDP). The agent selects tactics (actions) in a deterministic environment with a finite horizon (proof length). The policy mapping states to action distributions is assumed Lipschitz, ensuring stability. To model the benefit of reusable structures, they introduce a latent random variable \(Z\) representing a proof DAG (directed acyclic graph) that encodes high-level decomposition (cuts/lemmas). The hierarchical learner first infers \(Z\) from the current state and target theorem, then conditions its tactic policy on \(Z\). Generalization is analyzed via top-\(k\) search, converting one-step imitation errors into misranking probabilities under a Tsybakov margin condition (Assumption 4). The overall risk is controlled using sequential Rademacher complexity and covering numbers (Assumption 5). The flat learner lacks access to \(Z\) and must directly search over the exponentially larger space of cut-free proof trees. By comparing the effective number of decisions (branching factors) in the two setups—\(\lambda^D\) for hierarchical vs. \(\Lambda^D\) for flat—the authors derive an exponential separation in sample complexity, mediated by the margin exponent \(p\) and learning curve exponent \(\gamma\).

## Key Results
- The empirical success of subgoal decomposition in LLM-based proving is theoretically explained by a latent proof DAG model and Lipschitz MDP analysis.
- Under standard margin and complexity conditions, a hierarchical learner (cut-aware) achieves exponentially lower sample complexity than a flat (cut-free) learner.
- The separation factor is \((\Lambda/\lambda)^{D/(p\gamma)}\), where \(\Lambda/\lambda\) is the cut-elimination blow-up, \(D\) proof depth, \(p\) the Tsybakov margin exponent, and \(\gamma\) the learning curve exponent.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The hierarchical approach compresses the space of proof strategies by exploiting reusable intermediate results (cuts/lemmas). Instead of searching through an exponentially branching tree of atomic inferences (cut-free), the agent first predicts a high-level DAG sketch that outlines the proof structure. This sketch reduces the effective decision complexity at each step: from a large atomic action space (size \(\Lambda\)) to a smaller space conditioned on the latent DAG (size \(\lambda\)). The Lipschitz property ensures that small perturbations in the proof state do not drastically change the optimal tactic distribution, enabling generalization from limited data. The Tsybakov margin condition guarantees that errors in imitating a single step translate into controlled misranking probabilities, so that top-\(k\) search can recover the correct tactic with high probability. Meanwhile, sequential Rademacher complexity bounds the overall generalization error over proof trajectories. Together, these elements show that as long as \(\lambda \ll \Lambda\) (i.e., reusable cuts significantly shrink the branching factor) and the margin/complexity conditions hold, the hierarchical policy learns with exponentially fewer examples than the flat alternative.

## Foundational Learning
- **Lipschitz policy**  
  *Why needed:* Ensures that nearby proof states have similar tactic distributions; critical for generalization bounds and to apply covering arguments.  
  *Quick check:* Can you bound the change in tactic probability when the goal is perturbed by a small syntactic edit?  

- **Tsybakov margin condition**  
  *Why needed:* Relates the gap in optimal tactic scores to the probability of misranking; enables conversion of one-step imitation error into global success probability under top‑\(k\) search.  
  *Quick check:* If the margin exponent is \(p\), how does the misranking probability decay as the score gap \(\Delta\) shrinks?  

- **Sequential Rademacher complexity**  
  *Why needed:* Measures the complexity of sequence-dependent policies (like proof trajectories); used to bound the generalization error for MDP-based learning.  
  *Quick check:* How would you compute the Rademacher complexity for a policy class that takes a history of actions as input?  

- **Latent proof DAG distribution**  
  *Why needed:* Captures the distribution over high‑level proof structures (cuts/lemmas) that can be reused across theorems; the hierarchical learner infers this to guide tactic selection.  
  *Quick check:* What properties must the DAG distribution satisfy for the hierarchical sample complexity gains to hold?  

- **Cut‑elimination blow‑up factor (\(\Lambda/\lambda\))**  
  *Why needed:* Quantifies the compression achieved by representing proofs as DAGs instead of trees; directly drives the exponential separation.  
  *Quick check:* If a proof DAG has depth \(D\) and branching factor \(\lambda\), what is the size of its cut‑free tree expansion with branching \(\Lambda\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
State (current goals) -> Latent DAG inference (r_φ(z|x,y)) -> Tactic policy (π_θ(a|x,z)) -> Top‑k search controller -> Applied tactic -> Next state

**Critical path:**  
1. Encode current proof state and target theorem.  
2. Infer latent DAG structure \(z\) (e.g., a sketch of needed cuts/lemmas).  
3. Compute tactic distribution conditioned on state and \(z\).  
4. Select top‑\(k\) tactics via search, apply one, and repeat until proof closure.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Hierarchical vs. flat:* Adding latent DAG inference introduces computational overhead and potential inference error, but drastically reduces the effective action space and sample complexity.  
- *Inference quality:* The hierarchical guarantee depends on accurate inference of \(Z\); poor inference (e.g., posterior collapse) can erode the exponential advantage.  
- *Search depth:* Top‑\(k\) beam search trades off computational cost against success probability; larger \(k\) mitigates margin violations but increases branching.

**Failure signatures:**  
- The inferred DAG is overly generic or degenerate (e.g., always predicting a trivial structure), leading to no effective decomposition.  
- The correct tactic consistently falls outside the top‑\(k\) set, causing systematic search failure.  
- Training instability: The ELBO gap (inference error) remains large, indicating the posterior approximation is inadequate.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Measure the cut‑elimination blow‑up \(\Lambda/\lambda\) on a synthetic corpus of depth‑controlled proofs to verify \(\lambda \ll \Lambda\).  
2. Estimate the Tsybakov margin exponent \(p\) on real LLM tactic proposal data by fitting the tail of the optimal‑tactic score gap distribution.  
3. Compare sample‑complexity curves of hierarchical vs. flat learners on a controlled dataset with known DAG parameters, checking for the predicted \((\Lambda/\lambda)^{D/(p\gamma)}\) ratio.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Is the Tsybakov-type margin condition (Assumption 4) empirically satisfied in real LLM-based theorem proving datasets, and how does its exponent β relate to the difficulty distribution of theorems?  
  **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Assumption 4 is used to convert one-step imitation errors into misranking probabilities, but the paper does not provide evidence that this condition holds in practice. The condition is introduced in Section 4.1.1 and invoked in Theorem 8.  
  **Why unresolved:** The validity of the margin condition directly impacts the tightness of the top‑k success guarantee. If real tactic proposal distributions violate it, the derived sample complexity bounds may be meaningless.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyze actual LLM tactic proposal logs from Lean or other provers to estimate the distribution of the top‑gap ∆k and fit a β value. Alternatively, generate synthetic distributions that mimic real theorem proving and test the tail bound.

- **Question:** Can the sequential Rademacher complexity bound (Section G) be refined for practical neural network policy classes to yield tighter sample complexity estimates?  
  **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper uses generic sequential Rademacher/covering complexity to control generalization (Assumption 5 and Section G). However, for large transformer‑based policies, this bound is likely very loose and may not reflect the true sample efficiency.  
  **Why unresolved:** Overly pessimistic bounds provide limited practical guidance for designing data‑efficient provers. A refined complexity measure aligned with the inductive biases of LLMs would give more accurate predictions.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive generalization bounds specific to transformer architectures (e.g., via uniform stability, PAC‑Bayes, or margin‑based arguments) and compare empirical validation against the existing bound on synthetic or real provers.

- **Question:** How can the latent proof DAG structure Z be automatically inferred from proof traces without oracle supervision, and what is the resulting inference error in practice?  
  **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The hierarchical pipeline (Section 4.2) relies on a variational posterior r₀(z|x,y) to approximate the true posterior, with the inference error captured by the ELBO gap (Assumption 6). However, the paper does not propose or evaluate concrete inference mechanisms beyond the EM/VAE abstraction.  
  **Why unresolved:** The quality of the inferred DAG is crucial for sample efficiency; if the inference error is large, the exponential separation may disappear. Yet we lack algorithmic solutions and empirical studies of the trade‑off between inference quality and proving performance.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement and train a VAE‑style architecture that inputs a full proof trace and outputs a latent DAG representation, evaluate the ELBO gap and the subsequent success probability of the hierarchical prover on benchmarks with ground‑truth DAGs (e.g., synthetic data). Also analyse whether posterior collapse or other issues arise.

- **Question:** For realistic mathematical theorem libraries, what are the cut‑elimination blow‑up factors Λ/λ, and do they exhibit exponential dependence on proof depth D as required by the separation theorem?  
  **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The exponential separation (Theorem 3) hinges on the existence of constants Λ ≫ λ such that the effective decision counts satisfy K_flat ≥ c·Λᴰ and K_hier ≤ C·λᴰ (Assumptions 7 and 18). While the paper presents a conceptual example, it does not provide empirical measurements from actual libraries like Mathlib.  
  **Why unresolved:** Without measuring Λ and λ in practice, we cannot confirm whether the theoretical separation explains the empirical success of subgoal decomposition. The constants may be much smaller or even constant, weakening the claimed exponential advantage.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyze a representative corpus of formal proofs in Lean/Mathlib to extract reusable lemmas and compute the ratio of the size of a cut‑free version to the size of a cut‑aware version across varying depths. Fit exponential models to estimate Λ/λ and their depth scaling.

## Limitations
- The analysis relies on idealized assumptions (deterministic MDP, Lipschitz policy, Tsybakov margin) that may not hold for stochastic, non‑differentiable LLM-based provers, limiting direct empirical mapping.
- The required blow‑up factor \(\lambda \ll \Lambda\) is an open empirical question; confidence that it commonly holds in real mathematical corpora is low.
- The theoretical sample‑complexity separation is for fixed proof depth \(D\) and does not account for how depth scales with problem difficulty or for overhead of hierarchical search, making practical advantage uncertain.

## Confidence
- Theoretical soundness under stated assumptions: **High**  
- Empirical relevance of assumptions (Lipschitz, margin) to real LLM provers: **Medium**  
- Prevalence of the required blow‑up \(\lambda \ll \Lambda\) in practice: **Low**  
- Practical end‑to‑end benefit for automated theorem proving: **Medium‑Low**
