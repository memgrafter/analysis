# Why do we Trust Chatbots? From Normative Principles to Behavioral Drivers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08707
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08707
- **Reference count:** 24
- **Primary result:** User trust in chatbots is manufactured via interactional design exploiting cognitive biases (e.g., linguistic fluency, anthropomorphism) rather than earned through normative trustworthiness pillars like transparency, creating a misalignment between perceived trust and actual system ethics.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a fundamental mismatch between established normative frameworks for trustworthy AI (e.g., the EU’s seven pillars) and the actual behavioral mechanisms that cause users to trust chatbots. Through conceptual analysis, it argues that trust is frequently *manufactured* by design choices that exploit cognitive biases—such as anthropomorphism, authority bias, and the halo effect—rather than being *earned* through demonstrated alignment with ethical principles like transparency and accountability. User trust is shown to hinge on surface-level cues: linguistic fluency, perceived social presence, interactional consistency, and frictionless usability. A key paradox is presented: increased transparency may actually reduce perceived trust. The authors propose reframing chatbots as "highly skilled salespeople" to expose the inherent goal misalignment between organizations and users, advocating for a dual research and design framework that separates the study of behavioral trust drivers from normative trustworthiness.

## Method Summary
The paper employs a conceptual analysis methodology, synthesizing existing literature from human-computer interaction, cognitive psychology, and AI ethics (24 references) to build its argument. It does not present original empirical data or model training. The core method involves contrasting two distinct constructs: (1) "behavioral trust"—the heuristic-driven, psychological formation of user trust—and (2) "normative trustworthiness"—the ethical and operational principles (e.g., EU pillars) that a system should embody. The synthesis points to a systematic gap where design for engagement leverages biases that increase perceived trust without necessarily improving, and sometimes undermining, normative alignment. The proposed outputs are a reframing metaphor ("highly skilled salespeople") and a call for a dual-track approach to research and intervention.

## Key Results
- **Mismatch Thesis:** Normative trust pillars (e.g., transparency, accountability) do not align with the actual psychological drivers of user trust in chatbots.
- **Behavioral Drivers:** Trust is primarily built on four interactional factors: (1) linguistic fluency/coherence, (2) perceived social presence/anthropomorphic cues, (3) interactional consistency/responsiveness, and (4) frictionless usability/instrumental value.
- **Paradoxical Effect:** Increasing transparency—a key normative pillar—can reduce perceived trust by revealing system limitations, uncertainty, or data practices, conflicting with user preference for smooth, authoritative interaction.
- **Reframing:** Chatbots are proposed to be understood as "highly skilled salespeople," a metaphor that highlights persuasive intent and potential misalignment with user interests, justifying regulatory scrutiny.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### 1. Linguistic Fluency as a Trust Heuristic
Users equate grammatical correctness, contextual relevance, and conversational smoothness with intelligence and reliability. This heuristic bypasses systematic evaluation of accuracy.
*Evidence anchor:* Section 3: “users tend to equate grammatical correctness, contextual relevance, and conversational smoothness with intelligence and reliability.” *Corpus note:* Empirical support cited (e.g., Brunswicker et al. 2025; Chiesurin et al. 2023).

### 2. Anthropomorphic Cues and Perceived Social Presence
Human-like language, simulated empathy, and conversational contingency elicit social responses, leading users to attribute intention and credibility. This reduces critical scrutiny.
*Evidence anchor:* Section 3: “chatbots that adopt human-like language, express empathy, or simulate understanding elicit social responses that bypass critical evaluation.” *Corpus note:* Supported by meta-analyses (Klein 2025) and foundational CHI research (Nass et al. 1994).

### 3. Interactional Consistency and Frictionless Usability
Timely, confident responses and seamless integration into workflows build trust through pragmatic success, not moral alignment. Trust becomes a functional decision.
*Evidence anchor:* Section 3: “interactional consistency and responsiveness reinforce trust… frictionless usability and perceived instrumental value contribute.” *Corpus note:* Empirical studies on responsiveness (Vidarshika et al. 2025) and reliability (Ali & Sheikh 2025).

**Paradox of Transparency:** The paper argues that increasing transparency may undermine trust by revealing uncertainty, data practices, or limitations, contradicting user preference for frictionless authority.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Behavioral vs. Normative Trust**
  *Why needed:* Distinguishes psychological trust formation (heuristic-driven) from ethical design requirements (e.g., EU pillars). This separation clarifies why user trust often misaligns with system trustworthiness.
  *Quick check:* “Can a chatbot be perceived as trustworthy yet fail normative standards like transparency? Give an example.”

- **Concept: Cognitive Biases in Human-AI Interaction**
  *Why needed:* Biases (anthropomorphism, authority bias, halo effect) are the mechanisms through which trust is *manufactured* by design. Recognizing them is key to critiquing interaction patterns.
  *Quick check:* “How might the ‘halo effect’ cause a user to overlook a chatbot’s factual error after a polite greeting?”

- **Concept: The Salesperson Metaphor**
  *Why needed:* Reframes chatbots as persuasive agents with organizational objectives, not neutral assistants. This exposes goal misalignment and justifies regulatory scrutiny.
  *Quick check:* “Why is calling a chatbot a ‘salesperson’ more ethically charged than calling it an ‘assistant’?”

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Input Processor -> Response Generator -> Bias Amplification Module -> Transparency Layer (optional) -> Interface

**Critical path:**
User input → Intent recognition → Generate response prioritizing fluency/social presence → Output → User forms trust heuristically → Potential over-reliance or data disclosure.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Fluency vs. Honesty: Higher linguistic polish increases perceived trust but may mask uncertainty.
- Transparency vs. Engagement: Explicit disclosures reduce over-trust but also lower satisfaction and perceived competence.
- Personalization vs. Privacy: Tailored, socially present responses improve trust but escalate privacy risks.

**Failure signatures:**
- Over-trust: Users follow harmful advice without verification.
- Privacy leakage: Users disclose sensitive information due to perceived safety/anonymity.
- Calibration mismatch: Users distrust a highly accurate system due to transparency cues, or trust a biased system due to fluency.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Fluency manipulation:** Compare user trust and reliance across chatbot responses varying in grammatical correctness and contextual coherence.
2. **Transparency intervention:** Measure changes in perceived trust, satisfaction, and verification behavior when adding statements like “I’m an AI and can make mistakes.”
3. **Anthropomorphism audit:** Deploy identical information via chatbots with vs. without empathetic language and first-name usage in high-stakes decisions; assess trust and compliance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can we empirically quantify the gap between users' perceived trustworthiness of a chatbot and its alignment with normative AI standards (e.g., EU pillars)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "there is a need for empirical research to quantify the gap between perceived trustworthiness and actual alignment with ethical or normative AI standards."
- **Why unresolved:** No established metrics or studies directly compare interaction-driven trust perceptions against systematic evaluations of compliance with normative principles.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Large-scale comparative studies where the same chatbot is evaluated by users (measuring trust) and by auditors (assessing compliance with EU pillars), across diverse application domains.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What specific interface design patterns can effectively calibrate user trust in chatbots without relying on or exacerbating cognitive biases like anthropomorphism or authority bias?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors call for developers to "provide transparency, error signals, and corrective feedback" but do not define concrete, tested patterns.
- **Why unresolved:** Lack of validated design interventions that successfully reduce over-trust while maintaining usability and perceived helpfulness.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments (A/B tests) comparing different trust-calibration interventions on measures of appropriate reliance, task performance, and user satisfaction.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent does the "highly skilled salesperson" framing accurately predict and explain the persuasive impact and goal-alignment outcomes of deployed commercial chatbots?
- **Query:** This is [explicit] in the core reframing proposal. The paper argues this metaphor highlights misaligned objectives but does not test its predictive power.
- **Why unresolved:** The metaphor is conceptual; it is unclear if chatbots' persuasive tactics systematically match those of human salespeople or vary by organizational goal.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Mixed-methods research analyzing commercial chatbot dialogues for sales tactics and correlating these with user outcomes (conversion rates, data sharing) and declared organizational objectives.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Under what specific conditions and for what user segments does increased transparency (e.g., explaining probabilistic outputs, data usage) lead to a *reduction* in perceived trust and engagement with chatbots?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note the paradox: "greater alignment with these seven pillars could actually undermine rather than strengthen user trust... Transparency... may reveal opaque training processes... potentially triggering privacy concerns and skepticism."
- **Why unresolved:** The phenomenon is asserted based on cognitive theory but lacks granular empirical delineation. Boundary conditions (e.g., topic sensitivity, user tech literacy, transparency format) are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments systematically varying the depth and framing of transparency disclosures across user populations, measuring changes in trust, continued use, and information disclosure.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What are the long-term longitudinal effects of repeated interactions with persuasive, high-trust chatbots on users' critical thinking, decision-making autonomy, and susceptibility to influence in subsequent non-AI contexts?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion calls for: "Longitudinal research should also examine the potential long-term effects on human decision-making, critical thinking, and behavior..."
- **Why unresolved:** The field lacks longitudinal studies beyond immediate post-interaction measures. Cumulative cognitive and behavioral adaptations are speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal cohort studies tracking users' interactions over months/years, assessing changes in heuristic vs. systematic reasoning, default reliance on AI, and transfer of persuasion susceptibility to human interactions.

## Limitations
- **No empirical quantification** of the "gap" between perceived trust and normative trustworthiness; claims are based on conceptual synthesis of prior literature.
- **Operationalization unclear** for "calibrating trust appropriately" or implementing the "dual framework" in real systems.
- **Paradox of transparency** is asserted but not systematically measured; the strength and conditions of the negative effect are unknown.
- **Generalizability limits**: Arguments are derived primarily from HCI/AI ethics literature; applicability across cultures, domains, and user expertise levels is not addressed.

## Confidence

- **Behavioral Drivers (Fluency, Social Presence, Consistency):** High
