# AIDG: Evaluating Asymmetry Between Information Extraction and Containment in Multi-Turn Dialogue

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.17443  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17443  
- **Reference count:** 26  
- **Primary result:** Frontier LLMs exhibit a consistent 350 ELO advantage in containment (defense) over deduction (offense) in adversarial multi-turn dialogue, with confirmation attacks 7.75× more effective than blind search and constraint violations causing 41.3% of offensive failures.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the AIDG framework to evaluate strategic reasoning in LLMs through dynamic, adversarial multi-turn dialogue. AIDG comprises two tasks: AIDG-I (pragmatic social deduction) and AIDG-II (constrained 20-questions). Across 439 games involving six frontier models, the study reveals a robust asymmetry: models are significantly better at maintaining a protected state (Holder role) than at actively extracting hidden information (Seeker role). Key bottlenecks include a vulnerability to confirmation-based queries—where the model’s cooperative priors lead to leakage—and a degradation in constraint adherence under conversational load. The findings suggest that current LLMs excel at local defensive coherence but struggle with global strategic inquiry, highlighting gaps in reasoning under adversarial pressure.

## Method Summary
The AIDG framework operationalizes dialogue as a two-player zero-sum game with imperfect information. In AIDG-I, aHolder evades detection of a secret via free-form truthful deflection while a Seeker infers it through questioning; leakage is judged semantically. In AIDG-II, aHolder responds with only “yes/no/maybe” to a Seeker’s yes/no queries to identify a target word from a fixed corpus, with a “no direct guess” constraint. A dual-ELO system tracks separate ratings for each role (C_ELO for Seeker, V_ELO for Holder), updating based on win/loss and turn efficiency. An LLM arbiter determines leaks and violations. Tournaments involve round-robin (AIDG-I) and ordered pairings (AIDG-II) across six models, yielding 300 and 150 games respectively.

## Key Results
- Models hold a ~350 ELO advantage on defense vs. offense (Cohen’s d = 5.47), with defensive capability showing low variance (σ = 1.9 ELO) vs. high offensive variance (σ = 53.3 ELO).
- Confirmation attacks (queries presupposing a plausible hypothesis) are 7.75× more effective than blind entropy-reducing queries (p < 0.00001).
- Constraint violations account for 41.3% of Seeker failures in AIDG-II; disqualification rate correlates negatively with win rate (r = –0.95).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Claim:** LLMs exhibit a structural capability asymmetry where maintaining a protected state is computationally easier than actively deducing a hidden state in multi-turn adversarial dialogue.  
**Mechanism:** The Holder role involves a largely static discriminative mapping (`f(query, S) -> response`) resolvable with local context and alignment-trained refusal behaviors. The Seeker requires continuous belief-space updating (`P(S|H_t)`), hypothesis generation, and strategic planning across turns, increasing cognitive load and reliance on on-the-fly reasoning not strongly optimized in standard training.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract: 350 ELO defense advantage; Section 6.1: Seeker’s belief maintenance increases computational burden; Corpus support is weak—claim is primarily paper-internal.  
**Break condition:** Seeker and Holder win rates converge (gap < 50 ELO, d < 0.5) across diverse formats and model families.

**Claim:** Cooperative dialogue priors create a “confirmation vulnerability,” making LLMs more susceptible to leakage when an adversary begins with a partially correct hypothesis versus conducting blind entropy-reducing search.  
**Mechanism:** The tension between the instruction-following goal of “helpfulness” and the containment goal causes failure. When a query presupposes a plausible hypothesis (`H ≈ S`), the model’s tendency to engage with and validate user premises can override protective instincts, leading to implicit confirmation or semantic paraphrase.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract: 7.75× effectiveness of confirmation attacks; Section 5.3: Mode A (21.9%) vs. Mode B (3.5%) success rates; Section 6.2: helpfulness–containment tension.  
**Break condition:** Mode A and Mode B success rates become statistically equivalent (Odds Ratio ~1, p > 0.05) across models.

**Claim:** Under adversarial conversational load, constraint adherence (e.g., output format rules) degrades, and this degradation is a primary failure mode for strategic deduction, independent of raw reasoning ability.  
**Mechanism:** Finite cognitive resources: allocation to multi-step strategic planning (tracking hypotheses, planning queries) degrades monitoring and enforcement of negative constraints (e.g., “do not guess directly”), creating a trade-off where improved reasoning can increase violation risks without robust metacognitive control.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract: 41.3% of failures due to violations; Section 5.4: strong negative correlation (r = –0.95) between disqualification and win rate; Section 6.3: computation allocation trade-off.  
**Break condition:** Constraint violation rates become negligible (<5%) and uncorrelated with strategic success across all models in AIDG-II.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Game-Theoretic Evaluation**  
  *Why needed:* AIDG frames dialogue as a two-player zero-sum game of imperfect information; understanding expected utility, strategy spaces, and the Bradley-Terry model (for Dual-ELO) is core to interpreting the capability gap.  
  *Quick check:* How does a turn-decay multiplier `M(t)` alter strategic incentives for a Seeker compared to a fixed-horizon game?

- **Concept: Information-Theoretic Entropy Reduction in Search**  
  *Why needed:* The Seeker’s goal in AIDG-II is efficient hypothesis narrowing; analyzing query effectiveness requires understanding how “yes/no/maybe” responses partition the search space and reduce uncertainty.  
  *Quick check:* Why might a “maybe” response be strategically valuable for a Holder despite providing less information than “yes”?

- **Concept: Alignment and Instruction-Following Under Pressure**  
  *Why needed:* Findings on constraint degradation relate to how safety/alignment tuning performs when cognitive resources are taxed by a concurrent complex task.  
  *Quick check:* What is the difference between a *capability* (e.g., logical deduction) and an *adherence* failure (e.g., violating a “no direct guess” rule)?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Game Orchestrator initializes roles and secret -> Turn loop: Seeker Agent generates query based on history -> Holder Agent responds based on secret -> Arbiter (Judge) checks for leak/violation using secret and history -> If game continues, repeat; else, outcome determined -> Dual-ELO Manager updates ratings.

**Critical path:** Initialization (model pool, role assignment, secret sampling) → Turn Loop (Seeker query → Holder response → Arbiter check after each turn in AIDG-I, at end in AIDG-II) → If no leak/violation, continue; else terminate → Compute outcome (win type, efficiency `t`) → Update Dual-ELO ratings.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Automated vs. Human Adjudication:* LLM arbiter enables scalability but risks judge bias; human validation is gold-standard but unscalable.  
- *Atomic vs. Composite Secrets:* Atomic facts simplify leakage detection but lack ecological validity for complex, multi-fact secrets.  
- *Constrained vs. Free-Form Response:* AIDG-II’s yes/no/maybe isolates logical deduction but removes pragmatic evasion layer from AIDG-I.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High Seeker disqualification rate (>50%) indicates constraint adherence breakdown.  
- Mode A / Mode B win rate ratio >> 1 confirms confirmation vulnerability.  
- Low V_ELO variance (σ ~2) vs. high C_ELO variance (σ ~50) suggests defensive capability is an architectural constant, while offensive varies.  
- Early violation timing (e.g., average turn 7.1) signals rapid constraint degradation in smaller/less aligned models.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate core asymmetry: Run 5 tournament seeds for AIDG-I; compute `V_ELO - C_ELO` gap; expect ~350 ELO gap, d > 5.0 (anchor: Tables 3, 4).  
2. Isolate confirmation effect: For a single model (e.g., GPT-5), run 50 games in Mode A (prior hypothesis) and 50 in Mode B (blind); expect ~20–30pp win rate differential (anchor: Table 5).  
3. Probe constraint-reasoning trade-off: In AIDG-II, categorize turns by response type (“maybe” vs. “yes”); correlate per-game “maybe” ratio with Holder win rate; expect positive correlation (ρ > 0.3) (anchor: Table 11, Section 5.7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. What computational factors drive the persistent advantage in information containment over deduction?  
   *Basis:* Hypothesized static vs. dynamic state tracking demands and training biases; only correlational evidence.  
   *What evidence:* Ablate training objectives, vary architecture, probe internal states.

2. Why does constraint adherence degrade under conversational load independently of model scale?  
   *Basis:* 41.3% of failures; no correlation with parameter count; mechanism (memory, attention, objective conflict) unidentified.  
   *What evidence:* Analyze model internals during violations; test alternative constraints or alignment regimes.

3. How do cooperative dialogue priors increase vulnerability to confirmation attacks?  
   *Basis:* 7.75× effectiveness; linked to helpfulness–safety tension; specific bias unknown.  
   *What evidence:* Quantify presupposition acceptance rates; adjust alignment objectives.

4. Do the observed asymmetries generalize beyond atomic secrets and closed ontologies?  
   *Basis:* Uses single-fact secrets and fixed 100-word corpus; real-world information is often compositional/open-domain.  
   *What evidence:* Extend AIDG to multi-fact secrets or unbounded search spaces.

## Limitations
- **Arbiter specification unspecified:** The low-temperature LLM judge’s model and prompt are not detailed, risking judge bias in leakage/violation labels.
- **Prompt fidelity critical:** Minor wording changes in role prompts (Appendix C) could alter cooperative priors and strategic behavior.
- **ELO initialization/aggregation under-specified:** Details across tournament seeds (e.g., initial ratings, convergence) are missing, affecting stability of the reported 350 ELO gap.

## Confidence
- **Existence of defense advantage:** High. Supported by consistent aggregate tournament results across 6 models (Tables 2–4).
- **Confirmation attack superiority & constraint degradation:** Medium. Strong within-paper statistics but depend on arbiter/prompt fidelity.
- **Mechanistic explanations (e.g., “computational burden”):** Low. Compelling post-hoc interpretations lacking direct neural or process-tracing evidence.

## Next Checks
1. **Arbiter Consistency Audit:** Run 100 game transcripts through 3 different low-T LLM arbiters (e.g., GPT-4o-mini, Claude-3.5-Haiku); measure inter-arbiter agreement on leak/violation labels; target >95% consistency.
2. **Constraint Violation Root-Cause Analysis:** For all AIDG-II disqualifications, classify violating queries as syntactic, semantic, or timing-based; correlate with model size and turn number.
3. **Prompt Sensitivity Ablation:** Using exact prompts from Appendix C, run minimal replication (n=20 games/model) for AIDG-I; then re-run with a single rephrasing of Holder’s “helpfulness” instruction; compare Holder win rates; significant shift (>5pp) indicates fragility.
