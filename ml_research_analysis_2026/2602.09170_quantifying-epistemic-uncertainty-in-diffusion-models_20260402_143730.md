# Quantifying Epistemic Uncertainty in Diffusion Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.09170
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09170
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: FLARE isolates epistemic uncertainty via Fisher-Laplace projection and random parameter subsampling, achieving near-perfect separation on synthetic time-series tasks.

## Executive Summary
Diffusion models often conflate epistemic (model) uncertainty with aleatoric (data) uncertainty, making it hard to assess true model confidence. This paper introduces FLARE, a method that separates epistemic uncertainty by approximating the posterior over network parameters using a Laplace approximation and propagating this uncertainty through the denoising steps via the Jacobian of the denoiser. To scale to large models, FLARE uniformly subsamples parameters across layers. Evaluated on synthetic time-series datasets (sine, chirp, damped sine), FLARE dramatically outperforms prior methods (BayesDiff, last-layer Laplace) in uncertainty quantification, as measured by gap closure and ROC-AUC. Theoretical analysis shows O(1/√m) convergence for the random estimator and demonstrates the insufficiency of last-layer Laplace approximations.

## Method Summary
FLARE consists of three main steps: (1) After training a diffusion model, compute the Hessian of the negative log-posterior (or Fisher information) to obtain a Gaussian approximation of the parameter posterior (Laplace approximation). (2) Instead of using all parameters, uniformly random subsampling across all layers selects a subset of parameters to reduce computational cost, while preserving statistical convergence properties (O(1/√m)). (3) For each reverse diffusion step, compute the Jacobian of the denoiser network with respect to the sampled parameters, and propagate the parameter covariance through to obtain epistemic uncertainty in the predicted noise. This uncertainty is then used to filter generated samples, removing those with high epistemic variance. The method is scalable and theoretically grounded.

## Key Results
- Near-perfect epistemic-aleatoric separation: gap closure 93.08% (sine), 74.31% (chirp), 85.0% (damped sine); ROC-AUC ~0.5.
- Substantially outperforms BayesDiff and last-layer Laplace (e.g., sine gap closure 13.37%/0.6153 and 47.19%/0.5814).
- All improvements statistically significant (p < 0.05); theory shows O(1/√m) convergence and insufficiency of last-layer Laplace.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
FLARE successfully isolates epistemic uncertainty by explicitly modeling parameter uncertainty via a Laplace-approximated posterior. The Fisher-Laplace projection ensures that only directions in parameter space that are poorly constrained by the data contribute to epistemic uncertainty. By propagating this covariance through the denoiser's Jacobian along the reverse trajectory, FLARE captures how parameter uncertainty translates into uncertainty in the denoising predictions at each step. Random parameter subsampling across layers preserves the directional information needed for accurate estimation while drastically reducing computational overhead, as shown by the O(1/√m) convergence rate. The filtering step then removes samples where the model's parameters are uncertain, leading to improved realism.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Epistemic vs. Aleatoric Uncertainty**
   - *Why needed*: To separate model uncertainty (due to limited data) from inherent data noise; FLARE aims to quantify only epistemic.
   - *Quick check*: Does uncertainty decrease with more training data? (Epistemic yes, aleatoric no).
2. **Laplace Approximation**
   - *Why needed*: Provides a Gaussian approximation to the posterior over network parameters around the MAP estimate, enabling tractable uncertainty propagation.
   - *Quick check*: Is the Hessian of the loss at the MAP point negative definite? (Typically yes for well-behaved nets).
3. **Fisher Information**
   - *Why needed*: Used in the Fisher-Laplace projection to identify parameter directions that are most uncertain; equivalent to the Hessian under certain conditions.
   - *Quick check*: Fisher matrix is positive semi-definite; can be approximated via gradients.
4. **Diffusion Models and Denoising**
   - *Why needed*: FLARE operates on the denoising network; understanding the forward/reverse process is essential.
   - *Quick check*: Given a noisy input, does the denoiser predict the noise or the clean sample? (Typically predicts noise).
5. **Jacobian Propagation**
   - *Why needed*: To propagate parameter covariance through the network to output uncertainty; FLARE uses the Jacobian of the denoiser w.r.t. parameters.
   - *Quick check*: Can compute Jacobian-vector products efficiently with autograd.
6. **Random Parameter Subsampling**
   - *Why needed*: Scalability: full covariance propagation is infeasible for large networks; random subsampling retains statistical accuracy.
   - *Quick check*: Does the estimator's error decrease as 1/√m with number of subsamples m?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Training -> Laplace Covariance Estimation -> Random Parameter Subsampling -> Jacobian Computation -> Uncertainty Propagation along Reverse Trajectory -> Sample Filtering

Critical path:
- Start with a trained diffusion model (denoising network).
- Compute the Laplace-approximated parameter covariance (requires Hessian or Fisher estimation).
- Randomly select m parameters across all layers.
- For each reverse diffusion timestep, compute the Jacobian of the denoiser output (predicted noise) with respect to the selected parameters.
- Propagate the parameter covariance through the Jacobian to obtain epistemic uncertainty in the predicted noise.
- Accumulate epistemic uncertainty over timesteps to get a per-sample score.
- Filter out samples with scores above a threshold.

Design tradeoffs:
- Accuracy vs. speed: increasing subsample size m improves uncertainty estimation but increases cost.
- Linearization error: Jacobian-based propagation approximates the effect of parameter perturbations; may be inaccurate for large steps or highly non-linear networks.
- Covariance estimation: full Hessian is expensive; using a diagonal or low-rank approximation might be necessary for large models.
- Layer bias: uniform random subsampling might under-sample critical layers; could use stratified sampling by layer.

Failure signatures:
- High epistemic uncertainty on in-distribution samples: indicates model is uncertain about parameters, may be due to insufficient training or poor Laplace approximation.
- Low epistemic uncertainty on out-of-distribution samples: suggests the method fails to detect novel data; may need to adjust filtering threshold.
- Runtime scales linearly with model size if using random subsampling? Actually should scale with number of subsamples and Jacobian computation; if subsampling is constant, it's independent of total parameters.
- Theoretical O(1/√m) may not hold if subsampling is not uniform or if parameters are highly correlated.

First experiments:
1. Replicate results on the three synthetic time-series tasks, computing gap closure and ROC-AUC for FLARE, BayesDiff, and last-layer Laplace.
2. Ablate subsample size m (e.g., 10, 100, 1000) to observe convergence behavior and runtime vs. uncertainty quality.
3. Test the impact of layer selection strategy: uniform random vs. sampling proportionally to parameter count per layer vs. focusing on early/late layers.

None.

## Limitations
- Evaluated only on low-dimensional synthetic time-series; not validated on high-dimensional data (images/text).
- Random parameter subsampling: subsample size and layer bias impact on uncertainty quality not fully characterized; linearization accuracy also untested.
- No runtime/memory scaling results; large-network applicability uncertain.

## Confidence
- Epistemic isolation via Fisher-Laplace: High – mathematically rigorous under Laplace approximation.
- Empirical superiority on synthetic tasks: Medium – strong results but narrow domain.
- Scalability via random subsampling: Low – theory exists but no large-scale validation.
