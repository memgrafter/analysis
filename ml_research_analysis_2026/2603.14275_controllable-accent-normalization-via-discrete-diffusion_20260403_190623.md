# Controllable Accent Normalization via Discrete Diffusion

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14275
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14275
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** DLM-AN achieves a word error rate (WER) of 11.19% on a multi‑accent English test set, the lowest among compared systems, and provides smooth, continuous control over accent strength and speaker similarity via a token‑reuse threshold.

## Executive Summary
Accent normalization transforms non‑native speech toward a target accent while preserving linguistic content. Existing methods, however, offer no fine‑grained control over the degree of accent reduction, limiting their utility in applications such as language learning and dubbing. This paper introduces DLM-AN, a system built on masked discrete diffusion over self‑supervised speech tokens. The core innovation is a Common Token Predictor (CTP) that identifies source tokens likely shared with native pronunciation; selectively reusing these tokens during generation creates a direct, interpretable knob for accent strength. Additionally, a Duration Ratio Predictor adjusts the total speech duration to better match native rhythm. On a multi‑accent English evaluation, DLM-AN achieves state‑of‑the‑art content preservation (11.19% WER) and enables continuous, user‑controlled accent modulation.

## Method Summary
DLM-AN first discretizes speech using WavLM features followed by k‑means clustering (1024 codes). A Transformer‑based token encoder processes the source token sequence into content‑rich representations, regularized by a CTC loss on phoneme labels. Two predictors then operate: the CTP, a sequence tagger trained on longest‑common‑subsequence (LCS) labels to estimate which source tokens are “common” to native speech; and the Duration Ratio Predictor (DP), a diffusion transformer with flow matching that outputs a scalar ratio for target duration. At inference, DP sets the target token length, and the target sequence is initialized by reusing source tokens where CTP confidence exceeds a threshold τ, otherwise masking. A masked discrete diffusion decoder (a non‑causal Transformer) iteratively refines all masked positions in parallel, conditioned on the content features. Finally, a flow‑matching vocoder (DiT + HiFT) synthesizes the waveform, optionally guided by a speaker embedding. Training proceeds in two stages: native‑only pretraining (Emilia‑EN) for the encoder and decoder, then fine‑tuning on parallel L2‑native data with a joint loss that combines diffusion, DP, CTP, and CTC terms.

## Key Results
- DLM-AN achieves 11.19% WER on the multi‑accent test set, outperforming all baseline systems.
- The token‑reuse threshold τ yields smooth, continuous control over accent strength and speaker similarity.
- The duration ratio predictor improves naturalness and maintains content preservation when scaling speech duration.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Masked Discrete Diffusion over Speech Tokens
- **Claim:** Masked discrete diffusion over self‑supervised speech tokens enables iterative, bidirectional generation of accent‑normalized token sequences, preserving content while allowing controllable accent strength via initialization.
- **Mechanism:** The forward process randomly masks tokens in the target sequence (Eq. 6). The reverse process uses a Transformer (DLM decoder) to predict all masked tokens in parallel, conditioned on content features from the token encoder (Eq. 7). This bidirectional context allows refining multiple tokens simultaneously.
- **Core assumption:** Speech tokens from a phonetic‑rich tokenizer capture pronunciation details such that accented and native renditions share many tokens in similarly pronounced regions but differ in accent‑affected regions.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “built on masked discrete diffusion over self‑supervised speech tokens.” [section 3.1] Forward/backward diffusion equations and loss. [corpus] Weak: Discrete diffusion applied to speech tokens in other works, though not specifically for accent control.
- **Break condition:** If the tokenizer does not encode phonetic content sufficiently (e.g., poor accent‑discriminative capacity), the shared‑token assumption fails and CTP‑based control becomes ineffective.

### Mechanism 2: Common Token Predictor (CTP)
- **Claim:** CTP provides an interpretable knob for accent strength by identifying source tokens likely shared with the native target; reusing these at initialization preserves more of the original accent.
- **Mechanism:** CTP is a sequence tagger trained on binary labels derived from the Longest Common Subsequence (LCS) between source and target token sequences (Fig 3). At inference, tokens with CTP confidence above threshold τ are reused to initialize the masked target sequence (Algorithm 1). Lower τ → more source tokens reused → stronger accent retention.
- **Core assumption:** Native and accented utterances share a subsequence of tokens representing correctly pronounced segments, and CTP can learn to predict these from source content features.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “Common Token Predictor identifies source tokens likely shared with the native pronunciation; these tokens are selectively reused… provides a simple yet effective mechanism for controlling accent strength.” [section 3.2] LCS‑based label extraction and binary cross‑entropy training. [corpus] Weak: Controllable accent TTS and accent conversion exist, but CTP’s LCS‑based training and thresholded reuse are novel.
- **Break condition:** If LCS does not correlate with perceptually shared pronunciation (e.g., due to tokenizer discretization artifacts), CTP confidence will not reliably indicate accent strength, making the control knob noisy.

### Mechanism 3: Duration Ratio Predictor (DP)
- **Claim:** DP adjusts total speech duration to match native rhythm, improving naturalness under duration control.
- **Mechanism:** DP uses a DiT backbone with conditional flow matching (Eq. 10). Given content features c (and optionally source token embeddings), it predicts the target‑to‑source duration ratio r = dur_tgt / dur_src. This ratio determines the length of the initialized target token sequence (Algorithm 1, line 1).
- **Core assumption:** L2‑accented speech often exhibits different speaking rates or rhythm than native speech; explicitly modeling a global duration ratio mitigates unnatural timing when preserving or scaling duration.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “A flow‑matching Duration Ratio Predictor… adjusts the total duration to better match the native rhythm.” [section 3.3] Flow matching objective and conditioning. [corpus] Moderate: CosyAccent also uses a duration ratio predictor; the concept is evidenced, though DP’s implementation differs.
- **Break condition:** If duration differences are primarily local (phoneme‑level) rather than global, a single scalar ratio may be insufficient, leading to unnatural timing in some segments.

## Foundational Learning
- **Self‑Supervised Speech Tokens (e.g., WavLM + k‑means):** Why needed: Provides discrete, phonetic‑rich units that enable applying text‑style diffusion models to speech; token quality directly impacts CTP’s ability to find shared tokens and DLM’s generation fidelity. Quick check: If the token vocabulary is too small (e.g., 256), would the tokens be fine‑grained enough to distinguish accented vs. native pronunciations? What evidence from the paper supports their choice of 1024 clusters?
- **Masked Diffusion Language Models (e.g., LLaDA):** Why needed: Forms the backbone of DLM‑AN’s token generation, allowing parallel, iterative refinement conditioned on content. Unlike autoregressive models, it supports arbitrary initialization (including partial token reuse). Quick check: How does the loss function (Eq. 5) with 1/λ reweighting ensure stable training compared to uniform cross‑entropy? What would happen if the masking rate λ were too high during training?
- **CTC‑based Phonemic Guidance:** Why needed: Encourages the token encoder’s output representations to align with phonetic content, making them suitable conditioning for accent normalization. Without this, content features might capture speaker or channel information instead. Quick check: The paper attaches a linear projection head to the token encoder and computes CTC loss against phoneme labels (Eq. 11). If the CTC loss weight β₃ were set to 0, what negative effect might this have on accent control?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** SSL Tokenizer (WavLM layer‑22 + online k‑means, 1024 clusters) → Token Encoder (Transformer with relative positional embeddings + CTC phoneme head) → content features c → {Common Token Predictor (CTP, Transformer sequence tagger), Duration Ratio Predictor (DP, DiT with flow matching)} → target token sequence initialization (length from DP, tokens from CTP threshold τ) → DLM Decoder (masked diffusion Transformer, T=32 greedy steps, CFG) → final token sequence → Speech Synthesizer (flow‑matching DiT + HiFT vocoder, two‑way CFG) → waveform.

**Critical Path:** The system processes an input waveform: (1) extract WavLM features and quantize to tokens; (2) encode tokens into content features; (3) predict token‑level commonality (CTP) and global duration ratio (DP); (4) initialize a masked target token sequence of appropriate length, reusing high‑confidence source tokens; (5) iteratively unmask all positions using the DLM decoder; (6) convert the final token sequence to a Mel‑spectrogram via the synthesizer, conditioned on a speaker embedding; (7) generate waveform with HiFT.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Discrete vs. continuous tokens:* Discrete tokens enable LCS‑based CTP and stable diffusion but may lose fine‑grained prosody; continuous flow‑matching (e.g., CosyAccent) avoids discretization artifacts but lacks a direct accent‑strength knob.  
- *CFG usage:* Classifier‑free guidance improves content preservation but may over‑smooth accent variations if set too high.  
- *Pretraining data:* Pretraining on native‑only Emilia‑EN aids generalization but might limit reconstruction of heavily accented phonemes unseen during pretraining.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- High WER (>15%) with low ACT reduction → CTP failing to identify shared tokens or DLM decoder improperly conditioned.  
- Accent strength not varying with τ → CTP poorly calibrated (all scores near 0 or 1).  
- Duration artifacts (gapped/choppy speech) → DP ratio inaccurate or synthesizer not adhering to duration.  
- Repeated tokens during decoding → Greedy unmasking causing loops; may need corrective diffusion.  
- Speaker identity not preserved despite token reuse → synthesizer timbre conditioning or CFG weights mis‑set.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. **CTP threshold sweep:** Vary τ from 0.0 to 1.0 and plot ∆PPG (to source) and ∆PPG (to target) as in Fig 6; expect monotonic trend: lower τ → higher similarity to source accent.  
2. **Duration ratio robustness:** Set target duration ratio to {0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5} and measure WER (Fig 7); DLM‑AN should degrade gracefully vs. baselines that fail at ratios <1.0.  
3. **Ablation study:** Disable CFG (w_DLM=0) and/or remove Emilia‑EN pretraining (train only on fine‑tuning data). Measure WER and SECS (Table 2); expect WER increase without CFG and without pretraining.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can we improve the token encoder’s robustness to heavy L2 accents without relying on phoneme supervision that may propagate errors?  
- **Basis in paper:** The authors identify that “the current pipeline relies on a recognition‑based token encoder for phoneme supervision, whose errors can propagate and degrade conversion quality for heavily accented inputs.”  
- **Why unresolved:** The phoneme‑supervised encoder is brittle under strong accents; errors in predicted phonemes lead to downstream degradation in accent normalization, limiting performance for speakers with heavy accents.  
- **What evidence would resolve:** Demonstrate that alternative encoder designs (e.g., self‑supervised training with accent‑invariant objectives, or training on L2‑accented data without phoneme labels) produce more robust content representations and yield lower WER and higher subjective quality on heavily accented test sets compared to the current approach.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What corrective mechanisms can effectively prevent repeated token pronunciations during the iterative unmasking process of discrete diffusion for accent normalization?  
- **Basis in paper:** The authors note a future research direction: “repeated pronunciations can arise during unmasking; incorporating corrective mechanisms may mitigate such artifacts.”  
- **Why unresolved:** The greedy iterative decoding in DLM‑AN may generate the same token at different positions consecutively, harming fluency and potentially reducing intelligibility.  
- **What evidence would resolve:** Introduce methods such as confidence‑based non‑maximum suppression, token rewriting, or post‑hoc repetition filtering; evaluate reduction in repetition frequency and improvements in WER and naturalness scores.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does incorporating L2‑accented speech data into the training of the SSL tokenizer and flow‑matching synthesizer improve accent normalization robustness?  
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state that “the SSL tokenizer and synthesizer are trained on native‑only data, potentially limiting reconstruction of highly accented speech; incorporating L2‑accented data could improve robustness.”  
- **Why unresolved:** Training exclusively on native speech may bias the discrete token space toward L1 pronunciations, making it harder to accurately represent and reconstruct heavily L2‑accented utterances.  
- **What evidence would resolve:** Train the tokenizer and synthesizer on a mixture of native and L2‑accented speech (e.g., L2‑ARCTIC) and compare objective metrics (WER, ∆PPG) and subjective scores (naturalness, speaker similarity) against the current native‑only models, especially on challenging accents.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Would replacing the K‑Means discrete tokenizer with a learned codebook (e.g., from a VQ‑VAE) yield better phonetic discriminability and finer‑grained accent strength control?  
- **Basis in paper:** The authors suggest: “replacing the K‑Means tokenizer with learned discrete codebooks (e.g., vector quantization [70]) may yield better phonetic discriminability and further enhance controllability.”  
- **Why unresolved:** K‑Means provides a static quantization that may not optimally separate phonetic categories or reflect subtle accent variations; learned codebooks can adapt to the data distribution and potentially create more meaningful discrete units.  
- **What evidence would resolve:** Implement a VQ‑VAE based tokenizer trained on the same corpora; compare the phonetic consistency of tokens (e.g., using phoneme classification accuracy) and examine whether the accent strength control via the CTP threshold produces smoother, more consistent changes in output accent compared to K‑Means.

## Limitations
- Tokenization: 1024 k‑means clusters may lack phonetic precision, affecting CTP accuracy and accent control.
- CTP: LCS‑based labeling may misalign with perceptual “common” tokens; it is unclear whether CTP is pretrained on native‑only data.
- Duration predictor: A single scalar ratio may not capture local rhythm differences, causing unnaturalness in some segments.
