# Evaluating Austrian A-Level German Essays with Large Language Models for Automated Essay Scoring
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06066
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06066
- **Reference count:** 35
- **Primary result:** The best-performing model (Llama3.3 70b) achieved only 40.6% sub-dimension agreement and 32.8% exact final-grade match with human raters, indicating insufficient accuracy for autonomous high-stakes grading.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluated four open-weight large language models (DeepSeek, Qwen, Mixtral, Llama3.3) for automated scoring of Austrian A-level German essays (n=101). Using rubrics and prompting techniques such as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and few-shot learning, the authors tested various context strategies and chain-of-thought reasoning. The top model, Llama3.3 70b, reached at most 40.6% agreement on sub-dimensions and 32.8% exact final-grade match. Other models exhibited severe instability or lacked grading diversity. The results suggest current LLMs are not accurate or reliable enough for autonomous grading in this high-stakes context.

## Method Summary
The dataset comprised 101 anonymized student essays from three Austrian A-level German text types (Commentary, Letter to the Editor, Literary Interpretation), originally scanned and OCR-processed. No model fine-tuning was conducted; instead, the study applied zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought prompting to four open-weight LLMs (DeepSeek-R1 32b, Qwen3 30b, Mixtral 8x7b, Llama3.3 70b). Grading rubrics covered four dimensions (Content, Structure, Language Norms, Style/Expression) with task-specific weightings and aggregation rules. Context strategies included fixed exemplars (best, average, worst), similarity-based retrieval, and grade-coverage ranges. Performance was assessed via Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Pearson correlation, and exact grade accuracy.

## Key Results
- Llama3.3 70b achieved maximum 40.6% sub-dimension agreement and 32.8% exact final-grade match.
- Few-shot prompting with mixed context lengths (Best-Average-Worst for longer Task 1, All-Grades for shorter Task 2) optimized scores per text type.
- DeepSeek, Qwen, and Mixtral were unusable due to high failure rates (invalid outputs) or extreme grade compression (e.g., always 3).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Fixed exemplars as grading anchors**
- **Claim:** Using fixed exemplars (best, average, worst) via RAG provides stable grading anchors for LLMs.
- **Mechanism:** Three pre-selected, human-graded essays representing the extremes and midpoint are included as fixed context in every prompt, giving the LLM concrete reference points to calibrate its scoring against the rubric.
- **Core assumption:** Human grading consistency can be communicated through a small set of exemplars that define the scale's boundaries.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and section 4.2 describe the fixed context; corpus lacks strong direct citations.
- **Break condition:** If exemplars do not cover the full rubric spectrum or the LLM cannot map its internal scoring to the exemplars' grades.

**Mechanism 2: Task-dependent context length optimization**
- **Claim:** Optimizing few-shot context length based on text type (long vs. short) improves scoring accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** For longer texts (Task 1), a smaller few-shot set (3 examples: best, average, worst) prevents context overflow and focuses on key criteria. For shorter texts (Task 2), a larger set (5 examples covering grades 1-5) provides finer calibration.
- **Core assumption:** Optimal context length is task-dependent; longer texts need fewer examples to avoid noise, shorter texts need more to establish a reliable scale.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states mixed context lengths optimized performance; sections 4.3 and 5.2.1 show Task 1 performed worse with All-Grade context, while Task 2 favored it.
- **Break condition:** Incorrect text length classification or rubrics varying independently of length.

**Mechanism 3: Chain-of-Thought accuracy-consistency trade-off**
- **Claim:** Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning increases absolute grade accuracy for short texts but reduces inter-rater consistency (QWK).
- **Mechanism:** CoT prompts the LLM to generate intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., assess content, then structure) before outputting a grade. For simpler, shorter texts, this forces methodical rubric application, leading to more correct final grades. However, the added steps introduce variability in criteria weighting, harming consistency metrics.
- **Core assumption:** Explicit reasoning mitigates heuristic scoring for straightforward tasks but adds stochasticity for complex evaluation.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes CoT improved absolute accuracy for short texts but not consistency; section 5.2.1/5.2.2 reports increased correct grades for Task 2 but lower QWK.
- **Break condition:** CoT steps misaligned with rubric dimensions or unstable internal reasoning across similar texts.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Rubric-Based Multidimensional Evaluation**
- **Why needed:** The system must grade across four dimensions per task, with specific weightings and aggregation rules. Understanding how dimensions interact (e.g., a single "5" in a mandatory sub-dimension can fail the entire exam) is crucial.
- **Quick check:** If a student receives a "2" in Content and "4" in Structure for Task 1, but a "5" in Language Norms for the same task, what is the likely outcome for K1 and the final grade per the rubric rules in section 3.2?

**Concept: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for Calibration**
- **Why needed:** The LLM lacks inherent knowledge of Austrian A-level standards. RAG supplies human-graded exemplars to anchor scoring, but retrieval strategy critically impacts performance.
- **Quick check:** Why might retrieving the "most similar" past essay by vector similarity fail to provide an effective grade anchor compared to a fixed "best-average-worst" set?

**Concept: In-Context Learning (Few-Shot vs. Zero-Shot)**
- **Why needed:** The study pivoted from zero-shot (floor performance) to few-shot, where the model iteratively grades examples and is corrected. This enabled any grade diversity and was the primary technique for improvement.
- **Quick check:** What is a key risk of few-shot prompting with dynamic, similarity-based retrieval (Most-similar-matches) versus a fixed exemplar set, as observed in section 4.3?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Processor → Rubric & Exemplar Database → RAG Retriever → Prompt Orchestrator → LLM Inference Engine → Output Parser & Validator

**Critical path:** Text Input → Type/Task ID → RAG/Few-shot Context Selection → Prompt Assembly → LLM Call → JSON Parse → Grade Calculation → Output.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Accuracy vs. Latency:* Best-Average-Worst (3 exemplars) is faster than Range-of-examples (5-10). Adding CoT increases latency 3-4×.
- *Stability vs. Diversity:* Fixed context (Best-Average-Worst) yields stable but narrow grade distributions (mostly 2-4). Dynamic retrieval (Range) increases diversity but risks skew (e.g., all 1s if an extreme dominates).
- *Compute vs. Model Size:* Only Llama3.3 70b was viable; smaller models (DeepSeek, Qwen, Mixtral) failed to produce diverse or valid outputs.

**Failure signatures:**
- *Output Invalid:* LLM returns non-JSON or missing fields (Mixtral 44%, DeepSeek 50%, Qwen 11% failure). *Action:* Implement strict JSON validation with retries.
- *Grading Collapse:* Model outputs nearly all the same grade (e.g., Mixtral always 3). *Action:* After 10 samples, check grade variance; if <0.5, switch model or prompt.
- *Context Overflow:* Long text + many exemplars + CoT exceeds context limit. *Action:* Enforce token limit on exemplars; prefer Best-Average-Worst for Task 1.
- *Rubric Misapplication:* LLM uses wrong dimension weights or ignores task-specific rules (e.g., fails K3/1 but passes overall). *Action:* Inject explicit decision rules into system prompt (e.g., "If any dimension in K1 is 5, K1=5").

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline Zero-Shot:** Send only rubric + candidate text. Expected: low accuracy (<25%), grade range 2-4, QWK ~0.2-0.3.
2. **RAG Best-Average-Worst:** Attach 3 fixed exemplars (grades 1,3,5) for both tasks. Expected: improved QWK (~0.4-0.5), stable but non-diverse grades.
3. **Few-Shot Mixed Context:** Task 1 (long) uses 3 exemplars (Best-Avg-Worst); Task 2 (short) uses 5 exemplars (all grades). Iterate candidate through exemplar sequence (1→3→5). Expected: highest Task 1 dimension QWK (~0.5) and final-grade accuracy (~33%).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scaling and multiple iterations:** How does using larger LLMs and running multiple inference iterations per method impact accuracy, consistency, and efficiency? The study was limited to Llama3.3 70b and single runs due to computational constraints.
2. **Confidence estimation:** Can confidence scores (majority voting, log-prob analysis) reliably indicate LLM grading uncertainty and correlate with human grader confidence? No confidence scoring was implemented.
3. **Bias and fairness:** What biases (gender, socio-economic, linguistic) do LLMs introduce or amplify in German AES, and how can fairness be ensured? No fairness audit was conducted; dataset lacks demographic metadata.
4. **Generalization across exam formats:** To what extent do dataset limitations (only 3/7 text types, OCR artifacts, no handwriting) constrain generalizability to the full A-level spectrum? The study acknowledges these gaps but cannot assess their impact.
5. **Multi-grader ground truth:** How might inter-rater variability among human graders affect perceived LLM accuracy? Ground truth came from a single rater, obscuring whether LLM disagreement reflects error or alternative valid judgment.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility blockers:** Exact prompt templates, JSON schemas, and exemplar text identifiers are unreleased, preventing faithful replication.
- **Generalizability:** Findings are based on a small, specialized corpus (n=101, Austrian A-level German) and may not transfer to other languages, educational systems, or essay types.
- **Model scope:** Only one model (Llama3.3 70b) proved viable; results may not hold for newer models or fine-tuned variants.

## Confidence
- **Main conclusion (LLMs not ready for autonomous high-stakes grading):** High
- **Mechanism 1 (Fixed Best-Avg-Worst anchors improve stability):** Medium
- **Mechanism 2 (Optimized context length per text type):** Medium
- **Mechanism 3 (CoT reasoning trade-off):** Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt & Schema Reconstruction:** Derive exact system/user prompts and JSON schemas from the paper; validate with a pilot (n=5) to match reported performance ballpark.
2. **Exemplar Sensitivity Ablation:** Test robustness of the Best-Average-Worst mechanism by substituting different grade-1, -3, and -5 exemplars; measure variance in QWK and accuracy.
3. **Cross-Dataset Generalization Probe:** Apply the best setup (Llama3.3 70b, Few-shot Mixed Context) to a public German AES dataset (e.g., GermEval) to test if the ≈40% agreement ceiling holds outside the Austrian A-level context.
