# Evaluating LLM-Generated Lessons from the Language Learning Students' Perspective: A Short Case Study on Duolingo

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.18873
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18873
- **Reference count:** 23
- **Primary result:** LLM-generated language lessons (e.g., Duolingo) lack profession-specific contexts; a survey of software engineers shows general scenarios are effective for foundational learning but work-related ones are rare and valued, indicating a need for personalized, domain-adapted content.

## Executive Summary
This short case study evaluates the relevance of Duolingo's AI-generated language lesson scenarios for learners aiming for professional fluency. Surveying five Filipino software engineers enrolled in a Korean class, the research finds that general, everyday scenarios (greetings, daily conversations) are frequently encountered and perceived as effective for building foundational grammar, vocabulary, and cultural knowledge. In contrast, work-related, domain-specific scenarios are rarely encountered despite being considered a crucial bridge to professional fluency due to their inclusion of specialized vocabulary and pragmatic patterns. Participants' suggested ideal lesson topics diverge significantly, underscoring the necessity for personalized content generation that adapts to individual professional contexts and goals, not just proficiency level.

## Method Summary
A cross-sectional survey was administered via Qualtrics to five Filipino software engineers actively using Duolingo to learn Korean and working in a Korean-language professional environment. The survey comprised nine questions: Likert-scale items on the frequency and perceived impact (enhancement/hindrance) of general and work-related scenarios, and open-ended queries for explanations and topic suggestions. Data analysis involved descriptive statistics for quantitative items and thematic qualitative analysis of open-ended responses to address two research questions comparing perceptions of scenario types and their effect on learning toward professional fluency.

## Key Results
- **General scenarios** (e.g., greetings, daily conversations) are encountered frequently and are perceived as effective for building foundational language competence (grammar, basic vocabulary, cultural knowledge).
- **Work-related scenarios** are encountered rarely but are perceived as highly valuable for bridging to professional fluency by introducing domain-specific vocabulary and professional communicative patterns.
- **Suggested lesson topics** diverge across participants (e.g., some want more fundamentals, others want negotiating deadlines or technical jargon), indicating a strong need for personalized, domain-specific content alongside general scenarios.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: General Scenarios Build Foundational Competence**
- **Claim:** General, relatable scenarios (e.g., greetings, daily conversations) are perceived as effective for building foundational language competence (grammar, basic vocabulary, cultural knowledge) for novice learners.
- **Mechanism:** These scenarios provide comprehensible, low-stakes input aligned with the learner's immediate lived experience, reducing cognitive load and allowing focus on core linguistic structures, consistent with Krashen's Input Hypothesis (`i+1`).
- **Evidence:** Participants explicitly linked the relatability and frequency of general scenarios to easier grasp of foundational concepts; the paper invokes Krashen's theory to underpin the pedagogical design.

**Mechanism 2: Work-Related Scenarios Bridge to Professional Fluency**
- **Claim:** Work-related, domain-specific scenarios are perceived as a necessary bridge to professional fluency but are currently encountered too rarely.
- **Mechanism:** These scenarios introduce specialized vocabulary and pragmatic patterns (e.g., discussing CI/CD pipelines) absent in general corpora, building *professional* communicative competence separate from general sociolinguistic competence (Hymes).
- **Evidence:** Respondents noted the scarcity of technical jargon in lessons (e.g., "CI/CD pipeline") and explicitly stated that work-related content helps bridge the gap toward professional fluency.

**Mechanism 3: Divergent Suggestions Indicate Need for Personalization**
- **Claim:** Divergent suggestions for ideal lesson scenarios indicate a strong need for personalized, learner-goal-oriented content generation beyond difficulty adjustment.
- **Mechanism:** Learners' professional contexts and immediate goals vary significantly; a one-size-fits-all scenario set fails to capture this heterogeneity. Personalization must incorporate learner *context* (profession, goals), not just proficiency level.
- **Evidence:** Aggregate analysis of suggested topics showed significant divergence across participants, leading the paper to propose personalized, domain-specific lesson scenarios and critique Duolingo's Birdbrain for adjusting only difficulty, not content domain.

## Foundational Learning
- **Input Hypothesis (Krashen)**  
  *Why needed:* Explains why scenario-based lessons work: they provide "comprehensible input" (`i+1`). The paper's design assumes this mechanism for effective acquisition.  
  *Quick check:* Are the generated scenarios understandable to the learner with some new elements, or are they too difficult (frustration) or too easy (stagnation)?

- **Communicative Competence (Hymes)**  
  *Why needed:* Defines the target ("professional fluency") as more than grammatical accuracy; it includes appropriateness to *social/professional context*. This justifies the study's focus on scenario *type* (general vs. work-related).  
  *Quick check:* Does a learner's success depend on using language that is not only correct but also appropriate for a specific professional setting (e.g., a team meeting vs. a client presentation)?

- **ITS Student Model (Diagnostic Function)**  
  *Why needed:* To enable personalization (Mechanism 3), the system must diagnose not just proficiency (Birdbrain) but also learner *context* (profession, use-case). This is a missing capability identified in the paper.  
  *Quick check:* Does the system know *what* the learner needs to talk about in the target language (their job), or only *how well* they currently speak it?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** User Interaction → Student Model (proficiency) & Context Profiler (profession/goals) → Expert Knowledge Module (LLM + designers, prompt-based generation) → Personalized Scenario → Tutoring Module (feedback) → Updates Student & Context Models.
- **Critical path:** User interacts with system → Student Model infers proficiency, Context Profiler identifies professional context → both inform Expert Module's prompt (e.g., "Generate a [beginner] lesson about [CI/CD pipelines] for a [software engineer].") → generated personalized scenario → Tutoring Module provides feedback → Student and Context Models updated.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - *Breadth vs. Depth:* Cover many general scenarios vs. deeply train a few high-value professional ones.
  - *Profile Accuracy vs. Friction:* Detailed context profiling improves relevance but may deter sign-up.
  - *Content Freshness:* Profession-specific jargon evolves rapidly; expert module prompts need constant updating.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Learner stalls at "false fluency": excels in app but cannot discuss work.
  - Low engagement from professionals who see no relevance (high drop-off in work-related lesson attempts).
  - High cost of generating niche scenarios with minimal payoff if few learners share that niche.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Survey Expansion:** Replicate study with n>100 across multiple professions (engineering, marketing, finance) to map heterogeneity of "professional fluency" needs and validate divergent suggestions finding.
  2. **Prompt Engineering Study:** For a single profession (e.g., software engineers), manually craft and evaluate LLM prompts that inject domain-specific vocabulary (CI/CD, debugging, sprint planning) into beginner/intermediate lessons vs. standard prompts. Measure perceived relevance and engagement.
  3. **Comparative Longitudinal Pilot:** Between-subjects study with 30 software engineers. Group A uses standard Duolingo. Group B uses a prototype that adds a "profession" selector and injects relevant vocabulary via prompt. Control for time-on-task. Primary metric: pre/post "Professional Context Vocabulary Recognition" test; secondary: self-efficacy in work-related Korean communication.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do language learners' perceptions of general vs. work-related scenarios differentially impact their progress toward professional fluency in a target language? The study's small sample provides anecdotal insights but insufficient quantitative data to establish causal or generalizable relationships between scenario type and fluency outcomes. Evidence needed: large-scale, controlled study measuring proficiency gains for learners using curricula with varying proportions of general vs. work-related content, correlated with self-reported perceptions.
2. What is the minimum effective threshold of domain-specific, work-related lesson content needed to bridge the gap between general conversational fluency and professional fluency for learners in specialized fields (e.g., software engineering)? The paper notes learners encounter work-related scenarios much less frequently and perceive them as valuable, but does not quantify the needed amount. Evidence needed: an A/B testing framework where groups receive different percentages of domain-specific lessons (e.g., 0%, 10%, 30%), with professional communication skills assessed via role-plays or writing tasks.
3. Can an ITS leverage a user's professional domain (e.g., via resume parsing or self-declared occupation) to automatically generate linguistically accurate and pedagogically sound lesson scenarios that are both relevant and effective for language acquisition? The paper proposes personalized, domain-specific scenarios and mentions future work on fine-tuning an LLM for the "technology industry," but feasibility is untested. Risks include generating pragmatically awkward or culturally insensitive scenarios. Evidence needed: development and user evaluation of a prototype system that ingests professional domain data (e.g., job descriptions) and generates lesson pipelines, followed by expert review for accuracy and learner studies on utility.
4. What is the longitudinal impact of a personalized, domain-adaptive language curriculum on learner retention, motivation, and eventual attainment of professional fluency compared to a standard general-scenarios curriculum? The current study is short-term with no longitudinal data. Hypothesized benefits of personalization (e.g., increased motivation leading to higher completion) are not empirically validated. Evidence needed: a multi-month or year-long randomized controlled trial tracking metrics like lesson completion, skill decay, self-efficacy, and final proficiency test scores between personalized and standard groups.
5. How should an ITS balance the pedagogical sequencing of foundational general-scenario lessons with higher-level domain-specific lessons to optimize the learning trajectory for professional fluency? Respondents noted work-related scenarios "are still not applicable at the beginner level," suggesting a sequential dependency. The paper proposes maintaining foundational support through general scenarios alongside domain-specific ones but does not specify integration logic. Evidence needed: learning analytics from an ITS that dynamically varies the introduction point of domain-specific content for learners at different proficiency levels, analyzing outcomes for knowledge retention, skill transfer, and frustration.

## Limitations
- Small sample (n=5) from a single company limits generalizability.
- Ambiguous definition of "scenario" for participants may affect response validity.
- Reliance on self-reported data; no objective learning outcome measures.
- Weak external evidence from cited corpus (low FMR, no direct citations for language-specific mechanisms).

## Confidence
- **General scenarios build foundation:** Medium (direct quotes + Krashen support, but no gain data).
- **Work-related scenarios bridge to professional fluency:** Low-Medium (participants noted rarity/value, but evidence thin; no experimental validation).
- **Divergent suggestions imply need for personalization:** Medium (clear qualitative diversity, but may reflect small homogeneous group).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the survey with a larger, diverse sample (>100 learners across at least three professions) to validate the heterogeneity of professional fluency needs and the divergence in suggested topics.
2. Conduct a controlled experiment comparing standard Duolingo vs. a prototype with profession-tailored vocabulary injection for software engineers, measuring professional vocabulary retention and self-efficacy in work-related communication.
3. Develop and pilot a context-profiler (onboarding survey + interaction mining) to test its impact on scenario relevance, learner engagement, and perceived usefulness for professional contexts.
