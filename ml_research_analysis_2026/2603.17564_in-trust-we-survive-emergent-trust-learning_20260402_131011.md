# In Trust We Survive: Emergent Trust Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.17564
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17564
- Reference count: 25
- Primary result: ETL reduces conflict and resource depletion while maintaining individual returns, achieves >90% survival in Tower dilemma, and attains highest success rate in IPD tournament.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Emergent Trust Learning (ETL), a lightweight plug-in algorithm for enabling cooperation among self-interested agents in competitive multi-agent environments without explicit cooperative rewards or communication. ETL equips each agent with an internal trust state, updated from local observations and individual rewards, which modulates memory, exploration, and action selection. Across three testbeds—a grid resource world, a hierarchical Tower social dilemma, and the Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma—ETL demonstrates reduced conflict and resource depletion, >90% survival rates, and superior performance against classic and adversarial strategies.

## Method Summary
ETL augments standard MARL agents (e.g., tabular Q-Learning) with three core components: dual memory buffers (short-term and long-term), a scalar trust state updated via locally derived support and dissatisfaction signals, and exploration control based on trust variance. At each timestep, an agent observes its local state, retrieves current trust, and selects actions via a trust-modulated policy: exploit known cooperative strategies when trust is high and stable, otherwise explore or act defensively. After receiving a reward, the agent updates its trust using a weighted combination of newly computed support/dissatisfaction and historical trust, and stores the experience in both STM and LTM. Exploration rate ε_t is increased if recent trust variance σ_T exceeds a threshold, otherwise decayed slowly. ETL requires only local information and no inter-agent communication.

## Key Results
- In a grid resource world, ETL reduces conflict frequency and long-term resource depletion while maintaining competitive individual returns.
- In the hierarchical Tower social dilemma, ETL achieves and sustains >90% survival rates and robustly recovers cooperation after enforced greedy phases.
- In Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, ETL attains the highest overall success rate against a tournament of classic and adversarial strategies, maintaining cooperation with reciprocal opponents while avoiding long-term exploitation by defectors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Trust-Modulated Action Selection
- **Claim:** ETL uses a compact trust state to bias action selection toward moderate, cooperative behaviors in competitive settings.
- **Mechanism:** An internal trust value T_ij is updated each timestep from locally observable signals (s_t^i: support/favorability, d_t^i: dissatisfaction/harm). This scalar trust directly influences policy: high trust (>0.5) and stable situation (q_t^i > θ_Q) promotes exploitation of learned cooperative strategies, while low trust increases exploration or defensive actions.
- **Core assumption:** Locally observable outcomes meaningfully correlate with the cooperative or exploitative intent of unseen partners, allowing trust to emerge without direct observation or communication.
- **Evidence anchors:** The formal trust update rule (Eq. 2) and action selection rule (Eq. 4) define this modulation; experiments show reduced conflict and stable resource use.
- **Break condition:** If local signals are poor proxies for partner behavior (e.g., environmental stochasticity masks true outcomes), trust updates become misaligned, potentially causing inappropriate cooperation with defectors or unnecessary conflict with cooperators.

### Mechanism 2: Dual-Memory Stabilization
- **Claim:** Separating short-term memory (STM) and long-term memory (LTM) allows agents to adapt to recent fluctuations while retaining knowledge of sustainable strategies.
- **Mechanism:** STM tracks the most recent N_s experiences for quick adjustment to changing conditions. LTM aggregates all experiences to inform trust development and policy refinement. The combined memory feeds into action decisions, allowing agents to reinforce actions that lead to stable, high-trust regimes and discard patterns causing depletion or conflict.
- **Core assumption:** Sustainable cooperation requires balancing responsiveness to new information with persistence of proven strategies; a single memory buffer would either be too volatile or too rigid.
- **Evidence anchors:** STM/LTM buffers and their roles are formally defined (Eq. 1, Fig. 2); ETL agents preserve more resources and maintain lower cooldown fractions, suggesting memory helps identify and repeat non-depleting actions.
- **Break condition:** If the STM window N_s is poorly sized relative to environmental dynamics, agents may react too slowly to sudden exploitation (window too large) or overreact to noise (window too small), destabilizing learned cooperation.

### Mechanism 3: Trust-Stability-Driven Exploration
- **Claim:** Exploration rate ε_t is dynamically adjusted based on trust variance σ_T, enabling agents to explore under uncertainty but exploit in stable, trusted regimes.
- **Mechanism:** Trust variance is computed from recent trust values. If variance exceeds threshold θ_T, exploration increases (up to 0.9) to encourage policy diversification. Otherwise, exploration decays slowly (×0.995) to a minimum ε_min. This links exploration directly to the stability of social expectations.
- **Core assumption:** In competitive multi-agent systems, high uncertainty about partner behavior (high trust variance) warrants more exploration to discover viable strategies, while low variance allows safe exploitation of known cooperative patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:** The update rule for ε_t (Eq. 3) and its grounding in trust variance; after enforced greed, ETL's success rate recovers rapidly, suggesting exploration adapts to re-establish cooperation after trust collapse.
- **Break condition:** If trust variance is a noisy indicator of true strategic uncertainty (e.g., due to sparse interactions), exploration may be mis-tuned—excessive in stable environments (wasting steps) or insufficient in volatile ones (failing to find new strategies).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
STM/LTM -> Trust Update Module -> Trust State -> (Exploration Controller & Policy Augmentation) -> Action Selection

**Critical path:**  
1. Initialize trust (e.g., 0), STM/LTM empty.  
2. Each step: observe local state s_t, retrieve current trust T_ij.  
3. Select action via Eq. 4 (trust/exploration-biased).  
4. Receive reward r_t, compute support s_t^i and dissatisfaction d_t^i from local features.  
5. Update trust via Eq. 2; push (s_t,a_t,r_t) to STM; periodically sync STM→LTM.  
6. Update ε_t based on recent trust variance σ_T.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Simplicity vs. Expressiveness: Scalar trust and linear updates vs. more complex models.  
- Local vs. Global Signals: ETL relies only on local observations; using global signals breaks decentrality but could improve alignment.  
- Parameterization: Fixed parameters (α,β,γ,θ_T,θ_Q) in paper; adaptive tuning may improve robustness but adds complexity.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Persistent low trust (T_ij → -1) leading to universal defection/conflict.  
- Unstable exploration (ε_t oscillates wildly) due to noisy σ_T or inappropriate STM size.  
- Resource depletion despite rising trust, indicating trust is gamed or LTM overfits.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Unit test in Grid World: run minimal 2-agent scenario; verify trust rises when agents avoid same tile, conflict count drops, cooldown fraction decreases.  
2. Ablation in Tower: disable ETL's exploration adaptation (fix ε=0.1); test if cooperation still recovers after enforced greed.  
3. Signal mapping stress test in IPD: replace s_t/d_t with noisy observations (e.g., flip payoff sign with 20% probability); measure success rate against Tit-for-Tat.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability to large, heterogeneous populations**: The paper identifies scaling ETL to massive multiplayer online games as future work. Current experiments used small fixed populations, so trust dynamics with dynamic entry/exit are unclear. Resolving this requires empirical evaluation in simulated large-scale environments with churn.  
2. **Integration with deep MARL**: The paper suggests combining ETL with deep architectures (e.g., QMIX, MADDPG). As ETL was only tested with tabular methods, its compatibility with function approximation remains unknown. Evidence would come from implementing ETL as a plug-in module in deep MARL benchmarks (StarCraft II, etc.).  
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The paper does not analyze sensitivity to key parameters (α, β, γ, θ_T, θ_Q, N_s). This limits understanding of robustness. Systematic parameter sweeps across environments would be needed.  
4. **Adaptation to non-stationary environments**: All testbeds had stationary mechanics; real-world environments often change. It is unknown if ETL can re-equilibrate after dynamics shift. Evidence requires injecting controlled changes mid-experiment and measuring adaptation speed and recovery.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter dependency: Unreported critical values may be environment-specific, affecting generalizability.
- Signal design vagueness: Unclear definitions of s_t and d_t in some testbeds limit reproducibility.
- External validity: Claims rely solely on paper-internal experiments; lacking direct comparisons with state-of-the-art trust-based MARL reduces confidence in novelty/robustness.
