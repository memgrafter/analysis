# Asking Forever: Universal Activations Behind Turn Amplification in Conversational LLMs

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.17778
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17778
*   **Reference count:** 40
*   **Primary result:** Query-independent activation subspaces can be steered to induce severe turn amplification (up to ~10× more turns, 200× more input tokens) via minimal modifications (LoRA rank-1, 25 bit-flips).

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies "turn amplification" as a novel failure mode where conversational LLMs systematically prolong interactions through persistent clarification-seeking. The authors discover that this behavior is governed by query-independent, universal activation subspaces. By optimizing linear direction vectors in the residual stream using synthetic data, they can induce substantial turn amplification across four open-source models (3B–22B) via activation steering. The effect is alarmingly robust, transferable via extremely sparse weight-level attacks (LoRA, bit-flips), and resistant to conventional prompt-level defenses. The work reveals a latent, brittle representational mechanism for conversational dynamics that poses a systemic cost-amplification risk.

## Method Summary
The method involves three stages: 1) **Synthetic data generation**: Using a capable LLM (Qwen2.5-32B), 5,000 multi-turn conversations are created where the assistant is *instructed* to never answer the original query, instead asking only clarification questions. 2) **Direction optimization**: For a target model, layer-specific steering vectors `{v_ℓ}` are learned via gradient descent to minimize the negative log-likelihood of the model generating the turn-prolonging assistant responses from the synthetic data when the vectors are added to the residual stream. 3) **Evaluation**: The learned vectors are applied additively to the final token of each user turn during inference. A multi-turn auditing framework, using an LLM-as-a-Judge (Qwen2.5-32B) and a simulated user, measures conversation length (number of assistant turns) until the original query is judged complete, up to a maximum of 9–10 turns.

## Key Results
*   Activation steering increased the average number of assistant turns by **8.6×** (up to **9.9×**) and input tokens by **88.2×** (up to **200.1×**) across four models.
*   The effect is implantable via minimal modifications: **LoRA fine-tuning (rank=1)** and **targeted bit-flips (25 weights)** each induced up to **4.6–9.2×** more turns.
*   Existing prompt-level defenses (defensive system messages, few-shot prompting) proved **largely ineffective** against this interaction-level threat.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Query-Independent Universal Subspaces
*   **Claim:** The model's internal representations contain linear directions that, when activated, increase the probability of generating clarification-seeking responses, thereby prolonging multi-turn interactions.
*   **Mechanism:** These directions are discovered by optimizing a loss that encourages turn-amplifying outputs on synthetic conversations. The core assumption is that clarification-seeking behavior is encoded in a low-dimensional, linear subspace of the residual stream and is consistent across diverse prompts and tasks.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   Abstract: "...identifies query-independent, universal activation subspaces associated with this behavior using gradient-based direction optimization."
    *   Section 3.2: Describes the turn-amplifying direction optimization and synthetic data generation process.
    *   Section 4.3: Shows learned directions align more strongly with natural activations during turn-prolonged conversations than with default conversations.
*   **Break condition:** If the model's architecture or training data lacks coherent linear representations for conversational dynamics, the discovered directions may not generalize across prompts.

### Mechanism 2: Efficacy of Early-Layer Steering
*   **Claim:** Activation steering at early-to-intermediate layers is particularly effective for inducing turn amplification.
*   **Mechanism:** Adding the learned direction vectors to the residual stream at intermediate positions (e.g., layers 0–20) shifts the distribution toward clarification-seeking tokens. Early interventions propagate through many subsequent layers, exerting broad influence. Steer at the last token of a user turn biases the next assistant response.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   Section 4.3: "Directions identified in earlier layers are more effective. Steering exclusively at layer 0 achieves 85.9–94.5% of the performance observed under full multi‑layer steering."
    *   Figure 3: Quantifies degradation in turn amplification when steering only at specific layers.
*   **Break condition:** If the turn-amplifying computation is concentrated in the final layers or involves nonlinear interactions across many layers, early-layer additive steering may fail.

### Mechanism 3: Brittleness and Sparse Attack Feasibility
*   **Claim:** Turn-amplifying behavior can be implanted via extremely sparse weight-level modifications (e.g., LoRA rank-1 or ≤25 bit-flips), indicating a low-rank, brittle representational basis.
*   **Mechanism:** The activation directions are linearly realizable by weight-level changes. Thus, training a LoRA adapter with a single rank or flipping a handful of critical bits can shift the model into the turn-amplifying subspace.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   Section 5: "LoRA fine‑tuning with as few as 0.03% of parameters induced up to 9.2× more turns" and "targeted bit‑flips modifying just 25 weights induced up to 4.6× more turns."
    *   Table 2: Shows comparable turn amplification from LoRA (r=1) and bit‑flips relative to full steering.
*   **Break condition:** If the directions are not approximable by low-rank weight updates or the model is robust to individual bit errors, sparse attacks would be ineffective.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** Activation Steering
    *   **Why needed:** The core method uses additive vector interventions in the residual stream to shift model behavior; understanding this mechanism is essential for interpreting how turn amplification is induced and for designing defenses.
    *   **Quick check question:** Why does adding a fixed direction to hidden states at inference time change the model's output distribution?
*   **Concept:** Synthetic Behavior Elicitation
    *   **Why needed:** The paper relies on synthetic multi-turn dialogues where the assistant is explicitly instructed to prolong the conversation—this is how the turn-amplifying directions are trained without natural examples.
    *   **Quick check question:** How does instructing an LLM to generate "clarification‑seeking" responses create a dataset that reveals a generalizable feature direction?
*   **Concept:** Interaction-Level Auditing
    *   **Why needed:** Turn amplification is an interaction-level failure; standard single-turn benchmarks cannot measure it. The paper repurposes QA datasets with an LLM-as-a-judge.
    *   **Quick check question:** What are the key components (judge, simulated user, termination condition) of a framework that evaluates multi‑turn task completion?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Synthetic data generator (Qwen2.5-32B) -> Training conversations -> Direction optimizer (gradient descent on Eq. 2) -> Learned steering vectors `{v_ℓ}` -> Intervention module (applies `{v_ℓ}` at user turn end) -> Evaluated model -> Evaluation loop (with LLM judge & simulated user) -> Turn count / token metrics
*   **Critical path:** 1) Generate high-quality synthetic prolongation dialogues. 2) Optimize steering vectors to converge (monitor loss on validation set). 3) Apply vectors correctly to the final user token during autoregressive evaluation. 4) Ensure judge LLM reliably classifies conversation completeness.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   *Intervention scope:* Steering all layers vs. single layer (effectiveness vs. speed/compute).
    *   *Synthetic data source:* Larger models yield higher-quality prolongation but increase cost; smaller models may fail to follow instructions.
    *   *Evaluation judge:* Must be sufficiently capable to distinguish "complete" vs. "incomplete"; weaker judges distort metrics.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   Direction optimization fails to converge (loss plateaus) → check learning rate, batch size, gradient clipping.
    *   Steering does not increase turns → verify intervention token position; confirm synthetic data quality.
    *   Judge LLM declares "incomplete" after correct answer → improve judge prompt or use a more robust model.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Baseline reproducibility:** Run the auditing framework on the unmodified model to establish normal turn count distribution on a held-out prompt set.
    2.  **Single-layer steering sweep:** For each layer ℓ, optimize and apply steering only at ℓ; plot turn amplification vs. layer index to identify effective intervention zones (as in Figure 3).
    3.  **Direction generalization test:** Apply directions learned on Alpaca to a different dataset (e.g., GSM8K) without retraining; measure turn amplification to confirm query-independence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** Do closed-source commercial LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, Claude) exhibit the same turn amplification vulnerability when subjected to activation steering or fine-tuning attacks? *Basis:* Demonstrations are limited to open-source models.
*   **Open Question 2:** What specific architectural changes or training objectives can prevent turn amplification while preserving helpful conversational behavior? *Basis:* Evaluated defenses (prompt-based) are ineffective; model-level defenses are untested.
*   **Open Question 3:** What semantic or functional role do the discovered universal activation directions play in the model's normal conversational decision-making? *Basis:* The paper establishes existence but does not interpret the natural function of these structures.
*   **Open Question 4:** How does turn amplification generalize to real human conversations where user responses are not simulated by an LLM and where completion criteria are dynamically negotiated? *Basis:* Evaluation relies on an LLM-as-a-Judge and simulated users, not human data.
*   **Open Question 5:** To what extent does the method for generating synthetic training dialogues (using diverse base models) affect the transferability of learned turn-amplifying directions to target models? *Basis:* The synthetic data generation process is central but its impact on cross-model transfer is not systematically studied.

## Limitations
*   **Synthetic data dependency:** Discovered subspaces may be biased by using a single model (Qwen2.5-32B) for data generation, limiting validity to models with similar internal representations.
*   **Evaluator uncertainty:** The LLM judge (Qwen2.5-32B) could systematically misclassify conversation completeness, potentially inflating or deflating measured turn counts.
*   **Generalization scope:** Testing is restricted to English QA datasets (Alpaca, GSM8K); performance in other languages, domains (e.g., creative dialogue), or with varied cultural norms is unknown.

## Confidence
*   **Universal subspaces exist:** **Medium** (Cross-model evidence but synthetic data confound).
*   **Early-layer steering efficacy:** **High** (Consistent layer-sweep results across models).
*   **Sparse attack feasibility (LoRA/bit-flips):** **Medium** (Promising results but transfer robustness to all architectures not proven).
*   **Prompt-level defenses are ineffective:** **High** (Empirically demonstrated across multiple baselines).
*   **Interaction-level threat severity:** **High** (Token amplification factors are substantial and consistent).

## Next Checks
1.  **Model diversity test:** Apply steering to 5+ models with varied architectures (e.g., Mixtral, GPT-2) and training objectives; measure amplification variance.
2.  **Judge calibration:** Human-annotate 200 conversations for true completion; compute judge accuracy and adjust prompts if error >15%.
3.  **Cross-domain generalization:** Evaluate on non-English prompts (e.g., multilingual MedQA) and task types (creative writing) to test subspace invariance.
