# Dynamic Theory of Mind as a Temporal Memory Problem: Evidence from Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14646  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14646  
- **Reference count:** 13  
- **Primary result:** Across six LLMs, dynamic Theory of Mind evaluations reveal a large and persistent asymmetry: post‑update belief accuracy is high (63.9%) while pre‑update recall is low (27.7%), indicating a strong recency bias that exceeds difficulty on standard false‑belief tasks.

## Executive Summary
Static Theory of Mind (ToM) benchmarks fail to capture the dynamic nature of belief tracking in real‑world interactions. This paper introduces DToM‑Track, a framework that uses controlled multi‑turn conversations with hidden inner speech and planned belief updates to test three core abilities: recall of prior beliefs, inference of current beliefs, and detection of belief change. Evaluating six large language models shows a consistent asymmetry: models are much better at answering questions about the agent’s updated belief than about the prior belief, even when controlling for task difficulty. This pattern suggests that LLMs, much like humans, suffer from a recency bias that impairs retention of earlier mental states.

## Method Summary
The authors built a dataset of 5,794 multiple‑choice questions derived from synthetic dialogues. Generation proceeds as follows: (1) A scenario template defines roles, private information, goals, and a sequence of planned belief changes. (2) Two LLM agents (using LLaMA‑3‑70B‑Instruct) engage in a 10‑turn role‑play, emitting inner speech prefixed with labels like “Belief:” to expose their evolving mental states. (3) A belief tracker parses the inner speech to maintain a ground‑truth belief state across turns. (4) From each dialogue, questions are automatically generated for six types (pre‑update, post‑update, update‑detection, temporal, second‑order, false‑belief) and for five mental state categories. (5) Three LLM‑based filters prune low‑quality items: update verification (F1 ≥ 0.5 for detecting updates), QA verification (judging correctness, soundness, quality), and sample validation (ensuring unambiguous multiple‑choice answers). Finally, target models are evaluated zero‑shot by prompting them with the full dialogue and the question.

## Key Results
- **Large pre/post‑update asymmetry:** Accuracy on pre‑update questions is only 27.7%, while post‑update questions reach 63.9%.
- **Asymmetry exceeds standard false‑belief difficulty:** The false‑belief baseline (44.7%) falls between the two, indicating that the recency effect is not merely a by‑product of task complexity.
- **Robustness across models and scales:** All six tested LLMs (including GPT‑4 and Claude variants) exhibit the same pattern, suggesting a fundamental limitation in temporal belief maintenance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The observed asymmetry likely stems from the next‑token prediction objective inherent to LLMs, which privileges recent context. When later dialogue turns are provided, the model’s internal representations are dominated by the most recent information, making it easier to answer questions about the current belief. In contrast, recalling an earlier belief requires retrieving a representation that may have been overwritten or attenuated—a recency bias analogous to human memory interference. The absence of explicit memory mechanisms in standard transformer architectures further hampers stable retention of prior mental states across multiple updates.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Theory of Mind (ToM):** Understanding that others have beliefs, desires, and intentions.  
   *Why needed:* To evaluate whether LLMs can attribute mental states.  
   *Quick check:* Can the model infer a character’s belief after being misinformed?

2. **Dynamic ToM:** Extending ToM to track how beliefs evolve over time.  
   *Why needed:* Real‑world interactions involve belief updates.  
   *Quick check:* Does the model remember a character’s prior belief after an update?

3. **Recency Bias:** Tendency to recall or weigh recent information more heavily.  
   *Why needed:* Explains the asymmetry between pre‑ and post‑update questions.  
   *Quick check:* Compare accuracy on questions about recent versus earlier events.

4. **Inner Speech:** Private verbalized thoughts (e.g., “Belief: …”) used to expose reasoning.  
   *Why needed:* Provides a reliable ground‑truth belief state during dialogue generation.  
   *Quick check:* Are inner speech prefixes present and consistently formatted?

5. **Belief Tracking:** The process of maintaining a representation of an agent’s mental state across turns.  
   *Why needed:* Enables automatic extraction of ground‑truth for evaluation.  
   *Quick check:* Does a belief tracker correctly update with each inner speech entry?

6. **Zero‑Shot Evaluation:** Testing a model without task‑specific fine‑tuning.  
   *Why needed:* Measures innate capabilities of LLMs on dynamic ToM.  
   *Quick check:* Does the model answer correctly when prompted only with the dialogue and question?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Scenario Definition → Dialogue Generation (LLM role‑play with inner speech) → Belief Tracker → Question Generation → Verification Pipeline (Update Verifier, QA Verifier, Sample Validator) → Curated Dataset → Zero‑Shot Model Evaluation

**Critical path:** Generation of high‑quality dialogues and questions is critical; any bottleneck in the verification pipeline directly affects dataset size and composition, thereby influencing downstream evaluation results.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Synthetic vs. naturalistic:* Controlled generation ensures belief updates are detectable but may lack ecological validity.  
- *LLM‑based verifiers:* Automated filtering scales but introduces opacity and potential bias.  
- *Fixed dialogue length:* Facilitates uniform evaluation but limits exploration of longer interactions with multiple updates.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Missing inner‑speech prefixes in dialogues → belief tracker fails to extract updates.  
- Update verifier F1 below threshold → excessive rejection of dialogues, reducing dataset size.  
- QA verifier inconsistently judging quality → inclusion of ambiguous or unanswerable questions.  
- Sample validation errors → multiple‑choice format not standardizable.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Replicate the DToM‑Track dataset generation pipeline using the specified steps and compute the pre‑/post‑update accuracy gap for at least three LLMs.  
2. Conduct an ablation removing explicit update markers (e.g., “Actually”) from the dialogues to assess their contribution to the recency bias.  
3. Compare LLM performance on DToM‑Track to performance on a standard false‑belief task (e.g., Sally‑Anne) to quantify the additional difficulty.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can fine‑tuning on DToM‑Track data reduce or eliminate the recency bias in pre‑update belief recall?  
   *Basis:* The authors state that their analysis focuses on zero‑shot performance and does not examine fine‑tuning.  
   *Why unresolved:* It is unknown whether targeted training could improve retention of prior beliefs.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Fine‑tune LLMs on a balanced corpus of DToM‑Track dialogues and questions; measure change in the accuracy gap relative to zero‑shot baselines.

2. To what extent do the linguistic markers of belief change (e.g., “Actually,” “I just realized”) cue the model and contribute to the recency bias?  
   *Basis:* The authors note that future work will examine ablations manipulating update cues.  
   *Why unresolved:* It is unclear whether models rely on surface cues rather than tracking underlying belief states.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Generate parallel versions of the dataset with systematic variations: remove all markers, replace with neutral language, add misleading markers; compare pre‑update recall across conditions.

3. Do humans exhibit a similar recency bias and asymmetry in belief recall as LLMs when tested with the same controlled DToM‑Track paradigm?  
   *Basis:* The authors propose testing whether similar patterns emerge in human judgments.  
   *Why unresolved:* No direct human behavioral data are provided; ecological validity and cognitive parallels remain speculative.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate the DToM‑Track experiment with human participants (e.g., reading scripted dialogues or engaging in LLM‑mediated role‑play); compare accuracy patterns to those of LLMs.

4. How do interaction length and the frequency of belief updates affect the magnitude of the recency bias?  
   *Basis:* The authors suggest scaling studies to test how patterns scale with interaction length and belief revision.  
   *Why unresolved:* DToM‑Track uses a fixed structure (up to turn 9) and a minimum number of changes; the impact of longer, more densely updated interactions is unknown.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematically vary the number of turns and the density of planned belief updates in the generation template; measure decay in pre‑update recall and growth of the post/pre gap.

5. Can structured, mechanistic computational models of ToM (e.g., Bayesian or memory‑augmented networks) reproduce the recency bias pattern observed in LLMs?  
   *Basis:* The authors conclude that integrating DToM‑Track with computational ToM models can support mechanistic accounts.  
   *Why unresolved:* LLMs are treated as black‑box probes; without a process‑tracing model, the source of the asymmetry (representation, interference, optimization) is unclear.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Implement existing computational ToM models and evaluate them on DToM‑Track; compare their error patterns (pre‑ vs. post‑update) to those of LLMs and analyze whether architecture inherently yields recency bias.

## Limitations
- **Dataset reconstruction** depends on unspecified prompt details and verifier thresholds, risking shifts in distribution and affecting the reported asymmetry.  
- **Internal validity** is threatened by the opacity of LLM‑based filtering; unknown false positive/negative rates may bias the retained sample toward certain linguistic patterns or difficulty levels.  
- **Generalizability** is limited to the specific synthetic dialogue structure (inner speech, update markers); performance may not transfer to other dynamic ToM paradigms or real‑world narratives.

## Confidence
- **Persistent pre/post‑update accuracy asymmetry:** Medium  
- **Asymmetry exceeds standard false‑belief difficulty:** Medium  
- **DToM‑Track as a valid dynamic ToM benchmark:** Low
