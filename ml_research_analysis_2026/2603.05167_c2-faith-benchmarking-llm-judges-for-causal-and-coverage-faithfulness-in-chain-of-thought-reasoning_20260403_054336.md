# C2-Faith: Benchmarking LLM Judges for Causal and Coverage Faithfulness in Chain-of-Thought Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.05167
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05167
- **Reference count**: 29
- **Primary result**: The C2-Faith benchmark evaluates LLM judges on causal and coverage faithfulness in CoT reasoning, revealing task-dependent reliability and systematic biases (e.g., a detection-localization gap and coverage score inflation).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces C2-Faith, a benchmark built from the PRM800K dataset to assess whether large language model judges can evaluate process faithfulness in chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning. Process faithfulness encompasses causality (each step logically derives from prior steps) and coverage (the reasoning chain includes all necessary intermediate inferences). The authors create controlled perturbations: for causality, a single middle step is replaced with an acausal variant; for coverage, a fraction of middle steps is uniformly deleted. Three frontier models (GPT-4.1, DeepSeek-V3.1, o4-mini) are tested on binary detection, step localization, and coverage scoring. Results show that no single judge dominates all tasks; each model exhibits distinct strengths and weaknesses. All judges suffer from a large detection–localization gap, over-inflate coverage scores, and display an early-prediction bias in localization. Coverage assessment remains particularly challenging, with DeepSeek-V3.1 nearly uncorrelated with the reference at low deletion rates. The findings highlight the need for more reliable process faithfulness evaluation.

## Method Summary
The benchmark is derived from the PRM800K training split. Chains are filtered to those with all step-level human labels +1 and length ≥5, yielding ~450 “perfect chains”. For causality, one middle step (indices 30%–90% of the chain) is replaced by an acausal step generated via an Azure OpenAI model with a NEGATE prompt. Judges are prompted to detect whether the chain contains an invalid step (binary) and, if so, to localize the erroneous index. For coverage, a uniformly random subset of middle steps is removed at deletion rates d ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7}; judges assign a coverage score 0–4. Reference coverage scores are produced by GPT-4.1 with a modified prompt that includes the original chain and the removed steps. Three frontier models serve as judges; no training is involved. Metrics include detection rate, exact match/MAE for localization, and mean score, inflation fraction, and Spearman correlation for coverage. Statistical significance is evaluated via bootstrap confidence intervals and McNemar’s test.

## Key Results
- **No single judge dominates**: DeepSeek-V3.1 leads in causality detection (94.7%), o4-mini excels in step localization (68.0% exact match) and high-deletion coverage correlation (ρ=0.331), while GPT-4.1 performs moderately across tasks.
- **Systematic gaps and biases**: All judges show a large detection–localization gap (26–33 percentage points) and systematically over-inflate coverage scores. DeepSeek-V3.1’s coverage correlation collapses to near zero at 10% deletion (ρ≈-0.006).
- **Early-prediction bias**: In localization, all models exhibit a negative mean signed error, consistently identifying the unfaithful step earlier than its true position.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The benchmark isolates specific failure modes through deterministic perturbations: acausal replacement introduces a clear logical inconsistency at a known position, while step deletions simulate missing intermediate reasoning. By evaluating judges on these curated tasks, the study measures their sensitivity to causal inconsistencies and coverage gaps. The use of perfect chains provides a clean baseline, and the controlled generation of errors ensures that any judge failure can be attributed to the evaluation of faithfulness rather than to noise or ambiguous inputs. The choice of binary detection, precise localization, and graded coverage creates a multi-faceted picture of judge capabilities.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
PRM800K (train) → Filter perfect chains → Branch  
&nbsp;&nbsp;→ *Causality*: Sample middle index → Acausal replacement → Judge (detect/localize) → Causality metrics  
&nbsp;&nbsp;→ *Coverage*: Sample deletion rate & indices → Step deletion → Judge (coverage) + Reference scoring → Coverage metrics  

**Critical path**:  
1. Successfully filter chains (all +1, len≥5).  
2. Generate valid perturbations (acausal step or deletions).  
3. Judge API returns parsable JSON (binary/detection, index, or score).  
4. Metrics compute without error (e.g., exact match for localization, correlation for coverage).  

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Perfect chains* ensure clean ground truth but limit generalizability to noisy or longer chains.  
- *Single-step acausal replacement* isolates causal errors but may not reflect complex multi-step mistakes.  
- *LLM-generated reference coverage scores* provide a scalable gold standard but may bias results toward models similar to GPT-4.1.  
- *Uniform random deletion* creates varying degradation levels but does not target semantically critical steps.  

**Failure signatures**:  
- Detection rate near 100% or 0% suggests perturbations are too obvious or ineffective.  
- Judge coverage scores clustering at the maximum (4) across deletion rates indicates a ceiling effect.  
- Missing or malformed JSON outputs imply prompt engineering issues.  
- Early-prediction bias absent or reversed may indicate indexing misalignment.  

**First experiments**:  
1. *Filter validation*: Confirm the filtered dataset contains ~450 chains with all +1 labels and acceptable length distribution; print basic statistics.  
2. *Perturbation sanity*: Manually inspect 20–30 acausal steps to ensure they introduce a plausible but invalid inference. Also verify that step deletions remove the correct number of middle indices.  
3. *Pilot run*: Execute a small-scale evaluation (e.g., 10 examples per condition) with one judge and check that all prompts yield valid responses, parsing works, and preliminary metrics align with expected ranges (e.g., detection >80%, localization gap present).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: Does the reliability of LLM judges as evaluated by C2-Faith generalize beyond the MATH dataset to other reasoning domains such as commonsense or code?  
*Basis*: The benchmark draws from 450 unique mathematical problems; generalization to other mathematical domains or reasoning tasks (e.g., commonsense, code) is untested.  
*Why unresolved*: Different domains may present varied reasoning structures, error modes, and surface characteristics that could affect judge performance in ways not captured by the controlled perturbations on math problems.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Apply the identical perturbation pipeline (causal replacements and coverage deletions) to reasoning chains from diverse domains (e.g., commonsense QA, program synthesis), then re-evaluate the same set of judges and compare the patterns of detection, localization, and coverage scoring across domains.

**Open Question 2**: How would human-annotated coverage reference labels change the assessment of judge performance and the observed inflation bias?  
*Basis*: GT coverage labels are LLM-generated (GPT-4.1) rather than human-annotated, which may favor models similar in style to GPT-4.1.  
*Why unresolved*: Human notions of “completeness” might differ from the LLM rubric, potentially altering the measured correlation (Spearman ρ) and the degree of score inflation for incomplete chains.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Collect human annotations for coverage (0–4) on a substantial subset of the degraded chains (multiple annotators for reliability). Recompute judge metrics against this human gold standard and compare to the LLM-generated references to see if rankings and inflation trends hold.

**Open Question 3**: Why do all judges exhibit a systematic early-prediction bias in causal step localization, and can architectural or prompting interventions mitigate this?  
*Basis*: The paper reports that all three models exhibit a negative mean signed error, meaning they consistently predict the unfaithful step earlier than it actually occurs. The cause is not explained.  
*Why unresolved*: The early bias could arise from attentional preferences, context window limitations, or heuristic strategies that favor earlier steps; understanding this is critical for reliable step-level auditing and automated correction.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Conduct controlled experiments varying the true error position (e.g., injecting errors at the very beginning vs. end) while keeping other factors constant; measure changes in localization bias. Inspect attention patterns or use probing to see if models disproportionately attend to early steps. Test whether explicit prompting to “be precise” or training on localization data reduces the bias.

**Open Question 4**: What underlying factor causes DeepSeek-V3.1’s near-zero correlation on coverage scoring at low deletion rates, and is this a general limitation of certain model architectures or training objectives?  
*Basis*: DeepSeek-V3.1 exhibits a distinctive failure mode on the coverage task: at 10% deletion, 95.1% of its scores are 4, yielding a Spearman ρ of −0.006 (p=0.906). This “ceiling collapse” is not seen in other judges.  
*Why unresolved*: The failure suggests DeepSeek relies on a global coherence heuristic that overlooks missing intermediate steps, but the root cause (e.g., training data distribution, model size, decoding strategy) is unknown. This could limit its use as a completeness judge.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Compare DeepSeek’s internal representations (e.g., attention, hidden states) on complete vs. slightly-degraded chains to other models to see if it encodes coverage differently. Analyze its training data and objectives for signs of emphasis on holistic fluency over stepwise completeness. Test whether few-shot prompting or fine-tuning on a small coverage-labeled set improves sensitivity. Investigate if other models from the same family show similar patterns.

## Limitations
- **Perturbation generation**: The Azure OpenAI model used to create acausal steps is unspecified; different base models could alter perturbation subtlety and thus judge performance.
- **Reference coverage scoring**: The reference scores rely on a specific GPT-4.1 prompt that includes original and removed steps; minor formatting deviations shift the score distribution and correlations.
- **Generalizability**: Chains are drawn from PRM800K training split and filtered to perfect chains; findings may not extend to noisier, longer, or non-mathematical reasoning traces.
