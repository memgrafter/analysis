# Test-Time Learning of Causal Structure from Interventional Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19131
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19131
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** TICL achieves a 13.62% average F1 improvement on I-CPDAG discovery and a 50.21% F1 boost on intervention target detection over prior state-of-the-art methods across 14 bnlearn benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
TICL addresses the challenge of learning causal structures from interventional data when intervention targets are unknown. It synergizes test-time training with the Joint Causal Inference (JCI) framework to unify diverse interventional settings. At test time, it generates instance-specific training data via an Interventional Structural MCMC (IS-MCMC) sampler, then employs a two-phase PC-inspired supervised learner to predict the identifiable interventional CPDAG (I-CPDAG). Experiments on 14 discrete Bayesian network benchmarks show consistent outperformance of existing methods, with significant gains in both causal discovery and intervention target detection, while demonstrating strong scalability.

## Method Summary
TICL first applies JCI pooling to convert any collection of interventional datasets into an augmented observational dataset by adding binary environment nodes and edges to intervention targets. This unified representation allows handling unknown, soft/hard, single/multiple interventions uniformly. At test time, an IS-MCMC sampler draws candidate augmented causal graphs from the posterior P(G|D) under strict constraints (e.g., no system→environment edges). For each accepted graph, parameters are re-estimated via MLE and forward sampling produces a synthetic dataset. These ⟨graph, dataset⟩ pairs train a two-phase supervised classifier: Phase 1 predicts the skeleton using features derived from conditional independence tests and local structure; Phase 2 orients v-structures using vicinity features. Meek rules, informed by JCI priors (environment→system edges), finalize the I-CPDAG and reveal intervention targets.

## Key Results
- **13.62%** average F1 improvement on I-CPDAG discovery versus prior state-of-the-art.
- **50.21%** F1 boost on intervention target detection.
- Strong scalability and efficiency, with linear time growth in graph size due to parallel IS-MCMC chains.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Self-Augmentation via IS-MCMC
The IS-MCMC sampler generates instance-specific training pairs ⟨G_i, D_i⟩ from the posterior P(G|D) while respecting intervention constraints (e.g., no edges from system to environment nodes). This aligns the training distribution with the test interventional data, avoiding distribution shift. Evidence: The abstract and Section 3.1 describe using IS-MCMC to sample graphs and forward-sample compatible data. Break condition: Poor initialization or constraint violations during mutation lead to slow convergence or invalid augmented graphs.

### Mechanism 2: Two-Phase PC-Inspired Supervised Learning
A binary classifier first predicts edge existence in the skeleton using multi-order conditional independence features and structural metrics. A second classifier then orients unshielded triples into v-structures based on separating set features and vicinity statistics. Meek rules subsequently orient remaining edges, leveraging JCI priors (e.g., environment→system edges are directed a priori). This preserves the asymptotic correctness of the PC algorithm in a supervised setting. Evidence: Abstract and Section 3.2; feature engineering details in Appendix B.2.3. Break condition: Insufficient training diversity or noisy conditional independence estimates cause skeleton or orientation errors.

### Mechanism 3: Joint Causal Inference (JCI) Unification
JCI pools all interventional datasets into a single augmented graph with environment nodes, transforming any intervention family into an observational-like problem. The I-CPDAG of this augmented model encodes the maximal identifiable structure, and intervention targets appear as directed edges from environment nodes. Evidence: Abstract, Section 2.2, and Section 3 describe JCI pooling and its assumptions (exogeneity, generic context). Break condition: Violations of JCI assumptions (e.g., confounders between system and environment nodes) break identifiability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Joint Causal Inference (JCI)**: Reduces interventional causal discovery to an observational problem over an augmented model by adding environment nodes. Why needed: Provides a unified representation for any intervention setting (unknown/soft/hard, single/multiple). Quick check: How do environment nodes encode intervention targets in the augmented graph?
- **Interventional-Complete Partial DAG (I-CPDAG)**: The maximal partial DAG representing all causal relations identifiable under a given intervention family. Why needed: TICL’s output must be I-CPDAG to be provably correct under JCI. Quick check: Why are some edges unidentifiable from interventional data even with multiple interventions?
- **Test-Time Training (TTT)**: Generates training data at test time from the test distribution itself. Why needed: Avoids distribution shift between pre-training on synthetic graphs and real interventional data. Quick check: What is the key difference between TICL’s self-augmentation and standard pre-training?
- **IS-MCMC**: An MCMC sampler tailored to interventional data that respects constraints like no system→environment edges. Why needed: Efficiently explores the posterior over augmented causal graphs to produce diverse training pairs. Quick check: Why are intervention constraints critical during graph mutation?
- **Markov Equivalence Class (MEC) and I-MEC**: The set of DAGs encoding the same conditional independencies; shrinks to I-MEC under interventions. Why needed: I-CPDAG represents I-MEC; understanding MEC is foundational for identifiability. Quick check: How does a soft intervention on node X help distinguish between X→Y and Y→X?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: JCI Pooling → Self-Augmentation (IS-MCMC) → Two-Phase SCL (Skeleton model + Orientation model) → I-CPDAG and intervention targets.

**Critical path**:  
1. Immediately apply JCI pooling to the test data collection D to form augmented data D_I.  
2. Initialize IS-MCMC with a proxy algorithm (e.g., JCI-BLIP) for faster convergence; run multiple parallel chains to collect ~400 accepted graph-parameter pairs.  
3. Extract skeleton features (k-order CI test statistics, local structural metrics) and orientation features (vicinity CI, overlap statistics) from each pair.  
4. Train XGBoost binary classifiers: skeleton model first, then orientation model conditioned on predicted skeleton.  
5. At inference, predict skeleton and v-structures, apply Meek rules with JCI priors (env→sys edges directed), and output I-CPDAG plus intervention targets.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Self-augmentation vs. Pre-training: Self-augmentation adapts to test data but incurs runtime cost; pre-training is faster but may generalize poorly across intervention types.  
- PC-inspired vs. End-to-End: PC-inspired offers interpretability and theoretical guarantees but relies on manual feature engineering; end-to-end neural networks could automate feature learning but require more data and risk overfitting.  
- Discrete vs. Continuous: Current implementation assumes discrete data with G-tests; continuous data would require alternative CI measures (e.g., HSIC) and possibly different modeling assumptions (e.g., additive noise).

**Failure signatures**:  
- IS-MCMC trace plots show no convergence (score plateaus) → poor initialization or insufficient iterations.  
- Skeleton F1 drops sharply on larger graphs → inadequate training instance count or feature set.  
- Intervention target recall near zero → JCI priors not enforced during orientation or threshold mis-set.  
- High SHD on simple graphs (e.g., Earthquake) → intervention constraints violated in MCMC or Meek rules over-orient.  
- Memory/time blowup on graphs >50 nodes → quadratic feature extraction cost; reduce max_order or parallel chains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Continuous data and nonlinear mechanisms**: How does TICL generalize to continuous-valued data and complex non-linear causal models beyond discrete CPTs?
2. **Scalability of IS-MCMC**: What are the computational limits of the IS-MCMC sampler for very large graphs (e.g., >200 nodes), and can parallelization fully mitigate its inherent exponential complexity?
3. **Robustness to JCI assumption violations**: Under what violations of JCI assumptions (e.g., exogeneity, no confounding between system and environment) does TICL’s identifiability break down, and can the method be made robust?
4. **End-to-end learning**: Can the manual feature engineering in the two-phase SCL be replaced by an end-to-end differentiable architecture without sacrificing identifiability guarantees?

## Limitations
- **IS-MCMC implementation details** (mutation probabilities, proposal distribution, convergence diagnostics) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- **Feature engineering specifics** (exact k-order selection, residual conditional dependence formulation, XGBoost hyperparameters) are missing.
- **Soft intervention parametrization** (parent-preserving vs. full resampling) and MLE update rules are incompletely described.
