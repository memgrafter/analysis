# Joint Imaging-ROI Representation Learning via Cross-View Contrastive Alignment for Brain Disorder Classification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10253
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10253
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Primary result:** Joint cross-view contrastive alignment of global volumetric and ROI graph embeddings improves classification accuracy and AUC over single-modality and simple fusion strategies on ADHD-200 and ABIDE.

## Executive Summary
Current machine learning approaches for brain disorder diagnosis often rely either on whole-brain 3D MRI or on region-of-interest (ROI) derived graphs, but their complementary potential is not well understood. This paper introduces a unified cross-view contrastive framework that simultaneously processes 3D volumes and AAL-parcellated ROI graphs, aligning their embeddings in a shared latent space using a bidirectional InfoNCE loss. The alignment explicitly bridges the heterogeneity between the two views while preserving modality-specific information. On the ADHD-200 and ABIDE datasets, joint learning with contrastive alignment consistently outperforms both single-branch baselines and alternative fusion methods (e.g., concatenation, cross-attention). For instance, using 3DSC-TF and NeuroGraph backbones, the joint model reaches 69.29% accuracy/72.73% AUC on ADHD-200 and 62.54% accuracy/64.08% AUC on ABIDE. Grad-CAM visualizations further reveal that the volumetric and ROI branches highlight distinct yet complementary neuroanatomical patterns, offering a plausible explanation for the performance gains.

## Method Summary
The method comprises two parallel encoders: a 3D CNN (e.g., 3DSC-TF) extracts subject-level imaging embeddings from T1-weighted MRI volumes, and a graph neural network (e.g., NeuroGraph) processes AAL-parcellated connectivity graphs to obtain ROI-graph embeddings. To fuse these heterogeneous representations, both embeddings are projected into a common latent space via separate projection heads. A bidirectional InfoNCE contrastive loss is applied, where embeddings from the same subject across the two views are treated as positive pairs and all other subjects as negatives. This objective encourages the two representations to be mutually consistent while retaining their unique information. For classification, the embeddings (either individually or jointly) are fed into a classifier (typically an MLP) to predict disorder labels. The framework is trained end-to-end with a combination of classification loss and contrastive loss (weighted by λ). Systematic evaluations compare imaging-only, ROI-only, and joint configurations, as well as alternative fusion schemes like feature concatenation and cross-attention.

## Key Results
- Joint learning with contrastive alignment achieves 69.29% accuracy / 72.73% AUC on ADHD-200 and 62.54% accuracy / 64.08% AUC on ABIDE, surpassing both single-branch baselines and simple fusion methods.
- The bidirectional InfoNCE loss provides a more effective fusion mechanism than naive concatenation or cross-attention, demonstrating the benefit of explicit view alignment.
- Grad-CAM interpretability shows that the volumetric branch emphasizes global structural patterns while the ROI branch focuses on connectivity-related regions, confirming complementary information contributions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The 3D volumetric encoder captures coarse, whole‑brain morphological patterns (e.g., cortical thickness, ventricular enlargement) that are often linked to neurodevelopmental disorders. The ROI graph encoder, on the other hand, encapsulates topological and connectivity properties of parceled brain regions, which may reflect network‑level disruptions. By aligning these two representation spaces, the contrastive loss acts as a regularizer that discourages each branch from overfitting to dataset‑specific noise while encouraging them to learn features that are consistent across views. This alignment also facilitates implicit knowledge transfer: each encoder can refine its features based on the other view’s constraints, leading to more robust and discriminative embeddings. The observed Grad‑CAM maps confirm that the two branches attend to non‑overlapping sets of regions, and their combination yields a more comprehensive description of disease‑related alterations, thereby improving classification performance.

## Foundational Learning
**Contrastive Learning**  
- *What*: A self‑supervised paradigm that learns representations by minimizing distance between positive pairs and maximizing distance between negative pairs.  
- *Why needed*: Forms the basis of the cross‑view alignment; the InfoNCE loss is a specific instance.  
- *Quick check*: How does the temperature parameter affect the sharpness of the contrastive distribution?

**InfoNCE Loss**  
- *What*: A discriminative loss that approximates the mutual information between two views; widely used in SimCLR and similar frameworks.  
- *Why needed*: Quantifies the alignment quality between volumetric and ROI embeddings.  
- *Quick check*: Given paired embeddings, write the formula for the InfoNCE loss.

**Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)**  
- *What*: Neural networks that operate on graph-structured data via message passing and aggregation.  
- *Why needed*: To process brain graphs where nodes correspond to AAL regions and edges encode structural/functional connectivity.  
- *Quick check*: What is the difference between a graph convolution and a standard 2D convolution?

**3D Convolutional Neural Networks**  
- *What*: CNNs that extend to three spatial dimensions, processing volumetric data.  
- *Why needed*: To extract hierarchical spatial features directly from 3D MRI scans.  
- *Quick check*: How does the parameter count of a 3D convolution compare to a 2D convolution with the same kernel size?

**Grad‑CAM**  
- *What*: Gradient‑based class activation mapping that highlights important regions in the input for a given prediction.  
- *Why needed*: To interpret which neuroanatomical areas drive the model’s decision, providing insight into the learned representations.  
- *Quick check*: Which layers are most suitable for Grad‑CAM in a deep 3D CNN?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
MRI Volume → 3D Encoder (e.g., 3DSC‑TF) → Imaging Embedding (z_vol)  
AAL Graph → Graph Encoder (e.g., NeuroGraph) → ROI Embedding (z_graph)  
z_vol → Projection Head → Projected Imaging (p_vol)  
z_graph → Projection Head → Projected ROI (p_graph)  
Bidirectional InfoNCE Loss aligns p_vol and p_graph  
[z_vol, z_graph] → Fusion (e.g., concatenation) → Classification Head → Diagnosis

**Critical Path**  
1. Both input modalities are processed independently by their dedicated encoders.  
2. The resulting embeddings are projected into a shared latent space.  
3. The contrastive loss is computed over the projected embeddings and backpropagated to align the two views.  
4. The original (or projected) embeddings are fused and passed through a classifier to predict the disorder label.  
5. The total loss combines classification loss and contrastive loss (weight λ).

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Contrastive alignment* vs. *concatenation*: Alignment explicitly bridges the modality gap and regularizes features but requires tuning of temperature, projection dimension, and loss weight. Concatenation is parameter‑free but may suffer from feature incompatibility and lack of interaction.  
- *Encoder specialization*: Using separate 3D CNN and GNN allows each to exploit its inductive bias, increasing representational power; however, it also increases model size and risk of overfitting on limited data.  
- *Projection heads*: Non‑linear projections add expressiveness but can overfit; linear projections are simpler but less flexible.

**Failure Signatures**  
- *Poor alignment*: High average distance between positive pairs in the shared space, or low accuracy on a retrieval probe that checks if same‑subject embeddings are nearest neighbors.  
- *Over‑regularization*: If λ is too large, the contrastive loss may erase modality‑specific discriminative signals, causing joint performance to fall below single‑branch baselines.  
- *Noise‑sensitive Grad‑CAM*: Scattered or noisy saliency maps may indicate unstable training, poor model calibration, or excessive gradient noise.  
- *Missing‑view brittleness*: Although the paper reports robustness, a failure could be a sharp drop when one modality is absent, suggesting the model relies too heavily on the other view rather than having learned complementary features.

**First Three Experiments**  
1. **Baseline replication** – Train and evaluate the joint model on the official ADHD‑200 and ABIDE train/test splits with the reported encoder backbones, confirming the published accuracy/AUC numbers.  
2. **Fusion‑method ablation** – Replace contrastive alignment with feature concatenation and cross‑attention fusion while keeping all other settings identical, and compare performance to quantify the benefit of explicit alignment.  
3. **Missing‑view simulation** – Systematically mask one modality (e.g., set the graph input to zero or dropout nodes) at varying missing rates and measure classification accuracy to validate the claim of implicit knowledge transfer and bounded degradation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What are the specific neural representational mechanisms—such as feature hierarchies, invariance to noise, or capture of different spatial scales—through which global volumetric and ROI-level graph representations provide complementary information for brain disorder classification?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The introduction and conclusion explicitly state that "their relative contributions and potential complementarity remain insufficiently understood" and that interpretability analyses reveal "distinct yet complementary discriminative patterns."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates performance gains from joint learning and shows non-overlapping Grad-CAM activation maps, but it does not disentangle the underlying representational properties (e.g., whether one branch captures coarse atrophy while the other captures connectivity disruptions) that lead to synergy.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments using representational similarity analysis (RSA) or targeted adversarial perturbations to isolate and measure the specific types of information (e.g., texture vs. topology, global vs. local) encoded by each branch, followed by correlation with clinical or genetic biomarkers.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does cross-view contrastive alignment consistently outperform alternative fusion strategies like concatenation or cross-attention in integrating heterogeneous imaging and ROI embeddings?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Table 2 shows contrastive alignment (Contra) achieving the best performance in most encoder/fusion settings, but the paper does not analyze the latent space structure or optimization dynamics that explain this superiority.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper attributes success to "explicitly aligning heterogeneous representations" but lacks ablation or analysis (e.g., on alignment quality, feature redundancy, or gradient flow) to clarify whether gains stem from better feature compatibility, regularization effects, or implicit knowledge transfer.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Quantitative metrics of embedding alignment (e.g., mean nearest‑neighbor distance across views, mutual information), ablation of contrastive loss weighting (λ) with analysis of embedding space topology (using t-SNE/UMAP), and comparison of gradient magnitudes in each branch under different fusion schemes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Under simulated missing-view scenarios, what are the implicit mechanisms by which the remaining branch partially compensates for the absent modality—does it learn more robust features during joint training, or does cross-view alignment enable feature imputation at inference?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Figure 2 shows bounded performance degradation as missing rates increase, and the text suggests "implicit knowledge transfer," but no diagnostic experiments probe how each branch's representations change when the other is masked.
- **Why unresolved:** The observation of robustness is noted, but the paper does not investigate whether the intact branch's features become more discriminative or generalizable due to contrastive constraints, or if the classifier learns to rely more heavily on a subset of features from the available view.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare the feature distributions (e.g., activation statistics, principal component variance) of each branch under full‑view vs. missing‑view training; perform linear probing of each branch's embeddings trained with/without contrastive alignment when the counterpart is absent.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Do the Grad‑CAM highlighted regions from the joint model reflect causally relevant neural substrates for ADHD and autism, or are they merely correlations driven by dataset biases or preprocessing choices?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The contribution analysis states: "attribution maps do not establish causal neurobiological mechanisms" and rely on "clinical face validity" via overlap with prior literature.
- **Why unresolved:** While the highlighted regions align with known ADHD/ASD circuits, this is a post‑hoc validation against prior studies, not a causal test. The maps could be influenced by confounds (e.g., motion, scanner differences) or atlas selection (AAL).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Validate attribution maps via causal intervention experiments, such as lesioning/masking highlighted regions in input volumes and measuring performance drop, or testing generalizability to independent datasets with different acquisition protocols or atlases (e.g., Schaefer parcellation).

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility:** No code or data release is noted, hindering verification and independent replication.
- **Generalization:** Evaluation is limited to two datasets (ADHD‑200, ABIDE) using AAL parcellation and specific backbones; results may not hold for other disorders or acquisition protocols.
- **Clinical interpretability:** Grad‑CAM for 3D CNNs is not well‑standardized, and the neurobiological significance of the contrastively aligned representations remains speculative without clinician validation.
