# TERMINATOR: Learning Optimal Exit Points for Early Stopping in Chain-of-Thought Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.12529
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12529
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: TERMINATOR reduces chain-of-thought length by 14%–55% while matching or exceeding accuracy across multiple benchmarks, outperforming prior early exit methods.

## Executive Summary
Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) often generate excessive chain-of-thought tokens even after the final answer is determined—a phenomenon called overthinking. This paper introduces **TERMINATOR**, an inference-time early-exit strategy that predicts the earliest logical arrival of the final answer within the CoT. The method trains a binary probe classifier on the LRM’s final-layer hidden states, using a dataset of hindsight-optimal reasoning lengths (HORL) curated via an automated extract‑identify‑verify pipeline. At inference, a sliding window of predictions (last 10) triggers early exit when the majority exceeds a threshold, injecting `` to stop reasoning. Evaluated on Qwen3 and Ministral models across MATH‑500, AIME 2025, HumanEval, and GPQA, TERMINATOR reduces CoT length by 14%–55% on average while matching or exceeding vanilla accuracy and outperforming prior methods, achieving strong Pareto trade‑offs between efficiency and performance.

## Method Summary
No reproduction notes were provided. The method constructs a hindsight‑optimal reasoning length (HORL) dataset by applying an extract‑identify‑verify pipeline to the target LRM’s full CoTs. The pipeline extracts the final answer, identifies the earliest text span that logically leads to it, verifies correctness, and records the token index, yielding binary labels (0 before HORL, 1 after). A lightweight probe classifier—a single transformer layer initialized from the LRM’s final layer with an MLP head—is trained on final‑layer hidden states using class‑weighted cross‑entropy. During inference, the probe runs in parallel; after each token, its probability is thresholded (0.7) and a sliding window of the last 10 predictions is considered. If more than half exceed the threshold, a stop token is injected to terminate reasoning.

## Key Results
- Reduces average chain‑of‑thought length by 14%–55% across MATH‑500, AIME 2025, HumanEval, and GPQA.
- Maintains or improves accuracy compared to vanilla (no early exit) and outperforms prior methods (DEER, Dynasor, Thought Calibration).
- Achieves strong Pareto trade‑offs between computational efficiency (reduced tokens) and task performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Event‑Locked Averaging Signal
- **Claim**: The first occurrence of the final answer in a CoT is marked by a detectable spike in token confidence when multiple CoTs are aligned by that position.
- **Mechanism**: Averaging token‑level confidence (or log‑probability) over many CoTs, after aligning them to the first answer position, produces a sharp peak at that position, indicating a consistent internal state change.
- **Evidence**: The abstract notes a “sharp transition in the LRM’s token‑level confidence”. Section 3 (Fig. 2) shows event‑locked averaging for Token‑Confidence and log‑probabilities across data sources, demonstrating the spike.

### Mechanism 2: Automated Hindsight‑Optimal Dataset Construction
- **Claim**: A dataset of hindsight‑optimal reasoning lengths (HORL) can be created automatically via an extract‑identify‑verify pipeline using an LRM.
- **Mechanism**: The pipeline (Fig. 4) uses the LRM to (1) extract the final answer from the final solution, (2) identify a text span in the CoT that leads to that answer, (3) verify the span contains the answer, and (4) extract the exact token index. This yields binary labels (0 before HORL, 1 after) for training.
- **Evidence**: Section 4.1 details the pipeline and its challenges (numerical answers, expressions, code). Fig. 4 illustrates the pipeline steps.

### Mechanism 3: Binary Probe Classifier with Sliding Window
- **Claim**: A lightweight probe classifier, trained on final‑layer hidden states to predict answer arrival, can trigger early exit at inference via a sliding window majority vote.
- **Mechanism**: The probe (one transformer layer initialized from the LRM’s final layer plus a binary head) is trained with class‑weighted cross‑entropy on HORL labels. At inference, predictions on the last 10 tokens are collected; if >50% exceed 0.7, the stop token is injected to stop reasoning.
- **Evidence**: Section 4.2 describes the probe architecture, loss, and inference procedure. Section 5.2 (Table 1) shows TERMINATOR achieves state‑of‑the‑art accuracy‑efficiency trade‑offs. Section 5.4 (Figs. 7, 8) confirm exit positions recover the event‑locked spikes and token usage biases observed in training data.

## Foundational Learning
- **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) reasoning**: Why needed: CoT is the reasoning format that TERMINATOR aims to optimize by early exit. Quick check: What does an LRM generate during the “thinking” stage? (Intermediate tokens forming a reasoning chain before the final answer.)
- **Overthinking**: Why needed: The core problem TERMINATOR addresses is overthinking, where LRMs continue reasoning after the answer is determined. Quick check: Why do LRMs overthink? (They may double‑check or explore alternatives even after a correct answer appears.)
- **Token‑Confidence**: Why needed: This metric detects answer arrival; it measures model certainty per token. Quick check: How is Token‑Confidence computed? (Average negative log‑probability over the top‑K predicted tokens at each position.)
- **Hindsight‑Optimal Reasoning Length (HORL)**: Why needed: Defines the ideal exit point (first logical arrival of the final answer) for creating training labels. Quick check: What distinguishes the “first logical arrival” from any later occurrence of the answer? (It is the earliest reasoning step that yields the final answer, not merely any copy of the answer string.)
- **Probe classifier**: Why needed: The probe is the core model that predicts answer arrival at each token during inference. Quick check: Why not simply use the LRM’s own token predictions? (The probe operates on hidden states, potentially capturing internal shifts before the answer token is emitted, and is more efficient.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data Curation Pipeline → Probe Training → Inference Integration.

**Critical path**:
1. Generate CoTs from target LRM on training mix (AIME, MATH, OpenCoder‑SFT, OpenScience).
2. Run extract‑identify‑verify on each CoT to get HORL positions (accept ~70–80%).
3. Collect final‑layer hidden states and assign binary labels (0 before HORL, 1 after).
4. Train probe; select best model on validation Macro‑F1.
5. At inference, load probe weights; during autoregressive generation, compute probe probability after each token, update sliding window, and inject stop token when condition met.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Probe capacity: Single transformer layer minimizes overhead but may limit expressivity; initializing from the final layer helps.
- Thresholds: Fixed probability (0.7) and window size (10) avoid data‑calibrated tuning but may not be optimal for all distributions.
- Training data mix: Mixing domains improves generalization but still risks OOD gaps (e.g., GPQA).
- Inference overhead: Probe adds a small forward pass; latency reduced by ~2× but throughput overhead ~10% for Qwen3‑8B (Table 2).

**Failure signatures**:
- Accuracy drop on hard tasks (e.g., AIME25, GPQA) at moderate compression (Table 1).
- OOD performance: Training on one domain yields high accuracy but low compression on others (Fig. 5).
- Pipeline failure: 20–30% of CoTs fail extraction, reducing training data.
- Poor signal recovery: For some datasets (AIME25, GPQA), probe predictions lack sharp transitions (Figs. 21–24), indicating difficulty.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Reproduce Table 1 comparisons (TERMINATOR vs. baselines) on Qwen3‑8B across MATH‑500, AIME25, HumanEval, GPQA using identical CoTs to verify reported trade‑offs.
2. Generate per‑dataset probability streams for test samples; compute event‑locked averages to confirm spike alignment with HORL positions and validate core signal.
3. Conduct threshold/window sweeps (τ=0.1–0.9, window size 5–20) on MATH‑500 to map accuracy‑compression Pareto frontier and assess optimality of fixed thresholds.

## Limitations
- Event‑locked confidence spike may not generalize to all model architectures or tasks.
- Automated pipeline fails for 20–30% of CoTs, reducing training data and potentially introducing label noise.
- Probe classifier exhibits OOD performance degradation on hard tasks (AIME25, GPQA) and lacks sharp transitions in some datasets.
