# From FusHa to Folk: Exploring Cross-Lingual Transfer in Arabic Language Models
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.09826
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09826
*   **Reference Count:** 27
*   **Primary Result:** MSA-pretrained models show functional transfer to Arabic dialects but with high disparity; dialect-specific models with ample data outperform on their native tasks, while MSA models remain competitive on structural tasks (POS, NER), with transfer success strongly correlated with geographic proximity to a Yemen-based MSA anchor.

## Executive Summary
This study examines whether Modern Standard Arabic (MSA)-pretrained language models can effectively transfer knowledge to various Arabic dialects. Using linear probing on sentiment analysis, named entity recognition, and part-of-speech tagging, alongside representational similarity analysis (CKA) on parallel sentences, the authors quantify transfer quality. Results indicate transfer is possible but highly disproportionate: dialect-specific models with sufficient pretraining data significantly outperform MSA and multi-dialect models on their native dialect tasks, particularly for sentiment analysis. MSA models maintain competitive performance on more structural tasks like POS and NER. CKA scores are consistently lower when models process different dialects, confirming significant representational divergence. A strong correlation between transfer performance and geographic distance from a Yemen-based MSA anchor supports a dialect continuum effect. Critically, multi-dialect models show evidence of negative interference, underperforming on high-resource dialects despite larger overall training data.

## Method Summary
The study employs a multi-faceted evaluation framework. First, linear probes—simple, frozen classifiers—are trained on the hidden-state embeddings of various pre-trained Arabic models (MSA-only, multi-dialect, dialect-specific) for three tasks (POS, NER, SA) across multiple dialects. Probe accuracy (macro-F1) measures the functional transfer of task-relevant knowledge. Second, Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) computes the representational similarity between model pairs (e.g., an MSA model and a dialect model) on parallel sentences from the MADAR corpus, providing an intrinsic, task-agnostic metric of structural alignment. Finally, dialect-level probe and CKA scores are correlated with geographic distance from Yemen (used as a proxy for "core" Classical Arabic) to test the dialect continuum hypothesis. The analysis controls for dialect purity by filtering datasets with a dialect identification model.

## Key Results
*   Transfer is highly disproportionate: dialect-specific models with ample data (e.g., DziriBERT, CAMeLBERT-DA) dramatically outperform MSA and multi-dialect models on their native dialect tasks, especially for sentiment analysis.
*   MSA models remain relatively competitive on structured tasks (POS, NER) compared to semantic tasks (SA), and representational similarity (CKA) between MSA and dialect models is low, confirming divergence.
*   Geographic proximity to a Yemen-based MSA anchor strongly predicts both probing performance and CKA similarity, supporting a dialect continuum; however, multi-dialect models exhibit negative interference, underperforming on high-resource dialects.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Linear Probing as a Functional Transfer Diagnostic
*   **Claim:** Linear probe performance on dialect-specific datasets directly measures the degree of functional knowledge transfer from a source model to a target dialect. High probe accuracy indicates the source model's frozen representations contain linearly accessible, task-relevant features for that dialect.
*   **Mechanism:** A simple linear classifier is trained on top of frozen model embeddings to predict linguistic labels. The probe's success hinges on pre-existing information in the representations, not on learning the task from scratch.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** The paper explicitly uses linear probing to "assess transfer quality" and describes keeping probes "deliberately kept simple." Consistent patterns in probe results (Figures 3, 5) across models and dialects validate the mechanism.

### Mechanism 2: CKA as a Structural Alignment Metric
*   **Claim:** Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) similarity between a source model (e.g., MSA) and a target dialect model, computed on parallel sentences, is an intrinsic measure of representational alignment. Higher CKA suggests the models encode inputs more similarly, facilitating transfer.
*   **Mechanism:** CKA compares the geometry of representation matrices (hidden states) from two models on the same sentences, invariant to rotation and scaling. Layer-wise CKA reveals where alignment peaks.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** The paper states CKA "provides an intrinsic metric of cross-lingual transfer" and reports CKA scores are "consistently lower when models processed different dialects."

### Mechanism 3: Geographic Continuum as a Predictive Proxy
*   **Claim:** Geographic proximity to a chosen MSA anchor (Yemen) is a strong external predictor of both functional transfer (probe performance) and structural similarity (CKA), supporting the dialect continuum hypothesis.
*   **Mechanism:** Dialect-level probe and CKA scores are aggregated and plotted against the great-circle distance from Yemen. A positive correlation (scores decrease with distance) indicates linguistic similarity decays geographically.
*   **Evidence Anchors:** The paper justifies Yemen as a proxy for MSA/Classical Arabic based on linguistic literature. Figure 7 shows a clear trend where "bubbles" (scores) shrink with increasing distance from the anchor.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Cross-lingual/Cross-dialectal Transfer**
    *   *Why needed:* Central to the paper's question; explains why knowledge from one linguistic variety (MSA) might or might not apply to another (dialects). Factors like linguistic similarity and data size are key.
    *   *Quick Check:* If a model pretrained on Egyptian Arabic is evaluated on Moroccan Darija via probing, what factors would you expect to influence the transfer performance? (Linguistic similarity, orthographic overlap, data size for each dialect during pretraining).
*   **Concept: Linear Probing as a Diagnostic Tool**
    *   *Why needed:* The primary method to assess *functional* transfer. It isolates whether a frozen model's representations contain linearly separable information for a task.
    *   *Quick Check:* A linear probe on frozen BERT layers achieves 95% F1 on POS tagging for dialect X. Does this prove BERT was trained to do POS tagging? What does it actually prove? (No. It proves the model's representations encode POS information in a linearly accessible way, either accidentally or as a byproduct of its original training objective).
*   **Concept: Representational Similarity Analysis (CKA)**
    *   *Why needed:* Provides a complementary, *structural* view of transfer. It measures if two models' internal geometries are aligned, which is theorized to underpin transfer success.
    *   *Quick Check:* Two models have identical probing scores on a dialect task but very different CKA scores with an MSA model. What might this suggest? (They may have learned task-relevant information via different internal mechanisms or representations; functional similarity does not guarantee structural similarity with the source model).

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component Map:** Dialect-Probing Datasets & MADAR Corpus -> Frozen Pre-trained Models (MSA, Multi-DA, DA-specific) -> Embedding Extraction Layer -> (1) Linear Probe Training/Evaluation Module (-> Task F1 Scores) and (2) CKA Computation Module (-> Representational Similarity Scores) -> Analysis Layer (Aggregation by Dialect -> Correlation with Geographic Distance).
*   **Critical Path:**
    1.  Filter dialect-specific probing datasets using a dialect ID model to ensure purity.
    2.  For every model-layer-dataset combination: extract frozen embeddings.
    3.  Train and evaluate linear probes (80/20 split) to record best-layer macro-F1.
    4.  For every model-pair on MADAR parallel sentences: compute layer-wise CKA, aggregate to dialect level.
    5.  For each dialect: calculate average relative probe performance (vs. dialect-specific model) and average CKA (vs. MSA model).
    6.  Correlate these dialect metrics with geographic distance from Yemen.
*   **Design Tradeoffs:**
    *   **Dataset Noise vs. Coverage:** Using silver-standard NER for Gulf dialects increases coverage but introduces potential annotation noise.
    *   **Model Heterogeneity:** Comparing models with different pretraining scales, vocabularies, and corpora makes it difficult to isolate the effect of dialect coverage from sheer data size.
    *   **Geographic Proxy:** Using Yemen as the MSA anchor is a literature-informed heuristic; results are sensitive to this choice.
*   **Failure Signatures:**
    *   **Probing:** A dialect-specific model performs worse than MSA/multi-dialect models on its *own* dialect test set. (Severe underfitting or data mismatch).
    *   **CKA:** Scenario 3 CKA (same variant input) is not the lowest, or Scenario 1/2 CKA (different variant input) is very low (<0.5). (Challenges assumptions about alignment).
    *   **Geography:** No trend in Figure 7b for large-data models, or a trend contradicting known linguistic groupings (e.g., Maghrebi dialects closer to MSA than Levantine).
*   **First 3 Experiments:**
    1.  Replicate the baseline functional disparity (Figure 3): Probe all models on MSA datasets to confirm MSA model superiority and identify which dialect models generalize best/worst to MSA.
    2.  Validate structural alignment (Figure 6, Scenario 3): Compute CKA between CAMeLBERT-MSA and each major CAMeLBERT-DA on MADAR sentences; verify CKA is lower when input variants differ.
    3.  Reproduce the proximity correlation (Figure 7): For each dialect country, calculate average best-layer macro-F1 (for MSA/Multi-DA/MIX models) and average CKA with MSA. Plot against great-circle distance from Sana'a, Yemen; inspect for the continuum effect.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   Do MSA-centric or multi-dialect models transfer equitably across Arabic dialects?
*   Why do multi-dialect models exhibit negative interference, and how can it be mitigated?
*   What explains the functional-structural gap where high CKA similarity fails to predict probing performance?

## Limitations
*   Probe hyperparameters (learning rate, epochs, batch size) are unspecified ("small LR"), affecting score reproducibility and best-layer selection.
*   Dialect ID filtering thresholds and rules for mapping cities to countries are ambiguous, risking dataset noise.
*   The geographic distance metric (great-circle vs. road) and precise dialect centroid coordinates are not defined, making the continuum analysis non-transparent.
*   Silver-standard NER for Gulf dialects lacks validation, and model heterogeneity confounds dialect coverage effects with pretraining data size.

## Confidence
*   **Disproportionate Transfer:** High. Consistent patterns across tasks and models, but dependent on correct dialect filtering and probe setup.
*   **Representational Divergence:** High. Standard CKA methodology with clear layer-wise trends, though potentially confounded by vocabulary/length.
*   **Geographic Continuum:** Medium. Correlation sensitive to unstated distance metric and anchor choice; may reflect data/resource distribution.
*   **Negative Interference:** Medium. Observed underperformance lacks root-cause investigation; could be due to data imbalance or optimization issues.

## Next Checks
1.  **Standardize probing and filtering:** Re-run probes with explicit hyperparameters (LR, batch size, epochs, multiple seeds) and audit the dialect ID filtering process via confidence distribution analysis and manual sample checks.
2.  **Robust geographic analysis:** Define and use a consistent great-circle distance metric from capital cities (or linguistic centroids); test alternative anchors (Mecca, Cairo); plot partial residuals controlling for pretraining data volume.
3.  **Isolate dialect coverage from data size:** Compare models with matched pretraining token counts but different dialect inclusion (e.g., MSA-only vs. multi-dialect subsets). If the geographic correlation vanishes for size-matched models, the continuum effect is likely data-driven.
