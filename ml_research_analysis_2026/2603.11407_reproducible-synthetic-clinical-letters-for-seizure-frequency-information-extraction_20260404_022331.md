# Reproducible Synthetic Clinical Letters for Seizure Frequency Information Extraction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11407
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11407
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** Fine-tuning open-weight LLMs on 15k fully synthetic NHS-style letters achieved micro-F1 scores up to 0.788 (fine-grained) and 0.847 (pragmatic) on a clinician-verified real test set, enabling robust seizure frequency extraction without using sensitive patient text.

## Executive Summary
This study addresses the challenge of extracting normalized seizure frequency from unstructured epilepsy clinic letters, which are difficult to annotate and share due to privacy concerns. The authors propose a reproducible framework that uses fully synthetic, NHS-style clinical letters generated by GPT-5, paired with structured labels, rationales, and evidence spans. This synthetic data allows training open-weight language models (4B–14B parameters) without exposing real patient information. Experiments show that models trained solely on synthetic data generalize well to real clinic letters, with structured label prediction outperforming numeric regression and coarse classification. Evidence-grounded chain-of-thought outputs support clinical verification and error analysis, demonstrating the viability of synthetic supervision for sensitive clinical NLP tasks.

## Method Summary
The synthetic data generation pipeline begins with 10 manually anonymized real NHS letters (base templates). GPT-5 generates 500 diverse textual descriptions of seizure frequency, which human annotators map to structured label templates (rates, ranges, clusters, seizure‑free intervals). GPT-5 then rewrites a base letter, inserting a synthetic description and generating separate fake demographics. After placeholder replacement with synthetic demographics, each draft letter is re‑evaluated by GPT-5 to extract a label and chain‑of‑thought; only letters where the label matches the original and the CoT is high‑quality are retained, yielding 15,099 synthetic training instances. For model training, open‑weight LLMs (Qwen, Llama, Gemma families) are fine‑tuned using LLaMA‑Factory with DeepSpeed on 8×V100 GPUs for 3 epochs. Four output formats are compared: (1) numeric (e.g., “4 per month”), (2) 4‑way pragmatic class, (3) structured label scheme, and (4) chain‑of‑thought with evidence spans. Training sets vary: real‑only, real + synthetic, or synthetic‑only with different scales (500–15k). Models are evaluated on a held‑out set of 300 real clinic letters from King’s College Hospital, with outputs mapped to Purist (fine‑grained) and Pragmatic (grouped) label schemes for micro‑F1 scoring.

## Key Results
- Models trained on 15k synthetic letters achieve micro‑F1 up to 0.788 (Purist) and 0.847 (Pragmatic) on real test letters; a medically pretrained 4B model (MedGemma) reaches 0.787 and 0.858.
- Structured label prediction (Format 3) consistently outperforms numeric regression (Format 1) and pragmatic classification (Format 2) across multiple models.
- Evidence‑grounded chain‑of‑thought outputs (Format 4) provide interpretable rationales and quoted evidence spans, facilitating clinical verification and error analysis.
- Performance saturates beyond several thousand synthetic examples, suggesting limited diversity in the seed templates (only 10 base letters) as a bottleneck.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Synthetic Clinical Letter Generation with GPT‑5
- **Claim:** Synthetic clinical letter generation with GPT‑5 enables privacy‑preserving, reproducible model training without exposing real patient text.
- **Mechanism:** A teacher LLM (GPT‑5) generates NHS‑style letters from privacy‑checked templates and parametrically instantiated seizure frequency descriptions. The pipeline separates synthetic demographics (filled post‑generation) from letter content, ensuring no real identifiers are ever used or released. This creates a large‑scale, fully synthetic corpus that mimics real documentation patterns.
- **Assumption:** High‑capacity teacher models can produce clinically coherent text that captures the linguistic variability of real clinic letters sufficient for training smaller student models.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “fully synthetic, NHS‑style clinical letters generated by GPT‑5… without using sensitive patient text.”  
  - [section 2.6] Describes the multi‑step generation with base templates, description instantiations, placeholder separation (`@NAME@`, `@ADDRESS@`), and final filtering.  
  - [corpus] The neighboring corpus focuses on EEG‑based seizure detection, not clinical NLP; thus, direct evidence for this specific synthetic clinical text mechanism is weak or missing.
- **Break condition:** If synthetic letters fail to capture enough lexical and contextual diversity (as hinted by reviewer stylistic artifacts), generalization to real letters degrades, especially for out‑of‑distribution clinics.

### Mechanism 2: Structured Label Representations
- **Claim:** Structured label representations (rates, ranges, clusters, seizure‑free intervals) improve learnability and stability over compressed numeric or coarse categorical targets.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of forcing a single numeric value (e.g., “4 seizures/month”) or a 4‑way class, the model predicts a normalized string label (e.g., “4 to 5 per month”) that preserves clinically salient structure. This aligns the output format with the surface forms in clinical text, reducing the burden of implicit temporal reasoning during decoding.
- **Assumption:** Seizure frequency expressions have inherent structure (ranges, clusters) that is lost in numeric compression; preserving this structure in the target space yields better gradient signals and more stable training.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “Structured label prediction consistently outperformed direct numeric regression.”  
  - [section 2.7] Defines Format 3 (Label Scheme) vs. Format 1 (numeric) and Format 2 (pragmatic class), with examples.  
  - [section 3.1] Table 6 shows `Our label`/`Our CoT` often outperform `x per M` and `Categ.` on Real(300).
- **Break condition:** If the structured label space becomes too fine‑grained or complex relative to model capacity, performance could drop due to increased output space sparsity.

### Mechanism 3: Evidence‑Grounded Chain‑of‑Thought Supervision
- **Claim:** Evidence‑grounded chain‑of‑thought (CoT) supervision provides interpretable rationales that support clinical verification without sacrificing accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** The teacher model produces not only a final label but also a step‑by‑step analysis and explicit evidence spans (quoted text) supporting the decision. The student model is trained to mimic this multi‑part output. At inference, the evidence span allows a clinician to quickly check if the prediction is textually supported.
- **Assumption:** Clinicians will trust and adopt model outputs more if they can see the grounding evidence, even if raw accuracy is similar to a black‑box model.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “Evidence‑grounded outputs supported rapid clinical verification and facilitated error analysis.”  
  - [section 2.7] Format 4 definition: `{"analysis": "...", "seizure_frequency_number": ["label", "evidence span"]}`.  
  - [section 4.3] Dedicated paragraph on value of CoT and evidence grounding for interpretability and auditability.
- **Break condition:** If evidence span extraction becomes unreliable (e.g., model hallucinates spans not in the input), clinical trust would plummet despite correct final labels.

## Foundational Learning

### Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) Prompting
Why needed here: The synthetic data generation and final model output format rely on generating intermediate reasoning steps. Understanding CoT is key to grasping how the teacher model produces structured rationales and how the student learns to reason.  
Quick check: How does asking a model to “think step‑by‑step” before answering help it handle multi‑step temporal normalization (e.g., “2 to 3 per month for the last 6 months”)?

### Label Scheme Design for Clinical Variability
Why needed here: The study’s success hinges on a label taxonomy (`<value> per <time>`, clusters, seizure‑free durations) that mirrors clinical documentation. A poor scheme would force unnatural mappings and hurt performance.  
Quick check: Why would mapping “seizure free for 9 months” to a single number (e.g., 0 seizures/month) be less informative than a dedicated “seizure‑free duration” label?

### Knowledge Distillation from Teacher to Student LLM
Why needed here: GPT‑5 is the teacher generating synthetic data and CoT rationales; smaller open‑weight models are the students trained on this synthetic supervision. This is the core transfer learning paradigm enabling deployment on NHS hardware.  
Quick check: What are the risks if the teacher model (GPT‑5) has systematic biases in its seizure frequency normalization that get transferred to the student?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map

### Critical Path
Base Template → Description → Template Instantiation → Letter Drafting → Placeholder Fill → GPT‑5 Verification → Filter → Training

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Template diversity vs. control:** Fewer base templates simplify generation but risk stylistic homogeneity. The paper uses only 10, noted as a limitation.
- **Synthetic scale vs. verification cost:** Step 7 (GPT‑5 re‑evaluation) discards ~30% of candidates, adding compute cost but ensuring label fidelity.
- **Output richness vs. decoding stability:** Format 4 (CoT+evidence) is most interpretable but requires strict structured output, risking format drift during fine‑tuning.

### Failure Signatures
- High discard rate in Step 7 (Table 2) indicates GPT‑5 struggles with complex narratives even in “reasoning mode.”
- Performance drop on `Real(150)` balanced set vs. `Real(300)` imbalanced set suggests models overfit to natural class distributions.
- Larger models (14B) sometimes underperform smaller ones (4B‑MedGemma) on pragmatic F1, indicating scale isn’t the sole factor; medical pretraining helps.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline replication:** Fine‑tune one model (e.g., Llama‑3.1‑8B) on 1,548 synthetic CoT letters and evaluate on Real(300) for Purist/Pragmatic F1. Confirms synthetic‑only viability.
2. **Output format ablation:** For the same model and synthetic data, compare `x per M`, `Categ.`, `Our label`, and `Our CoT` formats. Verifies structured label superiority.
3. **Scaling curve:** Train MedGemma‑4B with `Our CoT` on 500, 1,500, 5,000, 15,000 synthetic letters. Plot Purist/Pragmatic F1 vs. data size to observe diminishing returns and select cost‑effective scale.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to other healthcare systems:** How do models trained on synthetic UK NHS letters generalize to clinic letters from other healthcare systems (e.g., US, non‑English speaking regions) with different documentation conventions? *Basis:* The authors state in limitations that real clinic letters were drawn from a single UK centre; external validation is required.
2. **Semi‑automated conversion of numeric annotations:** Can semi‑automated pipelines convert existing numeric seizure frequency annotations for real clinic letters into the structured label scheme (ranges, clusters, seizure‑free durations) to enable joint training on real and synthetic data? *Basis:* Real KCH letters are annotated only with numeric labels, preventing joint training for structured output formats.
3. **Increasing synthetic template diversity:** Does increasing the diversity of synthetic base templates and clinical contexts overcome the observed scaling saturation and further improve model robustness on real letters? *Basis:* Scaling experiments showed diminishing returns beyond several thousand synthetic examples, and authors hypothesize this is due to limited diversity in the synthetic‑generation seed set (only 10 base letters).
4. **Clinical utility of evidence‑grounded outputs:** Do evidence‑grounded outputs with chain‑of‑thought reasoning and cited evidence spans meaningfully reduce clinician verification time and increase trust compared to standard numeric or categorical predictions? *Basis:* The authors propose as future work a human‑in‑the‑loop evaluation; no prospective study was conducted.
5. **Uncertainty‑aware outputs:** Can uncertainty‑aware outputs (e.g., calibrated confidence scores, predicted ranges) better represent the inherent ambiguity in seizure frequency documentation and improve clinical decision‑making over single‑point estimates or fixed categories? *Basis:* The authors list as future work integrating uncertainty‑aware outputs; current models output deterministic values.

## Limitations
- Synthetic data pipeline relies on unreleased base templates and GPT‑5 specifics, preventing independent replication of the generation process.
- Training hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, etc.) are not fully specified; using defaults may diverge from reported performance.
- Limited base template diversity (10 letters) may cause stylistic homogeneity and limit generalization across clinics.
