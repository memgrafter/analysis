# DoAtlas-1: A Causal Compilation Paradigm for Clinical AI

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19158
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19158
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Transforms narrative medical evidence into executable causal estimand objects, achieving 98.5% canonicalization accuracy and 80.5% executability across six causal query types, with external validation against real-world data.

## Executive Summary
Medical AI systems that generate narrative explanations lack the ability to quantify intervention effects, detect evidential conflicts, or validate claims—limiting their auditability in clinical settings. DoAtlas-1 introduces a causal compilation paradigm that converts heterogeneous study results into structured, executable causal estimand objects. Each estimand explicitly defines intervention contrast, effect scale, time horizon, and target population. The system canonicalizes effect measures, builds conflict-aware evidence graphs, and grounds claims in a large real-world cohort (Human Phenotype Project, n≈10,000). By compiling 1,445 kernels from 754 studies and supporting six query operators (e.g., do-calculus, counterfactuals), DoAtlas-1 shifts clinical AI from text generation to auditable, verifiable causal reasoning.

## Method Summary
The pipeline comprises three phases: (1) extraction of structured evidence cards from published studies, capturing population, intervention/contrast, outcome, time horizon, effect measure, and estimate; (2) canonicalization, which standardizes heterogeneous effect representations (e.g., HR→logHR) and aligns time horizons via deterministic bins; (3) graph construction, where comparable canonical claims are grouped into buckets, conflicts are detected using three decidable predicates, and a quality score selects a default kernel per bucket. An optional validation engine emulates each claim against the Human Phenotype Project to produce consistency flags. Six query operators then execute over the resulting evidence graph, returning answers with provenance, conflict annotations, and flags.

## Key Results
- Compiled 1,445 effect kernels from 754 clinical studies (cardiovascular, diabetes, metabolic disorders).
- Achieved 98.5% canonicalization accuracy (semantic preservation and scale/time correctness).
- Executability across six query types averaged 80.5%, with near-perfect correctness on executable queries.
- External validation against HPP achieved 62.5% coverage with 100% directional agreement and zero false positives.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1  
Standardizing heterogeneous effect measures and time horizons into a canonical representation enables cross-study comparability and deterministic bucket grouping. Each paper is parsed into an estimand ε = (Π, ι, o, τ, µ). The canonicalization operator N applies within-type reparameterizations (e.g., HR→logHR) and deterministic horizon alignment align(τ). Two claims are comparable iff their canonical bucket keys coincide.

### Mechanism 2  
Conflict detection formalized as decidable predicates makes evidential disagreements explicit and auditable. After canonicalization, comparable claims are collected into buckets B_s. Three predicates—Dir(B) (directional conflict), Int(B) (interval non-overlap), and Het(B) (high heterogeneity)—are evaluated over canonical parameters. Any detected conflict is stored in F(B) and returned in query results.

### Mechanism 3  
Validating literature claims against a large, multimodal real-world cohort grounds the causal atlas in empirical data. For each mappable claim, an HPP emulation replicates the exposure, outcome, adjustment, and time semantics. The resulting estimate is compared to the literature’s canonical (θ, CI). Agreement metrics produce validation signals that influence default selection and conflict adjudication.

## Foundational Learning
- **Intervention Contrast and Target Population**: These estimand components (ι, Π) define the precise causal question; misunderstanding them leads to invalid comparisons.  
  *Quick check*: In a study comparing “Finerenone vs placebo” in adults with type 2 diabetes, what are the contrast type and population bucket?

- **Effect Measure Families and Canonical Scales**: Different statistical models report effects in different parameterizations; knowing the family (ratio vs difference) and canonical scale (log vs identity) prevents mixing incompatible measures.  
  *Quick check*: Why is a hazard ratio placed on the log scale for canonicalization, while a mean difference stays on the identity scale?

- **Decidable Conflict Predicates**: Conflicts are captured by computable conditions that dictate how the system reports uncertainty.  
  *Quick check*: If two bucket claims have logHR values 0.2 [0.05, 0.35] and –0.1 [–0.25, 0.05], which specific conflict(s) does the detector raise?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Evidence Card Extractor -> Canonicalizer -> Graph Builder -> Validation Engine -> Query Engine

Critical path:  
1. Raw paper → Evidence Card (extraction).  
2. Evidence Card → Canonical claim (N).  
3. Canonical claims → Bucket grouping → Conflict check → Graph node/edge construction.  
4. Optional: Validation Engine mapping and HPP check → annotate buckets/kernels.  
5. User query → Query Engine determines required edges → aggregates answers with provenance and flags.

Design tradeoffs:  
- Strict canonicalization rules improve auditability but may exclude marginally comparable studies.  
- Three‑predicate conflict detection yields clear flags but may miss nuanced disagreements (e.g., effect modification).  
- External validation requires accurate variable mapping; ambiguous matches lower coverage but avoid false positives.

Failure signatures:  
- Invalid/empty fields → α records “missing field” and claim may be non‑executable.  
- Mixed measure types in bucket → query returns “mixed mtype” flag (e.g., HR vs RR).  
- Validation mapping failure → flags like “hpp missing X”, “contrast mismatch”, or “tau short”.

First experiments:  
1. Compilation correctness: 200 kernels reviewed; 98.5% semantic preservation, 99.0% scale correctness.  
2. Query executability: 1,445 kernels across six query types; average 80.5% executability with ~100% correctness on the executable subset.  
3. External validation: Against HPP, achieved 62.5% coverage with 100% direction agreement and zero false positives in negative controls.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the causal compilation pipeline be generalized to other medical domains (e.g., oncology, neurology) where evidence structures and data mappings differ? The current system is limited to cardiovascular, diabetes, and metabolic disorders; extending coverage requires adaptation of extraction schemas, canonicalization rules, and real‑world data availability.

2. Can automatic proxy derivation increase external validation coverage when literature variables lack direct counterparts in the HPP data dictionary? The current system aborts validation without a close match, missing opportunities to partially assess evidence credibility.

3. How to detect conflicts originating from methodological heterogeneity (e.g., differing covariate adjustments) or subgroup effect inconsistencies, which are not captured by the current conflict taxonomy? The taxonomy includes only three types; additional flags like “subgroup inconsistency” and “adjustment mismatch” appear in the mismatch analysis but are not formally defined.

4. Can the fixed parameters in DoAtlas (evidence quality weights, time binning, tolerance thresholds) be learned from data to improve default evidence selection and bucket comparability? The quality score uses manually chosen weights and time alignment policy is preset; learning could adapt to domain-specific patterns.

## Limitations
- Data opacity: Evidence cards (1,445 kernels) and HPP variable mappings are not publicly released, hindering independent verification.
- Hyperparameter gaps: Time bin definitions (B), alignment tolerance (ρ), heterogeneity threshold (Δ_het), and quality score weights are unspecified.
- Implementation details: Conflict detection predicates (interval non-overlap for asymmetric CIs, heterogeneity weight rule) and HPP emulation specifications lack full disclosure.
- Coverage constraints: External validation covers only 62.5% of kernels; many literature variables lack HPP equivalents.

## Confidence
- Canonicalization accuracy (98.5%): **Medium** (relies on unreleased corpus and extraction assumptions).  
- Query executability (80.5%): **Medium** (depends on bucket grouping rules and conflict handling; thresholds not reproducible).  
- External validation (100% directional agreement): **Low** (coverage is limited and mapping details unavailable).  
- Conflict detection completeness: **Low** (taxonomy intentionally narrow and not evaluated on comprehensive disagreement set).
