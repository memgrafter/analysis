# Sensitivity-Aware Retrieval-Augmented Intent Clarification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06025
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06025
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Proposes a three-step research framework for building retrieval-augmented conversational systems that prevent sensitive information leakage during iterative intent clarification, focusing on attack model definition, retrieval-level defenses (k-anonymity, differential privacy), and dual-metric evaluation.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a critical privacy risk in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) for conversational search: the potential leakage of sensitive information during multi-turn intent clarification dialogues, particularly in high-stakes domains like healthcare or government. The authors argue that existing defenses focused on the final answer are insufficient for exploratory scenarios where the system only emits clarifying questions, as an adversary could infer the presence of sensitive documents from patterns in the dialogue. As a vision paper, it outlines a research framework in three steps: first, formally define an attack model specific to iterative clarification; second, design sensitivity-aware interventions at the retrieval level (e.g., using k-anonymity or differential privacy) rather than relying on LLM prompt guardrails; and third, develop evaluation methods that jointly measure privacy protection (via attack success rate) and system utility (via downstream task performance). No empirical results are presented; the contribution is a structured research agenda.

## Method Summary
The proposed methodology is a three-stage framework for developing sensitivity-aware retrieval-augmented intent clarification systems. Stage 1 requires defining a rigorous attack model that characterizes the adversary’s goal (inferring sensitive document membership), knowledge (system architecture, defense types), capabilities (oracle access, jailbreaking), and the granularity of sensitivity (passage, document, collection). Stage 2 involves implementing retrieval-level defenses, considering two paradigms: “protect-then-search” (e.g., clustering documents into k-anonymous groups so individual membership is hidden) and “search-then-protect” (e.g., adding calibrated noise to retrieval scores via differential privacy). Stage 3 focuses on evaluation, proposing to measure the trade-off between privacy (attack success rate under the defined model) and utility (performance on a downstream task like relevant document retrieval after clarification), using datasets such as SARA. The approach prioritizes intervening before sensitive data reaches the LLM, which is seen as less trustworthy for enforcing constraints.

## Key Results
- The paper presents a formal research framework with three sequential steps: attack model definition, retrieval-level defense design, and dual-metric evaluation.
- It identifies retrieval-level interventions (k-anonymity, differential privacy) as preferable to LLM-centric prompt-based guardrails against jailbreaking.
- As a vision paper, it provides no empirical validation or specific performance metrics; the outcome is a proposed agenda for future work.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A formally defined attack model is the necessary foundation for designing effective sensitivity-aware interventions in retrieval-augmented intent clarification.
- **Mechanism:** The attack model explicitly characterizes the adversary’s goal (inferring the presence of specific sensitive documents via observed clarification questions), knowledge (system architecture, possible defense types), and capabilities (oracle access, jailbreaking). It also defines sensitivity granularity (passage, document, collection) to scope protection.
- **Core assumption:** Without a rigorously specified adversarial perspective, defense designs will be reactive and incomplete, overlooking indirect leakage pathways unique to exploratory dialogue.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract lists “define an attack model” as Step 1. Section 3 (Page 3) states a clear attack model must include attacker goal, knowledge, capabilities, and granularity. Neighbor papers on membership inference attacks do not address iterative clarification, creating a gap this model fills.
- **Break condition:** If the attack model cannot generate feasible adversarial strategies that exploit iterative clarification (e.g., via question phrasing patterns or topic sequencing), it fails to capture the true risk surface.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Implementing sensitivity-aware defenses at the retrieval level creates a more robust security boundary than relying on LLM-centric prompt-based guardrails, which are vulnerable to jailbreaking.
- **Mechanism:** Two retrieval-level paradigms are proposed: (1) *protect-then-search* via k-anonymity abstractions (e.g., clustering documents into indistinguishable groups of size *k*), and (2) *search-then-protect* via differential privacy (adding calibrated noise to retrieval scores to obscure document inclusion).
- **Core assumption:** The LLM component is inherently less trustworthy for enforcing sensitivity constraints due to instruction-following brittleness; intervening earlier prevents sensitive data from ever reaching the LLM.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract’s Step 2 specifies “design sensitivity-aware defenses on the retrieval level.” Section 4 (Page 4) contrasts retrieval-level defenses with “relying on the LLM itself for defense,” proposing k-anonymity and differential privacy. Prior SAS work (e.g., McKechnie) handles sensitivity in traditional search but not in intent clarification contexts.
- **Break condition:** If the abstraction (k-anonymity) or noise (DP) mechanisms substantially degrade the semantic utility of retrieved context—measured by collapse in the LLM’s ability to generate coherent, intent-relevant questions—the defense undermines the system’s primary function.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Validating sensitivity-aware interventions requires a dual-metric evaluation framework that jointly quantifies privacy protection (attack resistance) and conversational utility (downstream task performance), as standard IR metrics are insufficient.
- **Mechanism:** Privacy is measured by the success rate of the defined attack model (e.g., adversary’s accuracy in inferring document membership from the dialogue). Utility is measured by performance on a concrete downstream task (e.g., retrieval of relevant documents after intent clarification), not by superficial question quality. The trade-off is mapped by varying defense parameters (e.g., *k* in k-anonymity, ε in DP).
- **Core assumption:** There is a fundamental tension between obscuring sensitive information and preserving the informational value of the retrieval context; optimizing one metric alone leads to a failed system.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract’s Step 3 calls for “evaluation methods to measure the trade-off.” Section 4 (Page 4) specifies measuring protection at multiple privacy budgets and utility on a downstream task, referencing datasets like SARA. The SARA dataset paper provides “sensitivity-aware relevance assessments,” anchoring joint evaluation feasibility.
- **Break condition:** If attack success rate does not correlate with empirical leakage in human or simulator studies, or if downstream utility metrics remain unchanged across drastically different defense strengths (indicating insensitivity), the evaluation framework is invalid.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Exploratory search paradigm  
  **Why needed here:** The paper reconceptualizes intent clarification as an evolving, iterative process (berrypicking) rather than a single-turn lookup. This framing justifies retrieval augmentation and reveals that leakage risks accumulate across turns via indirect signals (e.g., question topics, sequence), not just final answers.  
  **Quick check:** How does the iterative nature of exploratory search create *different* privacy risks compared to a standard single-turn Q&A system with RAG?

- **Concept:** Sensitivity-aware search (SAS)  
  **Why needed here:** SAS is the direct precursor on protecting sensitive information *during* search. The paper builds on SAS concepts (e.g., cascading pipelines, bi-objective optimization) but extends them to conversational intent clarification where the output is a question, not a document list.  
  **Quick check:** What are the two main SAS paradigms (‘protect-then-search’ vs. ‘search-then-protect’), and why might the conversational output medium change their applicability?

- **Concept:** Membership Inference Attacks (MIA) in RAG  
  **Why needed here:** MIAs are the canonical threat model for inferring whether a specific document was in a private database via model outputs. Understanding standard RAG MIAs is prerequisite to seeing why they are *insufficient* for exploratory clarification, which requires new attack models.  
  **Quick check:** Why can't standard RAG MIAs, which often involve asking the system to complete or confirm specific facts, be directly applied to attack a system that only emits clarification questions?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** UserInterface -> DialogueManager -> RetrievalModule (SensitivityDefenseLayer) -> IntentClarificationModule -> DialogueManager (loop); SensitiveDocumentCollection -> RetrievalModule; AttackSimulator -> System; EvaluationMetrics -> System
- **Critical path (for a single clarification cycle):**  
  1. User provides initial ambiguous query via UserInterface.  
  2. DialogueManager triggers RetrievalModule (with active SensitivityDefenseLayer).  
  3. RetrievalModule fetches and processes context from SensitiveDocumentCollection.  
  4. IntentClarificationModule receives sanitized context and generates a clarification question.  
  5. User answers; DialogueManager updates state; loop back to step 2 until intent is formalized.  
  6. Downstream task (e.g., FinalRetrieval) executes with clarified intent.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Privacy-Utility:** Stronger privacy (higher *k*, larger noise ε) reduces leakage risk but may make retrieved context too abstract/noisy for useful clarification.  
  - **Granularity:** Protecting at the passage level is stricter but more utility-destructive than at document or collection level.  
  - **Evaluation Scope:** Prioritizing attack success rate may lead to over-defenses that cripple utility; downstream metrics may not capture subtle leakage. Balanced Pareto analysis is needed.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Privacy failure:** Attack success rate remains near chance (<<10% above random) despite strong defenses, or humans can still identify sensitive document presence.  
  - **Utility failure:** Downstream task performance (e.g., nDCG@10) degrades >20% vs. no-defense baseline, or user satisfaction plummets due to irrelevant/vague questions.  
  - **Stability failure:** System outputs become incoherent or repetitive due to over-abstraction (k-anonymity) or excessive noise (DP).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline Attack Validation:** Implement the proposed attack model against a standard RAG clarification system (no defenses). Measure upper bound on leakage risk using simulated dialogues on a dataset like SARA.  
  2. **k-anonymity Intervention:** Implement a passage-level k-anonymity layer (e.g., via topic clustering). Systematically vary *k* (e.g., 1,5,10,50). For each, measure (a) attack success rate and (b) downstream retrieval nDCG after simulated clarification.  
  3. **Differential Privacy Intervention:** Implement DP noise (e.g., Gaussian) on retrieval scores. Vary privacy budget ε (e.g., 0.1,1.0,10.0). Measure same metrics as Experiment 2. Compare privacy-utility curves to k-anonymity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** How should the attack model for exploratory intent clarification be concretely defined, including the adversary’s specific goals, prior knowledge, and capabilities?  
  **Basis:** The paper states the first step is to “define an attack model” that includes “the attacker’s goal, knowledge and capabilities,” but provides no specifics.  
  **Why unresolved:** Without a formalized attack model, it is impossible to design targeted defenses or evaluate their effectiveness. The exploratory nature of the dialogue, where attacks use indirect signals from clarifying questions rather than direct answers, introduces unique challenges not covered by existing RAG membership inference attacks.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** A rigorous threat model that specifies the adversary’s objectives (e.g., inferring document membership, reconstructing sensitive content), feasible attack strategies (e.g., manipulating the dialogue to elicit revealing retrieval patterns), and practical constraints (e.g., query budget, black-box access).

- **Question:** At what granularity (e.g., text spans, whole documents, document groups) should sensitivity be defined and protected within the retrieval-augmented clarification pipeline?  
  **Basis:** The paper notes that “sensitivity can exist at different levels, such as individual passages of text, whole documents, or entire collections” and calls for a “very careful description of what exactly is considered sensitive information.”  
  **Why unresolved:** The granularity choice fundamentally impacts the design of defenses (e.g., k-anonymity on document groups vs. passage-level sanitization) and evaluation. An inappropriate granularity could either leak sensitive details or render the system unusable by over-protecting.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** An analysis of typical sensitive data structures in target domains (e.g., healthcare records, FOIA documents) combined with a risk assessment of exposure scenarios at different granularities, supported by stakeholder requirements (legal, ethical).

- **Question:** How can the proposed retrieval-level defenses—k-anonymity via document abstraction and differential privacy via result noise—be operationalized to balance protection with the utility of generating useful clarifying questions?  
  **Basis:** The paper proposes these two directions as alternatives to LLM-based guardrails but offers no implementation details or theoretical guarantees for the exploratory intent clarification setting.  
  **Why unresolved:** Applying k-anonymity requires defining suitable abstractions (topics, labels) that preserve semantic utility for question generation. Applying differential privacy requires calibrating noise to the sensitivity of retrieval scores without disrupting the iterative, intent-forming dialogue. Both lack concrete algorithms or parameters.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Formulations of these interventions within a retrieval framework, including algorithms for creating k-anonymous groups or adding calibrated noise, followed by pilot experiments measuring their impact on the quality and coherence of generated clarifying questions in a controlled setting.

- **Question:** What evaluation methodology best captures the trade-off between privacy protection and the downstream utility of the clarification process, particularly for non-factual tasks like intent evolution?  
  **Basis:** The paper calls for “new evaluation methods” to measure the trade-off, suggesting using “success rate of attacks” for protection and “effect on a downstream task” (e.g., relevant document retrieval) for utility, with datasets like Avocado and SARA.  
  **Why unresolved:** Existing metrics (e.g., retrieval relevance scores) may not fully capture the utility of an *exploratory* process, where success is measured by the user’s evolving understanding, not just final document retrieval. Additionally, the “success rate of attacks” is ambiguous without a defined attack model.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** A suite of metrics that combine privacy guarantees (e.g., empirical attack success under the defined model) with utility measures tailored to exploratory search (e.g., reduction in user clarification turns, improvement in final query specificity, or simulated user satisfaction in a task-oriented simulation).

## Limitations
- The attack model operationalization is unclear; it is unknown which dialogue features (e.g., question phrasing, topic sequence) leak membership in iterative clarification.
- Utility evaluation lacks a standardized protocol for measuring intent clarification effectiveness on downstream retrieval; simulating evolving intent is non-trivial.
- Defense feasibility is uncertain: k-anonymity may over-abstract, DP noise may distort semantics, and the impact on question relevance is unknown.
