# Pre-Deployment Complexity Estimation for Federated Perception Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.28282
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28282
- **Reference count:** 22
- **Primary result:** Introduces a classifier-agnostic complexity metric F(d,X) that combines intrinsic dataset properties and client composition; strong negative correlation with FL accuracy (R² up to 0.85) and positive correlation with communication effort, enabling pre-deployment feasibility assessment.

## Executive Summary
Federated learning (FL) for edge perception systems faces challenges due to data heterogeneity and resource constraints, but there is a lack of tools to estimate difficulty before deployment. This paper proposes a pre-deployment diagnostic framework that quantifies FL complexity using two components: intrinsic dataset characteristics (dimensionality, sparsity, heterogeneity) and distributed environment characteristics (client participation patterns). The combined metric F(d,X) is classifier‑agnostic and computed without training. Experiments on three MNIST variants show high correlation between the estimated complexity and observed FL accuracy and communication cost, demonstrating its utility for resource planning and feasibility checks.

## Method Summary
The framework first extracts intrinsic dataset properties in a label‑free manner: heterogeneity via pixel entropy, sparsity via PCA/Isomap variance thresholds, and intrinsic dimensionality via k‑NN estimators. These form an intrinsic complexity vector X. Next, the distributed complexity f(d) is computed from the participation frequencies m_j of clients across conceptual federated paths as f(d)=∑(1/m_j), capturing fragmentation and imbalance. The final complexity score is F(d,X)=β·||X||₂ + f(d), where β is a normalization constant. The metric is then correlated with FL outcomes (maximum/average accuracy, rounds to reach a target) from a pilot FedAvg run with a fixed shallow CNN. A strong negative correlation with accuracy and positive correlation with communication effort validates the approach.

## Key Results
- F(d,X) yields R² = 0.81 and 0.85 for maximum and average FL accuracy, respectively, on MNIST variants.
- The metric correlates positively with communication rounds needed to achieve 60% accuracy.
- Ablation experiments confirm both intrinsic (f(X)) and distributed (f(d)) components contribute additively to predictive power.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Intrinsic Dataset Complexity (f(X))**: The framework measures three fundamental aspects: dimensionality (intrinsic dimensionality estimate), sparsity (fraction of variance explained by PCA/Isomap components), and heterogeneity (Shannon entropy of pixel distributions). These are computed without any classifier, providing a scalar f(X) that reflects inherent learnability challenges. Higher f(X) indicates data that is harder to represent, regardless of distribution.

**Client Composition Complexity (f(d))**: By analyzing the hypothetical set of federated training paths and the frequency m_j with which each client participates, the metric f(d)=∑(1/m_j) captures fragmentation and participation imbalance. More uneven involvement leads to higher f(d), which destabilizes model aggregation and increases communication rounds, as shown by controlled experiments varying client numbers and data skew.

**Additive Combination (F(d,X))**: Summing f(X) and f(d) produces a single pre‑deployment score. The reported high R² values for correlation with FL accuracy and communication effort demonstrate that the two sources of difficulty combine approximately linearly and that the metric successfully predicts FL outcomes. Isolation experiments confirm each component independently affects performance, and the additive model outperforms using either alone.

## Foundational Learning
- **Federated Learning (FedAvg)**  
  *Why needed*: FL is the target setting; understanding round‑based aggregation, client selection, and non‑IID effects is essential to interpret f(d) and experimental results.  
  *Quick check*: What is the core difference between local training and federated averaging, and why does non‑IID data make FL harder?

- **Intrinsic Dimensionality Estimation (e.g., Levina‑Bickel)**  
  *Why needed*: Provides a label‑free measure of the latent manifold size, a key component of f(X). It distinguishes true dimensionality from raw feature count.  
  *Quick check*: How does intrinsic dimensionality differ from the number of raw features, and why is a lower ID potentially easier for learning?

- **Entropy and Dimensionality Reduction (PCA, Isomap)**  
  *Why needed*: Entropy quantifies heterogeneity of pixel distributions. PCA/Isomap quantify sparsity by measuring how compactly variance is captured in a few components. Both are computed without training a classifier.  
  *Quick check*: What does high PCA‑based sparsity (variance captured in few components) imply about the dataset’s structure, and why might that reduce complexity?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Data Pre‑processor → Intrinsic Complexity Module → Integrator; Distributed Environment Configurator → Integrator.

**Critical path**: Raw data is fed to the Data Pre‑processor, which outputs basic statistics to the Intrinsic Complexity Module. The module computes the intrinsic measures to form vector X. In parallel, the Configurator defines the federated environment and outputs f(d). The Integrator combines f(X)=β·||X||₂ and f(d) into the final metric F(d,X). The Diagnostic Evaluator correlates this score with FL outcomes from a pilot run.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- PCA (linear, fast) vs. Isomap (non‑linear, sensitive to manifold) for sparsity measurement.  
- Classifier‑agnostic design enhances generality but may miss model‑specific failure modes.  
- Simple additive combination is interpretable but may not capture nonlinear interactions between intrinsic and distributed factors.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Low R² (<0.7) when correlating with new datasets, indicating missing complexity factors.  
- High variance in f(d) under highly dynamic client participation, not captured by static m_j.  
- Discrepancy between correlation with maximum vs. average accuracy, suggesting the metric predicts sustained performance better than peak.

2. Isolate f(d) effect: With one dataset (e.g., Handwritten‑MNIST), vary number of clients and data partition skew to change f(d) while keeping f(X) constant; observe monotonic decrease in FL accuracy and increase in communication rounds.  
3. Test generalizability on CIFAR‑10: Apply the pipeline with different non‑IID partitions; compute F(d,X) and check correlation with FL outcomes to identify any missing intrinsic measures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does the proposed additive complexity model F(d,X) = f(X) + f(d) optimally capture the interaction between intrinsic and distributed complexity, or would a multiplicative or learned combination better predict federated learning difficulty?
- How does the complexity metric perform on datasets with fundamentally different data modalities (e.g., 3D point clouds, audio, or multimodal streams) and more realistic, non‑uniform client data distributions?
- Is the complexity metric F(d,X) truly classifier‑agnostic, or does its predictive power diminish for model architectures significantly different from the shallow CNN used in experiments (e.g., Vision Transformers, or models with varying capacity)?
- How does the metric account for, or fail to account for, dynamic client participation patterns and straggler effects, which are hallmarks of real‑world federated learning?
- What specific aspects of the intrinsic complexity measures (dimensionality, sparsity, heterogeneity) contribute most to predictive power, and can the metric be simplified or streamlined by identifying a minimal subset of measures?

## Limitations
- Missing experimental details (exact CNN architecture, non‑IID partitioning scheme, normalization constant β) hinder exact replication and may introduce variability.
- The additive assumption may oversimplify interactions between intrinsic and distributed complexities; no evidence that this model is optimal.
- Evaluation is confined to three MNIST‑variant image datasets, limiting generalizability to other perception modalities (e.g., point clouds, audio) and more realistic, highly skewed client distributions.
