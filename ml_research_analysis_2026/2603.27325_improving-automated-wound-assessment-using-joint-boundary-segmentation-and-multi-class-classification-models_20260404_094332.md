# Improving Automated Wound Assessment Using Joint Boundary Segmentation and Multi-Class Classification Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.27325
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27325
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Unified YOLOv11 achieves segmentation F1 up to 0.9341 and classification F1 up to 0.8736; YOLOv11n maintains 0.9285/0.8607 at 1/4 training cost.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the lack of automated tools for comprehensive wound assessment by introducing a single deep learning model that simultaneously delineates wound boundaries and classifies wound type among five common categories (burn, pressure, diabetic foot, vascular, surgical). Using a balanced dataset of 2,963 clinical images, augmented to 7,147 with geometric and color transformations, the authors train YOLOv11 variants via stratified five‑fold cross‑validation. The largest model (YOLOv11x) reaches F1‑scores of 0.9341 for segmentation and 0.8736 for classification, while the lightweight YOLOv11n attains competitive scores (0.9285, 0.8607) with a quarter of the training cost. Augmentation is shown to significantly improve detection of subtly presented burn injuries, indicating the model’s robustness to challenging cases.

## Method Summary
The authors employ YOLOv11, an anchor‑free instance segmentation framework, to perform joint wound boundary segmentation (WBS) and multi‑class wound classification (WC). The model shares a common backbone (C3k2 blocks) and neck (SPPF and PANet) to extract hierarchical features, which feed two task‑specific heads: an anchor‑free detection head for class and bounding box regression, and a prototype‑based segmentation head for mask generation. Training uses a compound loss combining binary cross‑entropy/IoU for masks, cross‑entropy for classification, and distribution focal loss for boxes. The dataset comprises 2,963 balanced images from public wound repositories, resized to 640×640 pixels. To improve generalization, the training set is expanded to 7,147 images via horizontal/vertical flips, 90° rotations, and adjustments to brightness (±15%), saturation (±25%), and exposure (±10%). Models are trained for 150 epochs with AdamW (default configuration) under a stratified five‑fold cross‑validation protocol. Performance is evaluated using F1‑score, mAP50, mAP50‑95, precision, recall, and AUROC for both tasks.

## Key Results
- YOLOv11x (largest) achieved segmentation F1 0.9341 and classification F1 0.8736.  
- YOLOv11n (lightweight) achieved segmentation F1 0.9285 and classification F1 0.8607, with only ~25% of the training cost.  
- Data augmentation markedly improved detection of subtle burn injuries, reducing false negatives and enhancing overall generalizability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A unified instance segmentation architecture (YOLOv11) performs joint wound boundary segmentation (WBS) and multi‑class wound classification (WC) by sharing a common backbone‑neck feature extraction pipeline before task‑specific heads, reducing error propagation and computational overhead compared to separate models.
- **Mechanism:** The YOLOv11 backbone (C3k2 blocks) and neck (SPPF, PANet) generate hierarchical feature maps. These are fed in parallel to two heads: an anchor‑free detection head for class/box regression and a prototype‑based segmentation head for mask generation. A compound loss function (BCE/IoU for masks, Cross‑Entropy for classification, DFL for boxes) jointly optimizes both tasks.
- **Core assumption:** The shared visual features sufficient for precise localization (bounding box) are also sufficiently discriminative for classifying wound etiology (BI, DFU, etc.), and the multi‑task loss balancing prevents one task from dominating learning.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “a unified deep learning model for simultaneous wound boundary segmentation and five‑class classification”
  - [section] “the architecture follows a backbone–neck–head design… The head is composed of two branches: an anchor‑free detection head… and a segmentation head”
  - [corpus] Corpus neighbors largely describe separate segmentation or classification models, implying this joint approach is a distinguishing architectural choice.
- **Break condition:** If the feature representations required for pixel‑accurate segmentation (fine textures, edges) are fundamentally misaligned with those for image‑level classification (global context, patterns), joint optimization could lead to degraded performance on one or both tasks. Evidence of this would be a significant performance drop versus dedicated single‑task models.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Extensive geometric and photometric data augmentation mitigates the model’s initial difficulty in detecting visually subtle burn injuries (BI) by forcing it to learn invariant features from low‑contrast, ambiguous presentations, thus reducing background false negatives (BG FN).
- **Mechanism:** Augmentation (rotations, flips, brightness/saturation/exposure shifts) synthetically expands the dataset’s distribution. For BI—which may have indistinct borders and color similar to surrounding skin—these variations present the model with altered lighting, orientation, and context, compelling it to rely on more robust, subtle textural and shape features rather than spurious color or placement correlations.
- **Core assumption:** The core discriminative signal for subtle BI exists in the original images but is drowned out by dataset‑specific biases (e.g., consistent lighting, patient positioning). Augmentation acts as a regularizer to expose these signals.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “augmentation significantly improved model performance, particularly in detecting visually subtle BI cases”
  - [section] “BI detection accuracy was relatively lower… dataset was augmented… to help the model learn more generalized and invariant features”
  - [corpus] Neighbors like “Dual‑Attention U‑Net++… for Precise Wound and Scale Marker Segmentation” [43233] use attention to focus on subtle features, supporting the need for specialized mechanisms to handle difficult cases.
- **Break condition:** If augmentation

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi‑task Joint Learning** – Combines segmentation and classification in one network to avoid error cascades and share parameters. *Why needed:* Reduces computational overhead and ensures features optimized for localization also benefit classification. *Quick check:* Verify a single model outputs both masks and class probabilities; compare total FLOPs vs two separate models.
- **YOLOv11 Backbone‑Neck Architecture** – Utilizes C3k2 blocks for efficient feature extraction and SPPF/PANet for multiscale fusion. *Why needed:* Provides hierarchical features that support both fine‑grained mask delineation and high‑level class discrimination. *Quick check:* Inspect model summary for C3k2 and SPPF layers; visualize feature maps at different scales.
- **Data Augmentation for Invariance** – Geometric (flips, rotations) and photometric (brightness, saturation, exposure) transformations. *Why needed:* Exposes the model to varied appearances, crucial for subtle wounds like burn injuries that blend with skin. *Quick check:* Compare per‑class recall with and without augmentation on a held‑out set.
- **Stratified K‑Fold Cross‑Validation** – Ensures each fold preserves the class distribution of wound types. *Why needed:* Provides reliable performance estimates despite moderate dataset size. *Quick check:* Check class ratios in train/validation splits across folds; they should be similar.
- **Compound Loss Function** – Combines BCE/IoU for segmentation, cross‑entropy for classification, and DFL for bounding box regression. *Why needed:* Simultaneously optimizes different output types with appropriate losses. *Quick check:* Monitor individual loss components during training; ensure none diverge or dominate.
- **Anchor‑Free Detection** – YOLOv11 uses anchor‑free predictions for bounding boxes. *Why needed:* Simplifies the head and adapts better to variable wound shapes. *Quick check:* Verify model outputs center‑offsets and scales without predefined anchors.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Image (640×640) → Backbone (C3k2) → Neck (SPPF + PANet) → [Detection Head (class & box), Segmentation Head (prototype)] → Outputs (class labels, bounding boxes, masks)

**Critical Path:**  
Resize → Backbone feature extraction → Neck feature aggregation → Parallel head inference → Loss calculation → Backpropagation.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Joint vs separate models: Joint reduces compute but risks feature misalignment.  
- Model size: n/s/m/l/x variants trade accuracy for speed/memory.  
- Augmentation: Improves robustness but may distort realistic wound textures if excessive.  
- Loss weighting: Balancing BCE, CE, DFL influences task priorities.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Recall for burn injuries remains low despite augmentation.  
- High variance in per‑fold metrics for specific wound types.  
- Segmentation masks leak into background or fail to capture full wound area.  
- Training loss plateaus while validation loss rises (overfitting).

**First Three Experiments:**  
1. Train without any augmentation to quantify its impact, especially on BI recall.  
2. Train isolated segmentation‑only and classification‑only models to measure the cost/benefit of joint learning.  
3. Compare YOLOv11n, s, and m on accuracy vs. training time to identify the practical sweet spot for deployment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Demographic robustness:** How to ensure consistent performance across skin tones, ages, sexes, and body locations? The current dataset lacks this diversity.
- **Clinical metadata integration:** Can incorporating wound location, patient history, or comorbidities improve classification of ambiguous wounds?
- **Semi‑supervised learning:** Can reducing annotation effort via unlabeled data maintain performance?
- **Edge deployment:** What optimizations (quantization, pruning) are needed for real‑time use on point‑of‑care devices?
- **Explainable AI:** Which XAI methods yield explanations clinicians trust, and how do they affect adoption?

## Limitations
- Single‑source public dataset limits diversity in imaging devices, skin tones, wound stages, and clinical settings.
- Hyperparameter configuration (learning rate schedule, batch size) is unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Shared backbone may cause task interference under certain distribution shifts.

## Confidence
- Unified model joint efficacy: **Medium** – strong internal validation but no external testing.
- Augmentation for subtle BI detection: **High** – explicit improvement in BI recall.
- Generalizability to new environments: **Low** – no cross‑dataset evidence.
