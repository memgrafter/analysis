# Designing for Disagreement: Front-End Guardrails for Assistance Allocation in LLM-Enabled Robots

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16537
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16537
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Proposes bounded calibration with contestability—a front-end pattern that constrains LLM robot assistance allocation to governance-approved prioritization modes, ensures legible deferral disclosures, and provides outcome-specific contest pathways, mitigating hidden value skews and burdensome configuration.

## Executive Summary
LLM-enabled robots operating in multi-user environments face value pluralism and behavioral variability when allocating scarce assistance. This paper introduces a procedural design pattern—bounded calibration with contestability—to impose transparent, contestable guardrails. The pattern restricts prioritization to a predefined menu of governance-approved modes, makes the active mode legible at the moment of deferral, and offers a lightweight contest pathway that addresses specific grievances without reopening global rules. This shifts focus from achieving fair outcomes to ensuring a fair process. The authors outline an evaluation agenda but present no empirical data, positioning the work as a conceptual framework for responsible deployment.

## Method Summary
The proposed method comprises three integrated components. First, a governance layer (Define) allows authorized personnel to select from a small, fixed set of prioritization modes (e.g., urgency-first, queue-order). Second, the robot’s allocation engine uses the active mode to guide LLM-based decisions during concurrent user requests. Third, when deferring a user, the robot explicitly states the active mode and its immediate reason via a legibility renderer. Users can contest a specific deferral through a dedicated gateway (e.g., a “Challenge” button or voice command) that routes to human escalation without altering the global mode. All actions are logged for audit. The evaluation plan targets legibility (comprehension of active mode), procedural legitimacy (fairness perception), and actionability (contest pathway usability) via vignette studies, wizard-of-oz experiments, and governance workshops.

## Key Results
- Introduced the bounded calibration with contestability pattern as a front-end guardrail for LLM-enabled robots in multi-user assistance scenarios.
- The pattern addresses hidden value skews and configuration burdens by limiting prioritization principles to a governance-approved menu and ensuring real-time legibility and contestability.
- Outlines an evaluation agenda but provides no empirical metrics; contribution is a procedural design framework.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Bounded Menu of Prioritization Modes
- **Claim:** Constraining prioritization to a pre-defined, governance-approved menu prevents silent defaults that embed unexamined value commitments.
- **Mechanism:** An upstream "Define" governance layer restricts the system's actionable prioritization principles (e.g., urgency-first, queue-order) to a bounded set, prohibiting discriminatory or extreme configurations. This transforms an open-ended, LLM-mediated value decision into a selectable parameter within institutional boundaries.
- **Core assumption:** Deployer organizations have the capacity and legitimacy to define a limited set of contextually appropriate prioritization principles for a given setting.
- **Evidence anchors:** The pattern is described in the abstract and Section 3.2; neighbor papers on safety guardrails and multi-object navigation provide indirect support but not specific to allocation menus.
- **Break condition:** If the governance layer cannot define a defensible, non-harmful menu, or if the menu is too limited to cover legitimate contextual variation, the pattern fails to address pluralism and may force inappropriate defaults.

### Mechanism 2: Interaction-Relevant Legibility at Deferral
- **Claim:** Continuous, interaction-relevant legibility of the active prioritization mode during deferral makes the robot's value commitment inspectable and mitigates the "black box" perception of LLM-driven decisions.
- **Mechanism:** At the moment of allocating scarcity, the robot explicitly communicates the active, selected mode in terms relevant to the interaction (e.g., "Priority mode: urgent needs first — I’ll return to you next"). This ties an abstract principle to the concrete experience of deferral.
- **Core assumption:** Users can comprehend a short, principled label in the moment of being deferred and connect it to the robot's action.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 4 provide examples; HRI transparency literature informs the assumption but does not validate this specific disclosure moment.
- **Break condition:** If the mode label is ambiguous, culturally incongruent, or fails to map onto the user's perception (e.g., disagreement about urgency), legibility breaks down and may exacerbate perceived unfairness.

### Mechanism 3: Outcome-Specific Contest Pathway
- **Claim:** Providing an outcome-specific, low-barrier contest pathway that does not require renegotiating the global rule affords procedural recourse and maintains stability of the broader governance framework.
- **Mechanism:** When a user contests a specific deferral, the system routes them through a dedicated pathway (e.g., a button, spoken phrase, operator channel) that addresses that instance—e.g., escalating to a human staff member—without allowing the user to change the overarching mode for everyone. This separates resolution of a single incident from revision of the standing rule.
- **Core assumption:** Users will perceive a contest pathway as meaningful and usable for specific grievances even if they cannot change the general rule, and that staff/humans are available to receive escalated contests.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract, Section 3.2, and Section 4; references on procedural justice provide theoretical basis but not the specific instantiation.
- **Break condition:** If the contest mechanism is burdensome, inaccessible, or perceived as futile (e.g., escalations ignored), it fails as a safeguard. If global rule renegotiation is the only perceived meaningful recourse, the pathway is seen as tokenistic.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Value Pluralism in Allocation**  
Why needed: The pattern assumes reasonable people will disagree on prioritization principles; understanding this rejects a single "correct" silent default.  
Quick check: Can you articulate two different, reasonable prioritization principles for a robot assisting multiple people in a train station, and why choosing one over the other as a silent default is a value-laden governance act?

**Concept: LLM Behavioral Variability & Uncertainty**  
Why needed: The problem includes stochastic, context-sensitive LLM outputs; any implicit, unconstrained value encoding may be unstable and un-inspectable at interaction time.  
Quick check: How could an LLM's internal "reasoning" about who to assist first vary across two similar scenarios, and why would that variability be a governance concern even if the outcome seems acceptable?

**Concept: Procedural Justice & Contestability (HCI)**  
Why needed: Legitimacy and fairness perception hinge on the *process* (transparent, contestable) not just the outcome; shifts design from "fair robot" to "fair process".  
Quick check: According to procedural justice theory, what are two key features of a "meaningful" contestation mechanism that go beyond simply having an "appeal" button?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Governance Layer (Define) → Allocation Engine (LLM-based reasoning) → Legibility Renderer → Contest Gateway → Audit & Review Log. Feedback: Audit Log informs future Governance Layer decisions.

**Critical path:**  
1. Define: Admin selects active PrioritizationMode from menu.  
2. Allocation Engine: LLM reasons with mode to produce DeferralDecision.  
3. Legibility Renderer: generates disclosure statement at deferral.  
4. User experiences deferral; may trigger Contest Gateway.  
5. Contest Gateway logs ContestEvent and optionally escalates to human.  
6. Audit & Review Log stores all tuples; periodic review feeds back to Define.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Abstraction Level: High (principled modes) vs. Low (contextual rules). High abstraction more robust but blunt; low risks context-blindness.  
- Contest Pathway: Low-barrier (e.g., single button) vs. High-information (requires explanation). Low-barrier increases usability but may generate noise; high-information may deter use.  
- Mode Stability: Long-duration mode windows (predictable) vs. Short-duration (responsive). Long reduces confusion but inflexible; short increases overhead and user surprise.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Silent Default Recurrence: Logs show periods with no active PrioritizationMode, implying fallback to an implicit rule.  
- Legibility Breakdown: Spike in user confusion/contests immediately after a mode change.  
- Contest Channel Atrophy: Sparse ContestEvent logs or dominance by one user group, indicating uneven accessibility or perceived futility.  
- Governance Bypass: Attempts to set a mode not in the approved menu are logged but succeed (role-gating failure).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Vignette Legibility Study: Present participants with text/video vignettes of a robot deferring using different mode disclosures; measure comprehension of the rule and anticipated fairness.  
2. Wizard-of-Oz Timing & Interruption Study: Simulate a busy concourse with confederates; measure how often users notice/contest deferrals under different disclosure timings and contest channel modalities.  
3. Governance Workshop for Menu Feasibility: With stakeholders, run card-sorting/ranking to define a minimal, admissible menu of 3–4 modes for a specific context; assess consensus, conflicts, and perceived exclusions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**1. How do the evaluation benchmarks (legibility, procedural legitimacy, actionability) empirically manifest and correlate?**  
Basis: Paper outlines an agenda but no empirical metrics. Unresolved: Pattern is untested; interdependence unknown. Resolution: Controlled vignette and HRI studies comparing guardrail conditions (e.g., silent default vs. legible mode vs. legible+contestability).

**2. How equitably usable are contestation pathways across diverse user groups?**  
Basis: Limitations explicitly note "contestability may be unevenly usable across user groups and access needs". Unresolved: Pathway design unspecified; accessibility untested. Resolution: Studies recruiting diverse users (language, age, disability) in simulated contention; measure contest initiation/completion, cognitive load, ease-of-use.

**3. What organizational and governance capacities are needed to sustainably define and maintain a bounded, admissible menu?**  
Basis: Pattern depends on governance-approved menu but ignores curation process. Unresolved: Feasibility, overhead, and risk of bias/stagnation unknown. Resolution: Organizational ethnographies or participatory workshops with deployer institutions to map mode definition process and stakeholder conflicts.

**4. Does continuous legibility of the active mode induce automation bias, causing users to forgo contestation even when allocations are problematic?**  
Basis: Automation bias listed as a risk. Unresolved: Transparency may increase trust and reduce vigilance; net effect unknown. Resolution: Longitudinal/repeated-measures studies comparing contestation rates with/without persistent legibility, measuring perceived system competence and responsibility.

## Limitations
- **Governance assumption:** Pattern relies on deployers’ ability to define a bounded, non-harmful menu of modes; feasibility and legitimacy in practice are unknown.
- **Label mapping gap:** No specification of how abstract modes (e.g., "urgency-first") translate into LLM prompts or action-selection rules; risk of mode leakage or inconsistency.
- **Contest pathway viability:** Assumes users perceive outcome-specific escalation as meaningful and that staff/human escalators are available and responsive; potential for tokenism or access inequality.

## Next Checks
1. **Legibility stress-test:** In a controlled vignette study (n≥50), measure comprehension and fairness perception when the robot discloses the active mode at deferral using varied phrasing (jargon vs. plain language). Primary check: Can users accurately identify the governing principle?
2. **LLM adherence audit:** Run an automated test suite simulating 100 contention scenarios per mode; log LLM outputs and score alignment with the declared mode using a rubric. Flag deviations >20%.
3. **Contest pathway pilot:** Deploy a prototype with 3 user groups (e.g., station staff, visitors, elderly) in a low-stakes setting; track contest initiation rates, completion rates, and post-contest satisfaction surveys. Check for statistically significant disparities in access or perceived efficacy.
