# NerVE: Nonlinear Eigenspectrum Dynamics in LLM Feed-Forward Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.06922
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06922
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Introduces a unified eigenspectral framework for LLM FFNs, showing that nonlinearities reinject variance into underutilized directions and optimizers modulate this effect, yielding stable spectral signatures correlated with generalization.

## Executive Summary
NerVE presents a novel framework for analyzing the high-dimensional dynamics within the feed-forward networks (FFNs) of large language models. By computing four scale-invariant eigenspectral metrics—spectral entropy, participation ratio, eigenvalue early enrichment, and Jensen-Shannon divergence—from activation covariances, the approach provides a window into how these networks utilize their latent space during training. The method is designed for low memory overhead and can be applied online across various model architectures, activations, normalizations, positional encodings, and optimizers.

The study reveals that FFN nonlinearities play an active role in redistributing variance across latent dimensions, effectively flattening the eigenspectrum and reactivating underutilized directions. Moreover, the choice of optimizer significantly modulates this variance reinjection process, with optimizers like Muon preserving well-conditioned pre-activation spectra while AdamW induces collapse requiring aggressive repair. These spectral signatures correlate strongly with generalization performance and are stable across model families, offering a principled tool for architecture and optimizer selection beyond trial-and-error.

## Method Summary
NerVE operates by attaching forward and pre-forward hooks to the up-projection (pre-activation) and down-projection input (post-activation) of each FFN layer during training. At predefined intervals, it collects activation tensors, flattens the batch and sequence dimensions into a token matrix of shape [N, D], and computes the unbiased covariance matrix. An eigendecomposition yields the eigenvalues in descending order. From these, the four scale-invariant metrics are derived: spectral entropy (SE) measures dispersion, participation ratio (PR) estimates effective dimensionality, eigenvalue early enrichment (EEE) captures top-heaviness, and Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence quantifies the distributional shift between pre- and post-activation spectra. The pipeline processes layers sequentially, discarding intermediate data to keep memory overhead low (e.g., ~1.3% for D=3072 at 1000-step intervals). This enables online monitoring across diverse model families (GPT-2, LLaMA, MLP-Mixer) and under variations of activations (GELU, ReLU, SwiGLU), normalizations (PreLN, PostLN, MixLN), positional encodings, and optimizers (AdamW, Muon, Dion).

## Key Results
- Introduced a unified eigenspectral framework for LLM FFNs, enabling online tracking of spectral entropy, participation ratio, eigenvalue early enrichment, and Jensen-Shannon divergence with low memory overhead.
- Discovered that FFN nonlinearities reinject variance into underutilized latent directions, flattening the eigenspectrum; optimizers modulate the extent of this variance reinjection.
- Validated across GPT-2, LLaMA, and MLP-Mixer, showing stable spectral signatures that correlate with generalization and respond predictably to activation, normalization, positional encoding, and optimizer choices.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: FFN nonlinearities actively reinject variance into underutilized latent directions, flattening the eigenspectrum.
- **Mechanism**: The pre-activation eigenspectrum is typically top-heavy (high eigenvalue early enrichment, EEE), indicating variance concentrated in few directions. After the nonlinearity, spectral entropy (SE) and participation ratio (PR) increase while EEE decreases. This indicates that the nonlinearity redistributes variance more evenly across dimensions, reactivating previously inactive subspace directions.
- **Core assumption**: The pre-activation covariance matrix captures a collapsed or anisotropic representation that the nonlinearity can reshape.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Our key insight is that FFN nonlinearities reinject variance across eigenmodes, fundamentally governing latent dimension utilization."
  - [section 3.1] Figure 1 and accompanying text show consistent SE↑, PR↑, EEE↓, and nonzero JS across training for GPT-2, demonstrating variance reinjection and flattening.
  - [corpus] The related work "Spectral Scaling Laws in Language Models" (2510.00537) independently investigates FFN latent space utilization, providing external context that spectral metrics track meaningful capacity, though it does not directly test variance reinjection.
- **Break condition**: If the activation function is linear or if the pre-activation spectrum is already perfectly uniform (SE near max, EEE near 0), the nonlinearity would have no effect; JS divergence would be near zero.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Optimizer geometry modulates the extent to which FFN nonlinearities must repair versus refine the eigenspectrum.
- **Mechanism**: Optimizers shape the weight updates, which in turn influence the pre-activation covariance. Optimizers like Muon maintain well-conditioned, high-dimensional pre-spectra (high PR pre), so the nonlinearity performs only minor refinement (low PR gain, low JS). Optimizers like AdamW induce pre-activation collapse (low PR pre), forcing the nonlinearity into aggressive repair mode (high PR gain, high JS) without necessarily achieving better post-activation PR.
- **Core assumption**: The optimizer's update rule establishes an inductive bias that determines the spectral state of FFN pre-activations.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "optimizer geometry strongly modulates the extent of this variance reinjection."
  - [section 3.7] Figure 8 comparing AdamW, Muon, and Dion shows AdamW has largest PR gains and highest JS but lowest PR post; Muon shows smallest gains and lowest JS yet highest PR post. Figure 9 confirms Muon maintains highest PR pre across layers.
  - [corpus] The corpus includes papers on optimizer dynamics (e.g., "Why Transformers Need Adam" 2404.03865) that link optimizer choice to representational properties, supporting the premise that optimizers leave distinct spectral signatures, though not specifically through FFNs.
- **Break condition**: If the optimizer had no effect on the spectral distribution of pre-activations (e.g., using a trivial optimizer or if training converges to a fixed point independent of optimizer), then all optimizers would yield identical pre/post spectral dynamics.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: LayerNorm placement governs how FFN width is converted into usable latent dimensions; PreLN sustains utilization across depth while PostLN shows diminishing returns.
- **Mechanism**: LayerNorm re-centers and re-scales activations before (PreLN) or after (PostLN) the FFN, altering the variance flow into the FFN. PreLN stabilizes the input distribution to the FFN, allowing added width to expand effective dimensionality (PR post scales with D). PostLN applies normalization after the FFN, so the FFN itself operates on less-normalized inputs, leading to spectral concentration (EEE↑) and poor width utilization.
- **Core assumption**: The location of normalization determines the statistics of activations entering the FFN, which affects how the nonlinearity can reshape the spectrum.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.4] Figure 6 and Table 7 show normalized PR post (PR/D) is highest and stable for PreLN across width sweep; PostLN PR/D decreases with D, indicating wasted capacity. MixLN is intermediate.
  - [corpus] "Spectral Scaling Laws in Language Models" (2510.00537) also examines FFN width utilization and finds effective dimensionality scales with width, aligning with PreLN behavior; however, it does not compare norm placements, so the correlation is indirect.
- **Break condition**: If normalization were removed entirely (norm-free models), the pattern changes drastically: Figure 4 shows norm-free ReLU compensates aggressively, while norm-free GELU exhibits spectral inertia, so the placement effect is not simply about presence/absence of norm but its positioning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Eigendecomposition and covariance estimation**:  
  Why needed here: NerVE relies on computing the covariance matrix of FFN activations and extracting eigenvalues to quantify variance distribution. Without understanding covariance (as a measure of second-order relationships) and eigendecomposition (to obtain principal directions and eigenvalues), the spectral metrics cannot be defined.  
  Quick check: "Given an activation matrix X ∈ ℝ^{N×D}, how would you compute the covariance matrix and what does each eigenvalue represent in terms of variance direction?"

- **Scale-invariant spectral metrics (Spectral Entropy, Participation Ratio, Eigenvalue Early Enrichment, Jensen-Shannon Divergence)**:  
  Why needed here: These four metrics are the core outputs of NerVE. They transform raw eigenvalues into interpretable, bounded measures of dispersion, effective dimensionality, top-heaviness, and distributional shift. Understanding their formulas and tradeoffs (e.g., PR vs. SE sensitivity, JS symmetry) is essential to interpret results.  
  Quick check: "What is the key difference between Spectral Entropy and Participation Ratio? Which one is more sensitive to small eigenvalues?"

- **Transformer feed-forward network (FFN) architecture and activation functions**:  
  Why needed here: NerVE specifically targets FFNs within LLMs. Knowing the typical FFN structure (W_up, nonlinearity σ, W_down) and common activations (GELU, ReLU, SwiGLU) is necessary to understand what is being measured and how design choices might affect the eigenspectrum.  
  Quick check: "In a transformer FFN, at what point do we collect 'pre-activation' and 'post-activation' features for NerVE? How might a gated activation like SwiGLU change the measurement?"

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Activation collector → Covariance engine → Eigen decomposer → Metric calculator → Memory manager

### Critical Path
During training, every T steps (e.g., 1000), for each transformer layer:  
(a) Forward pass triggers hooks to capture pre/post activation tensors of shape [B, S, D].  
(b) Flatten to [N, D] with N = B×S, compute mean μ and unbiased covariance Σ.  
(c) Perform eigendecomposition to obtain λ_pre and λ_post.  
(d) Compute metrics; log and aggregate results.  
(e) Clear activations and free memory before processing the next layer.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Full-batch vs. token sub-sampling**: Full-batch yields exact covariance but O(D²) memory/time; sub-sampling (e.g., 10% tokens) reduces cost but introduces noise, especially in post-activation metrics (Appendix G).
- **Low-rank approximation (RandSVD/Lanczos)**: Saves computation but truncates tail eigenvalues, severely biasing PR and JS; not recommended for correlation analyses.
- **Logging frequency**: Higher frequency provides finer dynamics but more overhead; Table 10 shows full-batch adds ~1.3% overhead at every 1000 steps for D=3072, so logging every 200 steps may be unnecessary.
- **Granularity**: By default aggregates all tokens; if position-specific insights are needed, stratify tokens by position before covariance (Appendix J).

### Failure Signatures
- **Metrics flat across training**: Could indicate measurement bug (e.g., hooks capturing wrong tensors) or that the model’s FFNs are not learning (e.g., collapsed to linear).
- **SE & PR diverge wildly from expected trends**: Check covariance calculation (centering? divisor N-1?) and eigenvalue sorting.
- **JS near zero for all layers while loss decreases**: Nonlinearity may be inactive (e.g., dead ReLUs) or hooks placed incorrectly (capturing after normalization instead of immediately after σ).
- **Cross-configuration correlations vanish**: Likely due to sampling or low-rank truncation; revert to full-batch.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline replication**: Train a standard GPT-2 (PreLN, GELU) on a small dataset (e.g., CodeParrot). Verify that post-activation SE/PR increase and EEE decrease over time (Figure 1). Compute Pearson r between metrics and validation loss; expect |r|≥0.97 for pre-metrics (Table 3).
2. **Activation ablation**: Compare GELU vs ReLU in same architecture. Expect similar qualitative trends (SE↑, PR↑, EEE↓) but ReLU stabilizes faster, GELU pushes PR higher (Section 3.1).
3. **Optimizer sensitivity**: Train identical GPT-2 with AdamW, Muon, Dion. Expect Muon to show smallest PR gains and JS but highest PR post; AdamW shows largest gains and JS but lowest PR post (Figure 8). If this pattern fails, check hook placement and logging consistency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do eigenspectral dynamics during training directly influence downstream task performance beyond language modeling perplexity? The paper states in Section 5 (Limitations): "While NERVE eigen-metrics analyze how FFNs organize variance in LLMs, they do not directly predict downstream task quality." The current evidence is limited to next-token prediction metrics; it remains unknown whether these spectral signatures predict performance on tasks like question answering, reasoning, or few-shot learning.
2. What causes the optimizer-dependent "repair vs refinement" regimes in FFN spectral dynamics? The paper shows empirically that AdamW induces large pre-activation collapse requiring aggressive nonlinear repair (high JS, high PR gains), while Muon maintains well-conditioned pre-activation spectra requiring only modest refinement. However, the mechanistic reason why Muon's geometry preserves pre-activation spectra is not theoretically derived.
3. Do position-dependent FFN spectral effects generalize beyond GPT-2 to other architectures, and what architectural biases cause them? Appendix J finds strong position dependence in GPT-2 FFNs (late tokens use higher effective dimensionality) but not in MLP-Mixer, and speculates that LayerNorm coupling may be responsible. It is unclear whether position dependence is a generic property of Transformer FFNs with LayerNorm, whether it generalizes to other modalities, and what precise architectural features drive it; the authors provide speculation but no controlled ablation.

## Limitations
- Covariance estimation depends on batch composition and may not reflect the full data distribution; sub-sampling can bias metrics.
- Analysis is restricted to FFNs; interactions with attention modules remain unstudied.
- All causal claims are correlational; the paper does not include direct manipulation of spectra.

## Confidence
- Nonlinearity-driven variance reinjection: **High**
- Optimizer modulation of pre-spectrum: **High**
- LayerNorm placement effects: **Medium**
- Generalization correlation: **Medium**
