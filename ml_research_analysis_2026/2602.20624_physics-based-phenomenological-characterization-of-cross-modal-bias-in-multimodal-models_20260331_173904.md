# Physics-based phenomenological characterization of cross-modal bias in multimodal models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20624
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20624
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** Cross-modal bias in multimodal LLMs is determined by the balance of self- and cross-attention levels in transformer dynamics, not merely by feature strength; balanced attention equalizes modality contributions and reduces error, while imbalance leads to modality dominance and increased prediction error.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates cross-modal bias in multimodal large language models (MLLMs), where combining modalities (e.g., face and voice) unexpectedly reinforces rather than mitigates dominance of a single modality. The authors propose a physics-based multi-oscillator surrogate model to capture transformer dynamics and conduct perturbation-based emotion classification on Qwen2.5-Omni and Gemma 3n using the CREMA-D dataset. Their analysis reveals structured error-attractor hierarchies when emotion labels are systematically removed, indicating that models collapse predictably into specific wrong categories. The surrogate model shows that low self- and cross-attention parameters lead to one modality dominating (high normalized mean squared error), while balanced attention equalizes contributions (dynamical SHAP difference near zero) and minimizes error. This suggests bias arises from dynamic interactions within the transformer, not static representations.

The work introduces a phenomenological explainable AI approach, modeling transformer layers as coupled phase oscillators. This enables systematic exploration of attention parameter space and its effect on multimodal integration. Open questions remain about generality across tasks and architectures and whether insights can guide interventions to mitigate bias.

## Method Summary
The empirical analysis evaluates two MLLMs on the CREMA-D dataset for zero-shot emotion classification. Models are prompted with modality combinations (face+voice, face-only, voice-only) and with systematic removal of subsets of emotion labels to force constrained choices. By tracking predictions, directed graphs and Sankey diagrams visualize error-attractor hierarchies, exposing modality-locked error patterns. The theoretical component constructs a multi-oscillator surrogate model: 50 oscillators per modality arranged in a Watts–Strogatz network, driven by a chaotic Lorenz system. Self-attention and cross-attention strengths are controlled by parameters β_self and β_cross. Oscillator phases evolve via coupled differential equations; a linear readout trained on the first 30 time units predicts the Lorenz z-coordinate, with performance measured by normalized mean squared error (NMSE) on a test period. Dynamical SHAP values quantify each modality’s contribution, with the difference ϕ(Y)−ϕ(X) indicating bias.

## Key Results
- Cross-modal bias emerges from the dynamics of self- and cross-attention; low values of both cause single-modality dominance and high prediction error.
- Systematic removal of emotion labels reveals structured error-attractor hierarchies where errors consistently collapse into specific categories (e.g., Neutral), and these hierarchies are modality-locked.
- Balanced attention (moderate β_self and β_cross) equalizes modality contributions (dynamical SHAP difference ≈ 0) and minimizes error, as shown in the oscillator surrogate’s phase diagram.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Attention Balance Determines Modality Dominance
- **Claim:** The relative levels of self-attention (β_self) and cross-attention (β_cross) within transformer dynamics determine whether one modality dominates inference, not the statistical strength of any modality’s features.
- **Mechanism:** The multi-oscillator surrogate maps transformer layers to coupled oscillators. Low β_self and β_cross cause oscillators representing one modality to fail to synchronize with the other, leading to dominance (high NMSE). As both increase, synchronization improves, equalizing contributions (|ϕ(Y)−ϕ(X)| ≈ 0) and reducing error.
- **Evidence:** Empirical phase diagrams from the Lorenz prediction experiment show the NMSE landscape and SHAP difference across (β_self, β_cross), confirming the relationship.

### Mechanism 2: Error-Attractor Hierarchies Under Perturbation
- **Claim:** Cross-modal bias manifests as a hidden hierarchy of “error attractors” in the output distribution, revealed only when prompts constrain the choice set.
- **Mechanism:** Under full-label settings, overall accuracy may mask bias. Systematically removing emotion labels forces a forced-choice scenario, exposing preferential collapse into specific categories (e.g., Neutral) within modality-correlated basins.
- **Evidence:** Directed graphs and Sankey diagrams from CREMA-D experiments consistently show such hierarchies for both Qwen2.5-Omni and Gemma 3n.

### Mechanism 3: Dynamical, Not Representational, Bias
- **Claim:** Traditional representation-level analyses cannot capture cross-modal bias because it emerges from the dynamic interactions of attention operations over time, not static feature vectors.
- **Mechanism:** The phenomenological approach focuses on the temporal evolution of token states through transformer layers. Imbalance in β_self vs. β_cross leads to one modality’s state vector dominating the final prediction, a process invisible to static similarity metrics.
- **Evidence:** The surrogate model’s success in characterizing bias via dynamics, contrasted with the expectation that embedding probes would miss such effects.

## Foundational Learning
### Phenomenological Explainable AI (XAI)
**Why needed:** The paper adopts a phenomenological stance to explain model behavior by analyzing the physical entities the machine experiences (attention weights, state trajectories) rather than attributing semantic meaning to internal representations. This is prerequisite for understanding the surrogate model's rationale.
**Quick check:** What is the key difference between explaining a model's behavior by probing its activations versus by simulating its dynamical system?

### Multi-Oscillator Dynamical Systems as Surrogate Models
**Why needed:** The analytical framework replaces black-box transformer layers with interpretable coupled phase oscillators. Understanding phase oscillators, coupling terms, and driven dynamics is essential to grasp the Lorenz experiment and SHAP definition.
**Quick check:** How do the parameters β_self and β_cross in the oscillator model map to transformer architecture components?

### Dynamical SHAP
**Why needed:** Dynamical SHAP is the primary metric for quantifying each modality's contribution to the prediction process within the dynamical system, not just the final output.
**Quick check:** Why is the SHAP difference ϕ(Y)−ϕ(X) a more informative measure of modality bias than comparing NMSE_X and NMSE_Y directly?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Input Encoders (modality-specific, e.g., Whisper for audio, SigLIP for vision) → Projection Layers (linear to shared dimension) → Fusion Transformer Stack (alternating self-attention, cross-attention, FFN, layer normalization) → Output Head (task-specific)

**Critical Path:** The bias mechanism resides in the Fusion Transformer Stack, where the balance of β_self (self-attention strength) and β_cross (cross-attention strength) across layers determines whether modality-specific state vectors synchronize (balanced) or one dominates (imbalanced). The path from input projections to final [CLS] state via these layers is where the distortion occurs.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- Higher β values increase coupling but can amplify noise from weaker modalities.
- Token count imbalance: a modality with more tokens can implicitly dominate attention norms.
- Layer depth: early vs. late layers may exhibit different (β_self, β_cross) regimes; the model assumes uniformity.

**Failure Signatures:**
1. Modality-locked error: Face+Voice errors mirror Face-only errors far more than Voice-only errors.
2. Structured attractor collapse: Under label perturbation, predictions consistently collapse to 1–2 specific wrong categories (e.g., Neutral).
3. High NMSE with low self-attention in the surrogate.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. Replicate CREMA-D perturbation analysis on Qwen2.5-Omni/Gemma 3n: run zero-shot emotion classification under full-label and perturbed prompts, reconstruct error-attractor graphs and Sankey diagrams.
2. Implement oscillator surrogate: generate Watts–Strogatz networks, integrate ODEs driven by Lorenz trajectories, sweep β_self and β_cross, train linear readout, compute NMSE and dynamical SHAP, reproduce phase diagram.
3. Ablate attention pathways: intervene by zeroing or scaling cross-attention outputs at specific layers, measure error rate and output distribution; expect increased modality dominance when cross-attention suppressed.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What specific mechanistic pathways within transformer dynamics cause multimodal inputs to reinforce rather than mitigate modality dominance?
- Is the observed reinforcement of modality dominance an inevitable property of current multimodal pretraining and fusion schemes, or can it be fundamentally altered by architectural interventions?
- Do the error-attractor hierarchies and dynamical SHAP patterns generalize to other multimodal tasks and modalities with asymmetric token distributions?
- Can the physics-based multi-oscillator surrogate model be operationalized as a practical bias-detection tool for MLLM training, for instance by estimating β_self and β_cross from model weights to predict modality dominance before deployment?

## Limitations
- Exact phrasing of label perturbation prompts is unspecified, potentially affecting constraint compliance.
- Oscillator simulation parameters (integrator, step size, ridge α) are not detailed, hindering exact replication.
- Mapping between transformer attention weights and surrogate β parameters lacks direct empirical grounding.

## Confidence
- Modality dominance determined by self/cross-attention levels: Medium
- Structured error-attractor hierarchies under perturbation: High
- Bias is dynamical, not representational: Medium
