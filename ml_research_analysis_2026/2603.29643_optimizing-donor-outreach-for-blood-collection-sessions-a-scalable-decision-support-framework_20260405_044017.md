# Optimizing Donor Outreach for Blood Collection Sessions: A Scalable Decision Support Framework

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.29643
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29643
- **Reference count:** 38
- **Primary result:** A greedy heuristic achieves near‑optimal demand fulfillment (86.1% vs. 90.0%) with 115× faster runtime and 188× less memory than exact optimization, and the prospective pipeline can close supply‑demand gaps by mobilizing inactive donors.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the operational challenge of assigning blood donors to collection sessions across a multi‑site network while balancing medical eligibility, site capacity, blood‑type demand targets, geographic convenience, and donor fatigue. The authors propose a decision support framework that includes an exact binary integer linear program (BILP) and a computationally efficient greedy heuristic. Validated on real‑world data from Lisbon, the greedy heuristic attains 86.1% demand fulfillment compared to 90.0% for the BILP, but with dramatic reductions in runtime (115× faster) and memory usage (188× less). The framework also integrates a prospective pipeline that forecasts organic attendance and targets residual demand, demonstrating potential to close regional supply‑demand gaps by effectively mobilizing eligible inactive and lapsing donors.

## Method Summary
The method comprises four stages:  
1. **Data Preprocessing:** Construct a feasible donor‑session pair set based on age (18–65), suspension status, last donation ≥60 days before session, and haversine distance ≤3 km. Expected attendance probability \(p_i\) is derived from donor‑type categories.  
2. **Forecasting Module:** Estimate monthly blood‑group demand targets via trend‑corrected quantiles; predict organic attendance using a multilayer perceptron (returning donors) and Holt‑Winters (first‑time donors); allocate organic supply to sites with a gradient‑boosted regression model.  
3. **Residual Problem Construction:** Subtract organic predictions from demand (\(R_{mg} = \max(0, D_{mg} - \hat{O}_{mg})\)) and from session capacities (\(\tilde{C}_j\)); exclude donors predicted to attend organically from the invitation pool.  
4. **Optimization Engine:** Solve the residual assignment problem either exactly with a BILP (minimizing weighted travel distance, invitation burden for non‑high‑frequency donors, and adverse‑reaction exposure) or approximately with a greedy heuristic that processes scarce demand classes first and selects donor‑session pairs lexicographically.

## Key Results
- Greedy heuristic achieves 86.1% demand fulfillment vs. 90.0% for exact BILP, with 115× faster runtime and 188× less peak memory.
- Trade‑offs of the greedy approach include slightly larger donor travel distances, higher exposure of donors with prior adverse reactions, and a greater invitation burden for non‑high‑frequency donors.
- Prospective pipeline (integrating organic forecasting) shows potential to close regional supply‑demand gaps by effectively mobilizing eligible inactive and lapsing donors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Constrained Optimization via BILP**  
The BILP formulation encodes donor‑session invitations as binary variables \(x_{ij}\). Linear constraints enforce eligibility (age, suspension), inter‑donation intervals, annual donation limits, session capacity, monthly blood‑group demand, and geographic feasibility (≤3 km). The objective minimizes a weighted sum of travel distance, invitation burden for non‑high‑frequency donors, and penalties for inviting donors with prior severe adverse reactions. This yields a globally optimal assignment under the model. Evidence: The BILP achieves 90.0% demand fulfillment on Lisbon data, confirming that the constraint set is both feasible and effective. Break condition: The solver becomes intractable for very large instances (e.g., multi‑year donor pools) due to exponential runtime and memory exhaustion (observed >117 GB).

**Mechanism 2: Greedy Heuristic for Scalable Allocation**  
The greedy algorithm ranks demand classes (month × blood group) by scarcity \(\sigma_{mg} = |F_{mg}| / R_{mg}\) (feasible pairs divided by residual demand). Within each class, donor‑session pairs are sorted lexicographically: donors without prior severe adverse reactions precede those with; within those, pairs with smaller travel distance precede larger. Assignments are made only if they respect capacity, inter‑donation gaps, annual limits, and invitation caps, updating residual state after each acceptance. Evidence: Greedy achieves 86.1% fulfillment (3.9 pp gap) with 115× speedup and 188× memory reduction versus BILP. Trade‑offs: larger average travel distances, higher adverse‑reaction exposure, and more invitations per non‑high‑frequency donor. Break condition: Myopic local decisions can lead to suboptimal global assignments, particularly when demand is highly imbalanced across blood groups or geographic clusters.

**Mechanism 3: Prospective Residual‑Demand Pipeline**  
The pipeline first sets conservative demand targets \(D_{mg}\) using trend‑corrected quantiles of historical consumption. It then subtracts predicted organic supply \(\hat{O}_{mg}\)—from an MLP for returning donors and Holt‑Winters for first‑timers—to obtain residual demand \(R_{mg}\). Site‑level organic predictions are derived via a top‑down reconciliation using a gradient‑boosted regression model. Finally, the optimizer solves the residual problem using the chosen solver on a donor pool excluding those predicted to donate organically, and with adjusted session capacities \(\tilde{C}_j\). Evidence: Organic forecasts show reasonable accuracy (MLP ROC‑AUC 0.822, monthly MAE 720; Holt‑Winters MAE 133) and the pipeline demonstrates potential to close gaps. Break condition: Systemic forecast failures (e.g., during a pandemic) can bias \(\hat{O}_{mg}\), causing over‑invitation (wasted resources, donor fatigue) or under‑invitation (unfilled demand).

## Foundational Learning
- **Binary Integer Linear Programming (BILP)**  
  *Why needed:* The assignment problem requires binary decisions (invite or not), linear constraints (capacity, eligibility, demand), and a linear objective. BILP provides an exact framework to find optimal solutions and understand theoretical limits.  
  *Quick check:* Given binary variables \(x_{ij}\) for donor \(i\) invited to session \(j\), how would you formulate a linear constraint ensuring that if donor \(i\) is invited to sessions \(j_1\) and \(j_2\), then the start date of \(j_2\) is at least 60 days after the end date of \(j_1\)?

- **Greedy Heuristic Design for Combinatorial Allocation**  
  *Why needed:* Exact BILP is too slow for operational use on large‑scale data; a greedy heuristic offers scalable, fast, high‑quality approximate solutions.  
  *Quick check:* In the greedy algorithm, why is it important to sort demand classes by scarcity (\(|F_{mg}|/R_{mg}\)) *before* processing them lexicographically within each class? What would happen if you processed classes in arbitrary order?

- **Hierarchical Time Series Forecasting for Demand and Supply**  
  *Why needed:* Prospective planning requires coherent monthly forecasts of blood demand (by group) and organic donor supply (by site), across multiple hierarchical levels. Top‑down reconciliation ensures consistency.  
  *Quick check:* If you have a national‑level forecast for total red cell demand next month and historical site‑level shares of that total, how would you produce site‑level forecasts that guarantee the sum equals the national total? (This is “top‑down reconciliation”.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Preprocessing → Forecasting Module → Residual Problem Construction → Optimization Engine (`BILP` or `greedy`)

**Critical path:** Load state → Forecast demand (\(D_{mg}\)) → Forecast organic supply (per donor & site) → Compute residuals (\(R_{mg}\), \(\tilde{C}_j\)) → Build feasible set \(A\) → Run chosen solver → Output invitations \(P \subseteq D \times S\)

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Exact vs. Approximate optimization:* BILP guarantees global optimality but is slow/memory‑heavy; greedy is fast but yields ~4% lower fulfillment and worse secondary metrics.  
- *Forecast accuracy vs. lead time:* More accurate forecasts (MLP, Holt‑Winters) improve residual targeting but require longer training; simple baselines (prior‑year) are faster but error‑prone for rare blood types.  
- *Geographic radius (R):* Smaller \(R\) enforces convenience and reduces feasible pair set size but may cause infeasibility; a “radius sweep” trades convenience for feasibility.

**Failure signatures**  
- *Solver infeasibility:* Residual demand exceeds feasible assignments (e.g., \(R_{mg} > \sum p_i\) within \(R\)). Mitigation: increase \(R\), soften demand, or lower \(R_{mg}\).  
- *Poor demand fulfillment:* Actual collections fall far short of \(R_{mg}\). Cause: over‑optimistic \(p_i\) or poor organic forecast. Mitigation: recalibrate \(p_i\), improve forecasting.  
- *Excessive memory/time:* BILP solver exhausts resources (e.g., >117 GB). Mitigation: switch to greedy, tighten \(R\), shorten planning window.  
- *High invitation burden:* Average invitations per non‑high‑frequency donor spikes. Cause: greedy local decisions or low weight \(W_{inv}\). Mitigation: increase \(W_{inv}\) or add caps.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Unit test feasibility check:** For a tiny instance (2 donors, 2 sessions), manually verify feasibility conditions (age, suspension, distance ≤ R, inter‑donation gap, annual limit). Run greedy step‑by‑step to ensure all constraints are respected.  
2. **Solver comparison on controlled instance:** Build a small solvable instance (e.g., 50 donors, 10 sessions). Solve optimally with Gurobi and with the greedy heuristic. Compare objective value gap, runtime, and memory usage.  
3. **Forecast validation on hold‑out year:** Train organic forecasts (MLP for returning, Holt‑Winters for first‑timers) on years <2020. Predict 2020 monthly organic attendance. Compare aggregated predictions with actual 2020 attendance (MAE, correlation) to assess residual pipeline foundation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Geolocation Precision**  
   *Question:* How can the framework replace approximate donor geolocation (postal code inference) with precise, donor‑provided location data to improve the accuracy and practical feasibility of geographic convenience constraints?  
   *Basis:* The paper explicitly states that current proxy‑based distances introduce noise and may mark invitations as “convenient” when they are not.  
   *Why unresolved:* Without ground‑truth coordinates, the model’s convenience objective may not reflect actual donor travel burden.  
   *Resolution evidence:* A study collecting actual donor and session coordinates, then re‑evaluating optimized plans with true distances to quantify discrepancy.

2. **Calibrated Show‑up Probabilities**  
   *Question:* What are the empirically calibrated show‑up probabilities for invited donors across segments (inactive vs. active, age groups) and invitation types (call vs. SMS), and how does incorporating these probabilities affect demand fulfillment and capacity planning?  
   *Basis:* The framework uses generic probabilities from donor‑type categories, not from a dedicated outreach study.  
   *Why unresolved:* The entire planning model—residual demand calculation and session capacity constraints—depends on \(p_i\). Uncalibrated values risk over‑ or under‑invitation.  
   *Resolution evidence:* A prospective controlled study tracking donor responses to invitations to build granular probability models, then re‑running the pipeline with calibrated \(p_i\).

3. **Rare Blood Group Forecasting**  
   *Question:* For rare blood groups (e.g., AB-, B-), what alternative forecasting strategies or constraint relaxation mechanisms (e.g., flexible substitution policies, inter‑regional sharing triggers) can be integrated to prevent chronic under‑fulfillment without excessively inflating overall invitation volume?  
   *Basis:* Quantile‑based demand targets show uneven performance; carry‑forward error is materially higher for rare groups (e.g., 69.6% relative MAE for AB‑).  
   *Why unresolved:* High forecast volatility leads to poorly set residual demand targets, causing either shortages or infeasible plans. The current one‑size‑fits‑all quantile approach does not address this.  
   *Resolution evidence:* Compare strategies such as blood‑group‑specific quantiles, substitution allowances, and multi‑stage planning via simulations; measure impact on fulfillment and total invitations.

4. **Weight Parameter Sensitivity**  
   *Question:* How do the specific weight parameters in the BILP objective (\(W_{dist}=1.0\), \(W_{inv}=1.0\), \(W_{adv}=10.0\)) and the greedy heuristic’s lexicographic ordering affect the trade‑off frontier between demand fulfillment, donor travel convenience, adverse‑reaction exposure, and invitation burden? Are the chosen defaults optimal?  
   *Basis:* The authors set these values as reasonable but admit they are not optimized; no sensitivity analysis was performed.  
   *Why unresolved:* Different blood services may have different risk tolerances and donor‑relationship priorities; unexplored parameter space means reported trade‑offs may not be best achievable.  
   *Resolution evidence:* Systematic sweep over weight values for BILP and variations of the greedy priority tuple; map Pareto frontiers for key metrics.

5. **Greedy Heuristic Enhancement**  
   *Question:* Can the greedy heuristic’s performance be improved to narrow the 3.9 pp fulfillment gap with the exact BILP (86.1% vs 90.0%) while maintaining its computational advantages (e.g., 115× speedup)? Possible approaches include limited lookahead, partial re‑optimization of local neighborhoods, or problem‑specific decomposition.  
   *Basis:* The paper notes the gap reflects “local versus global optimization” but does not explore enhancements to the greedy method.  
   *Why unresolved:* The greedy algorithm makes irrevocable myopic decisions; a modest increase in computational effort might yield significant quality gains, making the heuristic even more practical.  
   *Resolution evidence:* Design and test augmented variants (k‑swap, beam‑search, local re‑optimization) on the full Lisbon instance; compare computational profile and fulfillment rate to base greedy and BILP.

## Limitations
- **External validity:** Results are based on a single region (Lisbon, 2020); generalizability to other geographies, time periods, or larger scales is unknown.
- **Model input fidelity:** Dependence on approximate donor locations (postal code proxies) and uncalibrated attendance probabilities may bias outcomes; systemic shifts (e.g., post‑pandemic donor behavior) could degrade forecast and assignment accuracy.
- **Implementation gaps:** Exact geographic radius policy (fixed vs. adaptive), full hyperparameters for MLP/Holt‑Winters models, and soft‑demand mode details are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- **High:** Internal validity of greedy vs. BILP trade‑offs (runtime, memory, fulfillment gap) on Lisbon 2020 data.
- **Medium:** Prospective pipeline’s ability to close supply‑demand gaps; relies on forecast accuracy (MLP ROC‑AUC 0.822) but notes COVID‑19 disruption in 2020.
- **Low:** Generalizability to other regions or large‑scale national deployment; no out‑of‑sample validation beyond Lisbon.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑region replication:** Run the full pipeline on a distinct blood service dataset (e.g., another country) and compare fulfillment and computational metrics to Lisbon results.
2. **Parameter sensitivity analysis:** Systematically vary geographic radius \(R\) and demand target quantiles on hold‑out Lisbon data; quantify impacts on feasibility rate, fulfillment, and average donor travel distance.
3. **Pilot deployment test:** Implement the greedy heuristic in a live, limited‑scale invitation campaign (1–2 months, subset of sites); measure actual vs. predicted demand fulfillment, donor response rates, and operational burden.
