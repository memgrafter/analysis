# CyclicJudge: Mitigating Judge Bias Efficiently in LLM-based Evaluation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01865
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01865
- **Reference count:** 21
- **Primary result:** CyclicJudge minimizes benchmark score variance under fixed budget, outperforming single-judge and all-judges baselines across all tested budgets.

## Executive Summary
LLM-as-judge evaluations suffer from systematic judge bias that distorts model rankings and cannot be alleviated by collecting more scenarios or generations. This paper introduces a variance decomposition separating scenario difficulty, generation stochasticity, judge bias, and residual noise. It proves that round-robin judge cycling (CyclicJudge) is optimal for a fixed judge-call budget: it exactly eliminates judge bias while maximizing generation diversity at no extra cost per item. Empirical validation on MT-Bench (general) and MindEval (clinical) with five models confirms that judge bias dominates variance (>94% at default settings) and CyclicJudge reduces variance by 27–40% at small budgets compared to random single-judge assignment, precisely matching theoretical predictions.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a crossed random effects linear model to decompose the variance of benchmark scores into four independent components: scenario effects (σ²α), generation effects (σ²β), fixed judge biases (γℓ) whose sampling variance includes a finite population correction, and residual error (σ²ε). This decomposition reveals that judge bias (σ²γ) is unaffected by increasing the number of scenarios or generations and dominates the total variance. For a fixed per-scenario budget B of judge calls, they compare three allocation strategies: (A) all Ktot judges on B/Ktot generations, (B) a single judge on B generations, and (C) CyclicJudge, which uses Ktot judges cyclically across B generations (so m=B). Closed-form variance expressions show VC ≤ min(VA, VB) under the model assumptions. The claims are empirically validated on two benchmarks using five LLMs as both judges and evaluated models; variance components are estimated via crossed ANOVA, and thousands of bootstrap subsamples confirm CyclicJudge achieves the lowest variance at every budget.

## Key Results
- Judge bias dominates benchmark variance (>94% at default settings) and cannot be reduced by adding more scenarios or generations.
- CyclicJudge (round-robin cycling) yields the lowest variance across all budget levels, outperforming both single-judge and all-judges baselines.
- At small budgets (B=5), CyclicJudge reduces variance by ~27–40% relative to random single-judge assignment, aligning perfectly with theoretical predictions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Variance Decomposition
The benchmark score variance is shown to be additively separable into four independent components: scenario difficulty (σ²α), generation stochasticity (σ²β), systematic judge bias (σ²γ), and residual noise (σ²ε). This stems from a crossed random effects linear model under a balanced design, with judge biases treated as fixed constants that sum to zero. The sampling variance of the mean judge bias includes a finite population correction because judges are drawn without replacement from a finite pool. This decomposition is the foundation for the subsequent optimality analysis.

### Mechanism 2: Judge Bias Dominance
The judge bias term σ²γ depends solely on the number of judges per item (K) and is independent of the number of scenarios (n) or generations (m). Hence, increasing n or m does not reduce its contribution to the overall variance. Empirical ANOVA on MT-Bench and MindEval reveals that σ²γ exceeds all other variance components by over 94% at standard settings, confirming that judge bias is the primary source of instability in LLM-based evaluations.

### Mechanism 3: Optimality of CyclicJudge
Given a fixed per-scenario budget B, strategies must balance K (judges per generation) and m (generations per scenario). CyclicJudge uses K = Ktot (the full judge pool) on every generation but cycles judges across generations, resulting in m = B and exactly zero mean judge bias (¯γ=0). Comparing the closed-form variances for the three strategies (all-judges, random single-judge, CyclicJudge) yields VC ≤ min(VA, VB) for all B, with equality only under degenerate conditions (σ²β=0 or σ²γ=0). Empirical results on both benchmarks confirm that CyclicJudge achieves the minimum variance at every budget, as predicted.

## Foundational Learning

### Generalizability Theory (G-Theory)
- **Why needed here:** Provides the statistical framework for partitioning observed variance into facets (scenarios, generations, judges) in a crossed design. The paper's variance decomposition is a direct application of G-theory.
- **Quick check:** In a crossed design where each judge scores every scenario-generation cell, what distinguishes a "fixed" judge effect (as modeled here) from a "random" judge effect in classical G-theory?

### Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) for Crossed Random Effects
- **Why needed here:** Used to estimate the variance components (σ²α, σ²β, σ²ε) from the fully crossed data via method-of-moments estimators (e.g., MSG, MSJ, etc.). The empirical validation relies on these estimates.
- **Quick check:** Why does the between-generation mean square (MSG) estimate σ²ε + Kσ²β? How does the judge main effect factor into this calculation?

### Finite Population Correction (FPC)
- **Why needed here:** The judge bias term in the variance formula includes an FPC factor because the K judges per item are sampled without replacement from a finite pool of size Ktot. This correction is essential for accurately quantifying the variance of the sample mean bias.
- **Quick check:** If judges were sampled with replacement from an infinite pool, how would the variance formula change?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Dataset & Scenarios -> Model Under Test -> Judge Pool -> Allocation Engine -> Scoring & Aggregation -> Variance Estimation Module

### Critical Path
1. Estimate variance components (σ²α, σ²β, σ²γ, σ²ε) from pilot data or prior literature.
2. For a given per-scenario budget B and judge pool size Ktot, set m = B and K = Ktot for CyclicJudge.
3. Assign judges cyclically: for generation j (1..m), assign judge ℓ = (j mod Ktot) + 1 to that generation of every scenario.
4. Run the evaluation: generate m responses per scenario, have the assigned judges score them.
5. Compute the benchmark score (e.g., average over all collected scores) and its variance using the appropriate closed-form formula.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Balanced vs Adaptive:** The analysis assumes a balanced design (each scenario receives exactly B judge calls). Adaptive or unequal allocations could further reduce scenario variance but break the closed-form optimality proof.
- **Uniform vs Weighted Cost:** The model assumes all judge calls cost the same. If call costs differ (e.g., GPT-4 vs. Llama), a cost-weighted cycling scheme is required to maintain optimality per dollar.
- **Max Scenarios vs Max Generations:** With fixed total budget B, variance is minimized by maximizing m (generations) rather than n (scenarios) because σ²γ is independent of n. However, if scenarios are not exchangeable (some are more informative), this trade-off may shift.

### Failure Signatures
- **High residual variance (σ²ε):** Indicates noisy model outputs or inconsistent judge scoring; CyclicJudge cannot fix this—need more generations or better prompts.
- **Low judge variance (σ²γ ≈ 0):** Judge bias is negligible; simpler single-judge evaluation may be sufficient.
- **Negative σ²β estimate:** From ANOVA, suggests generation variance is negligible relative to residual; set σ²β = 0. CyclicJudge still optimal but gains over all-judges shrink.
- **Large FPC factor ((Ktot−K)/(Ktot−1) ≈ 1):** Judge pool is small; bias cancellation with few judges is limited. Consider increasing Ktot.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate variance decomposition:** On a small benchmark (e.g., 10 scenarios, 2 models, 3 judges), compute full crossed ANOVA and verify that σ²γ dominates the variance of the benchmark score at K=1, m=1.
2. **Validate CyclicJudge optimality:** For a fixed B (e.g., B=10), simulate thousands of random allocations under the three strategies. Confirm that empirical variance of CyclicJudge is consistently lower than both baselines and matches the theoretical formula.
3. **Test scalability & generalizability:** Apply CyclicJudge to a new domain (e.g., code generation evaluation) with a larger, more diverse judge pool (Ktot > 5). Measure whether judge bias still dominates and whether CyclicJudge’s variance reduction persists, even when judge call costs are non-uniform (simulate with weightings).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **GLMM-based optimality:** Can a generalized linear mixed model for ordinal scores replicate CyclicJudge's theoretical guarantees while properly handling bounded response scales? The current linear approximation may be inadequate.
2. **Scalability with larger judge pools:** Does CyclicJudge's advantage hold with more than five judges having heterogeneous biases? Theory suggests it should, but empirical validation with diverse panels is lacking.
3. **Scenario heterogeneity:** If scenarios differ in discriminative power or relevance, does the optimal allocation (maximizing generations) remain optimal? The analysis assumes exchangeable scenarios.
4. **Unbalanced/adaptive designs:** Can CyclicJudge be extended to unbalanced or sequential judge assignments (e.g., allocating extra judges to ambiguous items) while still canceling bias and minimizing variance?
5. **Cost heterogeneity:** How do varying judge costs (price, latency) affect the optimal strategy? A cost-weighted variant of CyclicJudge may be needed for real-world deployments.

## Limitations
- The theoretical model assumes a balanced crossed design with additive independent effects; violations such as judge-scenario interactions or bounded ordinal scores could invalidate the decomposition.
- Judge bias dominance (>94%) is demonstrated only on MT-Bench and MindEval using five model-as-judges; generalizability to other domains and human judges remains unproven.
- Reproduction details (exact prompts, rubrics, generation seeds, model versions) are not fully specified, introducing risk of implementation drift.
