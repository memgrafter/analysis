# Prune-then-Quantize or Quantize-then-Prune? Understanding the Impact of Compression Order in Joint Model Compression

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18426
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18426
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Applying weaker compression before stronger compression (Progressive Intensity Hypothesis) yields better model performance, with the order advantage increasing monotonically with the intensity gap between methods.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles a largely overlooked question in joint model compression: does the sequence of applying different compression techniques (e.g., pruning and quantization) affect the final model performance? The authors introduce the Progressive Intensity Hypothesis, which posits that weaker compression perturbations should be applied before stronger ones to minimize total error. Through theoretical analysis under assumptions like layer-wise independence and disjoint selectivity, they show that the performance advantage of the correct order increases monotonically with the difference in method intensity, measured by Compression Equivalent Ratio (CER). Extensive experiments on language models (LLaMA, Mistral) and vision models (ResNet, DeiT) validate the hypothesis across diverse settings, including multi-stage pipelines, mixed-precision quantization, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates compression order by sequentially applying two methods (pruning and quantization) in both possible orders on pre-trained models. Task-specific metrics are used: negative perplexity for language models (LLaMA, Mistral) on WikiText-2/C4, accuracy for vision models (ResNet, DeiT) on ImageNet, and Spearman correlation for STS-B. For each method pair and ratio, the **compression order advantage** \( A = M(f_2 \circ f_1) - M(f_1 \circ f_2) \) is computed, where \( M \) is the performance metric. Method intensity is compared via **Compression Equivalent Ratio (CER)** \( C^* \), which finds the ratio of one method that matches the performance of another at a given ratio. The core hypothesis is tested by correlating \( A \) with the difference in CER values, supported by theoretical proofs under granularity conditions (disjoint selectivity) and empirical measurements across multiple model families and compression techniques.

## Key Results
- \( A \) increases monotonically with the CER difference between pruning and quantization, confirming the Progressive Intensity Hypothesis.
- The trend holds across diverse models (LLaMA 2/3, Mistral, ResNet, DeiT) and compression methods (SparseGPT, Wanda, QuaRot, etc.).
- Disjoint selectivity (e.g., structured pruning with layer-wise quantization) yields deterministic order effects; granularity conflicts (unstructured pruning) introduce additive interference but preserve monotonicity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Compression order advantage increases monotonically with the performance gap (CER difference) between methods.
- **Mechanism:** When two compression methods are applied sequentially, the model performs better if the method with lower CER (weaker) is applied first. This is because total error depends on which model units are assigned to which method, and weaker methods are more tolerant when applied to a less-distorted model.
- **Core assumption:** Methods are "well-designed" (minimally disruptive) and interference scales monotonically with compression ratios.
- **Evidence anchors:** Hypothesis stated in abstract; formalized in Section 3.3; Figure 3 (LLaMA) shows monotonic \( A \) vs. CER difference.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Under disjoint selectivity (pruning granularity ≥ quantization granularity), order advantage depends only on units with differing assignments between orders (G1 vs G2).
- **Mechanism:** If pruning operates at coarser granularity (e.g., layer) than quantization (weight), each layer's pruned/not status determines whether quantization acts on original or pruned weights. Units with fixed assignment (G3/G4) contribute equally to both orders, canceling out.
- **Core assumption:** Layer-wise independent and additive errors; exclusive unit assignment.
- **Evidence anchors:** Definition 5 and Figure 2a; Theorem 1; Table 1 shows zero advantage for SLEB (structured pruning) in some regimes.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** When granularities conflict (pruning finer than quantization), interference adds error that scales with pruning ratio but preserves monotonic \( A \) vs. CER difference.
- **Mechanism:** Pruning before quantization alters the weight distribution for quantization, adding a bias term \( \Delta \) that depends on pruning ratio \( p \). This interference is additive to the base advantage from disjoint units.
- **Core assumption:** Quantization error behavior consistent; interference separable and monotonic in \( p \).
- **Evidence anchors:** Definition 6 and Figure 2b; Table 1 contrasts SparseGPT (unstructured) vs. SLEB; mixed-precision experiments (Section 5.4) avoid interference via independent bit-widths.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Compression Granularity
  - **Why needed:** Determines whether disjoint selectivity holds, simplifying analysis and prediction of order effects.
  - **Quick check:** If pruning removes entire feed-forward layers (coarse) and quantization acts on all remaining weights (fine), is \( t_P \geq t_Q \)? Does disjoint selectivity hold?
- **Concept:** Compression Equivalent Ratio (CER) and Performance Gap (G)
  - **Why needed:** Enables comparison of method intensity on a common scale; CER difference predicts order advantage.
  - **Quick check:** If method f1 at ratio \( C_1 \) gives metric 80% and method f2 at ratio \( 2C_1 \) gives 75%, what is \( C^*_{f1} \) relative to f2?
- **Concept:** Disjoint Selectivity
  - **Why needed:** Central theoretical condition where order advantage reduces to sum over order-dependent unit groups.
  - **Quick check:** In a transformer, if pruning is layer-wise and quantization is weight-wise, are units exclusively assigned to one method in each order?

## Architecture Onboarding
The paper's architecture is organized around a theoretical core, a hypothesis engine, and an empirical validation suite.

Theoretical core (granularity, intensity metrics, disjoint selectivity) -> Hypothesis engine (Progressive Intensity Hypothesis linking advantage to CER difference) -> Empirical validation suite (test on LLMs, vision models, multi-stage, PEFT, MPQ).

**Critical path:**
1. Define problem: Does compression order matter? (Problem 1)
2. Characterize method attributes: granularity (\( t_f \)), intensity (CER \( C^* \), gap \( G \)).
3. Formulate Progressive Intensity Hypothesis.
4. Prove under disjoint selectivity (Theorem 1) and well-designed assumptions (Theorem 2).
5. Extend to interfering cases via additive interference argument (Definition 6).
6. Empirically verify across language/vision models, multi-stage, PEFT, parameter sharing, MPQ.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Generality vs. precision: The hypothesis is broadly applicable but does not predict exact \( A \) values.
- Disjoint selectivity simplifies analysis but is restrictive; interference analysis adds complexity to cover more cases.

**Failure signatures:**
- Severe performance collapse at extreme compression ratios (e.g., 4-bit on small models).
- Full retraining after compression overrides order effects.
- Increasing compression ratio \( C_P \) leads to more order-affected layers, potentially inverting trend.
- Rotation without pruning awareness (Figure 5) introduces large errors.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Language models (LLaMA 2/3 7B/13B) with SparseGPT (pruning) and QuaRot (quantization): Validate monotonic \( A \) vs. CER difference across scales.
2. Vision models (ResNet-18, DeiT-Base) with PRACTISE/N2UQ (pruning) and SA-ViT/RepQ-ViT (quantization): Confirm hypothesis for CNNs and ViTs.
3. Mixed-precision quantization on ResNet-18: Progressive (high→low bits) vs. regressive allocation; advantage increases with total compression.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can interference across compression method combinations be systematically quantified and predicted beyond prune-quantize pairs?
2. Can explicit predictive formulas for order advantage \( A \) be derived from model and method properties in a tractable way?
3. How can optimal compression order be efficiently automated for any given set of methods and target ratios without exhaustive search?

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis relies on restrictive assumptions: well-designed methods, layer-wise independent errors, and often disjoint selectivity.
- CER estimation depends on linear interpolation between performance points; non-linearities may distort advantage estimates.
- Focus limited to prune+quantize; generalizability to other method pairs (e.g., distillation, low-rank) is unknown.
- Partial lack of hyperparameter details and restricted model access (LLaMA license) hinder full reproducibility.

## Confidence
- Progressive Intensity Hypothesis (weaker-first yields better performance): **Medium‑High**
- Monotonic increase of order advantage with CER difference: **Medium**
- Disjoint selectivity determinism: **High**
- Interference additivity (granularity mismatch): **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Broaden method diversity:** Test other compression pairs (e.g., pruning + knowledge distillation, quantization + low-rank) on architectures like BERT and ConvNeXt to verify the weaker‑first rule.
2. **Stress‑test assumptions:** Introduce poorly‑designed methods (random pruning, extreme quantization) to identify boundary conditions where monotonicity breaks.
3. **Refine CER estimation:** Collect dense performance curves for each method, fit smooth models (e.g., splines), and recompute \( A \) to assess interpolation error impact on monotonic trends.
