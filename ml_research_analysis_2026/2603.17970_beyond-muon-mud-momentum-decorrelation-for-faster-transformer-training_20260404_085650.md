# Beyond Muon: MUD (MomentUm Decorrelation) for Faster Transformer Training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17970
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17970
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** MUD replaces Muon's expensive multi-step orthogonalization with a single-pass triangular whitening operation, reducing dense linear algebra cost by ~12× and improving wall-clock time-to-perplexity by 10–50% over AdamW and Muon across LLM architectures.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces MUD (MomentUm Decorrelation), an optimizer for transformer training that accelerates the orthogonalized-momentum method Muon. Muon improves optimization by decorrelating momentum updates but relies on a costly multi-iteration Newton-Schulz approximation for matrix whitening. MUD substitutes this with a single-pass triangular (Cholesky-like) whitening surrogate, drastically cutting the dominant dense linear algebra overhead. Theoretically, MUD enjoys row-orthonormal fixed points and local quadratic convergence. Empirically, across various models, datasets, and hardware, MUD yields 1.3–2.6× higher token throughput over Muon and reaches target validation perplexity 10–50% faster than both tuned AdamW and Muon.

## Method Summary
MUD targets matrix-shaped parameters (2D tensors) in transformers. For a momentum direction matrix **M**, it performs:
1. Row-normalize **M** to unit row norm.
2. Compute the row Gram matrix **G = MMᵀ**.
3. Extract the lower‑triangular part **T = tril(G)**.
4. Solve the forward triangular system **T Q = M** (i.e., **Q = T⁻¹ M**).
5. Row‑normalize **Q** to produce the decorrelated update.
This replaces Muon’s 5‑iteration Newton‑Schulz process with one triangular solve, reducing FLOPs from ~30*k*²*d* to ~2.5*k*²*d*. The update is scaled by `0.2 * sqrt(max(n,m))` (inherited from Muon). All other parameters (1D tensors) are updated with AdamW. Default hyperparameters: learning rate 1e-3, weight decay 1e-2, MUD momentum β=0.95, AdamW betas=(0.9, 0.95), MUD passes=1.

## Key Results
- MUD improves wall‑clock time‑to‑perplexity by 10–50% over both tuned AdamW and Muon across GPT‑2 variants, ESM‑2, and datasets (OpenWebText, WikiText‑103, FineWeb‑Edu).
- Peak token throughput is 1.3–2.6× higher than Muon, up to nearly 3× for GPT‑2 large.
- On ESM‑2 150M protein LM, MUD matches Muon’s validation perplexity with substantially less wall‑clock time.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Single‑Pass Triangular Whitening
MUD replaces Muon’s multi‑step Newton‑Schulz orthogonalization with a single forward triangular solve. Given the look‑ahead momentum **M**, MUD forms the row Gram **G = MMᵀ**, takes its strictly lower‑triangular part **T = tril(G)**, and solves **Q = T⁻¹ M** followed by row‑normalization. This approximates the polar factor **(MMᵀ)⁻¹/² M** using only one Gram formation and one triangular solve, avoiding iterative approximations. The core assumption is that the lower‑triangular component of **G** sufficiently captures the whitening transform for practical decorrelation. Evidence: The paper’s Algorithm 2 and FLOP reduction analysis (Table 1) show ~12× lower dense linear algebra cost versus Muon5. The approach is distinct from other matrix‑whitening optimizers that use different approximations. Failure can occur if **G** becomes near‑singular, making **T** ill‑conditioned and causing numerical instability or large errors.

### Mechanism 2: Quadratic Convergence to Orthonormality
MUD’s iterative map converges quadratically to row‑orthonormal fixed points under a small initial perturbation condition. Define the correlation‑normalized Gram **Ĝ = D⁻¹/² G D⁻¹/²** where **D = diag(G)**. MUD updates **G ← Corr( tril(Ĝ)⁻¹ Ĝ tril(Ĝ)⁻ᵀ )**. Theorem 3 proves that if the initial Gram’s off‑diagonals are within a small ϵ of zero, the perturbation shrinks quadratically: ‖*E*ₜ₊₁‖ ≤ C‖*E*ₜ‖². This ensures rapid correction of small deviations from orthonormality. The core assumption is that momentum matrices during training are already somewhat decorrelated, so the initial perturbation is small. Evidence: Section 3.3 provides the proof and Corollary 4, showing eigenvalue clustering and condition‑number improvement near the fixed point. If the initial Gram deviates significantly (large off‑diagonals), convergence may be slow or diverge.

### Mechanism 3: Reduced Overhead → Better Wall‑Clock Efficiency
By lowering per‑step optimizer cost, MUD improves wall‑clock time‑to‑perplexity. The dominant dense linear algebra drops from ~30*k*²*d* FLOPs (Muon5) to ~2.5*k*²*d* (MUD1). This yields 1.3–2.6× higher tokens/s over Muon and 10–50% faster time‑to‑perplexity versus AdamW and Muon in empirical evaluations. The core assumption is that the slight reduction in per‑step decorrelation strength does not outweigh gains from lower overhead; the optimization trajectory remains effective. Evidence: Section 4.1.1 (throughput tables), Section 4.1.2 (GPT‑2 Medium speedups), and Section 4.1.4 (FineWeb‑Edu) consistently show wall‑clock improvements. The gain diminishes in compute‑bound regimes (e.g., large context length) where optimizer overhead is amortized, or if the problem requires stronger orthogonalization for very ill‑conditioned gradients.

## Foundational Learning

- **Gram matrix and whitening**  
  Why needed: MUD operates on the row Gram **G = MMᵀ** and aims to produce **Q** with orthonormal rows, equivalent to whitening the row distribution of **M**.  
  Quick check: Given **M**, what condition on **Q = W M** ensures **Q** has orthonormal rows? (Answer: **W G Wᵀ = I**, i.e., **W** is a whitening transform for the row Gram.)

- **Momentum and Nesterov lookahead**  
  Why needed: MUD decorrelates the Nesterov look‑ahead direction **M_t = g_t + β v_t**, where **v_t** is the momentum buffer.  
  Quick check: How does Nesterov look‑ahead differ from heavy‑ball momentum? (Answer: Nesterov uses **g_t + β v_t**, a gradient‑corrected look‑ahead; heavy‑ball uses just **v_t**.)

- **Triangular solves and Cholesky factorization**  
  Why needed: MUD’s core step solves **T Q = M** via forward substitution, where **T = tril(G)**.  
  Quick check: Why is a triangular solve generally cheaper than a matrix inversion or full Cholesky? (Answer: Forward/backward substitution costs **O(k² d)** vs. **O(k³)** for factorization.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: gradient -> momentum buffer -> Nesterov lookahead **M** -> `mud_whiten` (row‑normalize -> Gram -> lower‑triangular -> forward solve -> renormalize) -> scaled step -> weight update; other params: AdamW (momentum -> bias‑correct -> adaptive step -> weight decay)

Critical path: For each matrix‑shaped parameter, the per‑step latency is dominated by `mud_whiten`: (1) row‑normalize **M**, (2) form **G = MMᵀ**, (3) extract **T = tril(G)**, (4) forward triangular solve **Q = T⁻¹ M**, (5) renormalize rows of **Q**. All other ops (momentum update, AdamW for 1D params) are cheaper.

Design tradeoffs: Passes – more MUD passes increase decorrelation but cost ~2.5*k*²*d* each; default 1 pass gives best wall‑clock, 2 passes sometimes help harder tasks. Stability – row‑normalization with small ε prevents division‑by‑zero; triangular solve requires **T** non‑singular; if **G** rank‑deficient, solve may fail (NaNs). Scaling – factor `0.2 * sqrt(max(n,m))` adapts step size to matrix size (inherited from Muon). Failure signatures: NaNs in **Q** or loss after `mud_whiten` indicate likely singular **T** (ill‑conditioned Gram). No improvement over AdamW suggests problem may not benefit from matrix decorrelation (small matrices, non‑transformer) or insufficient MUD passes. Throughput not improved implies too few matrix params or hardware TRSM not faster than GEMM.

First 3 experiments: (1) Run MUD on a tiny 2‑layer transformer (`d_model=64`) on few thousand tokens; verify loss decreases, no NaNs; compare with AdamW. (2) Measure tokens/s on GPT‑2 small (batch 8, context 1024) on single GPU; record speedup vs. Muon/AdamW (expect ~1.5–2× over Muon). (3) Train GPT‑2 medium on WikiText‑103 for ~5000 steps; plot val perplexity vs. wall‑clock; confirm MUD reaches target perplexity faster than AdamW and Muon.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How should MUD be combined with other orthogonalization methods (Si et al., 2025; Amsel et al., 2025) to maximize speed and stability?** The authors state future work will explore composition with recent orthogonalization advances; no preliminary experiments or theoretical framework exist. Resolution requires systematic hybrid evaluation (e.g., MUD passes + Newton–Schulz) on LLM benchmarks, measuring time‑to‑perplexity, final quality, and robustness.
- **What algorithmic modifications are required to scale MUD efficiently to distributed memory systems?** MUD’s triangular solves on large dense matrices may face dominating communication costs in distributed settings; no communication‑efficient variant is proposed. Resolution requires a distributed implementation (e.g., tensor‑parallel) and scaling studies on multi‑GPU/nodes versus Muon/AdamW for large transformers.
- **Why does fine‑tuning an AdamW‑pretrained model with Muon/MUD (or vice versa) degrade generalization, and can this be mitigated?** The paper notes AdamW‑pretrained models do not fine‑tune well with Muon/MUD and vice versa (citing Liu et al., 2025), but provides no root‑cause analysis. Possible factors: divergent optimization trajectories, implicit regularization differences, poor initialization for orthogonalized updates. Resolution requires comparative weight/gram statistics when switching optimizers and development of a warm‑start adjustment protocol.

## Limitations
- Generalization beyond Transformers: MUD evaluated only on transformer architectures; performance on CNNs, RNNs, MLPs is unknown.
- Heuristic scaling factor: The `0.2 * sqrt(max(n,m))` constant lacks theoretical justification and may be suboptimal for other tasks/scales.
- Muon baseline fidelity: Reimplementation of Newton–Schulz could differ from original, affecting comparative speedup numbers.
- Hardware dependence: Throughput gains measured on specific GPUs (A100, GH200, MI250); other hardware may show different relative improvements.
- Quadratic convergence regime: Theoretical guarantee assumes Gram near identity; early training has highly correlated gradients, potentially delaying convergence.
- Numerical stability: Triangular solve may become unstable if Gram near‑singular; row‑normalization helps but no guarantee against NaNs.

## Confidence
- **Computational efficiency (FLOP reduction):** *High* – clear mathematical derivation and FLOP counts.
- **Quadratic convergence to row‑orthonormal fixed points:** *Medium* – proof provided but relies on strong assumptions not fully validated in practice.
- **Wall‑clock time‑to‑perplexity improvements over AdamW/Muon:** *Medium* – strong empirical results across several setups, yet limited to specific models/datasets and may not generalize.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑architecture evaluation:** Train MUD on non‑transformer models (e.g., ResNet‑50 on ImageNet, MLP on tabular data) to test generalizability of decorrelation approach.
2. **Scaling factor robustness:** Systematically vary the scaling constant (e.g., 0.05–1.0) and the shape argument (tall vs. wide matrices) across GPT‑2 scales; measure impact on convergence speed and final perplexity.
3. **Condition number tracking:** During transformer training, log the Gram matrix condition number and off‑diagonal correlation statistics; correlate these with MUD’s per‑step correction magnitude to verify whether the quadratic regime is entered and how it affects practical performance.
