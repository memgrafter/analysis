# Robust AI Evaluation through Maximal Lotteries
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21297
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21297
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Robust lotteries improve worst-case subpopulation win rates by over 10 percentage points, shrink train-test generalization gap from ~6% to ~2%, and produce diverse, stable top models while satisfying weak-clone invariance.

## Executive Summary
AI leaderboards typically collapse heterogeneous user preferences into a single ranking via pairwise comparisons and Bradley‑Terry models, obscuring pluralistic values. This paper introduces **robust lotteries**—probability distributions over models that maximize the worst‑case win probability across an ambiguity set of majority‑margin matrices (e.g., varying subpopulation weights). The approach extends maximal lotteries, which are nonparametric and rooted in zero‑sum game theory, to be distributionally robust and computationally tractable via linear programming.

On two large‑scale benchmarks (LMArena and HUMAINE), robust lotteries substantially outperform maximal lotteries: they raise the worst‑case win rate across language or demographic groups by more than 10 percentage points, reduce the train‑test generalization gap from about 6% to 2%, and yield a diverse, stable set of top models (the robust bipartisan set). They also satisfy key social‑choice axioms such as weak‑clone invariance, making them a principled foundation for pluralistic AI evaluation.

## Method Summary
Given pairwise preference votes, the data is partitioned into subpopulations (e.g., by prompt language or annotator demographics). For each subpopulation \(k\), a majority‑margin matrix \(M^{(k)}\) is computed with Laplace smoothing; it is skew‑symmetric and encodes net preference fractions. An ambiguity set over subpopulation weights \(w\) is constructed as an \(\ell_1\) ball \(W(w_0,\rho)\) around the empirical distribution \(w_0\), representing plausible distribution shifts. The robust lottery \(p\) solves the maximin problem: maximize the minimum expected margin \(\min_{w\in W}\min_{q}p^\top M(w) q\). This can be reformulated as a linear program (Theorem 17) and solved efficiently. Optional linear constraints (e.g., a budget on inference cost) can be imposed. The resulting mixed strategy \(p\) guarantees a worst‑case win rate of \(0.5 + 0.5\,t\) where \(t\) is the optimal robust value.

## Key Results
- Robust lotteries increase worst‑case subpopulation win rates by >10 percentage points relative to maximal lotteries (on language groups in LMArena).
- They shrink the train‑test generalization gap from approximately 6% to 2%.
- They output a diverse, stable robust bipartisan set instead of a single winner.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Maximal lotteries provide a nonparametric aggregation of pairwise preferences that avoids collapsing heterogeneous preferences into a single ranking.
- **Mechanism:** The method treats the empirical majority margin matrix as the payoff matrix of a symmetric zero‑sum game and computes a maximin mixed strategy (the maximal lottery). This strategy guarantees at least 50% expected win probability against any fixed opponent model or mixture.
- **Core assumption:** The observed pairwise win margins are representative of the underlying preference relation and the matrix is skew‑symmetric.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Maximal lotteries are described as aggregating pairwise preferences without imposing any assumptions on their structure, in contrast to Bradley–Terry rankings.
  - [section 3] Formal Definition 2 and the surrounding text define maximal lotteries via `min_{p} max_{q} p^T M q` and list properties (Condorcet consistency, clone invariance, efficient computation).
  - [corpus] The neighbor paper *Jackpot! Alignment as a Maximal Lottery* independently applies maximal lotteries to RLHF, indicating their established relevance in preference aggregation.
- **Break condition:** If the preference data are extremely sparse or nearly cyclic, the maximal lottery may concentrate on a single model that severely underperforms for some subgroups (see Figure 1), revealing sensitivity to distribution shifts.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Robust lotteries optimize worst‑case win probability over an ambiguity set of plausible majority‑margin matrices, hedging against preference heterogeneity across subpopulations.
- **Mechanism:** An ambiguity set \(\mathcal{M}\) (e.g., convex hull of stratum matrices) is defined. The robust value of a lottery \(p\) is \(V(p,\mathcal{M}) = \min_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\min_{q\in\Delta(A)} p^\top M q\). The robust lottery maximizes this value. For uncertainty over subpopulation weights, \(\mathcal{M} = \{ M(w) : w \in W(w_0,\rho) \}\) with an \(\ell_1\) ball \(W\).
- **Core assumption:** The true preference distribution lies within the chosen ambiguity set \(\mathcal{M}\) (or the worst‑case over \(\mathcal{M}\) is a reliable proxy).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Robust lotteries are introduced specifically to optimize worst‑case performance under plausible shifts in preference data.
  - [section 4] Definition 5 gives the robust lottery; Theorem 17 provides the linear program for the \(\ell_1\)-ball ambiguity set and discusses its interpretation.
  - [corpus] The corpus contains work on robust game theory (Aghassi & Bertsimas) and distributionally robust optimization; while not directly merged with maximal lotteries, this indicates a conceptual lineage for the approach.
- **Break condition:** If the ambiguity set is misspecified (e.g., the actual shift lies outside \(\mathcal{M}\)), the worst‑case guarantee may be vacuous or biased. The LP may also become intractable for very large numbers of groups \(K\) without sparsification.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Robust lotteries inherit key axiomatic properties from maximal lotteries, such as weak‑clone invariance and robust Condorcet consistency, making them suitable for pluralistic evaluation.
- **Mechanism:** The authors prove that adding weaker clones does not change the robust bipartisan set (Theorem 11). For alternatives that dominate all others across the entire ambiguity set (robust Condorcet winner), the unique robust lottery is the Dirac distribution on that model (Theorem 8). The proofs use projection arguments and dominance comparisons.
- **Core assumption:** Cloned models are strictly weaker or identical to the original in every matrix of the ambiguity set; the ambiguity set respects this ordering under expansion.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Robust lotteries are said to satisfy axioms like weak‑clone invariance.
  - [section 4.1] Theorems 8, 11, and 14 formalize these properties; proofs are in Appendix A.
  - [corpus] The neighbor *Evaluating Agents using Social Choice Theory* discusses invariance properties for voting rules, providing broader context for clone invariance in AI evaluation.
- **Break condition:** The properties rely on convexity of \(\mathcal{M}\) and the structure of the weak‑clone expansion. If clones are stronger in some strata, invariance may fail.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Social choice axioms (Condorcet winner, clone invariance)  
  - **Why needed here:** They define fairness and stability criteria for aggregating preferences, ensuring the evaluation rule behaves well under electorate changes and duplication of models.  
  - **Quick check question:** What does clone invariance require when a duplicate model is added to the candidate set?  

- **Concept:** Zero‑sum game representation of preferences  
  - **Why needed here:** Maximal and robust lotteries are defined as maximin strategies in a zero‑sum game whose payoff matrix is the majority margin matrix; this links ordinal pairwise comparisons to game‑theoretic equilibrium concepts.  
  - **Quick check question:** In the zero‑sum game interpretation, what guarantee does a maximal lottery provide against any pure opponent strategy?  

- **Concept:** Distributionally robust optimization (ambiguity set, total variation ball)  
  - **Why needed here:** The robust lottery formulation uses an ambiguity set of possible weight distributions over subpopulations; the \(\ell_1\) ball with radius \(\rho\) models total variation distance, controlling the extent of allowed distribution shift.  
  - **Quick check question:** What effect does increasing the radius \(\rho\) have on the set of distributions \(W(w_0,\rho)\)?  

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  - *Data ingestion*: parse pairwise votes and group identifiers.  
  - *Stratum split*: separate comparisons by subpopulation (e.g., language, ethnicity).  
  - *Margin computation*: for each stratum `k`, compute the skew‑symmetric matrix `M^{(k)}` with Laplace smoothing.  
  - *Ambiguity set builder*: choose reference weights `w0` (e.g., empirical group frequencies) and radius `ρ`; form `W(w0,ρ)` or a custom convex set.  
  - *Solver*: invoke the linear program from Theorem 17; optionally append linear constraints (inference cost, benchmark thresholds).  
  - *Output*: robust lottery `p`, robust value `v⋆(M)`, robust bipartisan set `RBP(M)`.  

- **Critical path:** Raw votes → group splits → stratum matrices → ambiguity set → LP solve → lottery. The LP step depends on the matrices and `ρ`; errors in earlier stages propagate forward.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Larger `ρ` increases robustness to distribution shift but may reduce average performance (Figure 3).  
  - Adding cost constraints biases the lottery toward cheaper models but respects real‑world budgets (Figure 1).  
  - The LP size is `O(mK)`; for many groups or models one may use the sparse `ε`‑approximation (Theorem 18) to reduce support size at a small accuracy loss.  

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Infeasible LP when constraints conflict (e.g., cost budget too low relative to required performance).  
  - Extreme sensitivity of the lottery to small changes in `ρ` or `w0`, indicating numerical instability or poorly connected comparison data.  
  - Large gaps between training and held‑out worst‑group win rates, suggesting the ambiguity set does not cover true shifts (overfitting).  

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate Figure 1: compute maximal lottery (`ρ=0`) and robust lottery (`ρ=1`) on LMArena grouped by language; observe the shift from a single winner (Gemini 2.5 Pro) to a diverse bipartisan set (four models).  
  2. Sweep `ρ` from 0 to 1 and plot worst‑group and overall win rates on held‑out data (Figure 3 left); verify that worst‑group improves while overall slightly declines, illustrating the robustness‑accuracy trade‑off.  
  3. Add a cost constraint to the LP and recover the cost–performance frontier (Figure 1 blue curve); note how the frontier trades off monetary cost and worst‑case win rate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can meaningful subpopulations be automatically discovered from preference data when predefined group labels are unavailable?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The robust lottery approach requires a partition of the data into subpopulations to construct the ambiguity set; the authors relied on available metadata (e.g., prompt language, annotator demographics) but did not address scenarios where such groupings are unknown.
- **Why unresolved:** Without a principled way to define subpopulations, the ambiguity set may fail to capture the true sources of preference heterogeneity, leading to weak worst‑case guarantees.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop an unsupervised method (e.g., clustering of pairwise preference patterns or latent mixture models) to infer subpopulations, then empirically show that these inferred groups yield more robust lottery guarantees compared to arbitrary or predefined partitions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can statistical uncertainty in the estimated subpopulation majority-margin matrices \(M^{(k)}\) be incorporated into the robust lottery optimization?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The robust formulation only considers ambiguity due to uncertain subpopulation weights, treating each group‑specific matrix \(M^{(k)}\) as fixed and known; the impact of finite‑sample estimation error in these matrices is not modeled.
- **Why unresolved:** Each \(M^{(k)}\) is constructed from a limited number of pairwise comparisons; noise in these estimates may distort the worst‑case margin calculations, resulting in overconfident or brittle robust guarantees.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend the ambiguity set to include confidence regions around each empirical \(M^{(k)}\) (e.g., via entry‑wise concentration bounds). Solve the resulting minimax problem approximately (e.g., using robust optimization techniques) and demonstrate on synthetic and real data that the resulting lotteries achieve better coverage of true worst‑case win rates.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can robust lotteries be computed efficiently for large model sets (e.g., thousands of models) while preserving theoretical guarantees?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors provide a linear program of size \(O(mK)\) and prove the existence of sparse \(\epsilon\)-optimal solutions with support logarithmic in \(m\) and \(K\), but they do not provide an efficient algorithm to obtain such sparse solutions at scale.
- **Why unresolved:** The full LP may become computationally prohibitive for very large \(m\); the existence result does not automatically yield a polynomial‑time construction method needed for practical, high‑frequency leaderboard updates.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design a polynomial‑time algorithm (e.g., greedy selection, multiplicative weights, or rounding schemes) that outputs an \(\epsilon\)-optimal robust lottery with \(O(\log m + \log K)\) support, and evaluate its runtime and solution quality on large‑scale synthetic and semi‑synthetic evaluation datasets.

## Limitations
- Ambiguity set misspecification can yield vacuous worst‑case guarantees.
- Sparse subpopulation data introduce noise, undermining robustness.
- Validation only on two datasets; generalizability uncertain.
