# A Short and Unified Convergence Analysis of the SAG, SAGA, and IAG Algorithms
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.05304
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05304
- **Reference count**: 30
- **Primary result**: Unified convergence analysis for SAG, SAGA, and IAG on finite-sum L-smooth µ-strongly convex objectives; SAG/SAGA achieve high-probability linear rate (1 - 1/(64τκ))^K with τ = O(N log(NK/δ)); IAG improves from O(exp(-K/(κ²N²))) to O(exp(-K/(κN))).

## Executive Summary
The paper presents a unified convergence analysis for three popular variance-reduced stochastic gradient methods—SAG, SAGA, and IAG—in the setting of finite-sum optimization. The objective is to minimize f(x) = (1/N)∑ᵢ fᵢ(x), where each fᵢ is L-smooth and the overall function f is µ-strongly convex. The authors employ a technique that bounds stochastic sub-sampling delays using concentration inequalities and constructs a Lyapunov function incorporating a shifted window of past gradient estimates. This framework yields high-probability linear convergence guarantees for SAG and SAGA, and sharpens the best known rate for IAG by removing a quadratic dependence on the condition number κ and the number of functions N. The analysis also extends to non-convex objectives and Markov sampling, highlighting its flexibility.

The work contributes a cohesive theoretical foundation that unifies the understanding of delayed gradient methods. The improved IAG rate—from O(exp(-K/(κ² N²))) to O(exp(-K/(κ N)))—is particularly impactful for large-scale problems where N and κ are large. However, the high-probability rate for SAG and SAGA is slower than previously established in-expectation rates, raising the question of whether this gap is inherent or an artifact of the analysis. The paper thus opens several directions for future research, including tight lower bounds, acceleration, and the interplay between delay and stochasticity.

## Method Summary
The algorithms address the finite-sum problem under smoothness and strong convexity. SAG and SAGA use uniform i.i.d. sampling (or a Markov chain) of a component index i at each iteration. They maintain stored gradients gᵢ for each component, initially zero (or optionally precomputed). A running sum S = ∑ᵢ gᵢ is kept. For sampled i, the new gradient ∇fᵢ(xₖ) is computed. The gradient estimator gₖ is formed differently: SAG uses gₖ = S/N + (∇fᵢ(xₖ) - gᵢ)/N, while SAGA uses gₖ = S/N + ∇fᵢ(xₖ) - gᵢ. Then xₖ₊₁ = xₖ - α gₖ, and the stored gradient gᵢ and S are updated. IAG employs deterministic cyclic sampling (i = k mod N) and uses the SAG-type estimator. The step-size α is set to 1/(16Lτ), where τ is a problem-dependent parameter: for SAG/SAGA, τ = ⌈(8N/3) log(NK/δ)⌉ ensures the maximum delay stays below τ with probability at least 1-δ; for IAG, τ = N (the fixed period). The key analytical tool is the Lyapunov function Vₖ = f(xₖ) - f(x*) + Lα² Wₖ, where Wₖ = ∑_{j=1}^τ (τ - j + 1) ||gₖ₋ⱼ||² aggregates squared gradient estimator norms over the τ most recent iterations, weighted by age. This windowed term captures the accumulation of errors due to stale gradients.

The proof proceeds by bounding the maximum delay via concentration (for uniform sampling, the delay distribution is geometric; for Markov, mixing arguments). Under the chosen step-size, the Lyapunov function satisfies a linear contraction: Vₖ₊₁ ≤ (1 - 1/(64τκ)) Vₖ with high probability for SAG/SAGA, and a similar bound for IAG with a better constant. This yields the stated convergence rates. The unified analysis shows that despite differences in estimator design (averaging in SAG vs. no averaging in SAGA, deterministic vs. stochastic sampling), the same Lyapunov recursion and step-size condition apply, highlighting the robustness of the approach.

## Key Results
- **SAG/SAGA high-probability rate**: For uniform i.i.d. sampling, with probability at least 1-δ, f(x_K) - f(x^*) ≤ 6L B² (1 - 1/(64τκ))^K, where τ = O(N log(NK/δ)) and B = max_i ||x_i^*||.
- **IAG rate improvement**: The best known rate improves from O(exp(-K/(κ² N²))) to O(exp(-K/(κ N))), removing the quadratic dependence on κ and N.
- **Extensions**: The framework also provides stationary point guarantees for non-convex objectives and convergence rates for Markov sampling that depend on the mixing time and minimum visitation probability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that the age of stored gradients (the delay) directly governs the bias and variance of the gradient estimator. By introducing a Lyapunov function that couples function suboptimality with a weighted sum of past estimator norms, the analysis accounts for the cumulative effect of stale information. The window length τ is chosen to bound the maximum delay with high probability; this bound is derived from concentration inequalities (e.g., for uniform sampling, the probability that any component remains unsampled for more than τ iterations is small). The step-size α = 1/(16Lτ) is tuned so that the Lyapunov function decreases geometrically. The contraction factor contains 1/(64τκ), explaining the slower rate when τ is large (due to the logarithmic factor in τ). For IAG, the deterministic cyclic schedule ensures the maximum delay is exactly N, so τ = N is much smaller than the stochastic bound, leading to a faster rate with dependence κN instead of κ²N². The unified approach demonstrates that controlling delay is the central challenge, and the windowed Lyapunov elegantly integrates it into a single scalar potential.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Finite-sum optimization**: The objective is an average of N component functions. *Why needed*: It arises in empirical risk minimization and enables variance reduction by exploiting finite sum structure. *Quick check*: If f(x) = (1/N)∑ᵢ fᵢ(x), what is ∇f(x)? How does a variance-reduced method use this structure differently from stochastic gradient descent that samples one f_i?

2. **Variance-reduced stochastic gradient methods**: Algorithms (SAG, SAGA, IAG) that maintain per-component stored gradients to form an unbiased estimator with reduced variance. *Why needed*: They achieve linear convergence on smooth strongly convex problems, whereas SGD only achieves sublinear rates. *Quick check*: Contrast the gradient estimators of SAG and SAGA. What role does the average S play in both?

3. **L-smooth and µ-strongly convex**: Assumptions on the smoothness constant L and strong convexity constant µ. *Why needed*: They provide curvature bounds that yield linear convergence when used with appropriate step-sizes. *Quick check*: How do L and µ relate to the Hessian of a quadratic function? What is the condition number κ and why does it appear in rates?

4. **High-probability convergence**: Guarantees that hold with failure probability δ, as opposed to in-expectation. *Why needed*: Offers reliability for real-world applications where worst-case performance matters. *Quick check*: What does "with probability at least 1-δ" mean? How does δ typically influence the algorithm's parameters (e.g., τ)?

5. **Delay in stochastic gradient methods**: The number of iterations since a component's gradient was last evaluated. In variance-reduced methods, the stored gradient may be stale. *Why needed*: Delays introduce bias; controlling them is essential for convergence. *Quick check*: In SAG, if a component hasn't been sampled for 100 iterations, how does that affect the current gradient estimate? What is the maximum delay τ in the analysis?

6. **Lyapunov function**: A scalar function Vₖ that decreases along the iterates (Vₖ₊₁ ≤ c Vₖ). *Why needed*: Provides a unified way to track both function error and gradient estimator errors. *Quick check*: The Lyapunov here is Vₖ = f(xₖ)-f(x*) + Lα² Wₖ. What does each term represent? Why include both?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (algorithmic flow):
x₀ = 0, stored_grads = [0]*N, S = 0 → for each iteration k: sample i (uniform/cyclic) → compute grad = ∇fᵢ(xₖ) → compute estimator g = S/N + (grad - stored_grads[i])/N (SAG) or g = S/N + grad - stored_grads[i] (SAGA) or IAG uses SAG estimator → x ← x - α g → update stored_grads[i] = grad, S = S - old_grad + grad.

**Critical path**:
- Sampling must match the assumed distribution (i.i.d. uniform or deterministic cyclic).
- Estimator construction must exactly follow the algorithm (SAG vs SAGA distinction).
- Step-size α must be 1/(16Lτ) with τ computed correctly for the desired δ and K.
- Update of stored_grads and S must be atomic and correct.
- For SAG/SAGA, ensure the bound on maximum delay holds; otherwise the high-probability guarantee may fail.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **Step-size**: α = 1/(16Lτ) from theory depends on total iterations K (via τ) and δ; fixed α may work empirically but sacrifices the high-probability guarantee.
- **Initial stored gradients**: Theory assumes zeros for unaccessed components; precomputing all ∇fᵢ(x₀) may accelerate early iterations but could increase B = max_i ||x_i^*||? Actually B is based on optimal solutions, not initial. However the Lyapunov analysis might rely on zero initialization; using precomputed gradients may affect the bound's constants or require adaptation.
- **Sampling**: Uniform i.i.d. is simplest; Markov sampling allows more complex data dependencies but complicates delay analysis.
- **Window size τ**: Larger τ yields smaller step-size but weaker contraction; there is a trade-off in the Lyapunov construction.
- **Algorithm choice**: SAG uses averaging (1/N factor) which reduces variance but also scales step-size; SAGA avoids that scaling, often leading to faster practical convergence but slightly different theoretical constants.

**Failure signatures**:
- **Divergence**: f(xₖ) increases or oscillates; typically α is too large (maybe due to underestimating L or τ).
- **Slow progress**: f(xₖ) decreases but extremely slowly; α may be too small or τ overestimated.
- **High-probability bound violation**: In SAG/SAGA runs, more than δ fraction of seeds exceed the theoretical bound; suggests maximum delay often exceeds τ or step-size not properly set.
- **Estimator bug**: Mixing up SAG and SAGA formulas; e.g., using SAGA's estimator for SAG or vice versa. Check early g values manually.
- **Cyclic indexing error**: For IAG, off-by-one in computing i = k mod N can cause a component to be skipped or double-sampled, increasing delay beyond N.
- **Loose bound**: f(x_K) is much smaller than bound; not a failure but indicates bound is conservative, possibly due to large B.

**First experiments**:
1. **Quadratic convergence test**: Generate synthetic quadratics with known κ and N, run each algorithm, plot f(xₖ)-f(x*) on log scale. After k > τ, expect straight line with slope -1/(64τκ) for SAG/SAGA and -1/(κN) for IAG.
2. **High-probability validation for SAG/SAGA**: For δ=0.1, compute τ accordingly. Run 200 independent seeds; count how many have f(x_K) > 6L B² (1-1/(64τκ))^K. The proportion should be ≤ δ.
3. **IAG rate improvement**: On same quadratics, compute empirical exponential rate (slope of log suboptimality) and compare to -1/(κN) (new) and -1/(κ²N²) (old). The empirical slope should be steeper than the old prediction, confirming the improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the gap between the high-probability convergence rate in this paper and the in-expectation rates from prior work arise from an artifact of the analysis or is it fundamentally unavoidable?
   - Basis in paper: Conclusion, Section 5: "there is a gap between our high-probability linear convergence rate and the in-expectation rates for SAG and SAGA in prior work. We would like to investigate further whether this gap is an artifact of our analysis or fundamental."
   - Why unresolved: The high-probability bound is slower than prior in-expectation bounds; it is unclear if this deterioration is inherent to high-probability guarantees or can be eliminated with a sharper analysis.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Proving a matching lower bound for high-probability convergence or deriving a refined analysis that recovers the in-expectation rate.

2. What are tight lower bounds for the convergence rate of SAG and SAGA under Markov sampling?
   - Basis in paper: Conclusion, Section 5: "deriving lower bounds for the Markov sampling case would be interesting."
   - Why unresolved: The paper provides an upper bound with dependence on mixing time and minimum visitation probability, but no corresponding lower bound exists to establish tightness.
   - What evidence would resolve it: A lower bound matching the upper bound up to constants would confirm tightness; a counterexample showing a worse rate would indicate looseness.

3. Can the unified proof framework (delay concentration + windowed Lyapunov) be extended to obtain high-probability bounds for accelerated or second-order variance-reduced algorithms?
   - Basis in paper: Conclusion, Section 5: "extending our proof template to more complex (e.g., accelerated, second-order) VR algorithms in the future."
   - Why unresolved: Accelerated methods (e.g., Katyusha, SPIDER) involve momentum or recursive variance reduction that may not directly fit the current Lyapunov construction or delay bounding.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Successfully adapting the core ideas to derive high-probability linear convergence for such algorithms, or proving impossibility via lower bounds.

4. Does the lower bound exp(-K/(τκ)) for deterministic delayed gradient descent (Arjevani et al., 2020) also apply to the stochastic high-probability setting of SAG/SAGA?
   - Basis in paper: Main text after Theorem 3.11: "Whether the lower bound in Arjevani et al. (2020) applies to our setting remains to be seen."
   - Why unresolved: SAG/SAGA use stochastic sub-sampling and bias-correction, differing from deterministic delayed GD; the applicability of the lower bound to high-probability stochastic guarantees is unknown.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Proving that any algorithm with maximum delay τ under the same sampling model cannot beat exp(-K/(τκ)) in high-probability would confirm; designing an algorithm that surpasses this rate would refute.

## Limitations
- The step-size for SAG/SAGA depends on total iterations K and failure probability δ via τ = O(N log(NK/δ)), which is atypical and may be impractical for fixed-budget runs.
- The bound contains B = max_i ||x_i^*||, an unknown and potentially large quantity, leading to conservative worst-case guarantees.
- The claimed improvement for IAG cannot be quantitatively verified without the exact constants from the prior work (Gurbuzbalaban et al., 2017), and the effect of initializing stored gradients to zero (as assumed) versus precomputed gradients remains untested.
