# Sparse Bayesian Deep Functional Learning with Structured Region Selection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.20651
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20651
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: sBayFDNN significantly outperforms baselines (e.g., ECG RMSE 12.07 vs 16.3, F1 0.634 vs 0.41) and provides interpretable region selection via posterior inclusion probabilities.

## Executive Summary
Scalar-on-function regression aims to predict a scalar response from a functional predictor while identifying which parts of the predictor curve are influential. This paper introduces sBayFDNN, a Bayesian deep neural network model that combines B-spline basis expansion of the functional coefficient with a ReLU feedforward network. A group spike-and-slab prior on first-layer weights enables sparse selection of relevant basis functions, which translates to localized active regions in the original curve. Posterior inclusion probabilities quantify uncertainty in selection. The method provides theoretical guarantees on approximation error, posterior consistency, and region selection consistency. Empirical evaluations on simulations and four real-world datasets (ECG, Tecator, Bike rental, IHPC) show consistent improvements over five baseline methods in both prediction (RMSE/MAE) and region recovery (F1, recall, precision). The learned active regions align with domain knowledge (e.g., ECG QRS complexes, Tecator fat content regions), demonstrating both competitive predictive performance and interpretability.

## Method Summary
sBayFDNN addresses scalar-on-function regression by first projecting the functional predictor \(X(t)\) onto a B-spline basis \(\{\!B_j(t)\!\}_{j=1}^J\), yielding a feature vector \(\eta(X) = (x_1,\dots,x_J)\) with \(x_j = \int X(t) B_j(t) \,dt\). A ReLU feedforward neural network \(F_\theta(\eta(X))\) models the potentially nonlinear relationship with the response \(Y\). Sparsity and uncertainty quantification are achieved through a group spike-and-slab prior on the first-layer weight columns: each column \(\mathbf{W}_{1,\cdot j}\) is conditionally on \(\gamma_j \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\lambda)\) drawn from either a small-variance Gaussian \(\mathcal N(0,\sigma_0^2 \mathbf I)\) (if \(\gamma_j=0\), non-informative) or a large-variance Gaussian \(\mathcal N(0,\sigma_1^2 \mathbf I)\) (if \(\gamma_j=1\), informative). All other weights receive standard Gaussian priors. The marginal posterior over \(\theta\) (integrating out \(\gamma\)) is maximized via stochastic gradient descent, with early stopping and multiple random restarts. After training, posterior inclusion probabilities \(q_j\) for each basis are computed from the MAP weights; bases with \(q_j>0.5\) are selected, and their spline supports are unioned to form the active region \(b\Omega\). Model selection (basis size \(J\) and hyperparameters) uses either Laplace-approximated evidence or validation loss.

## Key Results
- sBayFDNN outperforms five competing methods (including functional linear models, kernel methods, and other functional DNNs) in prediction across four real datasets, achieving up to 28% lower RMSE (ECG: 12.07 vs 16.3 for the next best).
- Region selection is substantially more accurate, with F1 scores up to 0.634 on ECG (vs 0.41) and 0.339 on Tecator; the selected active regions correspond to known important intervals (e.g., ECG QRS complex, high-frequency fat absorption bands).
- Theoretical results: the work provides the first approximation error bounds, posterior consistency, and region selection consistency guarantees for a Bayesian deep functional model under standard smoothness assumptions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works because it jointly addresses approximation, sparsity, and uncertainty. The B-spline basis expansion provides a flexible yet parsimonious representation of the functional coefficient, while the deep ReLU network can approximate any smooth nonlinear link function given sufficient width. The group spike-and-slab prior selectively shrinks entire basis coefficients toward zero, enabling interpretable region selection without sacrificing the capacity to model complex effects. Marginalizing the binary inclusion variables yields a posterior that inherently balances model fit and sparsity, and the MAP estimation via SGD scales to realistic functional data sizes. Posterior inclusion probabilities offer a calibrated measure of selection confidence, and the union of selected spline supports directly maps to an active domain on the input curve. Theoretical analysis underpins these empirical successes by ensuring that, under appropriate conditions, the posterior concentrates around the true model and the selected region converges to the true active set.

## Foundational Learning
- **B-spline basis expansion**: Why needed? Converts a continuous curve into a finite set of coefficients while preserving smoothness, enabling standard regression techniques. Quick check: Can any continuous function be approximated arbitrarily well by increasing knots? Yes, by the Stone-Weierstrass theorem.
- **Group spike-and-slab prior**: Why needed? Introduces sparsity at the level of basis functions (groups) while capturing uncertainty via the inclusion indicators. Quick check: Does the prior place positive probability at exactly zero? Yes, via the mixture with a point mass at zero.
- **ReLU feedforward network**: Why needed? Provides universal approximation of nonlinear functions, essential when the relationship between functional predictor and response is complex. Quick check: With enough hidden units, can a ReLU network approximate any continuous function on a compact set? Yes, according to universal approximation theorems.
- **Posterior inclusion probability (PIP)**: Why needed? Quantifies the probability that a given basis is part of the true model, enabling thresholded selection with uncertainty estimates. Quick check: Is PIP always between 0 and 1? Yes.
- **MAP inference via SGD**: Why needed? Optimizes the (marginal) posterior in a high-dimensional, non-convex landscape; SGD scales to large datasets and networks. Quick check: Does the algorithm maximize the posterior density? Yes, by minimizing the negative log-posterior.
- **Functional data analysis**: Why needed? Data are curves, not independent vectors; methods must account for the infinite-dimensional nature and temporal correlation. Quick check: Are observations indexed by a continuous domain (e.g., time)? Yes.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Functional predictor \(X(t)\) → B-spline basis expansion (\(J\) basis functions) → Feature vector \(\eta(X)\) (J‑dimensional) → Deep ReLU network (first‑layer weights with group spike‑and‑slab prior, other layers Gaussian) → Predicted response \(\hat{Y}\) → Gaussian likelihood → Marginal posterior (integrated over \(\gamma\)) → MAP estimate (\(\theta_{\text{MAP}}\)) via stochastic gradient descent → Compute posterior inclusion probabilities \(q_j\) for each basis → Threshold (\(q_j>0.5\)) to select active bases → Union of supports of selected splines yields active region \(b\Omega\).

**Critical path:**  
1. Choose basis size \(J\) and knot placement to cover the domain.  
2. Train the network with the sparsity‑inducing prior to obtain \(\theta_{\text{MAP}}\).  
3. Compute PIPs from \(\theta_{\text{MAP}}\) and prior hyperparameters.  
4. Apply threshold to determine active bases.  
5. Map active bases back to time/domain intervals.  

Each step depends on the previous: poor basis choice harms approximation; insufficient network depth/width limits model capacity; inappropriate \(\lambda\), \(\sigma_0^2\), \(\sigma_1^2\) lead to over/under‑sparsity; non‑converged MAP estimate yields unreliable PIPs; threshold choice affects selection.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Basis size (\(J\))**: Larger \(J\) increases resolution of potential active regions but also increases false positives and computational cost; too small \(J\) may miss narrow true active regions.  
- **Network capacity (depth/width)**: More layers/units improve nonlinear approximation but risk overfitting and slower training; too little capacity underfits.  
- **Prior hyperparameters (\(\lambda\), \(\sigma_0^2\), \(\sigma_1^2\))**: \(\lambda\) controls overall sparsity; \(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_0^2\) controls separation between “active” and “inactive” states. Tuning these is critical; inappropriate values lead to either no selection or all bases active.  
- **Model selection criterion**: Validation loss is straightforward but may overfit the validation set; Laplace‑approximated evidence aims to penalize complexity but is approximate.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Poor predictive performance** (high RMSE on validation) despite low training error: indicates overfitting or insufficient sparsity; consider increasing \(\lambda\) or reducing network size.  
- **No basis selected** (all PIPs near 0): priors too tight (\(\sigma_0^2\) too small) or \(\lambda\) too low; relax \(\lambda\) or increase \(\sigma_1^2\).  
- **All bases selected** (PIPs near 1): priors too weak (\(\sigma_0^2\) close to \(\sigma_1^2\)) or \(\lambda\) too high; increase separation or reduce \(\lambda\).  
- **Unstable PIPs across random restarts**: optimization stuck in local minima; increase restarts or adjust learning rate.  
- **Active region too fragmented**: basis functions too localized; increase basis smoothness or adjust knot placement.

**Three first experiments:**  
1. **Simulation replication**: Generate data with a known active interval (e.g., \(\beta(t)\) nonzero on \([0.4,0.6]\)), run sBayFDNN, and report RMSE, F1, and PIP heatmap; verify recovery of the true interval.  
2. **Ablation study**: Compare the full model (spike‑and‑slab) against a counterpart with Gaussian‑only priors (no sparsity) to quantify the trade‑off between interpretability (sparsity) and prediction accuracy.  
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Vary \(\lambda\) (e.g., 0.01, 0.1, 0.5) and basis size \(J\) on a real dataset (e.g., Tecator) to observe effects on validation RMSE and number of selected bases; identify robust settings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Would replacing the Gaussian l2 loss with a more robust alternative improve stability and region selection accuracy under heavy‑tailed noise or outliers?
- Can sBayFDNN be embedded within a generalized linear model (GLM) to handle binary, count, or survival outcomes while retaining interpretable region selection?

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (e.g., exact sparsity, smooth coefficients) are not empirically validated on real data.
- Hyperparameter selection (\(\lambda\), \(\sigma^2\), basis size \(J\)) details are sparse; risk of overfitting to validation set.
- Evaluation relies on a single train‑test split per dataset; no cross‑validation reported; real‑data region selection lacks objective ground truth verification.
