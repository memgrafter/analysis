# Spectral Condition for $μ$P under Width-Depth Scaling

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.00541
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00541
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: A spectral condition unifies µP formulations for joint width-depth scaling, enabling robust hyperparameter transfer across model sizes in GPT-2 models.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses a key limitation of maximal update parameterization (μP): while μP ensures stable feature learning and hyperparameter (HP) transfer under width scaling, it breaks down when both width and depth are increased jointly. The authors develop a unified spectral framework that precisely characterizes how weight norms and their per‑step updates must scale with model width n and depth L to satisfy the μP principle across residual architectures. The core result is a simple spectral condition: for each hidden layer, the product of the residual multiplier αₗ and the squared RMS norms of the two weight matrices in a two‑layer block must be Θ(1/L). This condition unifies previous fragmented µP formulations and yields a general recipe for HP parameterization for many optimizers (SGD, AdamW, Muon, etc.). Experiments on GPT‑2 models confirm that the proposed µP preserves stable feature norms (∥h_L∥_R ≈ Θ(1)) and enables robust HP transfer across widths and depths, whereas standard parameterization leads to exploding features and unstable optimal learning rates. The work also extends the analysis to k‑layer blocks and provides explicit hyperparameter scaling formulas for popular optimizers, making the framework readily applicable. Despite its theoretical derivation under Gaussian initialization and first‑step approximations, the approach shows strong empirical validity, offering a practical path to scaling foundation models without costly HP retuning.

## Method Summary
The method is centered on the spectral μP condition, which dictates scaling relations for weight norms, residual multipliers, and learning rates. Given a base architecture (e.g., GPT‑2 with width n₀ and depth L₀), the condition determines how to scale these quantities to any target (n, L). Specifically, the residual multiplier αₗ for hidden layers must scale as 1/L relative to base, while weight initialization variances and learning rates are adjusted according to optimizer‑specific mappings (e.g., for Muon‑Kimi, hidden layer learning rate scales as 1/√n). The training procedure initializes all matrices with appropriate RMS norms, applies the scaled αₗ in each residual block, and uses the derived hyperparameter recipes (learning rates, weight decay) for each optimizer component. After tuning the base hyperparameters (α_base, σ_base², η_base) on the small model, the same base values are used with the scaling formulas for larger models, ensuring direct HP transfer. Validation involves monitoring feature norms (∥h_L∥_R) and comparing optimal base learning rates across scales.

## Key Results
- Unified spectral condition dictates scaling of weight norms and updates with width and depth.
- Recipe for hyperparameter parameterization across optimizers (SGD, AdamW, Muon, etc.).
- Experiments on GPT-2 confirm stable feature norms (∥h_L∥_R ≈ Θ(1)) and robust HP transfer across widths/depths; standard parameterization fails.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1 (Spectral constraint).** The condition αₗ∥W²ₗ∥ᵣ∥W¹ₗ∥ᵣ = Θ(1/L) for hidden layers ensures that the expected per‑step feature norm change is Θ(1/L) rather than Θ(1/√L), preventing explosion when accumulated over L layers. Derived under Gaussian initialization and correlated gradient updates, the bounding arguments are tight (Appendix E).

**Mechanism 2 (Hyperparameter recipe).** For any optimizer, the update ΔWₗ = ηₗAₗ + λₗWₗ yields a per‑step norm. The spectral condition αₗ∥ΔWₗ∥ᵣ = Θ(1) (input/output) or αₗ∥ΔWₗ∥ᵣ∥Wₗ∥ᵣ = Θ(1/L) (hidden) uniquely determines the scaling of ηₗ and λₗ with width n and depth L. For example, Muon’s Aₗ ≈ ∇WₗL scaled by 1/√n gives η_hidden ∝ 1/√n. This mapping holds provided Aₗ’s scaling relative to the raw gradient remains fixed.

**Mechanism 3 (Empirical validation).** Training GPT‑2 models with the derived μP yields constant feature norms ∥h_L∥ᵣ across scales, whereas standard parameterization produces exploding features. Moreover, the optimal base learning rate η_base, tuned on a small model, transfers directly to larger models via the predicted ratios (e.g., η_hidden = η_base/√(n/n_base)), while SP requires retuning for each scale. These results confirm both the spectral condition and the optimizer-specific parameterizations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: RMS operator norm.**  
  *Why needed:* Core metric for spectral conditions; simplifies scalar constraints on matrix norms (∥A∥ᵣ = √(n_out/n_in)∥A∥₂).  
  *Quick check:* For a Gaussian matrix A with variance σ², how does ∥A∥ᵣ scale? If n_out = n_in = n, then ∥A∥ᵣ ≈ 2σ√n, i.e., Θ(√n).

- **Concept: Residual multiplier αₗ.**  
  *Why needed:* Controls effective strength of each residual block; must scale as Θ(1/L) (vs. Θ(1/√L) for single‑layer blocks) to compensate for accumulated feature growth.  
  *Quick check:* Why does a two‑layer residual block require stricter αₗ scaling? Because the block’s contribution is the product of two weight matrices, which amplifies feature norms; scaling with 1/L prevents explosion when summing L such blocks.

- **Concept: First‑order vs. second‑order update conditions.**  
  *Why needed:* For blocks with ≥2 layers, the second‑order weight product ΔW^{(2)}ΔW^{(1)} must also be controlled to Θ(1/L); this tightens initial scaling.  
  *Quick check:* How does the second‑order condition refine the initialization condition? It imposes an extra constraint on ∥W^{(1)}∥ᵣ∥W^{(2)}∥ᵣ and their update norms, leading to stricter αₗ and initialization variances for multi‑layer blocks.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Spectral condition → determines residual multiplier αₗ and learning rates ηₗ;  
Block structure (1‑layer vs. k‑layer) → dictates need for higher‑order update constraints and thus αₗ scaling;  
Optimizer‑specific ∥Aₗ∥ᵣ → translates spectral constraints into concrete ηₗ, λₗ;  
Ratio‑based transfer (rₙ = n/n_base, r_L = L/L_base) → applies base hyperparameters to target model.

**Critical path:**  
1. Choose base architecture (e.g., Transformer with two‑layer FFN blocks → treat as two‑layer residual).  
2. From Condition 3.1, enforce αₗ = Θ(1/L) for hidden layers.  
3. For chosen optimizer, derive ηₗ, λₗ from αₗ∥ΔWₗ∥ᵣ constraints using ∥Aₗ∥ᵣ formulas in Appendix C.  
4. Implement scaling: set αₗ = α_base / r_L (hidden), αₗ = α_base / r_n (output), etc.  
5. Validate: train small base model → tune α_base, σ_base², η_base → direct transfer to larger (n,L).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Simplicity vs. generality: Two‑layer block analysis captures core behavior; deeper blocks introduce higher‑order terms but no new algorithmic constraints.  
- Theoretical purity vs. practical deviations: Gaussian initialization and non‑vanishing updates assumptions may be violated by LayerNorm or momentum; empirical validation needed.  
- Optimizer coverage: The condition recovers many existing µP formulations but may not capture exotic optimizers with non‑uniform update scaling.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Norm explosion: ∥h_L∥ᵣ grows with L → αₗ too large (should be Θ(1/L)).  
- HP re‑tuning required: Optimal η_base changes with n or L → scaling ratios mis‑specified.  
- Training instability at large depth: Loss diverges → check Assumption F.1 or αₗ tightness.  
- Mismatched optimizer assignment: E.g., LM head optimized with wrong scaling → output layer blows up.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Coordinate‑check test: Train 10 steps, measure ∥h_L∥ᵣ vs. n and L. Stable Θ(1) under μP, exploding under SP.  
2. HP transfer sweep: Tune η_base on base model (n_base, L_base), apply scaled η = η_base/√(n/n_base) etc. to larger models; expect invariant optimal η_base across n, L.  
3. Optimizer extension validation: Apply derived parameterization (e.g., for Lion) to GPT‑2 scale; verify stable feature norms and HP transfer.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Non‑residual architectures:** The paper does not analyze plain MLPs; it is unknown whether the same scaling recipe works without skip connections. Extending the spectral analysis to non‑residual networks and testing experimentally would resolve this.
- **Extremely deep residual networks (L ≫ 256):** Experiments only go up to L=256. The theoretical derivation assumes finite L; deeper networks (thousands of layers) may break due to higher‑order effects or numerical issues. Training such models would test the asymptotic regime.
- **Normalization layers:** The theory does not incorporate LayerNorm/RMSNorm, though experiments use GPT‑2. It is unclear if the parameterization remains sufficient or needs modification when normalization is present. Introducing normalization into the linear model and deriving modified constraints would clarify.
- **Non‑Gaussian initializations:** The derivations assume i.i.d. Gaussian weights. Other initializations (orthogonal, Kaiming) have different spectral properties and may affect scaling. Generalizing the theory or testing empirically with alternative initializations would determine robustness.

## Limitations
- Transformer residual multiplier α assignment to sub‑layers (attention vs. MLP) and bias scaling are unspecified.
- Gaussian initialization and non‑vanishing update assumptions may be violated by LayerNorm or momentum; first‑step analysis may not hold for long training.
- Validation limited to GPT‑2 style language models; other architectures (e.g., ResNet, MLP‑Mixer) remain untested.
