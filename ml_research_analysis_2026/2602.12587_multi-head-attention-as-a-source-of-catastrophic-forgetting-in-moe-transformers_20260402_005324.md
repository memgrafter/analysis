# Multi-Head Attention as a Source of Catastrophic Forgetting in MoE Transformers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.12587
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12587
- Reference count: 25
- Primary result: MH-MoE reduces backward transfer (BWT) from -11.2% to -4.5% and improves overall performance (OP) from 0.378 to 0.467 on Qwen3-0.6B, substantially mitigating forgetting without task-specific mechanisms.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies multi-head attention as a key source of catastrophic forgetting in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) transformers during continual learning. The core mechanism is that concatenating head-specific feature signals before routing forces the router to operate on mixed feature compositions, leading to collisions where distinct task-relevant features map to the same expert. This collision increases interference during gradient updates. The proposed solution, MH-MoE, performs head-wise routing on sliced sub-representations, increasing routing granularity and reducing the route-wise effective composition number (Neff). Experiments on the TRACE benchmark show MH-MoE significantly reduces forgetting and improves overall performance compared to standard MoE routing, without requiring task-specific mechanisms.

## Method Summary
MH-MoE modifies the standard MoE layer by inserting a head splitter immediately after multi-head attention. Instead of concatenating all head outputs into a single vector for routing, it partitions the post-attention representation into H disjoint, head-aligned sub-vectors. Each sub-vector is routed independently using a private router and a dedicated bank of experts specific to that head. The final token representation is the sum of all head-specific expert outputs. This design increases the granularity of routing decisions, ensuring that head-specific features are less likely to collide on the same expert update path, thereby mitigating gradient interference across tasks.

## Key Results
- On the TRACE benchmark with Qwen3-0.6B, MH-MoE reduces backward transfer (BWT) from -11.2% to -4.5% and improves overall performance (OP) from 0.378 to 0.467.
- The route-wise effective composition number (Neff) correlates positively with forgetting; MH-MoE achieves lower Neff than standard MoE and LoRAMoE in matched-capacity comparisons.
- Matched route-space and capacity ablations (Table 2) confirm MH-MoE’s superiority: with H=8, per-head K=4, top-1, it outperforms LoRAMoE (K=26, top-5) despite identical combinatorial route space and activated parameters.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Pre-routing concatenation causes feature composition collisions.** Multi-head attention produces head-specific feature signals (e.g., encoding domain, stance, position). Concatenating these into a single router input (`h(ℓ)_t`) creates a multiplexed representation where multiple decodable features coexist. Routing based on this mixed vector cannot preserve head-wise separation, leading to collisions where distinct feature compositions are mapped to the same expert. Evidence: [abstract] explicitly states this; [section 2.1] Fig. 1 shows multiple features decodable from the same `h(ℓ)_t`, and Fig. 2 shows non-uniform feature importance across heads.

**Mechanism 2: Mixed compositions induce gradient misalignment, increasing forgetting.** Tokens with different feature compositions generate gradient updates that are coherent within a composition but have near-zero cosine similarity across compositions. When a single expert route aggregates tokens with diverse compositions (high `Neff`), its parameter update direction cannot preserve loss for all, causing interference on old-task compositions. Theoretical bounds (Theorem 2.2) and empirical correlation (Fig. 5) link higher route-wise `Neff` to greater old-loss increase. Evidence: [section 2.2] Fig. 3 shows within-composition gradient similarity high and cross-composition near zero; [section 2.3] Theorem 2.2 and Fig. 5 establish the `Neff`–`ΔL_old` relationship.

**Mechanism 3: Head-wise routing reduces collisions by increasing granularity.** MH-MoE splits `h(ℓ)_t` into H head-aligned slices and routes each independently with private experts. This yields a tuple of route decisions, reducing the number of distinct compositions colliding on any single (head, expert) update destination. Lower route-wise `Neff` follows, mitigating gradient interference. Evidence: [abstract] describes the granularity increase; [section 5.3] Table 2 and Fig. 6a show MH-MoE achieves lower `Neff` and higher BWT than LoRAMoE under matched conditions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Post-attention token state `h(ℓ)_t` → Head splitter (partition into `H` sub-vectors `h(ℓ)_t,m`) → Head-private routers (compute logits `a(ℓ)_t,m`, select top-k experts `S(ℓ)_t,m`) → Head-private expert banks (`E_j^(m)`) → Aggregator (sum outputs: `y(ℓ)_t = Σ_m Σ_{j∈S} α E_j^(m)(h(ℓ)_t,m)`).

**Critical path**:
1. Take token representation after multi-head attention.
2. Split into `H` head-aligned sub-representations.
3. For each sub-representation, compute routing scores and select `k` experts from that head’s private bank.
4. Process each sub-representation through its selected experts.
5. Sum all head-specific expert outputs to produce final token representation.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Granularity vs. overhead: More heads (`H`) increases virtual routing paths (reducing `Neff`) but adds per-head router compute and dispatch overhead (~1% latency reported).
- Per-head capacity vs. collision: Smaller per-head expert bank (`K`) may force more collisions within a head; paper uses `K=4` per head with `top-1` while LoRAMoE uses `K=4` total with `top-1` (matched overall parameters).
- Memory vs. specialization: Head-private experts increase total expert parameters (by factor `H`) but maintain same activated parameters per token as baseline if `k` and `K` scaled appropriately.

**Failure signatures**:
- High route-wise `Neff` persisting after MH-MoE implementation indicates insufficient splitting or head signals not separable.
- Degraded overall performance (OP) drop compared to LoRAMoE suggests overhead or capacity issues.
- Router imbalance (uneven expert utilization across heads) may indicate poor head-wise specialization.

## Limitations
- Head separability assumption may not hold for all model architectures or training stages, especially if heads converge to similar functions.
- The `Neff`–forgetting link is strongly evidenced empirically but remains correlational; the proposed causal gradient alignment mechanism is paper-contained.
- Results are validated on a single continual learning benchmark (TRACE) with two model scales (0.6B/8B); generalization to other CL protocols or larger models is unknown.

## Confidence
- Claim: Pre-routing concatenation causes feature composition collisions. **Confidence: High** (supported by analysis figures and ablation).
- Claim: Route-wise `Neff` directly quantifies collision-induced forgetting risk. **Confidence: High** (empirical link in Fig. 5).
- Claim: Head-wise routing (MH-MoE) effectively reduces `Neff` and mitigates forgetting without task-specific mechanisms. **Confidence: High** for TRACE results, **Medium** for general claim.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate `Neff`–Forgetting Correlation on a different CL benchmark (e.g., LAMBADA or a permuted MNIST sequence) to test robustness of the correlation.
2. Probe Head Separability: After pre-training, freeze the attention layer and train linear probes on each head’s output to decode hypothesized features (domain, stance, frequency). Quantify probe accuracy and inter-head similarity to test the premise of separable head signals.
3. Capacity-Matched Ablation: Implement MH-MoE and a standard MoE with identical total expert capacity (`K_total`) and activation budget (`k`). Compare `Neff`, BWT, and OP across varying `H` (e.g., `H=4,8,16`) to isolate the effect of granularity from raw capacity.
