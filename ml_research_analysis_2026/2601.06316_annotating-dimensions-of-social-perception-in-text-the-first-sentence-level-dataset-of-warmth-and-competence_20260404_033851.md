# Annotating Dimensions of Social Perception in Text: The First Sentence-Level Dataset of Warmth and Competence

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06316
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06316
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Introduces W&C-Sent, the first sentence-level dataset for trust, sociability, and competence, revealing that even state-of-the-art LLMs and fine-tuned BERT struggle with this nuanced inference task.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Warmth and Competence Sentences (W&C-Sent), the first dataset annotating social perception dimensions—trust, sociability, and competence—at the sentence level. Addressing a gap in NLP resources beyond word-level lexicons, the dataset contains 1,633 sentence-target pairs from social media annotated on a 7-point scale. Results show moderate inter-annotator agreement and poor performance from both fine-tuned BERT and zero-shot GPT-4o, with competence being the most challenging dimension. The work demonstrates that inferring speaker-attributed social traits from text is a distinct and difficult task, requiring pragmatic reasoning beyond lexical sentiment or stance.

## Method Summary
The study constructs W&C-Sent by sourcing sentences from SemEval-2016 Stance and ABCDE corpora, filtering for human targets (or remapping abstract targets like "Atheism" to "nonreligious people"). Sentences are preprocessed (anonymization) and annotated by 4–7 Prolific workers per instance on 7-point scales for each dimension, with attention checks. Label aggregation uses median scores, and coarsening into low/neutral/high via majority vote. Models evaluated include a TF-IDF logistic regression baseline, fine-tuned BERTweet, and several LLMs (GPT-4o, Llama, etc.) in zero/few-shot settings. Reliability is measured via split-half reliability (SHR) and Krippendorff’s α.

## Key Results
- **Dataset:** 1,633 sentence-target pairs; 55.7% of labels are negative; competence shows more positive instances than trust/sociability.
- **Reliability:** Moderate agreement (SHR: trust 0.76, sociability 0.68, competence 0.56; Krippendorff’s α: trust 0.60, sociability 0.50, competence 0.30).
- **Model Performance:** Fine-grained accuracy peaks at 0.46 (BERT sociability); competence is hardest (BERT 0.36, GPT-4o 0.34). Coarse-grained accuracy improves (BERT trust 0.79).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Sentence-level annotation captures contextual semantics of social perception more effectively than word-level lexicons because meaning arises from syntax, compositionality, and pragmatic cues that transcend individual word senses.
- **Mechanism:** Annotators evaluate entire sentences with explicit targets, integrating negation, sarcasm, and implicature that alter trait expression. Divergent dimension scores (e.g., high distrust but moderate competence) are detectable only in context.
- **Core assumption:** Social perception dimensions are non-atomic; they are constructed from holistic linguistic interpretation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: Word-level resources "do not completely capture their contextual expression in larger text units."
  - Section 1: Expressions "shaped by syntax, compositional semantics, and pragmatic cues" cannot be fully captured at word level.
  - Corpus: Extends lexicon work (e.g., Mohammad 2025b) to contextual units.
- **Break condition:** If context does not override lexical semantics (e.g., in literal text), sentence-level advantage diminishes. Moderate agreement suggests context is partially recoverable but not universally.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Fine-grained 7-point annotation reveals nuanced distributions and cross-dimensional divergences (e.g., high competence paired with low trust) that coarse labels mask.
- **Mechanism:** The scale captures intensity gradations and asymmetries. Divergences (e.g., Table 8: distrust -3, unsociability -3, competence +2) show warmth and competence are correlated but not redundant.
- **Core assumption:** Human social judgments vary continuously and independently across dimensions, even if correlated.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Section 3.3: Negative scores prominent; competence and trust have counts at +2.
  - Section 5: Heat maps show greater dispersion in trust–competence and sociability–competence grids versus tight trust–sociability diagonal.
  - Section 4.2: Model performance drops more for competence (BERT 0.36) than trust (0.35).
- **Break condition:** Very low α for competence (0.30) may indicate noise outweighs signal at fine granularity.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Model performance limitations stem from the intrinsic difficulty of inferring speaker-attributed social traits, which requires pragmatic reasoning, target-centric evaluation, and separation of literal vs. ironic meaning.
- **Mechanism:** Annotation guidelines require evaluating *what the author expresses about the target*, not the target's actual traits or author's general sentiment. This deictic, pragmatic inference is hard for LLMs optimized for next-token prediction.
- **Core assumption:** Social perception in text is a distinct NLP task from sentiment or stance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Section 4.2: LLMs struggle; GPT-4o zero-shot max 0.44 accuracy (sociability); competence hardest (0.34).
  - Section 5: Stance correlates strongly (ρ up to 0.71), but models still falter, implying over-reliance on lexical stance.
  - Appendix O.1: System message instructs handling sarcasm/irony, yet performance remains low.
- **Break condition:** Models fine-tuned on this dataset with target-aware architectures may improve (BERT fine-tuned surpasses GPT-4o on some metrics).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Warmth and Competence as Stereotype Content Model (SCM) dimensions**
  - **Why needed:** The dataset operationalizes Fiske's SCM; warmth splits into trust (moral/personal) and sociability (relational/social). Misconflating these leads to incorrect feature design.
  - **Quick check:** For "She’s a snake in the grass," trust low (betrayal), sociability low (maliciousness), competence high (cunning/agency). Why? (See Example 2 rationale.)

- **Concept: Sentence-level contextual semantics vs. lexicon-based approaches**
  - **Why needed:** The core contribution is moving beyond word-level dictionaries. A word like "bitch" may affect sociability more than competence; negations flip meanings.
  - **Quick check:** Why might "leader" alone not indicate competence in "He’s a leader of the gang that terrorized the neighborhood"? (Context frames "leader" as negative agency; competence present but morally loaded.)

- **Concept: Annotation reliability metrics for subjective ordinal tasks**
  - **Why needed:** Reported metrics (SHR, Krippendorff’s α) are moderate, not high. Misinterpreting them overestimates dataset quality; α=0.30 for competence is "expected" for fine-grained subjective tasks.
  - **Quick check:** If split-half reliability is 0.76 for trust but Krippendorff’s α is 0.60, what does the discrepancy suggest? (SHR measures consistency of aggregated scores; α measures individual annotator agreement. Higher SHR indicates stable aggregate trends despite individual variation.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Raw sentences (SemEval-2016 Stance, ABCDE) -> Target alignment & preprocessing (anonymization, remapping) -> Annotation (Prolific, 4–7 annotators/instance, dimension-specific tasks) -> Quality control (attention checks, pre-annotated screening) -> Label aggregation (median/mean, coarsening) -> Train/dev/test split (GroupShuffleSplit on Text) -> Model evaluation (baselines, BERTweet fine-tuning, LLM prompting) -> Postprocessing (JSON parsing, label mapping).
- **Critical path:** Annotation must complete before aggregation; split must respect identical sentences across targets.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - 7-point vs. 3-point scale: Fine-grained reveals nuances but lowers agreement; coarsening improves model F1.
  - Dimension separation: Reduces cognitive load but forfeits cross-dimensional interactions.
  - Target selection: Human targets prioritized; abstract topics remapped introduces interpretation variance.
  - Annotator pool: Mostly Global North; cultural specificity not controlled.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Low IAA: Krippendorff’s α < 0.4 for competence across targets.
  - Model collapse: Few-shot prompting often worse than zero-shot.
  - Output bias: Qwen defaults to neutral (49.6% coarse); Gemma overproduces negatives (54.2% low).
  - Target effects: Lowest agreement on social groups (Women, Religious People) vs. individuals (Trump, Clinton).
  - Correlation mismatch: Human T–S correlation high (ρ=0.79), but models may not learn this.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Reproduce annotation reliability: Compute SHR (1,000 splits) and Krippendorff’s α from raw responses; trust SHR 0.76, sociability 0.68, competence 0.56; α trust 0.60, sociability 0.50, competence 0.30.
  2. Baseline sanity check: Train logistic regression with TF-IDF on median scores (regression) and coarse labels (classification); expect poor performance (e.g., competence regression ρ=0.35).
  3. Prompt validation: Replicate GPT-4o zero-shot on 100 test samples using Appendix O prompts; measure accuracy against test labels; expect ~0.4 fine-grained sociability, lower for competence. If deviation >0.1, inspect postprocessing.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why does competence exhibit significantly lower inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff's α=0.30) and model performance (BERT accuracy 0.36) compared to trust and sociability?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 3.2 reports α scores: trust 0.60, sociability 0.50, competence 0.30. Section 4.2 shows competence has the lowest model accuracy.
- **Why unresolved:** Paper documents disparities but does not investigate whether competence is inherently more subjective, lacks clear linguistic cues, or if annotation guidelines were insufficient.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Error analysis comparing human-model disagreements on competence instances; linguistic feature importance studies; revised guidelines with competence-specific examples tested for reliability.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent do the dataset's English-language, Western social media sources limit the generalizability of warmth/competence models to other languages and domains?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Limitations: dataset is English, annotators mostly from Global North, sentences from social media may not cover all socioeconomic contexts.
- **Why unresolved:** No cross-lingual or cross-domain evaluation; impact of cultural framing on social perception unmeasured.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Annotation and modeling in diverse languages/cultures; testing on non-social media corpora (news, literature); studies on cultural variability.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What specific contextual linguistic features (e.g., sarcasm, pragmatic cues) are most critical for accurately inferring social perception dimensions, and why do current LLMs fail to leverage them?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Appendix O prompts instruct LLMs to handle sarcasm/irony; pilot experiments showed misinterpretation. Performance remains low despite instructions.
- **Why unresolved:** No analysis of which features are most predictive or how LLMs process contextual cues; only overall poor performance noted.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Feature ablation studies; probing LLM attention patterns on high-disagreement instances; correlation analysis between linguistic cues and human judgments.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does coarsening fine-grained scores into 'low/neutral/high' categories improve model performance by reducing annotator noise, or does it lose important perceptual nuance?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Tables 3 and 4 show coarse-grained accuracy increases (e.g., BERT trust 0.79 vs. 0.35 fine-grained). Section 3.3 notes soft agreement rises with coarsening.
- **Why unresolved:** Paper presents both granularities but doesn't evaluate which better aligns with human cognition or downstream needs; no analysis of lost nuance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Human studies validating coarse vs. fine labels for real-world tasks; error analysis showing which fine-grained distinctions are consistently confused; application-specific evaluation.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How do target types (individual politicians vs. social groups) systematically bias annotation reliability and model predictions?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section J.1.2: Krippendorff's α higher for Trump/Clinton (trust ~0.6) than for groups (e.g., Women α<0.41). Figure 8 shows SHR highest for Trump.
- **Why unresolved:** Paper notes pattern but doesn't hypothesize causes—target familiarity, stereotype consistency, or textual differences.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled annotation experiments with balanced target familiarity; analysis of linguistic markers per target type; modeling with target embeddings to isolate target effects.

## Limitations
- **Reliability Concerns:** Moderate inter-annotator agreement, especially for competence (Krippendorff’s α=0.30), raises label quality issues.
- **Cultural & Linguistic Scope:** Annotators mostly from Global North; dataset is English and social media-based, limiting cross-cultural and cross-domain generalizability.
- **Task Definition Ambiguity:** Target remapping (e.g., "Atheism" → "nonreligious people") introduces interpretation variance; distinguishing author-expressed traits from sentiment or stance remains challenging.

## Confidence
- **Sentence-level contextual capture:** Medium. Evidence from example divergences is strong, but moderate IAA suggests context is not consistently recoverable.
- **Fine-grained scale utility:** Low-Medium. Nuanced distributions observed, but very low α for competence indicates possible noise outweighing signal.
- **Inherent model limitation:** High. Consistent poor performance across LLMs and BERT, even with sarcasm-aware prompts, supports task difficulty.
