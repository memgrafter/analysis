# AgentSocialBench: Evaluating Privacy Risks in Human-Centered Agentic Social Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.01487
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01487
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Cross-domain coordination creates the strongest leakage pressure (2–3× higher than other dyadic interactions), multi-party dynamics vary by topology, and incremental privacy instructions induce an "abstraction paradox" where leakage decreases in cross-domain yet increases in mediated and cross-user settings.

## Executive Summary
AgentSocialBench introduces a systematic benchmark for evaluating privacy risks in human-centered agentic social networks, where personalized LLM agent teams coordinate across domains and users on behalf of individuals. The benchmark comprises 352 scenarios spanning seven dyadic and multi-party categories, grounded in synthetic user profiles with hierarchical sensitivity labels and directed social graphs. Evaluation of eight LLM backbones reveals that cross-domain coordination exerts the strongest leakage pressure, with rates 2–3× higher than mediated or cross-user interactions. Multi-party dynamics yield varied outcomes: group chat leakage matches dyadic mediation, competitive settings reduce self-leakage, and affinity-modulated scenarios achieve near-zero violations. While prompt-based privacy instructions (explicit rules plus abstraction templates) improve information abstraction, they trigger an "abstraction paradox"—leakage decreases in cross-domain but increases in mediated and cross-user settings because teaching agents how to discuss sensitive topics leads them to reference such topics more often. Full defenses reduce explicit violations but partial leakage persists, and task completion remains stable, indicating prompt-based interventions alone are insufficient.

## Method Summary
The benchmark constructs synthetic user profiles across six domains (Health, Finance, Social, Schedule, Professional, Lifestyle), each annotated with sensitivity scores (1–5) and detailed attributes. Scenarios are generated by combining profiles, coordination tasks, privacy boundaries (must-not-cross items and acceptable abstractions), and—for multi-party cases—a directed social graph with affinity tiers (close, friend, acquaintance, stranger). Agent teams consist of domain-specialized LLM agents, each holding a profile slice. Privacy instructions are injected at three levels: L0 (none), L1 (explicit rules), L2 (full defense: L1 plus domain boundary protocols, abstraction templates, and minimal information principle). Interactions are simulated via a turn-based engine for dyadic categories and a graph-driven engine for multi-party categories, respecting message visibility rules. An independent LLM judge classifies leakage (none/partial/full), computes Information Abstraction Score (IAS), Task Completion Quality (TCQ), and annotates behavioral patterns. Metrics are aggregated across eight LLM backbones, with statistical significance assessed via bootstrap confidence intervals and paired tests.

## Key Results
- Cross-domain coordination yields leakage rates 2–3× higher than mediated communication or cross-user interactions.
- Multi-party leakage varies: group chat matches dyadic mediation, competition reduces self-leakage, affinity modulation yields near-zero violations.
- Privacy instructions cause an abstraction paradox: IAS improves but leakage increases in mediated and cross-user settings, while cross-domain leakage decreases.
- Full defenses reduce explicit violations but implicit leakage remains; task completion stays stable across privacy conditions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Cross-domain coordination creates the strongest intrinsic privacy leakage pressure.** When specialized agents (e.g., health, finance) must exchange information to accomplish a task, domain-specific sensitive data spills across boundaries because agents lack robust mechanisms to abstract or withhold information while maintaining task utility. The coordination requirement overrides default privacy preservation. Evidence: Cross-domain leakage (CDLR) is consistently double to triple that of mediated communication and cross-user categories across models. Break condition: If agents are equipped with architectural information-flow controls that enforce strict domain isolation regardless of task pressure, or if tasks avoid requiring sensitive information exchange across domains.

**Mechanism 2: Multi-party social dynamics reshape privacy risks according to interaction topology.** The structure (broadcast vs. private channels, coordination, competition, asymmetric affinity tiers) changes incentives and opportunities for leakage. Group chat creates pressure comparable to dyadic mediation; competition suppresses self-disclosure; affinity tiers enable precise sharing control leading to near-perfect compliance. Evidence: Section 4.2.2 shows group chat leakage similar to mediation, competitive self-leakage substantially lower, and affinity compliance near-perfect. Break condition: If all interactions are strictly mediated through a privacy-preserving coordinator or affinity tiers are uniform, eliminating variation.

**Mechanism 3: Privacy instructions inducing an "abstraction paradox".** Providing abstraction templates (e.g., "has some health considerations") gives agents sanctioned language to reference sensitive topics. In contexts where the natural baseline is complete non-disclosure (e.g., mediated communication), this creates new partial disclosure channels that judges detect as leakage, even though full leakage decreases. Evidence: At L0 an agent might remain silent; at L2 it says "has some health considerations," which the judge classifies as partial leakage. Break condition: If agents naturally adopt a "silent unless necessary" default without templates, or evaluation treats all abstractions as non-leakage regardless of inferability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Contextual Integrity (Nissenbaum, 2004)**: The benchmark operationalizes privacy violations as breaches of context-specific norms (domain, user, mediation, affinity boundaries) rather than mere data exposure. Quick check: How does the benchmark translate the abstract principle of "norms governing information flow" into concrete must-not-cross lists and acceptable abstractions per scenario?
- **Hierarchical Sensitivity Labeling**: Sensitivity scores (1–5) per attribute enable fine-grained leakage assessment and drive the cross-domain leakage pressure analysis. Quick check: Why use a 5-point scale, and how does the threshold (τ=3) interact with the abstraction paradox in different categories?
- **Directed Social Graphs with Affinity Tiers**: Asymmetric edges with {close, friend, acquaintance, stranger} tiers modulate per-recipient sharing rules, creating the combinatorial leakage surface in multi-party scenarios. Quick check: In affinity-modulated scenarios, how do the monotonically restrictive tier rules affect the coordinator's information aggregation versus group chat broadcast decisions?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Profile Generator → Scenario Builder → Agent Teams (domain-specialized, holding profile slices) → Privacy Instruction Injector → Simulation Engines (turn-based for dyadic, graph-driven for multi-party) → Evaluator LLM.

**Critical path:** 
1. Generate synthetic user profiles with domain attributes and sensitivity scores.
2. For each scenario, define task, privacy boundaries, and social graph (if multi-party).
3. Instantiate agents with domain roles, profile slices, and privacy instruction level.
4. Run interaction simulation respecting visibility rules (private/group).
5. Evaluate with LLM judge to score leakage, abstraction, and task quality.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Synthetic profiles: controllable vs. lack real-world noise and authentic norms.
- LLM-as-judge: scalable vs. unvalidated against human judgments.
- Prompt-based defenses: easy to test vs. proven insufficient (abstraction paradox, implicit leakage).
- Category coverage: captures key dynamics vs. limited depth per category (e.g., only 28 affinity-modulated scenarios).

**Failure signatures:**
- High CDLR (>0.5) indicates domain boundary crossing failures.
- IAS improves L0→L2 but LR increases in MC/CU → abstraction paradox.
- Implicit disclosure remains high under L2 across categories.
- Affinity Compliance Score near 1.0 only in AM category; other multi-party show variance.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Baseline leakage comparison (RQ1): Run all models at L0 across dyadic categories to confirm cross-domain pressure (CDLR significantly higher).
2. Multi-party structure analysis (RQ2): Compare leakage, IAS, TCQ across GC, HS, CM, AM at L0 to identify topology-specific patterns (competition lowers self-leakage, affinity yields high compliance).
3. Defense escalation test (RQ3): For each model, compare L0 vs. L1 vs. L2 on leakage and IAS per category to isolate abstraction paradox (LR up in MC/CU despite IAS up).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Architectural modifications for provable privacy**: What architectural changes to LLM agent frameworks can provide provable privacy preservation beyond prompt engineering? Basis: Future work calls for information-flow control, learned privacy policies, or formal verification.
2. **Real-world deployment evaluation**: How do privacy risks manifest in real-world deployments with actual user data and diverse privacy norms? Basis: Need ecological validity that synthetic benchmarks cannot provide.
3. **Longitudinal, cross-session privacy**: What are the privacy risks associated with long-term interactions where agents accumulate memories? Basis: Single-session evaluation misses aggregation risks across sessions.
4. **Human vs. LLM judge alignment**: How do human judgments of privacy violations align with LLM-as-judge evaluations? Basis: Human-in-the-loop evaluation needed to strengthen construct validity and reveal divergence cases.

## Limitations
- Synthetic profiles lack real-world complexity and authentic privacy norms.
- LLM-as-judge scoring not validated against human judgments; potential bias.
- Defenses limited to prompt engineering; no architectural privacy mechanisms evaluated.
- Scenario counts uneven (e.g., only 28 affinity-modulated), limiting statistical power.

## Confidence
- Cross-domain leakage pressure: High
- Multi-party dynamics: Medium
- Abstraction paradox: Medium-Low

## Next Checks
1. Human audit of 50–100 judge decisions to measure inter-rater agreement and refine the evaluation rubric.
2. Implement and benchmark architectural privacy controls (e.g., taint tracking, policy guards) to test if cross-domain leakage can be eliminated.
3. Augment profile generation with real user behavior priors and cross-cultural privacy expectations to evaluate generalizability.
