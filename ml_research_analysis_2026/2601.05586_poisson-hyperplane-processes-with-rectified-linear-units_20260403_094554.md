# Poisson Hyperplane Processes with Rectified Linear Units

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.05586
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05586
- Reference count: 12
- Primary result: Two-layer ReLU neural networks with Gaussian priors are equivalent to Poisson hyperplane processes, enabling scalable Bayesian inference via decomposition and annealed SMC, achieving lower test RMSE and better calibrated uncertainty than baseline methods.

## Executive Summary
The paper establishes an equivalence between two-layer ReLU neural networks with Gaussian weight priors and Poisson hyperplane processes (PHP). Leveraging this connection, the authors derive three decomposition propositions that allow splitting the PHP into independent sub-processes, drastically reducing computational cost. Bayesian inference is performed via an annealed sequential Monte Carlo algorithm. Experiments on synthetic and real datasets show that the proposed 'pois' method outperforms classical ReLU nets, SVMs, random forests, decision trees, and linear models in test RMSE, and provides well-calibrated 95% credible intervals with shorter lengths than XGBoost and standard neural networks. Decomposition reduces runtime to 4.7–13.4% of the original non-decomposed PHP inference, with domain-based splitting being most efficient.

## Method Summary
The model is a two-layer network: f(x) = w0 + Σ_{j=1}^{|P|} w_j · ReLU(⟨x, n_j⟩ - μ_j) + ε, where hyperplanes (n_j, μ_j) follow a Poisson process on the product of the unit sphere and positive reals with intensity λ, weights w_j ~ N(μ0, σ0^2), noise ε ~ N(0, σ^2). Priors: σ^2 ~ Inverse-Gamma(a0, b0). The equivalence is proven by showing that the ReLU network with random hyperplanes and Gaussian weights yields a piecewise linear function whose distribution matches that of a PHP. For scalable inference, three decomposition propositions are presented: (1) splitting the intensity measure into independent PHPs; (2) restricting the process to disjoint subdomains; (3) combining both. Posterior approximation uses annealed SMC: intermediate distributions π_r(θ) ∝ p(y|θ)^{φ_r} p(θ) with φ_0=0, φ_R=1. At each annealing step, particles are moved by a Metropolis-Hastings update that either proposes a new hyperplane (μ uniform on [0,1], n uniform on sphere) or perturbs existing ones, then reweighted and resampled. Decomposition splits the full problem into smaller independent SMC runs, with predictions aggregated by summing submodel outputs.

## Key Results
- The 'pois' method achieves lower test RMSE than classic ReLU nets, SVMs, random forests, decision trees, and linear models on synthetic and real datasets (abalone, wine).
- Predictive uncertainty is well-calibrated: 95% credible intervals achieve coverage near nominal with shorter interval lengths than XGBoost and standard neural networks.
- Decomposition reduces computational time to 4.7–13.4% of the original non-decomposed PHP inference.
- Domain-based decomposition (splitting input space) is more efficient than splitting hyperplanes into groups.
- Real data experiments confirm improved performance over standard ReLU networks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The equivalence stems from the fact that a Poisson hyperplane process generates random affine half-spaces; applying ReLU to the signed distance to these hyperplanes yields a piecewise linear function whose random coefficients (weights) are Gaussian. This matches the distribution of a two-layer ReLU network with random hyperplanes (i.e., random first-layer weights and biases) and Gaussian second-layer weights. Decomposition works because Poisson processes are independently additive over disjoint intensity measure partitions and spatial domains; thus the full process can be split into independent sub-processes, each with its own SMC inference, and results combined without loss. Annealed SMC mitigates the multimodality of the posterior by gradually increasing the likelihood power, allowing particles to explore the space more effectively.

## Foundational Learning
- Poisson point processes: random sets of points in a space; essential for modeling the hyperplane locations. Why needed: The PHP provides the probabilistic structure for the first-layer weights/biases. Quick check: Verify that the intensity measure λ integrates to the expected number of hyperplanes.
- ReLU activation: rectifies linear projections, creating piecewise linear functions. Why needed: It maps the hyperplane output to a nonnegative basis function. Quick check: Confirm that ReLU(x) = max(0, x) is used consistently.
- Bayesian inference: placing priors on weights and noise variance. Why needed: Enables uncertainty quantification and posterior predictive distributions. Quick check: Ensure priors are proper (inverse-gamma for variance, Gaussian for weights).
- Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC): a particle filtering method for approximating complex posteriors. Why needed: Handles the high-dimensional and multimodal posterior of the PHP model. Quick check: Check that annealing steps reduce weight degeneracy.
- Metropolis-Hastings (MH): MCMC kernel for moving particles. Why needed: Proposes new hyperplanes or perturbs existing ones. Quick check: Verify acceptance ratio includes likelihood and prior.
- Decomposition principles: splitting intensity or domain yields independent processes. Why needed: Reduces computational cost by parallelizing inference. Quick check: Confirm that aggregated predictions equal full-model predictions on a small test set.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Data (x,y) -> Domain split / Intensity split -> Independent PHPs (submodels) -> Annealed SMC per submodel (particles, MH proposals, resampling) -> Aggregated posterior predictive -> Global prediction.

Critical path: 1. Define full PHP model with intensity λ, priors. 2. Apply decomposition (choose decomp1 or decomp2). 3. For each submodel, run annealed SMC: initialize particles, iterate over annealing steps with MH updates, resample. 4. After all submodels complete, combine their posterior predictive distributions (sum of submodel outputs plus bias w0 pooled? The paper suggests w0 is shared; need to verify from equations). 5. Evaluate on test set.

Design tradeoffs: 
- Decomposition scheme: Splitting hyperplanes (decomp1) may lead to imbalance if some sub-PHPs have many hyperplanes; domain-based splitting (decomp2) is more efficient but requires careful handling of boundaries and bias aggregation.
- Number of particles L and annealing steps R: Larger L improves accuracy but increases cost; more annealing steps improve mixing but add overhead. The paper uses L=1000, R=100 for main results.
- MH proposal scale: Must be tuned to achieve reasonable acceptance rates (~0.234 for high-dimensional problems). Too large leads to low acceptance, too small leads to slow exploration.

Failure signatures:
- High variance in predictions across runs: indicates insufficient particles or poor annealing schedule.
- Poor coverage (e.g., 95% CI far from nominal): likely misspecified priors or likelihood, or SMC degeneracy.
- Decomposition yields inconsistent aggregates: mismatch in bias handling or subdomain classification errors; check that each data point belongs to exactly one subdomain and that w0 is either included in all submodels or correctly pooled.

First experiments:
1. Run the provided simulation script for the simplest case (p=2, |P|=2) with default L=1000, R=100, and verify that test RMSE ~0.10.
2. Compare decomposed vs. full model on a small synthetic dataset: predictions should be nearly identical (difference < 1e-3).
3. Tune MH proposal scale (e.g., adjust variance of μ and n proposals) to achieve acceptance rate around 0.2-0.3; monitor ESS.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Treating the number of hyperplanes as an unknown parameter with a prior and using reversible jump MCMC for inference.
- Adapting the PHP framework for online or streaming data, requiring modifications to the SMC algorithm.
- Extending the theoretical equivalence to deeper neural networks or alternative activation functions.

## Limitations
- Prior hyperparameters (μ0, σ0², a0, b0) and intensity measure details are not fully reported in the paper; they must be extracted from code.
- Annealing schedule (φ_r) and exact particle counts per experiment are incompletely described, hampering exact replication.
- The domain decomposition rule for p>1 is vague, and the handling of the bias term across submodels is ambiguous (Eq.4 vs Eq.5).
- Empirical claims depend on unreported tuning choices; confidence in results is limited without full specification.
