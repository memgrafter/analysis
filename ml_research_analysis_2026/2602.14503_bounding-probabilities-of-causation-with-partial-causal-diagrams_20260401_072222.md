# Bounding Probabilities of Causation with Partial Causal Diagrams

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14503  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14503  
- **Reference count:** 8  
- **Primary result:** Tighter valid bounds on probabilities of causation by encoding partial causal information (e.g., non‑descendant covariates, backdoor sets, mediators) as constraints in an optimization program, without requiring full identification.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of bounding probabilities of causation (PoC) when causal knowledge is incomplete. Existing methods either ignore covariates, require fully specified causal graphs, or are limited to binary variables. The proposed framework represents partial causal information—such as the presence of non‑descendant covariates, valid backdoor sets, or mediators—as mathematical constraints on the space of admissible counterfactual distributions. These constraints are combined with an optimization that minimizes or maximizes the target PoC. Theoretical results extend to multivalued treatments and outcomes. Empirical simulations across 1000 random distributions show consistent improvements: average lower‑bound increases of 0.02–0.04 and narrower bound gaps compared to Tian–Pearl and Mueller–Li–Pearl baselines, with gains growing as more partial information is incorporated. The approach unifies covariate and mediator information, offering a practical tool for individualized causal reasoning under fragmentary knowledge.

## Method Summary
The method begins with a partial causal diagram (graph) and available probability distributions—which may include observational data, interventional data, or covariate‑specific conditionals (e.g., \(P(Y^x \mid Z_i)\)). Based on the diagram, sets such as non‑descendants of treatment \(X\), backdoor adjusters, or mediators are identified. Each structural assumption translates into constraints on the joint counterfactual distribution over \(Y^x\) and relevant covariates. For non‑descendants, these constraints are linear equations linking counterfactual probabilities to observed ones (e.g., \(P(Y^x, Z) = P(Y \mid X, Z)P(Z)\)). For mediators, conditional independences yield bilinear constraints. The target PoC (e.g., probability of necessary/sufficient causation) becomes the objective of an optimization problem: minimize and maximize it subject to all constraints, plus normalization and non‑negativity. When only covariate‑specific marginals are available, the optimization variables span a joint distribution that must satisfy all marginal constraints simultaneously, tightening bounds beyond applying any single covariate alone. The framework thus yields valid bounds even without full graph specification or joint covariate data.

## Key Results
- Average lower‑bound improves by 0.02–0.04 over Tian–Pearl and Mueller–Li–Pearl baselines.
- Bound gaps (upper minus lower) are consistently reduced; gains increase with the number of covariates or when mediator information is included.
- The unification of covariate and mediator constraints yields tighter bounds than using either type of information in isolation.
- Theoretical bounds are derived for multivalued treatments and outcomes, extending beyond binary cases addressed in prior work.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1**  
*Claim*: Partial causal knowledge imposes linear constraints on admissible counterfactual distributions, which when added to an optimization program yield tighter valid bounds without full identification.  
*Mechanism*: A partial diagram (e.g., \(Z\) is a non‑descendant of \(X\)) implies that certain counterfactual distributions are identifiable from observational data (e.g., \(P(Y^x \mid Z) = P(Y \mid X, Z)\)). These identifications become linear equations linking optimization variables (joint counterfactual probabilities) to observed probabilities. Conditional independences (e.g., \(Y^x \perp\!\!\!\perp X \mid Z\)) also translate into linear constraints. The optimization then searches over a smaller feasible set.  
*Evidence*: [section 3.1] Theorem 3.1 and Equations 8–9 show how non‑descendant covariates generate constraints; [abstract] “encode partial causal information … as constraints”.

**Mechanism 2**  
*Claim*: Even when only marginal covariate‑specific distributions are available (not joint), aggregating them within a unified joint counterfactual optimization yields bounds tighter than applying each covariate separately.  
*Mechanism*: The optimization variables represent the full joint distribution over all counterfactual outcomes and covariates. For each covariate \(Z_i\), constraints force the marginal over \((Y^x, Z_i)\) to equal the given covariate‑specific distribution. The feasible set is the intersection of all such marginal constraints, which is strictly smaller than the feasible set from any single covariate, thereby tightening bounds.  
*Evidence*: [section 4.1] Table 1 and Figures 2–7 show improvement over baselines; [abstract] “incorporates covariate information modularly without requiring joint covariate distributions”.

**Mechanism 3**  
*Claim*: Including a mediator variable and encoding the conditional independences it induces as nonlinear (bilinear) constraints further tightens bounds beyond using only backdoor covariates.  
*Mechanism*: If \(W\) mediates \(X \rightarrow Y\) and \(Z\) is a backdoor set, the structure implies \(Y^x \perp\!\!\!\perp X \mid W^x, Z\) and \(Y^x \perp\!\!\!\perp W^{x'} \mid W^x, Z\). These independences become bilinear equations (e.g., Theorem 3.3 Equations 14–15) within the joint counterfactual distribution. Adding these nonlinear constraints shrinks the feasible set more than linear constraints alone.  
*Evidence*: [section 4.2] Table 2 and Figures 8–9 demonstrate smaller gaps; [abstract] “The framework unifies covariate and mediator information”.

## Foundational Learning
- **Structural Causal Models (SCM) and counterfactual semantics**  
  *Why needed*: PoC are defined via counterfactual events (e.g., \(Y_x\)); the optimization operates directly over the joint distribution of such counterfactuals, relying on the structural semantics.  
  *Quick check*: What distinguishes the observational conditional \(P(Y \mid X)\) from the interventional \(P(Y \mid do(X))\) in an SCM?

- **Causal diagrams and d‑separation**  
  *Why needed*: The partial diagram is the source of structural assumptions; identifying non‑descendants, backdoor sets, and mediators depends on d‑separation and graph surgery.  
  *Quick check*: Given a diagram, how would you verify that a set \(Z\) satisfies the backdoor criterion relative to \((X,Y)\)? What if \(Z\) contains a descendant of \(X\)?

- **Linear programming and constraint‑based optimization**  
  *Why needed*: The bounding procedure reduces to an optimization program (linear for non‑descendant covariates; nonconvex with mediators). Formulating and solving it correctly requires understanding constraints, objectives, and feasibility.  
  *Quick check*: In Theorem 3.1’s linear program, what are the decision variables, and what role do Equations 8 and 9 play?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Input parser -> Constraint generator -> Optimization solver -> Bounds extractor  
Feasibility checker (parallel validation)

Critical path:  
1. Identify relevant sets (non‑descendants, backdoor sets, mediators) from the partial diagram.  
2. Determine which distributions are actually available (e.g., \(P(Y^x \mid Z_i)\), \(P(Y \mid do(X))\)).  
3. Assemble all constraints (linear for non‑descendants/backdoor; bilinear for mediators) plus normalization.  
4. Encode the target PoC (e.g., \(PNS(k)\)) as the objective function.  
5. Solve minimization and maximization problems.  
6. Validate that bounds lie in \([0,1]\) and are no wider than trivial bounds.

Design tradeoffs:  
- **Tightness vs. computational cost**: More constraints (more covariates, mediators) tighten bounds but increase problem size and may introduce nonlinearities, making solving slower and less reliable.  
- **Data availability vs. strength**: Joint covariate distributions yield tighter bounds than covariate‑specific marginals, but joint data are often unavailable. The framework accommodates both, but the engineer must choose the strongest constraints supported by the data.  
- **Solver choice**: Linear programs are polynomial‑time and give global optima; nonconvex programs from mediators may require heuristics, multi‑start strategies, or relaxations, trading tightness for reliability.

Failure signatures:  
- **Infeasibility**: Optimization reports no solution. Likely causes: incorrect diagram (e.g., assumed non‑descendant that is actually a descendant), incompatible covariate‑specific marginals (different populations), or mis‑specified constraints.  
- **Bounds wider than Tian–Pearl**: Indicates constraints not applied correctly or partial information too weak; check that all relevant identifiability conditions are encoded.  
- **Solver warnings (numerical instability, failure to converge)**: Common with nonlinear mediator constraints; may indicate poor scaling or nonconvexity causing local optima. Consider global solver or tighter tolerances.  
- **Bounds outside \([0,1]\)**: Usually a bug in constraint formulation or objective indexing.

First 3 experiments:  
1. **Baseline reproduction**: Implement Tian–Pearl bounds (Eqs. 4–7) for binary \(X,Y\) with no additional constraints. Verify that the optimization with no extra constraints reproduces these bounds.  
2. **Single non‑descendant covariate**: Use the setting of Mueller–Li–Pearl with full joint covariate data. Compare bounds from Theorem 3.1 against published MLP bounds.  
3. **Multiple covariates (covariate‑specific only)**: Generate 100 random distributions for the graph in Figure 1a with two covariates. Implement Corollary 3.2 and check that the average lower‑bound increase matches Table 1 (improvement over TP).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Linear encoding of mediator constraints?**  
  How can mediator constraints be expressed as linear constraints to avoid nonconvex optimization while preserving most of the tightening effect? (Section 6 notes this as future work.)

- **Effect of multiple mediators**  
  How does incorporating multiple mediators affect bound tightness and computational complexity? The paper restricts simulations to a single mediator, leaving extension open.

- **Generalization to other causal structures**  
  Can the optimization framework systematically incorporate other common structures (e.g., instrumental variables, front‑door criteria, m‑bias) as constraints on counterfactual distributions? The current theorems focus on non‑descendants and backdoor‑mediator patterns.

- **Sensitivity to diagram misspecification**  
  How robust are the bounds to errors in the provided partial causal diagram (e.g., false non‑descendant assumptions, omitted confounders)? The framework assumes the diagram is correct; robustness is unaddressed.

## Limitations
- Bounds rely on the correctness and compatibility of the supplied partial causal diagram and data; misspecification or incompatible covariate‑specific marginals can lead to infeasibility or invalid bounds.  
- Nonlinear mediator constraints introduce nonconvexity, raising numerical challenges and potential suboptimality for large state spaces.  
- Empirical validation is limited to simulations; real‑world applicability and scalability remain untested.

## Confidence
- *Partial knowledge → tighter bounds*: High  
- *Aggregating covariate‑specific data reduces gaps*: High  
- *Mediator constraints tighten bounds*: Medium (nonconvex solving may compromise practical tightness)  
- *Unified framework utility*: Medium (simulations promising but real‑data performance unknown)
