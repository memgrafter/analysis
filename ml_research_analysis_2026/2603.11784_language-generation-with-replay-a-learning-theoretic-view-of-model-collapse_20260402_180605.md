# Language Generation with Replay: A Learning-Theoretic View of Model Collapse

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11784
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11784
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** Training on model outputs (replay) does not harm uniform generation, creates a separation for non-uniform generation, allows countable in-limit generation with membership queries, but breaks proper generation even for finite classes.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates "model collapse" through a learning-theoretic framework called **language generation with replay**. A generator must produce fresh, valid examples from a hidden target language while an adversary mixes in the generator's own past outputs. The study categorizes generation by strength (uniform, non-uniform, in-the-limit, proper) and asks: when does replay make generation impossible? Key findings: uniform generation remains feasible with a simple adaptation; non-uniform generation suffers a strict separation for some countable classes; in-limit generation succeeds for countable classes using membership queries but fails for an uncountable class; proper generation fails under replay even for finite classes. The results provide theoretical grounding for empirical heuristics like data cleaning and watermarking, while highlighting their limits.

## Method Summary
The core method formalizes a repeated game between a generator and an adaptive **replay adversary**. The adversary selects a hidden target hypothesis $h^\star$ from a class $\mathcal{H}$ (satisfying Uniformly Unbounded Support). In each round $t$, the adversary presents an example $x_t$ that is either a fresh example from $\text{supp}(h^\star)$ or a replay of a previous generator output $o_s$ ($s < t$). The generator must output $o_t \in X$. Success is defined by four **generatability notions** requiring eventual (or uniformly bounded) output of fresh valid examples, with proper generation additionally requiring $\hat{h}_t \in \mathcal{H}$ and $\text{supp}(\hat{h}_t) \subseteq \text{supp}(h^\star)$. The analysis combines constructive algorithms (e.g., burn‑in adaptation, Witness Protection) with hard class constructions to prove separations. Membership query access to hypotheses is sometimes assumed.

## Key Results
- Uniform generation: Replay does not increase sample complexity; a generator can be converted via a burn‑in phase to succeed (Theorem 3.1).
- Non‑uniform generation: Replay creates a separation; there exists a countable hypothesis class generatable without replay but not with (Theorem 4.1).
- Generation in the limit: Every countable class is generatable with replay using membership queries (Theorem 5.1), but an uncountable class exists where replay causes failure (Theorem 5.6).
- Proper generation in the limit: Fails under replay even for finite classes (Theorem 6.3); also requires more than membership queries without replay (Theorem 6.1).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Burn-in Stabilization in Uniform Generation
Claim: Replay does not harm uniform generation; adapting a generator with a burn‑in phase preserves sample complexity.  
Mechanism: The adapted generator repeats the first example until it observes $d^\star$ distinct inputs. These repetitions are identical and thus cannot be counted as distinct. Once $d^\star$ distinct examples (all fresh, since replays matching previous outputs are detectable) are collected, the original uniform generator’s guarantees apply.  
Break condition: If the generator cannot reliably distinguish replays from fresh examples, the distinctness threshold may never be reached, dooming the burn‑in.

### Mechanism 2: Adversarial Distinctness Exhaustion in Non‑Uniform Generation
Claim: Replay creates a separation; a carefully constructed countable class is generatable without replay but not with.  
Mechanism: The adversary forces the generator to produce infinitely many fresh elements by always replaying the latest output. For the hard class (combining a hypothesis with finite support $[d]$ and countably many with co‑finite support), any generator with finite sample complexity for some hypothesis must eventually output only from $[d]$. But the adversary can ensure the input stream contains infinitely many elements outside $[d]$, forcing the generator to produce fresh numbers forever, leading to contradiction.  
Break condition: If the generator limits its output space (e.g., caps distinct outputs), the adversary cannot force unbounded distinctness; however, the UUS assumption typically forbids such limits.

### Mechanism 3: Witness Protection for In‑Limit Generation of Countable Classes
Claim: Every countable class remains generatable in the limit under replay using membership queries.  
Mechanism: The algorithm maintains a set of "sure" examples (non‑replays) and a dynamic set of "active witnesses" — elements that distinguish competing hypotheses. It never outputs a witness. Instead, it selects an output from the minimal indexed consistent hypothesis that avoids the sure set, past outputs, and witnesses. Eventually, a single hypothesis becomes uniquely consistent, and its support contains infinitely many elements not yet output, ensuring valid fresh outputs forever.  
Break condition: Fails for uncountable classes (Theorem 5.6) or without membership queries; witness set size may grow large computationally.

## Foundational Learning
- **Generatability notions**: Uniform (fixed sample complexity), Non‑uniform (per‑hypothesis), In‑the‑limit (eventual success). *Why needed*: They precisely define the strength of generation guarantees and determine replay’s impact. *Quick check*: What is the key difference between uniform and non‑uniform generatability regarding the allowed dependence of $t^\star$?
- **Replay adversary**: An adversary that may inject any past generator output into the input stream. *Why needed*: It abstracts the core phenomenon of model collapse where training data is contaminated by model outputs. *Quick check*: How does the replay adversary differ from standard label noise, and why is it considered stronger?
- **Closure dimension $\mathcal{C}(H)$**: Size of smallest set $C$ such that every $h \in \mathcal{H}$ is the intersection of $C$ with some $S \subseteq X$. *Why needed*: It characterizes uniform/non‑uniform generatability without replay and provides a baseline. *Quick check*: What is $\mathcal{C}(H)$ for a finite class, and why does that imply uniform generatability?
- **Uniformly Unbounded Support (UUS)**: Every $h \in \mathcal{H}$ has infinite support. *Why needed*: Ensures fresh examples always exist; avoids degenerate finite‑support cases. *Quick check*: Why would finite supports break the main positive results?
- **Membership query oracle**: Ability to ask $h(x)$ for any $h \in \mathcal{H}, x \in X$. *Why needed*: Enables the Witness Protection algorithm to reason about hypothesis supports without relying on examples. *Quick check*: Could the algorithms be implemented without membership queries, and what would be lost?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Input classifier (detects replay) -> Distinctness buffer (uniform) / Version space manager (non-uniform, in-limit) -> Output controller -> Output recorded. Membership query oracle feeds version space manager when available.

Critical path:
1. Receive $x_t$; classify as sure (not in $O_{t-1}$) or replay.
2. Update sure set $S_t$ and version space $V_t$ (hypotheses consistent with $S_t$).
3. Uniform: if $|S_t| < d^\star$ output first example; else call original uniform generator.
4. In‑limit countable: compute active witnesses $W^{(t,m)}$; pick output from minimal indexed $h \in V_t$ avoiding $S_t \cup O_{t-1} \cup W^{(t,m)}$.
5. Record $o_t$; increment $t$.

Design tradeoffs:
- Burn‑in: sacrifices early output diversity (repeating first example) to guarantee later correctness; cost is a fixed stall until $d^\star$ distinct sure examples appear.
- Witness avoidance: restricts output pool to non‑witness elements; may reduce coverage but prevents contaminating distinguishing information.
- Membership query reliance: provides strong theoretical guarantees but may be unrealistic in practice; approximation (e.g., model confidence) could weaken guarantees.

Failure signatures:
- Uniform: distinctness counter stalls indefinitely → likely misclassifying replays as fresh.
- Non‑uniform: version space grows without bound or outputs repeatedly become replay later → adversary exhausted distinctness.
- In‑limit: active witnesses become empty yet outputs still invalid → witness set logic flawed or replay misdetection.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Uniform conversion test**: Implement Algorithm 1 for a finite class (e.g., all intervals of length ≤ 5). Compare sample complexity of base uniform generator vs. converted generator under an adaptive replay adversary; expect no increase.
2. **Witness Protection on countable class**: Implement Algorithm 2 for $\mathcal{H}$ = all finite languages over $\mathbb{N}$. Simulate replay adversary (inject past outputs with probability $p$); measure convergence time and witness set size.
3. **Proper generation impossibility**: Instantiate the 4‑hypothesis class from Theorem 6.3. Attempt any proper generator (deterministic or randomized) under worst‑case replay; verify eventual output of a hypothesis with support not subset of target’s.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Complete characterization of non‑uniform generatability under replay**: Existing work characterizes non‑uniform generation without replay via closure dimension; no analogous condition is known with replay.
- **Stochastic replay adversary for proper generation**: Theorem 6.3 rules out deterministic proper generation for finite classes; a stochastic model (e.g., adversary replays a random past output) might evade the impossibility.
- **Fundamental limits of proper generation**: Proper generation lacks a systematic complexity‑theoretic treatment; open questions include necessary and sufficient conditions, role of query access, and computational hardness.
- **Replay‑robustness via structural properties**: Can combinatorial properties of $\mathcal{H}$ (e.g., bounded closure dimension) guarantee that replay does not degrade generatability, avoiding explicit filtering?
- **Breadth of generated outputs**: Replay might force a generator into a narrow subset of the language even when generation is feasible; defining and maintaining breadth under replay is open.

## Limitations
- Idealized assumptions: Uniformly Unbounded Support (UUS), perfect membership‑query oracles, and an omniscient adversarial replay are rarely met in practice.
- Asymptotic guarantees: Results do not provide finite‑time bounds or rates of convergence, limiting practical predictive power.
- Uncountable and stochastic settings: The uncountable‑class separation uses a specific construction; generalizability is unknown. Stochastic replay adversaries are not analyzed.
- Continuous domains: Framework assumes discrete $X$; applicability to LLMs and continuous data is unclear.
- Proper generation: Impossibility is proven for deterministic generators; stochastic variants remain unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Uniform replay benignity** (Theorem 3.1): High – constructive conversion matches non‑replay sample complexity.
- **Non‑uniform replay separation** (Theorem 4.1): High – explicit countable class where replay blocks generatability.
- **In‑limit countable with queries** (Theorem 5.1): High – Witness Protection algorithm guarantees eventual success.
- **In‑limit uncountable failure** (Theorem 5.6): High – specific uncountable class construction.
- **Proper in‑limit failure** (Theorem 6.3): High – four‑hypothesis impossibility.
- **Alignment with empirical heuristics** (e.g., watermarking): Medium – theoretical consistency but limited direct empirical validation.
