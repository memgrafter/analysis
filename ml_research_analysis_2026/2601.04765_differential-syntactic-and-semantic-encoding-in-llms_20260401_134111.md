# Differential syntactic and semantic encoding in LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04765  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04765  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Syntactic and semantic information are partially linearly encoded in LLMs, with syntax stable across layers and semantics peaking centrally; semantic representations rely on syntactic scaffolding, but not vice versa.

## Executive Summary
This paper examines how large language models separately encode syntactic and semantic information. Using DeepSeek-V3 as a primary case study, the authors construct *syntactic centroids* by averaging representations of sentences sharing the same part-of-speech sequence (syntax twins) and *semantic centroids* by averaging representations of translations or paraphrases of the same sentence. They then ablate (subtract) the projection of a sentence vector onto these centroids and measure the resulting change in similarity between matched pairs via a neighborhood rank-based metric. The analysis is performed across all layers and extended to Qwen2-7B and Gemma3-12B.

The findings reveal that both syntactic and semantic components are at least partially linearly accessible. Cross-layer profiles differ: syntactic similarity remains constant from input to output, while semantic similarity rises to a peak in middle layers and often declines slightly thereafter. Moreover, the two signals are asymmetrically decoupled: removing semantic information has little effect on syntactic similarity, but stripping syntactic structure significantly impairs semantic similarity. This suggests that syntax may serve as a scaffold for meaning representations. The results generalize across model scales and provide a geometric lens for interpreting LLM internals.

## Method Summary
The core method represents each sentence by a vector from the residual stream of a transformer at a specific layer, aggregated over the last *N* tokens (concatenation for syntax, averaging for semantics). Syntactic centroids are built per POS template by averaging vectors of syntax twins; semantic centroids are built per sentence by averaging vectors of its six translations. For a sentence vector **X**, ablation **X**⊥**C** = **X** − (**X**·**C**/|**C**|²)**C** removes the component along centroid **C**, isolating the orthogonal aspect. Similarity between matched sets (e.g., originals and twins) is measured using a rank-based neighborhood overlap metric that is robust to anisotropy. Baseline similarity is compared to similarity after ablating the relevant centroid, as well as control ablations (random centroids). Additional analyses decompose each vector into fractions aligned with syntactic and semantic centroids and train linear probes for POS-template classification and paraphrase recall. The pipeline is applied to DeepSeek-V3, Qwen2-7B, and Gemma3-12B.

## Key Results
- Both syntactic and semantic information are at least partially linearly encoded in LLMs, as ablating the relevant centroid strongly reduces similarity between matched pairs.
- Cross-layer encoding profiles differ: syntactic similarity remains stable across layers, while semantic similarity peaks in central layers.
- Decoupling is asymmetric: removing syntactic centroids significantly impairs semantic similarity, whereas removing semantic centroids leaves syntactic similarity largely intact; these patterns generalize across model sizes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Syntactic and semantic information are at least partially encoded through linearly accessible directions (centroids) in the representation space.
- **Mechanism:** For a given sentence vector **X**, a "syntactic centroid" **S** is constructed by averaging representations of other sentences sharing the same POS template (syntax twins). A "semantic centroid" **T** is constructed by averaging representations of translations/paraphrases. Subtracting the projection of **X** onto **S** (**X** ⊥ **S**) ablates syntactic information, drastically reducing similarity between syntax twins. The same holds for **T** and semantic pairs.
- **Core assumption:** Sentences sharing a superficial property (POS template or meaning) will have a statistically significant overlap in their high-dimensional neural activations along a specific direction, which can be captured by averaging.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...by averaging hidden-representation vectors of sentences sharing syntactic structure or meaning, we obtain vectors that capture a significant proportion... subtracting these... centroids... strongly affects their similarity..., suggesting that syntax and semantics are, at least partially, linearly encoded."
  - [section 2.1 & 3.1/3.2] Detailed construction of centroids and ablation effects shown in Figures 1 & 2. The ablation (green lines) causes a significant drop in matched-pair similarity compared to baseline (blue lines) or random ablation (red lines).
  - [corpus] Weak direct anchor. Neighbors like Caucheteux et al. (2021) use similar averaging for syntax, and Park et al. (2024) discuss linear representation hypotheses. The specific *ablation via projection* method is a core contribution here, not strongly mirrored in neighbors.
- **Break condition:** If the centroid vectors **S** or **T** are poorly estimated (e.g., too few twins/translations), or if the information is encoded in a highly nonlinear or distributed manner not captured by a mean direction, the ablation effect will be weak or nonexistent.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The cross-layer encoding profiles of syntax and semantics are distinct; syntax is salient across many layers, while semantics peaks in central layers.
- **Mechanism:** By computing matched-pair similarity (neighborhood rank) at every layer, syntax similarity (between syntax twins) remains relatively constant from input to output. Semantic similarity (between paraphrases/translations) is low in early layers, rises to a peak in middle layers, and often drops slightly in final layers.
- **Core assumption:** The network processes syntactic features (surface form, POS sequence) rapidly in shallower layers, while deeper, central layers integrate information to form a more abstract, meaning-based representation less tied to lexical/syntactic variation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.1/3.2] "syntactic similarity remains stable across layers" vs. "semantic similarity peaks in central layers." This is directly plotted in Figures 1b and 2b (averaged token case).
  - [abstract] "the cross-layer encoding profiles of syntax and semantics are different."
  - [section 1.1] Related work consensus on a "classic linguistic pipeline" where deep layers perform syntactic analysis followed by semantic processing, which this finding refines (syntax is stable, semantics peaks).
- **Break condition:** If the network architecture or training objective forces all processing to be equally distributed (e.g., extreme shallow processing), the distinct profiles would disappear. The profile might also differ for significantly longer sentences or different model scales.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Syntax and semantics can be partially decoupled, with an asymmetry: removing semantic information minimally impacts syntactic similarity, while removing syntactic information significantly reduces semantic similarity.
- **Mechanism:** Performing the *semantic ablation* (subtracting **T**) on syntax-twin pairs causes only a small drop in their similarity (Figure 3). Conversely, performing the *syntax ablation* (subtracting **S**) on paraphrase pairs causes a significant drop in their semantic similarity (Figure 4). This suggests semantic representations rely partly on a syntactic scaffolding, but syntactic representations can exist more independently of full semantic content.
- **Core assumption:** The "syntactic centroid" **S** captures a component that is necessary (though not sufficient) for building the semantic representation, while the "semantic centroid" **T** captures a component orthogonal enough to the syntactic skeleton that ablating it doesn't destroy the syntactic pattern.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3] "Remarkably, subtracting... **T**... largely preserves syntax similarity... instead... removing... **S**... decreases significantly." Figure 4 shows a clear gap between syntax-ablated (green) and random-ablated (red) semantic similarity.
  - [abstract] "the two signals can to some extent be decoupled... it seems easier to independently access syntactic information than the opposite."
  - [section 3.4] Decomposition shows syntax component larger in early layers, semantics in central layers, supporting a temporal/causal dependency.
- **Break condition:** If syntax and semantics are completely entangled (e.g., in a model trained on extremely correlated data), both cross-ablations would cause similar, large drops. The asymmetry might vanish for models with different inductive biases or training regimes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Linear Algebra & Vector Space Geometry**  
  - **Why needed here:** The core methodology relies on representing sentences as vectors, computing projections (**X**·**S**/|**S**|²), subtracting them, measuring L2 distances, and decomposing norms. Understanding subspaces, orthogonality, and norm decomposition is essential.  
  - **Quick check question:** Given a sentence vector **X** and a centroid **S**, what does the vector **X** ⊥ **S** = **X** - (**X**·**S**/|**S**|²)**S** represent geometrically, and why would it remove information correlated with **S**?

- **Concept: Information Theory & Similarity Metrics**  
  - **Why needed here:** The paper uses a *neighborhood rank-based similarity* (Eq. 2), not cosine similarity or CKA. This metric is invariant to global rotations/scalings and measures how well local neighborhoods are preserved. Understanding why this is preferred for high-dimensional, anisotropic LLM representations is key.  
  - **Quick check question:** Why might standard CKA or cosine similarity provide a "very weak signal" (as cited) in these settings, while a measure based on nearest-neighbor ranks is more sensitive?

- **Concept: Transformer/LLM Anatomy**  
  - **Why needed here:** The analysis is performed on *residual stream* activations at each *layer*. One must know that each layer processes the sequence, that representations evolve, and that "early" vs. "central" vs. "late" layers have hypothesized functional roles (feature extraction, integration, output shaping).  
  - **Quick check question:** If syntactic similarity is stable across layers but semantic similarity peaks centrally, what does this imply about the transformation happening in the central layers compared to the early and late layers?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
The component map flows as follows: a sentence is tokenized and embedded, then processed through N layers of a decoder-only transformer (e.g., DeepSeek-V3), with the residual stream at each layer **l** containing a sentence-level vector **X**^l (obtained by concatenating or averaging token vectors). The model outputs next-token logits. For analysis, the following components are added: (1) Centroid constructors build syntactic centroids **S**^l_i by averaging vectors of syntax twins (same POS template) and semantic centroids **T**^l_i by averaging translations of each sentence; (2) Ablation module removes the projection of **X**^l_i onto **S**^l_i or **T**^l_i, yielding **X**^l_i ⊥ **S**^l_i or **X**^l_i ⊥ **T**^l_i; (3) Similarity calculator computes neighborhood rank-based similarity between two sets of vectors; (4) Probe trainers train linear classifiers for POS template classification and evaluate paraphrase recall.

### Critical path
1. **Data prep:** Generate/curate three non-overlapping sets: Original sentences {**X**}, Syntax twins {**s**}, Translations/Paraphrases {**P**, **t**}. Ensure no leakage (e.g., **X** not in **S**^l_i construction).
2. **Forward pass:** Run all sets through the model, cache residual stream activations at every layer for the whole-sentence vector (via chosen aggregation: concatenation of last N tokens or mean pooling).
3. **Centroid calculation:** For each **X**^l_i, find all sentences in the *other* sets sharing its POS template (for **S**^l_i) or its meaning (for **T**^l_i). Average their vectors. *Crucially, do not include **X**^l_i itself.*
4. **Ablation:** For each **X**^l_i (and its pair, e.g., **s**^l_i or **P**^l_i), compute **X**^l_i ⊥ **C**^l_i where **C** is **S** or **T**.
5. **Similarity measurement:** For baseline and ablated sets, compute symmetric nearest-neighbor rank similarity (Eq. 2). Plot vs. layer depth.
6. **Validation:** Run control ablations (random centroids) and probes.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Token aggregation:** Concatenation preserves positional info (better for syntax), averaging destroys it (better for semantics). Choice must align with hypothesis.
- **Centroid robustness:** Using more twins/translations yields a more stable **S**/**T**, but is costly. Paper finds 4-6 languages sufficient for **T**.
- **Similarity metric:** Rank-based metrics are robust to anisotropy but computationally heavier (O(N²)) than cosine.
- **Ablation form:** Subtracting the *projection* (Eq. 1) is crucial. Direct centroid subtraction introduces spurious correlations (Appendix D).

### Failure signatures
- **Poor ablation effect:** Ablated similarity curve (green) nearly overlaps with baseline (blue) → centroids not capturing the intended component. Causes: too few neighbors, poor POS template definition, model not encoding the property linearly.
- **High residual norm:** In vector decomposition (Fig. 5), if "residual" fraction is very high (>80%), centroids are poor proxies. May indicate nonlinear encoding or that "syntax/semantics" are not monosemous directions.
- **Inconsistent cross-model behavior:** If smaller models (Qwen2-7b, Gemma3-12b) show wildly different layer profiles (e.g., no semantic peak), the effect may be scale-dependent or model-architecture-specific.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Reproduce syntax similarity baseline:** For DeepSeek-V3, compute similarity between **X** (original) and **s** (syntax twins) using concatenated last 6 tokens. Expect ~0.7+ similarity stable across layers (Fig. 1a). Validate with random-pair control (similarity ~0, Fig. 7).
2. **Validate ablation procedure:** Apply syntax ablation (**X** ⊥ **S**) to the syntax-twin pairs. Expect a large drop in similarity (Fig. 1a, green line). Also run "mismatched centroid" ablation (use **S** from a different POS template) as a control—effect should be smaller, especially in early/late layers.
3. **Check cross-ablation asymmetry:** Compute semantic similarity (paraphrases, averaged tokens) with and without syntax ablation (**X** ⊥ **S**). Expect a significant but incomplete drop (Fig. 4, green line). Then compute syntax similarity with semantic ablation (**X** ⊥ **T**). Expect minimal effect (Fig. 3, green line ~baseline). Quantify the asymmetry gap.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does combining centroid ablation with spectral/time-scale decomposition (à la Tamkin et al., 2020) reveal that syntactic and semantic information are encoded in distinct frequency bands of LLM activations?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in the Conclusion: "In future research, we want to combine our method to decouple syntax and semantics with a decomposition of neural activity into different time scales along the lines of Tamkin et al. (2020), under the hypothesis that syntax and semantics are predominantly encoded in high- and low-frequency bands, respectively."
- **Why unresolved:** The current study isolates syntax/semantics geometrically but does not analyze the temporal/spectral structure of the residual signals. The hypothesis links their finding that syntax is better captured by concatenated (high-frequency) tokens and semantics by averaged (low-frequency) tokens to a Fourier-type decomposition.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply a spectral analysis (e.g., Fourier transform or wavelet decomposition) to token-wise activations before and after centroid ablation. Test whether ablating syntax centroids disproportionately reduces high-frequency power, while ablating semantics centroids reduces low-frequency power, across layers.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What comprises the large residual component (~40-50% of norm) that remains orthogonal to both syntactic and semantic centroids? Is it purely lexical/register information, model-specific artifacts, or a distinct linguistic dimension?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In Section 3.4 (Decomposition), the authors report: "a big fraction of it is still not explained by them. We leave it to further research to determine whether this residual is genuinely orthogonal to syntax and semantics (e.g., including strictly lexical information) or, more plausibly, if it points to the fact that our syntactic and semantic centroids are only partial proxies..."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates centroids capture a significant but incomplete portion of the norm. The nature of the residual is critical for understanding the completeness of the linear superposition model for linguistic information.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Construct targeted probes (e.g., for lexical semantics, pragmatic register, topic) on the residual vectors (Xi minus projections onto Si and Ti). Alternatively, test if the residual contains information predictive of stylistic features (formality, genre) not controlled for in the synthetic datasets.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why is there an asymmetry in decoupling: removing syntactic centroids significantly harms semantic similarity, but removing semantic centroids barely affects syntactic similarity?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] This asymmetry is a core result discussed in Sections 3.3 and 4. The authors note: "Removing semantic information... will not affect their syntax-based similarity... On the other hand, when stripping syntax, we might remove information... that might affect semantic similarity." But the precise causal or representational reason is not explained.
- **Why unresolved:** The finding suggests syntax may be more "foundational" or entangled in the construction of meaning representations, but the mechanistic reason within the transformer architecture is unknown. Is it due to syntax scaffolding semantic composition in middle layers? Or because syntactic features are more broadly distributed?
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform layer-wise ablation studies combined with causal mediation analysis to trace how syntactic features influence the formation of semantic neighborhoods. Alternatively, train models with synthetically corrupted syntax (e.g., shuffled word orders) and measure the impact on semantic centroid efficacy.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Do the observed decoupling patterns and centroid-based linear encoding hold for longer, more syntactically complex sentences and for a wider diversity of languages (especially typologically divergent ones)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In the Limitations section: "We should moreover explore what happens when focusing on languages different from English... we constrained our datasets to be of the order of about 2,000 samples and, maybe more importantly, each sentence to be of at most 10 words in length." Also: "Future work should systematically ascertain the extent to which the results depend on model size..."
- **Why unresolved:** Current datasets are short, English-centric, and generated by LLMs, which may introduce biases. Longer sentences with embedded clauses could test the scalability of the centroid method. Cross-linguistic variation (e.g., free word order, morphology-rich languages) could challenge the POS-template approach and the universality of the syntax/semantics split.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the study with longer naturalistic sentences (e.g., from parsed corpora) and extend the syntactic matching to include deeper structural equivalence (e.g., dependency trees) beyond POS sequences. Test the centroid method on multilingual models using non-English-centric POS tagsets and translation datasets covering diverse language families.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can the syntactic and semantic centroids be used for targeted intervention in generation (e.g., controlling for syntax while preserving semantics, or vice versa), and what are the effects on model outputs and safety?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In the Limitations: "We did not perform interventional experiments during text generation. We expect our syntax and semantic centroids to be useful to steer the model away or towards specific meanings or syntactic structures, for example in relation to spurious correlations between syntax and semantics (Shaib et al., 2025), but we leave this application to future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The geometric decoupling suggests steering is possible, but it is unknown whether subtracting centroid projections from hidden states at a specific layer will produce the desired controlled outputs without degradation. This has implications for interpretability, controllable generation, and mitigating spurious correlations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement activation steering (e.g., as in Jørgensen et al., 2023) using the learned centroids. For a given input, subtract the syntax centroid to vary syntax while holding semantics constant (measured by paraphrase similarity), and vice versa. Evaluate the fluency, semantic preservation, and syntactic correctness of the generated continuations.

## Limitations
- Reliance on specific token aggregation (last N tokens) may inadvertently mix syntactic and semantic signals.
- Centroids are oversimplified as single mean directions; the true encoding could be nonlinear or distributed.
- Dataset biases (POS‑template frequencies, synthetic generation) and the lack of a public dataset hinder full replication; the observed asymmetry might be sensitive to the exact ablation formulation.

## Confidence
- Partial linear encoding: High  
- Distinct cross-layer profiles: High  
- Asymmetric decoupling: Medium  
- Generalization to other models: Medium  

## Next Checks
1. *Centroid orthogonalization:* Compute the angle between syntactic and semantic centroids across layers and models; high collinearity would undermine the decoupling interpretation.
2. *Token‑aggregation robustness:* Rerun core analyses with alternative aggregation (mean‑pool all tokens, first/last token only) to confirm that the layer profiles and asymmetry are not artifacts of the “last 6/3 tokens” choice.
3. *Cross‑lingual invariance:* Test whether semantic centroids derived from translations are consistent across diverse language families; strong invariance would support the claim that the semantic component is language‑agnostic.
