# Dynamic Intelligence Ceilings: Measuring Long-Horizon Limits of Planning and Creativity in Artificial Systems
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.06102
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06102
- **Reference count**: 13
- **Primary result**: Introduces the Dynamic Intelligence Ceiling (DIC) framework with Progressive Difficulty Ceiling (PDC) and Ceiling Drift Rate (CDR) metrics to distinguish static from dynamic long-horizon intelligence limits in planning and creativity tasks.

## Executive Summary
Traditional static AI evaluations fail to capture whether a system's performance frontier stabilizes prematurely or continues expanding over developmental time. This paper defines the Dynamic Intelligence Ceiling (DIC) as a trajectory-dependent bound on reliable problem-solving under resource constraints. It operationalizes DIC through two metrics: the Progressive Difficulty Ceiling (PDC), which identifies the hardest reliably solvable problem at a given phase, and the Ceiling Drift Rate (CDR), which measures how this frontier shifts over time. These are evaluated in a procedurally generated benchmark, Workshop World, which combines long-horizon planning with structural creativity demands. Results show a key qualitative distinction: some systems exhibit static ceilings (deepening but repetitive solutions), while others show positive CDR coupled with sustained novelty, indicating genuine frontier expansion. The framework reframes intelligence limits as dynamic rather than static.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses a procedurally generated benchmark called **Workshop World**. Each problem instance is defined by a difficulty vector `δ = (H, K, C, A)`, where `H` is the minimum planning horizon, `K` are goal constraints, `C` is the available module set, and `A` controls hidden synergistic interactions. A fixed action budget `B` is enforced. Systems are assessed across multiple developmental phases (`t₁...tₙ`), such as training checkpoints. For each phase and difficulty level, `N` independent instances are generated; the system attempts each once. Success is binary: constructing an artefact satisfying all `K` within `B`. From these trials, the **Progressive Difficulty Ceiling (PDC)** is computed as `bCPDC(t) = sup{δ : Sₚ(δ,t) ≥ τ}` where `Sₚ` is the success rate and `τ` (typically 0.7) is the reliability threshold. The **Ceiling Drift Rate (CDR)** is the slope of `bCPDC` across phases. Concurrently, **structural novelty** `S꜀` is calculated for successful solutions by comparing their signatures (module multisets + action skeletons) against a baseline set `B` of all earlier solutions. The joint trajectory of PDC and `S꜀` classifies ceiling dynamics: positive CDR with sustained novelty indicates dynamic frontier expansion; positive CDR with collapsing novelty suggests hidden convergence.

## Key Results
- The Progressive Difficulty Ceiling (PDC) provides a single scalar bound on reliably solvable difficulty at a given developmental phase.
- The Ceiling Drift Rate (CDR) quantifies temporal shifts in this frontier, distinguishing static ceilings (CDR ≈ 0) from expanding ones (CDR > 0).
- Sustained structural novelty alongside positive CDR is necessary to confirm genuine frontier growth; otherwise, gains may reflect overfitting to a fixed solution pattern.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Progressive Difficulty Ceiling (PDC).** PDC defines the maximal difficulty an AI can reliably solve by thresholding success rates across a parametrically controlled difficulty spectrum. For a phase `t`, `bCPDC(t)` is the supremum of `δ` values where the success rate `Sₚ(δ,t) ≥ τ` (e.g., 0.7). This compresses a distribution of binary outcomes into a frontier position. It relies on a procedural generator that yields monotonically degrading performance with increasing `δ` and a threshold `τ` that separates reliable solving from chance. If success rates never meet `τ`, PDC is undefined, indicating a collapsed frontier or an overly stringent threshold.

**Mechanism 2: Ceiling Drift Rate (CDR).** CDR captures the temporal evolution of the PDC frontier. It is computed as the slope `(bCPDC(tₙ) - bCPDC(t₁)) / (tₙ - t₁)` across specified developmental phases. A positive slope means the system's solvable difficulty range is increasing; a near-zero slope indicates a static ceiling. This assumes phases are comparable under identical resource budgets and fixed instance distributions, so observed shifts reflect internal system changes rather than external variance. Non-monotonic or volatile PDC values can destabilize CDR, requiring smoothing or more phases.

**Mechanism 3: Novelty-Coupled Frontier Diagnosis.** To distinguish true expansion from mere optimization within a fixed solution manifold, the framework couples CDR with mean structural novelty `S꜀`. `S꜀` measures how dissimilar new successful solutions are from all previously seen ones, using a signature based on module multisets and action skeletons. Positive CDR combined with `S꜀` above a baseline indicates the system is discovering new solution structures. If novelty collapses to near zero while CDR is positive, the progress is likely illusory—the system is converging to a repetitive pattern rather than expanding its frontier.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Trajectory-Centric Evaluation**  
Why needed: Static snapshots cannot reveal whether a system's performance frontier stabilizes prematurely; evaluating across multiple developmental phases is essential to detect drift.

**Concept: Procedural Difficulty Parameterization**  
Why needed: Workshop World uses a difficulty vector `(H, K, C, A)` to create a continuum of challenges, enabling measurement of PDC as a function of `δ`. Without this control, the frontier would be ill-defined.

**Concept: Competence-Novelty Dichotomy**  
Why needed: To avoid conflating efficiency gains with genuine intelligence growth, the framework couples success rate (competence) with structural novelty (creativity). Their joint trajectory reveals whether the ceiling is dynamic.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Instance Generator -> Evaluator -> Novelty Calculator -> PDC Estimator -> CDR Calculator -> Visualizer

**Critical path**:  
1. Define phase schedule (`t₁...tₙ`) and difficulty grid.  
2. For each phase, deploy the agent with fixed resources; for each `δ`, generate `N` instances.  
3. Execute agent's single attempt per instance, recording success and plan.  
4. For successes, compute structural signatures and update baseline `B` incrementally.  
5. Compute `Sₚ(δ,t)` and derive `bCPDC(t)`.  
6. Over all phases, compute CDR and correlate novelty with PDC shifts.  
7. Interpret results to classify ceiling type (static vs dynamic).

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Threshold `τ`: higher values (e.g., 0.9) yield conservative PDCs but increase variance; `τ=0.7` balances stability.  
- Difficulty granularity: too coarse misses small drifts; too fine increases instance count needs.  
- Novelty similarity metric: choice of representation (e.g., multiset Jaccard vs. edit distance) affects sensitivity to structural change.  
- Baseline set `B`: must be initialized with early-phase solutions; its composition influences novelty scores; seeding from multiple agents may reduce bias.

**Failure signatures**:  
- PDC saturates early at low difficulty with no drift → static ceiling.  
- High variance in `Sₚ` across `N` for a given `δ` suggests insufficient generation or agent instability.  
- Novelty scores near zero for all phases indicate an overly strict metric or truly homogeneous solutions.  
- CDR fluctuates strongly without trend → need more phases or larger `N`; possibly non-meaningful agent phases.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Baseline replication**: Run a simple heuristic agent across three phases; expect flat PDC and declining novelty → confirms static ceiling signature.  
2. **Novelty injection**: Equip an agent with an intrinsic reward for structural diversity; anticipate positive CDR and sustained novelty → demonstrates dynamic frontier.  
3. **Ablation of ambiguity**: Set `A=0` (no hidden synergies) while keeping other parameters; compare to `A>0`. Hypothesis: removing ambiguity leads to faster saturation (lower CDR) as planning becomes deterministic, reducing exploration need.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: How does the DIC framework generalize to embodied or open-world environments where action spaces and state dynamics are significantly richer than Workshop World?  
*Basis*: Explicitly noted in Section 7.4 as a future direction.  
*Why unresolved*: The current benchmark is simplified and diagnostic, focusing on controlled planning and creativity; it lacks embodied interaction, continuous control, or naturalistic ambiguity.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Empirical studies instantiating PDC and CDR in established embodied benchmarks (e.g., robotics simulations, 3D worlds) with parametrically controllable difficulty, checking if the static/dynamic ceiling distinction persists.

**Open Question 2**: What specific algorithmic or architectural mechanisms enable systems to sustain positive CDR while preserving novelty, as opposed to converging?  
*Basis*: Inferred from Sections 6.1–6.2 and Figure 2; the framework evaluates outcomes but not internal causes.  
*Why unresolved*: The study avoids algorithmic claims, treating system states as black boxes; it does not correlate PDC/CDR with design choices like exploration mechanisms or representation plasticity.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Controlled experiments comparing known variations (e.g., fixed vs. adaptable objectives, static vs. plastic architectures) while measuring PDC/CDR trajectories and analyzing internal representations (e.g., policy entropy, solution manifold clustering).

**Open Question 3**: Does DIC remain valid for collective or multi-agent systems where intelligence is distributed?  
*Basis*: Section 7.5 suggests the metrics could apply to multi-agent coordination but without validation.  
*Why unresolved*: Metrics are defined for a single agent with unified intent and resources; multi-agent settings complicate frontier definition, progress attribution, and group-level novelty measurement.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Adapt Workshop World to multi-agent scenarios with separate/shared goals, compute group-level PDC/CDR, and analyze if positive CDR correlates with adaptive roles/communication or introduces confounders like free-riding.

## Limitations
- The procedural generator's construction method is unspecified; it must guarantee a minimal solution horizon `H` and properly conceal hidden synergies `A`.
- The structural novelty metric's exact implementation (weights, normalization, similarity function) is underspecified, risking arbitrary or trivial results.
- Key hyperparameters (`N`, `B`, base recipes) and the mapping from training to developmental phases `t` are not provided, hindering replication.

## Confidence
- Dynamic ceilings concept (PDC/CDR as trajectory-dependent): **High**
- PDC and CDR definitions (assuming correct generator, sampling, phases): **Medium**
- Novelty metric's adequacy for frontier expansion claims: **Low**

## Next Checks
1. **Generator verification**: Implement the instance generator; for small `H`, exhaustively search to confirm minimal solution length equals `H` and hidden parameters are concealed from the agent.
2. **Similarity calibration**: Define a concrete similarity metric (e.g., weighted Jaccard + edit distance); correlate scores with human judgments on a curated solution set.
3. **Robustness trials**: Vary `N`, `B`, and phase schedule; assess stability of PDC and CDR estimates to propose reliable defaults.
