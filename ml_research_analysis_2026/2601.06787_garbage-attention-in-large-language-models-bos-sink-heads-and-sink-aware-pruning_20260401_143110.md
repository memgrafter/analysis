# Garbage Attention in Large Language Models: BOS Sink Heads and Sink-aware Pruning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.06787
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06787
- **Reference count**: 35
- **Primary result**: BOS sink-based pruning (e.g., 12.5% head removal) retains near-dense performance (accuracy within 0.007 of baseline) and outperforms Mag‑SP and Wanda‑SP.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses structural redundancy in transformer‑based large language models (LLMs) by identifying attention heads that disproportionately attend to the beginning‑of‑sequence (BOS) token. These “BOS sink heads” act as dumping grounds for superfluous attention weights, particularly in deeper layers where representations have stabilized. The authors propose using the BOS sink score—a simple, length‑invariant metric measuring average attention to the BOS token—to guide structured pruning of individual heads or entire layers. Evaluations on Gemma‑3, Llama‑3.1, and Qwen3 show that this method preserves model accuracy far better than weight‑ or activation‑based baselines, even under aggressive pruning ratios (e.g., 12.5% head pruning). The findings offer a robust, interpretable foundation for compressing LLMs with minimal performance loss.

## Method Summary
The method starts by collecting attention weights from a calibration dataset (e.g., MMLU) while the model runs in evaluation mode. For each attention head in each layer, the BOS sink score is computed as the average attention weight assigned to the BOS token across all query positions and over all calibration samples. Heads (or layers) are then ranked by this score; the highest‑scoring heads are considered redundant. Pruning is performed by zeroing out the columns of the output projection matrix (WO) corresponding to the selected head, or by removing entire higher‑layer transformer blocks. The first and last layers are always preserved to maintain input and output stability. No fine‑tuning is applied after pruning. This lightweight procedure requires only a single forward pass and leverages the stability of BOS sink scores across sequence lengths.

## Key Results
- BOS sink heads are predominantly located in deeper layers and exhibit high functional redundancy.
- Pruning 12.5% of heads with the highest BOS sink scores reduces average accuracy by only 0.007 points relative to the dense baseline on Gemma‑3, Llama‑3.1, and Qwen3.
- The BOS sink metric is length‑invariant and significantly outperforms magnitude‑based (Mag‑SP) and activation‑based (Wanda‑SP) pruning methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: BOS Token as a Dumping Ground**  
- **Claim**: Attention heads with high BOS sink scores are functionally redundant because they act as dumping grounds for superfluous attention weights.  
- **Mechanism**: The softmax operation forces the model to distribute all attention weights across tokens, even when some are semantically irrelevant. The BOS token—a constant non‑semantic anchor—becomes a target for excess attention. Heads that specialize in this pattern contribute little to semantic transformation and instead absorb unnecessary weights, preserving residual stream stability.  
- **Core assumption**: The BOS token provides an invariant numerical outlet that does not disrupt downstream processing.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: “heads with high <BOS> sink scores … effectively serve as dumping grounds for superfluous attention weights”; Section 5: “By anchoring to this constant feature, these higher‑layer heads maintain a nearly invariant contribution to the residual stream”; Corpus: Neighbor papers (e.g., “Attention Sink Forges Native MoE…”) similarly identify sink heads as redundant repositories.  
- **Break condition**: If the BOS token is omitted or its embedding changes (e.g., in models without explicit BOS), these heads may become necessary; also, in architectures that do not develop strong sink patterns, the correlation may weaken.

**Mechanism 2: Redundancy Concentrates in Deeper Layers**  
- **Claim**: Redundancy concentrates in deeper layers because representations stabilize, making additional attention heads unnecessary and repurposed as sinks.  
- **Mechanism**: Early layers build contextual representations; deeper layers primarily refine and stabilize them. Once a semantic consensus is reached, many deep‑layer heads no longer need to process content and instead default to the BOS sink to satisfy softmax constraints, leading to high BOS sink scores and minimal performance impact when pruned.  
- **Core assumption**: Core semantic extraction is largely complete by mid‑layers; deeper layers serve mainly as consistency checks.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: “BOS sink scores … concentrate in higher layers”; Section 4: “In the final blocks … surge in average <BOS> scores directly correlates with a high degree of mean functional redundancy per head”; Corpus: Layer pruning studies (e.g., ShortGPT) find deeper layers more sensitive to removal, supporting layered redundancy.  
- **Break condition**: For complex reasoning tasks (e.g., theorem proving), deeper layers may retain critical functionality and not exhibit high sink scores.

**Mechanism 3: BOS Sink Scores Offer a Robust Metric**  
- **Claim**: BOS sink scores provide a robust, length‑invariant metric for identifying redundant heads, outperforming weight‑ or activation‑based criteria.  
- **Mechanism**: Sink scores measure a head’s average attention to the BOS token across sequences. High‑scoring heads show low variance across sequence lengths, making them stable identifiers. In contrast, magnitude or activation metrics fluctuate with input distribution, leading to less reliable pruning decisions.  
- **Core assumption**: Attention patterns are inherent structural traits of pretrained heads rather than dynamic content‑driven responses.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: “BOS sink scores … provide a robust … basis for model compression” and “stable across sequence lengths”; Section 8: Figure 4 shows high‑µ heads have gradual slope with T; Figure 5 shows low CV for high‑µ heads, mostly in deeper layers; Corpus: Sink‑aware pruning in other modalities (e.g., diffusion language models) suggests cross‑architecture utility.  
- **Break condition**: If the calibration dataset is unrepresentative or the model lacks clear sink heads, the ranking may misidentify important heads; also, for very short contexts, the 1/T scaling might obscure differences.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Calibration dataset → Attention extraction → BOS sink score computation → Head/layer ranking → Pruning (zero WO or remove block) → Pruned model

**Critical path**:
1. Run inference on a representative dataset (e.g., MMLU) with the model in eval mode to extract attention weights.
2. For each head in layer ℓ, compute S(ℓ,h)_BOS = (1/T) Σ_{t=0}^{T-1} α_{t,0} where α is the attention weight from query t to BOS key at position 0.
3. For head pruning: sort all heads by S(ℓ,h)_BOS descending; select top K% and set their corresponding WO weight slices to zero.
4. For layer pruning: compute S(ℓ)_BOS as average over heads in layer ℓ; prune layers with highest scores (excluding first and last).
5. Validate pruned model on downstream benchmarks.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- BOS sink metric is simple (one forward pass), stable across sequence lengths, and directly targets heads that contribute minimally to semantics. It avoids costly gradient or reconstruction computations used by Mag‑SP/Wanda‑SP.
- However, it may overlook redundancy that does not manifest as BOS sinking (e.g., heads with diagonal self‑attention). It also assumes the presence of a BOS token; models without such token may require adaptation.
- Default preservation of first/last layers might miss some redundancy there; researchers could relax this if needed.

**Failure signatures**:
- Catastrophic drop (>10% absolute) on multiple benchmarks after small pruning ratios indicates misidentification of redundant heads.
- High variance in BOS sink scores across different calibration datasets suggests instability in the metric.
- If ablation experiments (as in Figure 2) show that high‑BOS heads actually cause significant accuracy loss when removed, the core assumption is violated.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Sink distribution check: Compute and plot average BOS sink scores per layer; expect increasing trend toward deeper layers (replicates Figure 3).
2. Single-head ablation validation: Randomly sample 20 high‑BOS and 20 low‑BOS heads; ablate each individually and measure MMLU accuracy. High‑BOS heads should show negligible impact (<0.1% drop) while low‑BOS heads cause larger variance.
3. Pruning comparison: Apply 12.5% head pruning using BOS, Mag‑SP, and Wanda‑SP on a test model; evaluate on MMLU and WikiText‑2. Expect BOS to retain accuracy closest to dense baseline (replicates Table 1).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **What theoretical principles explain the concentration of BOS sink heads in deeper layers?**  
   - Basis in paper: The limitations section states the authors “do not provide a formal theoretical derivation for the underlying cause of this positional concentration.”  
   - Why unresolved: The paper empirically shows the correlation but lacks a mechanistic model linking softmax normalization, representation stabilization, and layer depth.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: A formal analysis deriving conditions under which heads become BOS sinks as layers deepen, possibly involving stability arguments or optimization dynamics.

2. **Does the BOS sink phenomenon and its pruning efficacy generalize to ultra‑large models (>100B parameters) and non‑GQA architectures (e.g., standard multi‑head attention)?**  
   - Basis in paper: The conclusion lists “validating the BOS sink phenomenon across even larger‑scale models and a broader range of downstream tasks” and “investigating whether similar attention‑anchoring mechanisms exist in other Transformer‑based architectures beyond text, such as Vision‑Language Models (VLMs).”  
   - Why unresolved: The study only evaluated Gemma‑3‑4B, Llama‑3.1‑8B, and Qwen3‑4B, all GQA‑based and under 10B parameters.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Replicating the BOS sink score analysis and pruning experiments on models like GPT‑4, Claude, or older architectures like original Transformer, and measuring consistency of sink patterns and pruning performance.

3. **How do BOS sink heads develop during the pre‑training process? Do they emerge gradually or appear abruptly at certain stages?**  
   - Basis in paper: The conclusion states investigating “the developmental dynamics of these sinks during the pre‑training phase may offer deeper insights.”  
   - Why unresolved: The paper analyzes only fully‑trained models; the evolution of attention patterns throughout training is not studied.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Training a model from scratch with periodic checkpointing, computing BOS sink scores at each stage, and tracking when and how sink heads appear and stabilize.

## Limitations
- Unknown calibration data size: Paper does not specify how many MMLU examples were used to compute BOS sink scores; full test set vs. a small subset could affect score stability and pruning decisions.
- GQA implementation detail: The exact mapping of output projection (WO) columns to heads in Grouped‑Query Attention models is unspecified; incorrect slicing could invalidate pruning or require model‑specific adjustments.
- Generalization scope: Break conditions suggest BOS sink pruning may fail on models without explicit BOS tokens or for deep reasoning tasks where deeper layers are critical; these scenarios were not tested in the paper.

## Next Checks
1. **Calibration sensitivity test**: Compute BOS sink scores using 500, 1k, and full MMLU examples; rank heads by each and measure overlap. Low overlap would indicate dataset size materially affects pruning decisions.
2. **GQA WO‑slice verification**: For a GQA model (e.g., Llama‑3.1‑8B), inspect the transformer code to confirm WO’s shape and head grouping; validate that zeroing contiguous head‑dim columns correctly targets individual heads.
3. **Deep‑layer functional probe**: On a complex reasoning task (e.g., GSM8K), ablate high‑BOS heads from only final layers; if accuracy drops significantly (>2%), it contradicts the claim that deep‑layer redundancy is universal.
