# Power-Law Spectrum of the Random Feature Model

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14578
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14578
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** For data covariance with eigenvalues λ_j ∝ j^{-α} (α>1), a random feature layer with monomial activation f(y)=y^p yields feature covariance eigenvalues λ_j = Θ((log^{p-1}(j+1)/j)^α) for j ≤ c₁ d log^{-(p+1)}(d) and λ_j = Θ(j^{-α}) thereafter (up to polylog factors), preserving the power‑law exponent exactly up to a mild logarithmic correction. Experiments confirm the theory for monomials and show universality across activations (ReLU, tanh) and data distributions (Rademacher, Student‑t, CIFAR‑10); depth stability holds for MLPs/CNNs, and trained ResNets recover the input exponent at the final layer.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses whether the power‑law decay of eigenvalues in the input data covariance survives a random linear sketch followed by a pointwise nonlinearity. For monomial activations f(y)=y^p and Gaussian data with covariance H satisfying λ_j(H) ∝ j^{-α} (α>1), the authors prove matching upper and lower bounds on the eigenvalues of the resulting random‑feature population covariance. The bounds exhibit the same exponent α, modified only by a logarithmic factor that depends on p. The proof relies on a dyadic head‑tail decomposition of the sketch matrix, a Wick chaos expansion for the monomial moments, and random matrix concentration inequalities. Complementary experiments validate the theoretical predictions for monomials and demonstrate that the preservation of the power‑law spectrum extends to other activations (ReLU, tanh) and to a variety of data distributions (Gaussian, Rademacher, Student‑t, CIFAR‑10). Further investigation of deep networks shows that the exponent remains stable across layers; with skip connections (as in ResNets) and training, the final layer recovers the input exponent, highlighting the robustness of the phenomenon.

## Method Summary
The theoretical analysis begins by factorizing the input as x = √H z₀ with z₀∼N(0,I). For a monomial activation f(y)=y^p, the covariance of the features z = f(W^⊤x) can be written via the Wick chaos expansion as a sum over pairings, each term involving a quadratic form of the matrix Y_π = √{H_π} W_π, where H_π encodes the covariance structure of the paired Gaussian variables. The authors decompose W_π dyadically according to the magnitude of the entries of H_π. For each block, the normalised matrix (1/d) W_π^⊤ W_π concentrates sharply around the identity, with tail bounds that decay exponentially in the block size. The leading pairing (the principal term) corresponds to H^{\otimes p} and yields eigenvalues λ_j ≍ (log^{p-1}(j+1)/j)^α for j up to c₁ d log^{-(p+1)}(d) and λ_j ≍ j^{-α} afterwards. Lower‑order pairings are shown to be asymptotically smaller due to the fast decay (α>1) of the input eigenvalues. Weyl’s inequalities then combine these contributions to give two‑sided bounds on the entire spectrum. Monte‑Carlo simulations on synthetic data (Gaussian with prescribed power‑law spectra) and on real‑world image data (CIFAR‑10) are used to estimate feature covariances for various activations and depths, confirming the theoretical predictions and the universality observations.

## Key Results
- The random‑feature covariance eigenvalues satisfy λ_j = Θ((log^{p-1}(j+1)/j)^α) for j ≤ c₁ d log^{-(p+1)}(d) and λ_j = Θ(j^{-α}) for larger j (up to polylog factors), preserving the input power‑law exponent α with only a mild logarithmic correction.
- Experiments confirm the theory for monomials and show that non‑monomial activations (ReLU, tanh) and diverse data sources (Rademacher, Student‑t, CIFAR‑10) also exhibit the same power‑law decay.
- In deep MLPs and CNNs, the exponent remains stable across layers; trained ResNets with skip connections recover the input exponent at the final layer.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The preservation of the power‑law eigenvalue decay under random feature maps is explained by three key mechanisms:

- **Dominant principal term for monomials.** For f(y)=y^p and Gaussian input, the Wick chaos expansion reveals that the leading contribution to the feature covariance comes from pairings that match factors within the same monomial. This principal term corresponds to a rescaled H^{\otimes p} and yields eigenvalues λ_j ≍ (log^{p-1}(j+1)/j)^α for j up to c₁ d log^{-(p+1)}(d) and λ_j ≍ j^{-α} thereafter. The proof uses a dyadic head‑tail decomposition of W and concentration of (1/d)W_π^⊤W_π. *Break condition:* The argument relies on the monomial structure and Gaussianity; non‑polynomial activations or non‑Gaussian data invalidate the decomposition.

- **Universality across data and activations.** Experimental results show that even for activations like ReLU and tanh and for non‑Gaussian data (Rademacher, Student‑t, CIFAR‑10), the empirical eigenvalue spectrum retains the same power‑law exponent as the input covariance. This suggests that the leading eigenstructure is robust to higher‑order distributional details and activation shape, provided the activation is “tame’’ (continuous, not overly oscillatory). *Break condition:* Extremely heavy‑tailed data (e.g., infinite variance) or activations with unbounded higher moments (e.g., z²e^{-z²}) may break the trend.

- **Stability across depth and effect of skip connections.** Each random‑feature layer individually preserves the exponent (with a mild log correction). Stacking many such layers does not cause the exponent to drift; experiments on MLPs and CNNs confirm stable slopes across depth. Moreover, training with gradient‑based optimisation, especially when skip connections are present (ResNets), enables the final layer to recover the original input exponent, indicating that training can re‑align the feature map with the data covariance. *Break condition:* Without skip connections, the logarithmic per‑layer corrections may accumulate, flattening the spectrum in very deep narrow networks.

Evidence anchors: the principal‑term analysis appears in Section 4.1 and Lemma 13; universality is supported by Figure 3 and Section 2.1; depth stability is documented in Appendix E (Figure 8, Table 1).

## Foundational Learning
- **Power‑law eigenvalue decay**  
  *Why needed:* The input covariance H is assumed to have eigenvalues λ_j(H) ∝ j^{-α} (α>1). This property is central to the paper’s spectral analysis.  
  *Quick check:* If λ_j(H) ∝ j^{-α}, the partial trace ∑_{j=1}^J λ_j(H) behaves as Θ(J^{1-α}) for large J.

- **Random feature model**  
  *Why needed:* It models a single neural‑network layer: a random linear projection followed by a pointwise nonlinearity. The paper studies how this transformation affects the data’s spectrum.  
  *Quick check:* For linear activation, the feature covariance becomes (1/d) W^⊤ H W, which inherits the power‑law decay of H (i.e., λ_j((1/d)W^⊤HW) ≍ j^{-α} up to the effective rank limit).

- **Wick chaos expansion**  
  *Why needed:* For monomials of Gaussian variables, Wick’s theorem (Isserlis’ theorem) expresses high‑order moments as sums over pairings, reducing the covariance analysis to quadratic forms.  
  *Quick check:* For independent standard Gaussians u and v, the Wick product ⟨uv⟩ equals uv (since E[uv]=0 and the Wick product of order 2 is uv − E[uv] = uv).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input data x (dim v) → Sketch matrix W (v×d) → Activation f applied to W^⊤ x → Feature vector z (dim d) → Population covariance Σ = E[(1/d) z^{⊗2}].

- **Critical path (proof sketch):**  
  1. Represent x = √H z₀ with z₀∼N(0,I).  
  2. For f(y)=y^p, apply Wick chaos expansion to E[z_i^p z_j^p].  
  3. Decompose each transformed matrix Y_π = √{H_π} W_π dyadically according to the magnitude of H_π’s entries.  
  4. Show that (1/d) W_π^⊤ W_π concentrates around the identity with high probability.  
  5. Identify the principal term (π = (1,…,1)) and derive its eigenvalue asymptotics: λ_j ≍ (log^{p-1}(j+1)/j)^α for j ≤ c₁ d log^{-(p+1)}(d) and λ_j ≍ j^{-α} for larger j.  
  6. Treat lower‑order terms via additional Wick projections; they become subdominant due to α>1.  
  7. Combine bounds using Weyl’s inequalities to obtain matching two‑sided results.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Width vs. dimension:* Theory requires v ≥ d; the effective rank of H influences the sharpness of concentration.  
  - *Monomial degree p:* Larger p introduces a logarithmic factor log^{p-1}(j) that slightly flattens the tail eigenvalues.  
  - *Exponent α:* α>1 ensures finite total variance; as α→1, the logarithmic corrections become more significant.  
  - *Sketch distribution:* Gaussian W enables rotational invariance and simplifies concentration; non‑Gaussian W needs two‑moment matching and control of higher moments.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Premature flattening of eigenvalues before index d suggests violation of the regime (e.g., α ≤ 1 or insufficient width).  
  - Large variability across random seeds for the estimated covariance indicates concentration failure, possibly due to heavy‑tailed data or sketch entries.  
  - Unexpected bumps or deviations for exotic activations (e.g., z²e^{-z²}) show that the Wick decomposition no longer isolates a dominant term.  
  - In deep MLPs without residual connections, the fitted power‑law slope may drift downward with depth beyond the predicted logarithmic accumulation.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Validate the monomial theory: generate Gaussian data with α=1.31, set v=d=10 000, m=100 000 samples; compute eigenvalues for p=1,…,6; verify the predicted slopes and the logarithmic correction.  
  2. Test universality: replace the Gaussian data by Rademacher, Student‑t, and CIFAR‑10; use activations ReLU, tanh, x³; check that the fitted exponent α matches the input exponent within tolerance.  
  3. Assess depth stability: train MLPs (2–30 layers), a CNN, and a ResNet‑20 on CIFAR‑10; record the power‑law exponent of activation covariances per layer at initialization and after training; confirm stability and recovery of the input exponent in ResNets.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **General activations.** The paper cannot yet extend its results to non‑monomial activations like ReLU, tanh, or sigmoid because the Wick‑based proof does not combine cleanly across different Hermite degrees. A rigorous theorem for analytic activations or a counterexample would resolve this.
- **Eigenvalue coverage.** The sharp two‑sided bound is proved only for indices j up to c₁ d log^{-(p+1)}(d), leaving a polylogarithmic gap. Overcoming this limitation likely requires improved matrix concentration for Gaussian chaos.
- **Universality in W.** The analysis assumes a Gaussian sketch matrix. Extending to i.i.d. entries with only finite moments (e.g., two‑moment matching) is conjectured but open due to higher‑order moment dependencies in the chaos terms.
- **Test error under power‑law data.** Combining the spectral characterization with random‑feature ridge regression to obtain scaling laws for the generalisation error remains an important open problem.

## Limitations
- The theoretical results are restricted to Gaussian input distributions and monomial activations; generalisation to other data distributions and activation functions is unproven.
- The constant c₁ appearing in the bound on the regime of validity is not explicit, so the exact transition point where the logarithmic correction dominates is unknown.
- Empirical evidence for universality covers only a limited set of data sources and activations; failure cases (e.g., extremely heavy‑tailed data or highly oscillatory functions) may exist.
