# Structure-Aware Distributed Backdoor Attacks in Federated Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03865
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03865
- Reference count: 35
- Primary result: TFI achieves >85% attack success rate at 5–10% poisoning in multi-path architectures (ResNet, DenseNet) by exploiting SCC-guided client selection and frequency-domain fractal perturbations.

## Executive Summary
Federated learning backdoor attacks often ignore how model architecture shapes perturbation propagation. This paper introduces Structural Responsiveness Score (SRS) and Structural Compatibility Coefficient (SCC) to quantify a model’s sensitivity and preference for fractal perturbations. Using these, the authors propose the TFI attack framework, which selectively targets high-SCC clients and injects multi-scale fractal triggers via frequency-domain embedding. Experiments across ResNet, DenseNet, VGG, and ViT on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet-100 show that multi-path architectures amplify fractal perturbations, achieving high attack success rates (>85%) at very low poisoning ratios (5–10%). SCC strongly predicts ASR (Pearson r=0.91). TFI also demonstrates superior stealthiness (update similarity 0.87, anomaly detection 18.5%) and better survivability under robust aggregation and differential privacy compared to baseline attacks.

## Method Summary
TFI comprises three core modules: (1) a fractal trigger generator that creates multi-scale perturbations in the frequency domain; (2) a client structure evaluator that estimates each client’s SCC using a small probe dataset and gradient norms; and (3) a client selector that greedily picks clients maximizing utility V_i = γ_i · SCC_i, where γ_i is the client’s aggregation weight. Attack intensity ramps up over communication rounds via I(t) = I_max·(1−e^{−λt}). Selected malicious clients then embed the fractal pattern into their local training images with adaptive strength 𝜖_i^{(t)} before local training. The server aggregates updates via FedAvg. This approach exploits the observation that architectures with multi-path feature fusion (e.g., ResNet, DenseNet) provide low-attenuation propagation channels for fractal perturbations, enabling effective backdoor injection even with few poisoned samples.

## Key Results
- Multi-path architectures (ResNet, DenseNet) achieve ASR >85% at 5–10% poisoning; sequential (VGG) and self-attention (ViT) architectures show reduced ASR (~70–76%).
- SCC correlates strongly with ASR across architectures (Pearson r=0.91), confirming structural compatibility as a predictor.
- TFI exhibits high stealthiness (update similarity 0.87, anomaly detection rate 18.5%) and maintains effectiveness under robust aggregation (Krum) and differential privacy, outperforming baseline attacks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Structural Compatibility Drives Perturbation Amplification
**Claim:** Model architectures with multi-path feature fusion (e.g., ResNet, DenseNet) exhibit high Structural Compatibility Coefficient (SCC) for fractal perturbations, enabling effective backdoor injection at low poisoning ratios.  
**Mechanism:** SCC quantifies the relative sensitivity of a model to fractal versus static triggers (SCC = SRS_fractal / SRS_static). High-SCC architectures provide low-attenuation propagation channels (via skip/dense connections) that amplify multi-scale fractal signals during forward passes and gradient updates.  
**Core assumption:** Fractal perturbations' broadband frequency distribution aligns with multi-path feature reuse mechanisms, allowing them to survive layer-wise processing and federated aggregation better than concentrated static triggers.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "Networks with multi-path feature fusion can amplify and retain fractal perturbations even under low poisoning ratios"  
- [section] Section 3.2: "When SCC>1, the model responds more strongly to fractal perturbations... such perturbations are easier to encode into parameter updates"  
- [section] Page 9, Figure 4: ResNet-18/DenseNet-121 achieve >85% ASR at 10% poisoning, while VGG-16/ViT-Base drop to ~70–76%  
- [corpus] Weak: Neighboring papers discuss fractal triggers but not SCC quantification; this metric is novel to the target paper.  
**Break condition:** If the model lacks multi-path structures (e.g., sequential VGG, self-attention ViT), SCC < 1, and fractal perturbations fail to propagate/amplify, leading to sharply reduced ASR.

### Mechanism 2: SCC-Guided Client Selection Maximizes Attack Efficiency
**Claim:** Targeting clients with high SCC values yields disproportionately higher attack success rates per poisoned sample compared to random or uniform poisoning.  
**Mechanism:** The attacker estimates each client's SCC via probe gradients (Eq. 9–10) and selects clients maximizing utility (V_i = γ_i · SCC_i). Malicious updates from high-SCC clients carry perturbations that survive aggregation more effectively, concentrating backdoor signal in the global model.  
**Core assumption:** Federated aggregation weights updates proportionally to client influence (γ_i), and structurally compatible perturbations contribute more to the global model's backdoor behavior per unit of poisoning effort.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "TFI attack framework, which selectively targets clients with high SCC"  
- [section] Section 4.2: Client selection algorithm using greedy maximization of V_i = γ_i · ̂SCC(f_i)  
- [section] Page 12, Figure 9: SCC and ASR correlate at Pearson r=0.91 across architectures; high-SCC models reach 85% ASR at 5% poisoning vs. 10–12% for low-SCC.  
- [corpus] Not addressed; corpus focuses on trigger design, not client selection metrics.  
**Break condition:** If client selection ignores SCC (e.g., random poisoning), the attack requires significantly higher poisoning ratios to achieve comparable ASR, especially under low-participation regimes.

### Mechanism 3: Frequency-Domain Fractal Embedding Ensures Stealthy Persistence
**Claim:** Embedding fractal perturbations in the frequency domain with adaptive strength enables them to evade statistical and spectral detection while persisting through robust aggregation.  
**Mechanism:** Fractal patterns (multi-scale Gaussian-filtered base template) are mixed into the frequency components of training images (Eq. 7). The embedding weight β(ω) scales with SCC and round intensity (Eq. 8), ensuring perturbations blend with benign gradient distributions. Multi-scale energy dispersion avoids concentrated spectral artifacts.  
**Core assumption:** Broadband frequency perturbations are less distinguishable from natural image statistics and benign update noise, reducing anomaly detection. Multi-path architectures further disperse these signals across feature maps, masking their presence.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "multi-scale fractal triggers via frequency-domain embedding... superior stealthiness (update similarity 0.87, anomaly detection 18.5%)"  
- [section] Section 4.1: "frequency-domain hybrid embedding strategy... energy distribution is more dispersed... helps improve stealthiness"  
- [section] Page 11, Figure 7: Fractal triggers show dispersed frequency energy vs. concentrated static triggers, lowering detection rates.  
- [corpus] Supporting: Neighboring paper "Unveiling Hidden Threats" (arXiv:2511.09252) also finds fractal triggers improve stealthiness in distributed attacks, but does not tie to SCC or frequency-domain adaptive embedding.  
**Break condition:** If embedding uses fixed/static spatial triggers or lacks frequency dispersion, detection rates rise sharply (>60% in Figure 7). If adaptive strength control is disabled, high-SCC clients may be over-perturbed, increasing update similarity deviation.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Federated Learning (FL)
**Why needed:** The attack operates in FL's decentralized setting where clients upload model updates to a central aggregator. Understanding client selection, local training, and aggregation (FedAvg) is essential to see how malicious updates influence the global model.  
**Quick check:** What are the three main steps of a federated learning communication round? How does non-IID data partitioning affect client updates?

### Concept: Backdoor Attack Fundamentals
**Why needed:** TFI is a backdoor attack—it implants a trigger that causes misclassification only when present, while preserving clean accuracy. Distinguishing this from label-flipping or data poisoning is key.  
**Quick check:** How does a backdoor attack differ from a traditional data poisoning attack that degrades overall model accuracy? What is the "attack success rate" (ASR) metric?

### Concept: Model Architecture and Feature Reuse
**Why needed:** The paper hinges on architectural differences: residual/dense connections create multi-path propagation, while sequential (VGG) or global (ViT) architectures do not. This determines SCC and attack feasibility.  
**Quick check:** What are skip connections in ResNet? How does dense connectivity in DenseNet differ? Why might these pathways help propagate perturbations?

### Concept: Frequency-Domain Signal Processing
**Why needed:** Fractal perturbations are generated and embedded via frequency-domain operations (Fourier transform, Gaussian filtering). Understanding why frequency dispersion aids stealthiness is critical.  
**Quick check:** What does it mean for a perturbation to have a "broadband" vs. "narrowband" frequency spectrum? Why might a broadband perturbation be harder to detect via spectral analysis?

### Concept: Robust Aggregation and Defenses
**Why needed:** TFI's survivability is evaluated against defenses like Krum (statistical anomaly filtering) and differential privacy (noise injection). These alter the perturbation accumulation dynamics.  
**Quick check:** How does Krum select an update? How does differential privacy noise during training affect the accumulation of malicious signals?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Fractal Trigger Generator → Client Structure Evaluator → Client Selector → (per round) Temporal Coordinator → Adaptive Perturbation Injector → Local Training → FedAvg Aggregation → Global Model

### Critical path
1. **Initialization:** Precompute fractal template δ_fractal (one-time). Server distributes probe dataset to all clients.
2. **Client evaluation phase:** Each client returns gradient norms for clean, fractal-perturbed, and static-perturbed probe samples. Server estimates ̂SCC_i for all clients.
3. **Malicious client selection:** Server selects subset  with highest V_i = γ_i · ̂SCC_i, respecting attack budget  (max number or total weight).
4. **Per-round attack execution:**
   - Compute I(t) for round t.
   - For each malicious client i ∈  participating in round t:
     - Compute adaptive strength 𝜖_i^{(t)} based on V_i, I(t), and active attacker count.
     - Inject δ_fractal into client's local batch images via frequency mixing.
     - Client trains locally on poisoned data, sends update Δw_i^{(t)}.
   - Server aggregates all updates (including benign) to update global model w^{(t+1)}.
5. **Repeat** steps 4 for T rounds.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Stealthiness vs. effectiveness:** Higher 𝜖_base_i increases ASR but raises update anomaly. Adaptive scaling (Eq. 11–13) tries to normalize perturbation visibility across clients, but requires accurate SCC estimates.
- **Evaluation overhead:** SCC estimation requires one probe round with additional forward passes per client. Can be amortized if client models are stable, but may need re-estimation under personalization.
- **Client selection aggressiveness:** Greedy top-k selection maximizes per-round signal but risks concentration; under low client participation (|S_attack^| small), attack may fail to accumulate.
- **Fractal complexity:** Multi-scale generation (K scales) increases computational cost linearly with K; moderate K (e.g., 3–5) suffices per experiments.

### Failure signatures
- **Low ASR on high-SCC models (<70% at 10% poisoning):** Indicates faulty fractal generation (e.g., incorrect frequency embedding) or broken adaptive strength scaling.
- **High detection rate (>30% anomaly):** Check frequency window function w(ω) and perturbation strength clipping; likely too much high-frequency energy.
- **ASR decreases with training rounds:** Temporal coordination I(t) may be too slow (λ too small) or client participation too volatile; attack signal not accumulating.
- **No correlation between SCC and ASR:** Probe dataset may be too small or unrepresentative; gradient norm estimation noisy. Verify ̂SCC computation (Eq. 10) matches clean/fractal/static responses.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline replication on ResNet-18 (CIFAR-10):**  
   Setup: 100 clients, Dirichlet α=0.5, 10% poisoning, 10 rounds.  
   Expected: ASR >85%, MTA within 2% of clean model, update similarity ~0.87.  
   Validation: Confirms fractal injection and client selection work as described.
2. **Structural dependence test (VGG-16 vs. ResNet-18):**  
   Same setup as (1) but compare VGG-16 and ResNet-18.  
   Expected: ResNet-18 ASR ~85–90%, VGG-16 ASR ~70–75% (dropping).  
   Validation: Verifies SCC-ASR correlation; if VGG-16 matches ResNet, SCC estimation or fractal pattern may be flawed.
3. **Stealthiness under Krum defense:**  
   Setup: ResNet-18, 10% poisoning, but server uses Krum aggregation (m=10 byzantine tolerance).  
   Expected: ASR retention >80% (vs. no defense) and anomaly detection <25%.  
   Validation: Checks frequency-domain stealthiness and update similarity; if detection >50%, revisit frequency windowing or adaptive strength clipping.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
Do fractal perturbations propagate differently in non-convolutional architectures (e.g., graph neural networks, Transformer variants with sparse attention), and does SCC remain a strong ASR predictor?  
*Basis:* The authors' first key question asks about differentiated propagation across architectures, but experiments are limited to ResNet, DenseNet, VGG, and ViT. They note ViT's lower SCC but do not explore other modern architectures.  
*Why unresolved:* Connectivity patterns in GNNs or sparse Transformers may fundamentally alter multi-scale perturbation propagation; the SCC-ASR correlation may not generalize beyond the tested families.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Systematically measure SCC and ASR across a diverse set of architectures (e.g., GCNs, Perceiver, MLP-Mixers) to validate or refute the universality of the structure-compatibility relationship.

**Open Question 2**  
Can a precise, predictive relationship be established between a model's SCC value and the minimum poisoning ratio required to achieve a target ASR under specific federated aggregation rules?  
*Basis:* The second key question addresses leveraging compatibility for low-poisoning attacks. The paper shows high-SCC models achieve ASR>85% with 5–10% poisoning, while low-SCC models require more, but only provides empirical correlation (Pearson r=0.91), not a predictive formula.  
*Why unresolved:* No theoretical model links SCC to ASR thresholds; the interplay with aggregation noise, training rounds, and data heterogeneity remains phenomenological.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Derive an analytical model of perturbation accumulation in FedAvg that incorporates SCC as a gain factor, then empirically validate its parameters across architectures and defenses to predict minimum poisoning ratios.

**Open Question 3**  
In heterogeneous federated learning where clients have different model architectures, how does the variance in SCC across clients impact the optimal poisoning budget allocation and global ASR?  
*Basis:* The TFI client selection strategy assumes homogeneous client models (all experiments use the same architecture per run). Real-world FL often involves heterogeneous architectures (e.g., personalized or cross-device settings), which is not considered.  
*Why unresolved:* The additive effect of perturbations from clients with different SCCs during aggregation is unknown; a greedy high-SCC selection may be suboptimal or fail if only a few high-SCC clients exist.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Simulations with mixed-architecture client populations (e.g., some ResNet, some ViT) measuring ASR as a function of the malicious client SCC distribution and participation patterns, yielding an adaptive selection policy for heterogeneous settings.

**Open Question 4**  
What concrete, implementable architectural modifications or aggregation rules can reduce a model's SCC for fractal perturbations without sacrificing primary task accuracy, thereby enabling practical structure-aware defenses?  
*Basis:* The third key question asks whether attacks have structural constraints to inform defense design. Section 5.2 suggests "reducing multi-path propagation capacity," but provides no specific design and acknowledges the risk of hurting model performance.  
*Why unresolved:* Removing skip connections or dense links in ResNet/DenseNet is known to degrade accuracy; a viable defense must balance security and utility, but no such trade-off analysis exists.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Design and test modified architectures (e.g., ResNet with attenuated or gated skip connections, DenseNet with sparse connectivity) that maintain comparable accuracy but exhibit lower SCC and higher resistance to TFI, or develop aggregation rules that penalize updates from high-SCC clients.

## Limitations
- **Fractal trigger specification:** Exact construction of the self-similar template and the frequency window function w(ω) are not detailed, hindering exact replication.
- **SCC estimation protocol:** Probe dataset size, whether disjoint from training, layer weights in SRS, and precise gradient-norm ratio are unspecified, raising questions about estimation robustness.
- **Evaluation scope:** Experiments limited to four architectures on two datasets; generalizability to other models or extreme non-IID settings remains unclear.

## Confidence
- SCC-ASR correlation (Pearson r=0.91): High  
- Stealthiness metrics (update similarity 0.87, anomaly detection 18.5%): Medium  
- Overall TFI superiority over baselines: Medium  

## Next Checks
- Ablate fractal parameters: vary number of Gaussian scales (K), frequency cutoff, and blending weights to quantify impact on ASR and stealthiness.
- Evaluate robustness of SCC estimation: test sensitivity to probe set size (10–500 samples), distribution (IID vs. non-IID), and layer selection; compare estimated SCC to ground-truth SCC computed post-training.
- Extended defense evaluation: test TFI against modern FL defenses (FLTrust, FLAME, Multi-Krum) and under stronger differential privacy to assess practical survivability.
