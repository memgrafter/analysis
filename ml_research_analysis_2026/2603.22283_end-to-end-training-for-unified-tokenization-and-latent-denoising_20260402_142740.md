# End-to-End Training for Unified Tokenization and Latent Denoising

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22283  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22283  
- **Reference count:** 33  
- **Primary result:** End-to-end unified tokenization and denoising achieves FID 2.12 (Base) and 1.73 (Large) on ImageNet-256, and 99.37% reconstruction match on QM9 molecules, outperforming prior two-stage methods.

## Executive Summary
Current latent diffusion models (LDMs) require staged training: a tokenizer is first trained and frozen, then a separate diffusion model is trained on its latent space. This work introduces UNITE, a single-stage architecture that unifies tokenization and generation through a shared “Generative Encoder”. The key insight is that tokenizing an image (inferring latents) and denoising latents (generating images) are instances of the same latent inference problem under different conditioning. UNITE trains both objectives end-to-end with two forward passes through the same network, using weight sharing and a stop-gradient to avoid degenerate solutions. This eliminates the need for adversarial losses or pretrained encoders (e.g., DINO). On ImageNet 256×256, UNITE approaches state‑of‑the‑art generation (FID 2.12 Base, 1.73 Large) and excels on molecule generation (QM9 reconstruction match 99.37%, uniqueness 99.71%). Ablations confirm that weight sharing yields a superior reconstruction‑generation trade‑off and that the shared parameters encourage a common latent language, measured by representation alignment (CKA) and compression analysis.

## Method Summary
UNITE employs a shared “Generative Encoder” (GEθ) based on a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone. For an input image, patch tokens are concatenated with K Gaussian‑initialized register tokens; after Transformer updates, the registers output the clean latent z₀. A decoder Dϕ (a ViT‑style stack) then reconstructs the image, with loss L_recon = ∥x̂−x∥₁ + LPIPS(x̂,x). To train generation, a noisy latent zₜ is formed via rectified flow: zₜ = t·z₀ + (1−t)·ϵ, where t∼U[0,1], ϵ∼N(0,I). The same GEθ (without image patches) processes (zₜ, t) to predict a clean latent ˆz₀, incurring flow loss L_flow = ∥ˆz₀ − sg(z₀)∥²₂. The stop‑gradient (sg) on z₀ prevents flow gradients from interfering with tokenization. An additional reconstruction noise (σ≈0.7) is added before decoding to align the decoder with the distribution of latents seen during generation. Total loss L = L_recon + L_flow. At inference, iterative denoising (e.g., adaptive dopri5 ODE solver) converts pure noise into an image via the shared GE and decoder.

## Key Results
- ImageNet 256×256 generation: Base model FID 2.12, Large model FID 1.73, surpassing several two‑stage diffusion methods.
- QM9 molecule generation: 99.37% reconstruction match, 99.71% uniqueness; state‑of‑the‑art.
- Ablations show weight sharing yields the best rFID‑gFID trade‑off, and shared parameters foster representation alignment as measured by CKA and compression entropy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Weight sharing promotes a common latent representation
Sharing the same network parameters between tokenization and denoising forces the latent space to satisfy both reconstruction fidelity (preserving instance‑specific details) and generative robustness (stability under noise). This joint optimization yields a “common latent language” that balances the two objectives. Evidence: weight‑shared models lie on the rFID‑gFID Pareto frontier; ablations with separate encoder/denoiser show worse trade‑off; CKA alignment between pathways is higher under weight sharing.

### Mechanism 2: Stop‑gradient prevents degenerate solutions and stabilizes training
Applying stop‑gradient to the clean latent z₀ before corruption isolates the tokenization gradient from the flow loss. Without it, denoising gradients can drive z₀ toward a distribution that is easy to denoise but poor for reconstruction, harming the shared representation. Evidence: removing stop‑gradient reduces CKA alignment, produces noisier intermediate denoising trajectories (lower PSNR), and requires careful loss‑weight tuning; stop‑gradient stabilizes training and yields smoother denoising curves.

### Mechanism 3: Reconstruction noise regularizes the decoder
Injecting moderate Gaussian noise (σ≈0.7) into latents before decoding exposes the decoder to perturbed codes similar to those encountered near the end of the generative trajectory. This regularizer prevents overfitting to deterministic z₀ and aligns the decoder with the generative sampling distribution, improving gFID. Evidence: ablations show a U‑shaped curve; σ=0 degrades gFID, σ≈0.7 is optimal, σ=1.0 harms reconstruction. The robustness to exact σ level also supports its role as a useful regularizer.

**Break conditions:**  
- Mechanism 1 fails if CKA alignment between tokenization and denoising pathways drops below ~0.8 in mid‑layers, likely leading to worse rFID/gFID.  
- Mechanism 2 is necessary if training is unstable or intermediate decoded PSNR is low; removing stop‑gradient causes such symptoms.  
- Mechanism 3 breaks if σ is set too high (reconstruction collapse) or too low (generative mismatch).

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Tokenizer (tokenization pass):  
x → image patch tokens + K Gaussian registers → GEθ (ViT backbone) → clean latent z₀  
(registers are the latent representation)

Generator (denoising pass):  
zₜ (noisy latent registers) + time t → GEθ → predicted clean latent ˆz₀

Decoder:  
(˜z₀ = z₀ + σ·ϵ) → Dϕ (ViT decoder) → reconstructed image x̂

Losses:  
L_flow = ∥ˆz₀ − sg(z₀)∥²₂  
L_recon = ∥x̂−x∥₁ + LPIPS(x̂,x)

### Critical Path
1. Tokenization: compute z₀ = GEθ(x).  
2. Apply stop‑gradient to z₀, then corrupt: zₜ = t·z₀ + (1−t)·ϵ (t∼U[0,1], ϵ∼N(0,I)).  
3. Denoising: ˆz₀ = GEθ(zₜ, t).  
4. Compute L_flow.  
5. Reconstruction: add noise σ∼0.7 to z₀ → ˜z₀, decode x̂ = Dϕ(˜z₀), compute L_recon.  
6. Backprop total loss L = L_recon + L_flow; gradients update GEθ (via both passes) and Dϕ; sg(z₀) blocks flow gradients into tokenization output.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Weight sharing vs. separate parameters:** Sharing reduces parameters, encourages alignment (better rFID‑gFID trade‑off), and simplifies the model; separate parameters allow specialized features but lack the coupling benefit.  
- **Stop‑gradient vs. no stop‑gradient:** Stop‑gradient stabilizes training and yields cleaner denoising trajectories; no‑stop‑gradient may slightly improve rFID in separate‑weight settings but requires careful loss weighting and can degrade representation alignment.  
- **Reconstruction noise σ:** Moderate σ (~0.7) regularizes the decoder, improving generative fidelity; too low (σ=0) overfits and harms gFID; too high (σ=1.0) damages reconstruction.

### Failure Signatures
- **Training instability:** L_flow diverges or L_recon plateaus high; often due to insufficient warm‑up or missing stop‑gradient.  
- **Poor generation (high gFID):** Latent space fragmented; check CKA alignment (<0.7).  
- **Poor reconstruction (high rFID):** Decoder under‑trained or latent dimension too small; weight sharing removed without capacity increase.  
- **Noisy intermediate trajectories:** Decoded images at mid‑denoising steps (t≈0.6) show artifacts; indicates missing stop‑gradient or mis‑matched noise schedules.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Weight‑sharing ablation:** Train a model with separate encoder and denoiser (same architecture, no sharing) but keep stop‑gradient. Compare rFID/gFID to the shared model; expect shared to have a better trade‑off.  
2. **Stop‑gradient ablation:** Remove sg(z₀) from the weight‑shared model. Monitor layer‑wise CKA alignment and decoded PSNR across timesteps; expect alignment drop and noisier trajectories.  
3. **Reconstruction noise sweep:** Train variants with σ ∈ {0.0, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 1.0}. Report rFID and gFID; expect a U‑shaped curve with optimum around σ=0.7.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Alternative tokenization objectives:** How do DINO or JEPA‑style objectives, when combined with denoising, affect joint training and final performance?  
- **Robotics‑relevant latents:** Which tokenization objectives yield latents most useful for decision‑making in downstream control tasks?  
- **Iterative refinement for tokenization:** Can the image‑to‑latent mapping benefit from multiple iterative refinement loops within the unified framework?  
- **Faster generation via weight sharing:** Does the proposed joint training scheme enable high‑quality one‑ to few‑step generation, reducing the need for many ODE steps?

## Limitations
- Exact decoder architecture (ViT depth, unpatchification head) and precise training hyperparameters (learning‑rate schedule, EMA decay, loss weights) are not fully specified, making exact replication difficult.  
- External validation of weight sharing is limited; neighboring works mostly explore separate‑weight joint training, so the claimed advantage needs broader verification.  
- For molecule generation, preprocessing details (coordinate normalization, bond inference) are underspecified, which may affect reproducibility of reported metrics.

## Confidence
- **Weight sharing improves reconstruction‑generation trade‑off:** **High** (supported by clear ablations and CKA metrics).  
- **Stop‑gradient prevents degenerate solutions and stabilizes training:** **High** (consistent evidence from alignment and trajectory analyses).  
- **Moderate reconstruction noise (σ≈0.7) benefits generation:** **Medium** (ablation shows a U‑shaped trend but optimal σ may vary by domain).  
- **Exact decoder blueprint and training schedule as described:** **Low** (critical details missing, hindering precise replication).
