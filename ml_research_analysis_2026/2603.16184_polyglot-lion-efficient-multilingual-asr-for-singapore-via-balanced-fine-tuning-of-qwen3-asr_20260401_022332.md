# Polyglot-Lion: Efficient Multilingual ASR for Singapore via Balanced Fine-Tuning of Qwen3-ASR

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16184
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16184
- **Reference Count:** 31
- **Primary Result:** Polyglot-Lion-1.7B achieves a 14.85% average error rate across 12 benchmarks, matching the 6x larger MERaLiON-2-10B-ASR (14.32%) while reducing estimated training cost by 233× ($81 vs. $18,862) and improving inference throughput by 20×.

## Executive Summary
Singapore's four official languages (English, Mandarin, Tamil, Malay) pose a challenge for building a compact, cost-effective multilingual ASR system. Existing specialist models are prohibitively large, while general models underperform on low-resource languages. Polyglot-Lion addresses this by fine-tuning moderate-scale Qwen3-ASR models (0.6B/1.7B) with a two-stage deterministic upsampling strategy that enforces equal training data per language, and by omitting language-tag conditioning to enable implicit language identification. On 12 benchmarks, the 1.7B variant matches the accuracy of a 10B-parameter model at a fraction of the cost and latency, with dramatic improvements for Tamil and Malay.

## Method Summary
The core method involves fine-tuning Qwen3-ASR—a pretrained encoder-decoder transformer—on a balanced multilingual corpus. The raw training data is highly imbalanced; to counteract gradient dominance by high-resource languages, the authors apply a deterministic two-stage upsampling algorithm. Stage 1 upsamples each language’s internal datasets to match the largest within that language; Stage 2 then upsamples each language’s total corpus to match the largest language group. This guarantees exactly 25% of training samples per language. Crucially, no language identifier tokens are prepended to the decoder input during training or inference, forcing the model to learn language-discriminative features directly from audio. Training uses AdamW with a cosine annealing schedule (peak LR 2e-5), an effective batch size of 32, and runs for 48 hours on a single RTX PRO 6000 GPU.

## Key Results
- Polyglot-Lion-1.7B’s average error rate (14.85%) is statistically indistinguishable from MERaLiON-2-10B-ASR (14.32%) despite having 6× fewer parameters.
- Estimated training cost is reduced by 233× ($81 vs. $18,862), and inference latency improves by 20× (0.10 vs. 2.02 s/sample).
- Balanced sampling drastically reduces error on low-resource languages: Tamil Common Voice WER drops from 139.96% (base model) to 39.19%, and Malay Mesolitica WER reaches 21.51%, the best among all evaluated systems.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Deterministic Language-Balanced Upsampling**
The claim is that equalizing the number of training utterances per language prevents gradient dominance by high-resource languages and improves low-resource recognition without degrading high-resource performance. The mechanism is a two-stage deterministic upsampling: first balance within each language group, then across languages, guaranteeing exactly 25% of samples per language regardless of original distribution skew. The core assumption is that model updates are approximately proportional to sample count per language; enforcing parity yields roughly equal gradient contribution. Evidence anchors include the abstract’s statement that balanced sampling improves low-resource languages, Section 4.2’s Algorithm 1, and Table 1’s raw hour skew (English+Mandarin ~65% vs. Malay ~8%). A break condition would be if the base model’s pretrained representations for a low-resource language are too weak (<1% exposure), limiting learning even with balanced data.

**Mechanism 2: Implicit Language Identification via Tag Omission**
The claim is that removing explicit language-tag conditioning forces the model to learn language-discriminative features directly from acoustic patterns, enabling robustness to code-switching. The mechanism is that the decoder never receives a language token; during training, the model must associate spectral and phonological patterns with output tokens, inducing an internal language classifier. The core assumption is that typologically diverse languages (Sino-Tibetan, Dravidian, Austronesian, Indo-European) have sufficiently distinct acoustic-phonological manifolds for a 1.7B model to separate them without explicit supervision. Evidence anchors are the abstract’s note on implicit language identification and Section 4.3’s discussion of Singapore’s multilingual environment. A break condition is intra-sentential code-switching where language boundary cues are subtle or lacking in training data.

**Mechanism 3: Cost-Accuracy Trade-off via Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning**
The claim is that fine-tuning a moderate-scale pretrained multilingual model with balanced data yields near-SOTA accuracy at a fraction of the cost of larger specialist systems because the pretrained model already encodes robust multilingual representations. The mechanism is that Qwen3-ASR’s pretraining provides a strong acoustic and linguistic prior; balanced fine-tuning adapts the output distribution and reinforces low-resource representations without needing massive capacity. The small model size drastically reduces compute and memory. The core assumption is that the base model’s pretraining includes enough phonetic/lexical coverage for all four languages such that fine-tuning can bridge the gap with data balancing. Evidence anchors are the abstract’s cost/accuracy comparison and Section 7’s discussion that model size alone does not determine accuracy. A break condition is if a target language had near-zero exposure in pretraining, creating a representation ceiling.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Multilingual Training Imbalance**
Why needed: The raw corpus (e.g., English 248.56h, Malay 58.98h) is highly skewed. Without correction, gradient updates are dominated by high-resource languages, causing underfitting for low-resource ones. Quick check: What is the English-to-Malay sample ratio? If sampling uniformly from raw data, what fraction of minibatches contain Malay utterances? How might this affect Tamil WER?

**Concept: Language-Agnostic vs. Language-Conditioned ASR**
Why needed: Singaporean speech features frequent code-switching. A language-tagged system would fail if the tag is wrong or ambiguous; the model must infer language from audio alone for robustness. Quick check: If you prepend a `<tamil>` token to all Tamil training samples, what happens during inference on a code-switched English-Tamil utterance with no language tag? How does the decoder’s probability distribution change?

**Concept: Upsampling as a Data-Level Balancing Strategy**
Why needed: Stochastic sampling methods (e.g., Whisper’s temperature-based) require tuning. Deterministic upsampling guarantees exact per-language parity by replication and subsampling, making the training distribution fully controlled. Quick check: In Stage 1, if a Tamil dataset has 5,000 samples and another has 20,000, what is the replication factor for the smaller one? After Stage 1, if the largest Tamil corpus has 30,000 samples and the largest Malay corpus has 15,000, what is the Stage 2 replication factor for Malay?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**
Qwen3-ASR (encoder-decoder transformer with Conformer encoder) -> BalancedSampler (two-stage deterministic upsampling) -> Training loop (AdamW, cosine LR, no language tags) -> Inference (autoregressive decode, no language token)

**Critical path**
1. Preprocessing: Download corpora, resample to 16kHz, extract 80-dim log-Mel, discard >30s audio, normalize text (lowercase, strip punctuation).
2. Balanced sampler: For each language, find max dataset size N*_l; replicate smaller datasets to N*_l. Then find global max N**; replicate each language to N**. Yields exactly 4×N** utterances with 25% per language.
3. Training loop: Draw batch from BalancedSampler, forward through Qwen3-ASR (no language token), compute CE loss, backward, optimizer step.
4. Inference: Audio -> features -> autoregressive decode -> output text.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Simplicity vs. adaptability: Deterministic upsampling is hyperparameter-free but may cause overfitting on small datasets via replication. Language-agnostic approach simplifies deployment but may hurt monolingual benchmarks.
- Model scale vs. resource: 1.7B matches SOTA but is too large for edge; 0.6B is edge-viable but has accuracy drops, especially on Tamil.
- Public data only vs. performance: Using only public corpora limits hours (~783h vs. MERaLiON’s ~120,000h), likely capping max performance on Singaporean speech.

**Failure signatures**
- High Tamil WER (>40%): Insufficient Tamil representation in base pretraining or upsampling inadequate.
- High inference latency variance (std > 0.5s): Model/implementation inefficiency.
- Good Librispeech but poor NSC WER: Overfit to read speech, lacks Singaporean accent coverage.
- No code-switched test evaluation: Likely poor performance on intra-sentential mixing, an admitted limitation.

**First 3 experiments**
1. Ablation: Balanced vs. Raw Sampling. Train Qwen3-ASR-1.7B with raw multilingual sampling and with balanced upsampling. Compare per-language WERs; expect dramatic Tamil/Malay improvement without English/Mandarin degradation.
2. Ablation: Language-Tag Conditioning. Train with language tags prepended. Evaluate on a code-switched test set (if available) vs. monolingual benchmarks; expect tags help monolingual but hurt code-switched performance.
3. Scaling Analysis: Evaluate 0.6B, 1.7B, and base (no fine-tune) on all 12 benchmarks. Compute accuracy delta per language; expect largest delta for Tamil/Malay from fine-tuning, with 1.7B generally superior.

## Limitations
- Base model details (Qwen3-ASR checkpoint IDs, pretraining language coverage) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Training procedure ambiguities: 48-hour wall-clock time not translated to epochs/steps; no early stopping; deterministic upsampling may cause overfitting.
- Evaluation lacks code-switched tests despite Singapore’s multilingual reality; monolingual benchmarks may overestimate real-world utility.
- Tamil WER remains high (39.19%), indicating residual representation gap.
- Cost/latency estimates depend on specific hardware (RTX PRO 6000); edge deployment not demonstrated.

## Confidence
- Balanced upsampling improves low-resource languages: **Medium** (results show improvement but no direct ablation against alternative balancing).
- Implicit language identification handles code-switching: **Low** (no code-switched evaluation; mechanism speculative).
- Moderate-scale fine-tuning matches SOTA large models with ~233× lower cost: **Medium-High** (error rates close but comparability uncertain due to potential differences in test sets/training data).

## Next Checks
1. Replication with ablation: Train both raw (imbalanced) and balanced variants; isolate gain from balancing by per-language WER differences.
2. Code-switching evaluation: Test on a diverse code-switched corpus (e.g., SEAME) to directly assess robustness of tag-free approach.
3. Upsampling strategy comparison: Replace deterministic upsampling with stochastic temperature-based sampling; compare WERs to verify necessity of proposed method.
