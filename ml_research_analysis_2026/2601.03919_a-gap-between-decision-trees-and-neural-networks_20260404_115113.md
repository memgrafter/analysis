# A Gap Between Decision Trees and Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03919
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03919
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Axis-aligned decision tree boundaries have infinite Radon total variation in dimensions >1, preventing accurate approximation by bounded-norm shallow neural networks; a constructed smooth barrier score achieves finite complexity and exact box recovery under a mild tube-mass condition, revealing an interpretability-accuracy tradeoff.

## Executive Summary
The paper examines when the geometric simplicity of axis‑aligned decision tree boundaries (interpretability) conflicts with accurate approximation by shallow neural networks. Using Radon total variation (RTV) to quantify decision boundary complexity, the authors prove that the hard indicator of such a tree has infinite RTV for dimensions >1. Moreover, two natural continuous surrogates—ramp and sigmoidal smoothing—also have infinite RTV, whereas Gaussian smoothing yields finite RTV but with an explicit exponential dependence on dimension. By separating classification (thresholding) from score learning, the authors construct a smooth barrier score with finite RTV that exactly recovers an axis‑aligned box at a fixed threshold. Under a mild tube‑mass condition, this score attains polynomial L1(P) calibration bounds and an explicit RTV upper bound in terms of box face measures. Experiments on synthetic unions of rectangles confirm a trade‑off between accuracy and RTV complexity, and show that threshold selection can mask the underlying geometric complexity of learned scores.

## Method Summary
Reproduction of the experiments uses uniform data in [0,1]^d (main dimension d=5, also d=10). A target axis‑aligned box with bounds ℓ=0.0471381679 and u=0.9528618321 defines the positive class. The dataset is split into 80 000 training, 20 000 validation, and 20 000 test points. Models are single‑hidden‑layer ReLU networks with widths in {8,16,32,64,128,256}, trained via mean squared error on raw logits using Adam (learning rate and weight decay not fully specified). The RTV proxy is ½(‖W‖_F² + ‖a‖_2²), where W are hidden layer weights and a output coefficients. Evaluation reports test Intersection‑over‑Union (IoU) under two thresholds: τ = 0 (fixed) and τ* optimized on validation. Results show RTV proxy increasing with width, while IoU improves more under optimized thresholds, illustrating the masking effect.

## Key Results
- Hard tree indicators and their piecewise‑linear or sigmoidal smoothings have infinite RTV in d>1, placing them outside the bounded‑norm shallow ReLU class.
- Gaussian smoothing yields finite RTV but scales exponentially with dimension (∼ exp(d/σ)).
- A smooth barrier score can be constructed to exactly recover any axis‑aligned box at threshold τ=1 with finite RTV; under a tube‑mass condition it achieves polynomial L1(P) calibration error, and its RTV is bounded by an expression involving the lower‑dimensional face measures of the box.
- Empirical width sweep shows RTV proxy grows with network width, while test IoU improves more when thresholds are tuned on validation data, indicating that threshold selection can hide high geometric complexity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Hard thresholds and naive smoothings have unbounded RTV
The hard indicator of an axis‑aligned box and its ramp/sigmoid smoothings possess infinite Radon total variation in dimensions >1. The RTV seminorm equals the L1 norm of the (d+1)st derivative of the Radon transform across all directions. For a hard indicator, any line orthogonal to a split direction contains a jump; the corresponding derivative contains Dirac‑like singularities whose L1 mass diverges. Ramp and sigmoid smoothings still create kinks along multiple non‑parallel hyperplanes, leading to unbounded directional variation (Proposition 5). This places these functions outside the class RBV²(ℝᵈ) of finite‑RTV functions, which characterizes infinite‑width, bounded‑norm single‑hidden‑layer ReLU networks. Evidence: [abstract]; [section 3.1]; [section 5.1].

### Mechanism 2: Exact thresholded classification via smooth finite‑RTV barriers
For any axis‑aligned box B, the authors construct a smooth monotone score S_B with finite RTV such that {x: S_B(x) ≥ 1} = B. The construction uses a product of scaled cutoffs ϑ_{λ,ε} that are infinitely smooth, equal 1 on [0,∞), and decay exponentially on (−∞,−ε]. Derivatives are controlled via Leibniz rule, yielding an explicit RTV bound in terms of the lower‑dimensional face measures of B. Under a mild tube‑mass condition on the data distribution (P(dist(X,∂B) ≤ t) ≤ C t^β), S_B attains polynomial L1(P) calibration error (Lemma 8, Theorem 9). This separates learning a thresholded classifier from learning a full scoring function.

### Mechanism 3: Threshold selection masks geometric complexity
Experiments show that shallow networks trained with MSE on logits can achieve high test IoU even as their RTV grows with width. Post‑hoc threshold optimization on validation data shifts the decision boundary to align better with the true box, effectively hiding the high‑complexity geometry of the underlying score. This creates an accuracy‑complexity trade‑off that is invisible if only the thresholded performance is examined; the RTV proxy remains large, indicating non‑axis‑aligned undulations in the score.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Radon Total Variation (RTV)**  
  Why needed: RTV is the geometric complexity measure that characterizes function spaces representable by infinite‑width, bounded‑norm ReLU networks. It governs the "bending" of level sets; infinite RTV means the function lies outside the class.  
  Quick check question: Why does a one‑dimensional jump cause infinite RTV in any dimension?

- **Concept: Shallow ReLU network representation via ridgelet transform**  
  Why needed: The paper analyzes the infinite‑width bounded‑norm limit—the RTV norm equals the minimum‑norm representation width. This connects finite‑width training to functional‑space constraints.  
  Quick check question: How does the weight decay objective in a wide ReLU network correspond to minimizing RTV?

- **Concept: Axis‑aligned decision trees as unions of boxes**  
  Why needed: The target models produce interpretable, piecewise‑constant predictions with boundaries along coordinate axes. Their discontinuity and geometry drive the hardness results.  
  Quick check question: What property of a box’s boundary leads to infinite RTV when projected onto certain directions?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
The analysis involves functions on ℝ^d; the hard tree indicator 1_A for a union of axis‑aligned boxes A; smoothed approximations (ramp, sigmoid, Gaussian); the smooth barrier score S_B; the RTV seminorm ||·||_R; and the training setup (single‑hidden‑layer ReLU network, MSE loss, weight decay). These components are linked by the fact that 1_A and naive smoothings have infinite RTV, Gaussian has finite exponential RTV, while S_B achieves finite RTV and exact box recovery.

### Critical path
1. Define interpretability as geometric simplicity of decision boundaries.
2. Introduce RTV as a complexity measure for level sets.
3. Prove infinite RTV for hard trees and naive smoothings (Theorem 3, Propositions 5–6).
4. Show Gaussian smoothing yields finite but dimension‑exponential RTV (Theorem 7).
5. Separate classification vs score learning; construct a finite‑RTV barrier score S_B that recovers a box exactly at τ=1.
6. Derive L1(P) calibration bound (Lemma 8) and explicit RTV upper bound (Theorem 9) under tube‑mass condition.
7. Run experiments on synthetic box data: vary network width, measure RTV proxy, test IoU under fixed vs optimized thresholds.

### Design tradeoffs
- Hard threshold vs smooth score: Hard trees are interpretable but lie outside bounded‑RTV class; smooth barriers retain finite RTV but require careful construction.
- Classification‑only vs full calibration: Matching a thresholded classifier is easier (finite‑RTV barrier exists); matching the full score distribution (e.g., via MSE) may force high RTV to capture sharp transitions.
- Threshold flexibility: Tuning τ post‑training can mask a poorly calibrated, high‑RTV score, yielding high IoU without simple geometry.
- Dimensionality: Gaussian smoothing’s RTV grows exponentially with d; barrier score’s RTV scales with lower‑dimensional face measures, which can be smaller for simple boxes.

### Failure signatures
- Naive smoothing (ramp, sigmoid) in d>1 still yields infinite RTV even if the score is continuous/differentiable; training such models with weight decay may not converge to a bounded‑norm solution.
- If the tube‑mass assumption fails (e.g., data mass heavily concentrated near ∂B), the calibration rate degrades; L1 error may not decay polynomially in λ.
- High RTV proxy in experiments (increasing with width) indicates the learned score has complex geometry; if business rules require interpretable gradients, such models violate the simplicity objective.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline**: Fit an axis‑aligned decision tree (e.g., CART) on the synthetic box data; visualize its decision boundary on a 2‑D slice.
2. **Width sweep**: Train single‑hidden‑layer ReLU MLPs of widths {8,16,32,64,128,256} with MSE loss and weight decay; for each width record the RTV proxy ½(‖W‖_F²+‖a‖_2²) and test raw MSE; report the width‑RTV frontier.
3. **Threshold study**: For the trained MLP at each width, evaluate test IoU under fixed threshold τ=0 (logit zero) and IoU‑optimized threshold τ* selected on held‑out validation; visualize slice heatmaps and decision boundaries, showing how IoU improves with threshold tuning while the underlying score geometry (RTV) is unchanged.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the RTV‑based accuracy‑interpretability tradeoff persist in deep neural networks? The representer theorems linking RTV to single‑hidden‑layer ReLU networks may not apply to deeper architectures; depth could alter the geometry of level sets and the RTV required for approximation.
2. Can we design training algorithms that directly bias networks toward low RTV scores while preserving accuracy? RTV is defined via the Radon transform and is not directly differentiable; standard losses (e.g., MSE with weight decay) do not control it, leading to potential RTV inflation with increasing width.
3. Do the main findings generalize to non‑axis‑aligned decision trees or other interpretable model classes? The analysis heavily relies on the product structure of axis‑aligned boxes. Oblique splits break this separability and may lead to different RTV behavior for smooth surrogates and barrier constructions.

## Limitations
- Theoretical results assume infinite‑width networks; finite‑width empirical RTV uses a weight‑norm proxy without proven correspondence.
- Calibration bounds for the smooth barrier score depend on a tube‑mass condition that may fail for boundary‑concentrated data.
- Experiments are limited to simple axis‑aligned boxes; applicability to complex trees or real datasets is unclear.
