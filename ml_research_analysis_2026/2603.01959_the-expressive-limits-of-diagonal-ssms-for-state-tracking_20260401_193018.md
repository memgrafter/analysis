# The Expressive Limits of Diagonal SSMs for State-Tracking

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01959
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01959
- **Reference count:** 25
- **Primary result:** Diagonal SSMs can track exactly the solvable groups whose subnormal series length matches the number of layers; a single layer fails on any non-Abelian group, and even multi-layer models often fail to learn non-Abelian state-tracking despite theoretical capacity.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates the expressive power of diagonal state‑space models (SSMs) for sequential state‑tracking. It proves that a single‑layer input‑dependent complex diagonal SSM cannot represent any non‑Abelian finite group at finite precision, while a *k*-layer diagonal SSM can track precisely those groups admitting a subnormal series of length *k* with Abelian factors. This places the expressivity frontier exactly within the class of solvable groups. Empirically, the authors evaluate Mamba, Negative Mamba, and AUSSM on cyclic, product, and non‑Abelian groups (S₃, A₄, A₅). Single‑layer models succeed on all Abelian groups—including large cycles—but fail on every non‑Abelian group. Two‑layer models succeed on C₂×C₄ (derived length 2) up to hundreds of steps, confirming the depth‑expressivity link. However, even two‑layer models consistently fail to learn S₃ and A₄ beyond training horizons, revealing a gap between theoretical expressivity and learnability with standard training.

## Method Summary
The task is sequential state‑tracking: given a sequence of group elements, predict the cumulative product at each step. Datasets consist of synthetic sequences from various finite groups—cyclic (C₂, C₆, C₂₄, C₆₀), products (C₂×C₄, C₃×C₆), non‑Abelian solvable (S₃, A₄), and non‑solvable (A₅)—sampled uniformly at random. Models include Mamba (eigenvalues in (0,1]), Negative Mamba (eigenvalues in [-1,1]), and AUSSM (unit‑modulus complex eigenvalues), in single‑layer and two‑layer configurations. Training uses a curriculum that gradually increases sequence length up to 60, with AdamW optimizer, grid‑searched learning rates (1e‑4, 5e‑4, 1e‑3), state dimensions 32/64/128, batch size 256, and embedding dimension 698. After training, models are evaluated on sequences up to 1000; success is >90% accuracy on lengths ≥100, and the longest successful length is reported.

## Key Results
- Single‑layer diagonal SSMs succeed on all Abelian groups (e.g., Negative Mamba on C₂ generalizes to >1000 steps) but fail on any non‑Abelian group.
- Two‑layer models succeed on groups with a subnormal series of length 2 (e.g., C₂×C₄, up to 360 steps), confirming the theoretical expressivity bound.
- Even two‑layer models fail to learn S₃ and A₄ beyond training lengths, highlighting a gap between expressivity and learnability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Single‑layer limitation.** The state update of any input‑dependent diagonal SSM reduces to an elementwise affine transformation. For any coordinate, if the associated rotation is non‑neutral (eigenvalues ≠ 1), the state can be rescaled to a neutral rotation without loss of expressiveness. In the neutral rotation case (|λ|=1, λ≠1), distinct inputs that rotate around different centers cause a net translation when applied sequentially. This translation accumulates without bound under repeated application, contradicting finite‑precision tracking of a finite group. Hence all inputs must share the same rotation center in every coordinate, forcing commutativity. Therefore a single‑layer diagonal SSM cannot express non‑Abelian state‑tracking at finite precision. The argument assumes finite‑precision arithmetic and breaks if models use non‑diagonal transitions or reset mechanisms.

**Mechanism 2: Multi‑layer expressivity matches subnormal series.** Stacking *k* layers expands expressivity exactly to groups that possess a subnormal series of length *k* with Abelian factor groups. A layer‑peeling argument shows that the first layer tracks a quotient G/Gₖ₋₁ which must be Abelian because the layer’s diagonal transitions commute; inductively, subsequent layers reveal descending normal subgroups with Abelian quotients. Conversely, given such a series, one can construct an SSM where each layer simulates the corresponding Abelian factor via roots‑of‑unity rotations, and interlayer connections encode the semidirect product structure (Theorem 2 and S₃ construction in Section 5).

**Mechanism 3: Learnability gap despite theoretical capacity.** Although *k*-layer diagonal SSMs can theoretically track any solvable group with derived length *k*, standard gradient‑based training often fails to discover solutions that generalize beyond training sequence lengths. For instance, two‑layer Negative Mamba fails on S₃ and A₄ despite their derived length being 2. The paper shows that initializing parameters near the analytically derived S₃ solution improves training and yields generalization to four times the training length, implying that suitable solutions exist within the hypothesis class but are hard to find with random initialization and typical optimizers. This gap suggests optimization barriers or inductive biases that hinder learning of non‑Abelian group structures.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input *xₜ* → per‑layer parameters (*Aᵢ*(*xₜ*), *bᵢ*(*xₜ*)) (diagonal matrix and bias) → affine update: *hᵢ*ᵗ = *Aᵢ*(*xₜ*) *hᵢ*ᵗ⁻¹ + *bᵢ*(*xₜ*) (with initial state identity and interlayer skip connection) → final state *hₖ*ᵀ → decoder *ŷ* = dec(*hₖ*ᵀ) or dec(*hₖ*ᵀ, *x*ₜ).  
**Critical path:**  
1. Compute the group’s derived length (shortest subnormal series with Abelian factors); this equals the minimum number of diagonal layers needed.  
2. If derived length > available layers, expressivity is impossible regardless of training.  
3. For expressible groups, assign each layer to track one Abelian factor, choosing eigenvalues appropriate for the factor (e.g., negative for C₂, complex unit for Cₙ).  
4. Design interlayer connections to encode the semidirect product via rotation‑center offsets.  
5. Train with curriculum; if a solvable group with sufficient depth still fails, suspect learnability (optimization) rather than expressivity.  
**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Diagonal vs. block‑diagonal: Diagonal gives O(*d*) efficiency but restricts to solvable groups; 2×2 blocks can represent non‑solvable groups.  
- Eigenvalue range: Real positive (Mamba) cannot handle groups requiring sign flips (C₂); real negative (Negative Mamba) handles C₂; complex unit (AUSSM) handles any cyclic group.  
- State dimension *d*: Must be at least the sum of factor orders to avoid state collision; too small causes overlapping representations.  
- Decoder complexity: Must map continuous state to discrete group elements; may require nonlinear readout (e.g., MLP). Simple linear decoders suffice for Abelian groups but fail for non‑Abelian coset structures.  
**Failure signatures:**  
- Single‑layer works on Abelian groups but fails on any non‑Abelian group (expected).  
- Two‑layer fails on groups with derived length 2 (e.g., S₃, A₄) despite theoretical capability → learnability gap.  
- Model succeeds on training lengths (≤60) but fails to generalize to >100 steps → overfitting or shortcut solutions.  
- Manual initialization near analytic solution enables learning → indicates poor optimization landscape.  
**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Abelian baseline:** Train single‑layer Negative Mamba (and AUSSM) on C₂, C₆, C₂×C₄. Expect success and generalization to >1000 steps. Validates single‑layer expressivity.  
2. **Two‑layer derived‑length‑2 test:** Train two‑layer Negative Mamba on S₃. Expect failure (as per Table 2). Confirms learnability gap even when expressivity is sufficient.  
3. **Analytic initialization test:** Initialize two‑layer AUSSM near the analytic S₃ solution from Section 5.3 and train on short sequences (max length ≤20). Expect success and generalization to >4× training length (e.g., >100). Demonstrates that solutions exist but are hard to find with random init.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. *Why do multi‑layer diagonal SSMs fail to learn non‑Abelian state‑tracking despite theoretical expressivity?* The gap is noted but its root cause (optimization landscape, inductive bias) remains unknown.  
2. *What is the precise structure of the optimization landscape for diagonal SSMs on non‑Abelian tasks?* No characterization exists; the paper observed that good solutions lie in a basin but did not quantify curvature or connectivity.  
3. *Can systematic initialization strategies reliably enable learning of non‑Abelian state‑tracking across architectures?* Only one experiment (analytic S₃ init) showed promise; generalization to other models and groups is open.  
4. *Do modifications like block‑diagonal transitions or complex‑valued attention in Transformers match the expressivity frontier for non‑solvable groups?* The paper conjectures that block‑diagonal SSMs could represent non‑solvable groups, but this is untested.  
5. *Is the learnability gap fundamentally due to diagonality, or would similar issues arise in other structured SSMs (e.g., low‑rank, triangular)?* Non‑diagonal SSMs have higher expressivity, but their learnability on solvable groups is unexplored.

## Limitations
- Expressivity analysis limited to diagonal transition matrices; results may not extend to block‑diagonal or dense recurrent structures.  
- Theoretical proofs assume finite‑precision arithmetic; practical implementations using float32 might behave differently.  
- The decoder mapping is underspecified, leaving its role in learnability unclear.  
- Empirical evaluation is restricted to small groups; scalability to larger or more complex groups is unknown.  
- The learnability gap could stem from the specific training regime (curriculum, optimizer) rather than an inherent impossibility.

## Confidence
- Single‑layer diagonal SSMs cannot express non‑Abelian groups: **High** (rigorous proof under model assumptions).  
- *k*-layer expressivity exactly matches subnormal series length: **Medium** (construction given but relies on exact affine encoding and finite precision).  
- Multi‑layer learnability gap exists: **Medium** (empirical evidence strong but training‑dependent; may be mitigated by better optimization).
