# Kernel Single-Index Bandits: Estimation, Inference, and Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18938
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18938
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A kernelized ε-greedy algorithm (K-SIEGE) achieves Õ(√T) regret while providing asymptotically valid confidence regions for both the low-dimensional index direction and the nonparametric reward link function in single-index contextual bandits.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses contextual bandit problems where the reward depends on a latent linear projection of covariates through an unknown nonparametric link function—a semiparametric single-index model. Under adaptive sampling, the authors propose K-SIEGE, which integrates a Stein-based estimator for the index direction with inverse-propensity-weighted kernel ridge regression for the reward function. The analysis establishes asymptotic normality for the index estimator and a directional functional central limit theorem for the link function estimator, enabling valid pointwise inference. Finite-time regret scales as Õ(√T), showing that statistical efficiency is maintained despite the nonparametric uncertainty. Simulations demonstrate that the proposed confidence intervals achieve nominal coverage and are shorter than those derived from uniform RKHS bounds.

## Method Summary
K-SIEGE operates in two intertwined threads. First, a Stein-based estimating equation uses observed rewards and a (potentially estimated) score function to solve for the single-index direction β, exploiting the moment condition E[Y S(X)] = µ β. Second, inverse-propensity weighting (IPW) corrects for the bias introduced by adaptive data collection, allowing kernel ridge regression (KRR) to consistently estimate the unknown link function f in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). An ε-greedy exploration strategy ensures sufficient excitation for both estimation tasks. The regret analysis relies on Lipschitz continuity of f and bounds on the kernel ridge regression error under theIPW objective.

## Key Results
- Asymptotic normality for the Stein-based estimator of the index direction β under adaptive sampling, enabling asymptotically valid confidence regions.
- A directional functional central limit theorem for the IPW-KRR estimator of the link function f, yielding asymptotically pointwise valid confidence intervals.
- Finite-time expected regret of order Õ(√T) under standard Lipschitz and boundedness assumptions.
- Simulation studies confirm proper coverage for both β and f, with CLT-based intervals notably shorter than those from uniform RKHS concentration bounds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success hinges on two synergistic components: (1) The Stein estimator identifies β without needing to estimate f, leveraging a moment condition that remains unbiased under adaptive sampling when combined with IPW corrections. (2) IPW-KRR isolates the causal reward function f from the contextual distribution shift caused by the bandit’s exploration policy. The ε-greedy exploration provides the minimal overlap needed for consistent estimation of both components. The semiparametric structure reduces the problem dimensionality: learning a one-dimensional index plus a scalar function is statistically easier than a full multivariate nonparametric model, which is reflected in the √T regret rate.

## Foundational Learning
- **Contextual bandits:** A sequential decision-making framework where an agent selects arms based on context to maximize cumulative reward. *Why needed:* Formalizes the online learning problem with partial feedback. *Quick check:* Can you derive the regret decomposition into estimation error and exploration cost?
- **Single-index models:** Semi-parametric models where the response depends on a linear projection of covariates via an unknown link. *Why needed:* Reduces cardinality of nonparametric component, enabling efficient estimation. *Quick check:* Why is β identifiable only up to scale without normalizing f?
- **Kernel ridge regression (KRR):** Nonparametric regression in an RKHS with ℓ2 regularization. *Why needed:* Provides a flexible, smooth estimator for f with computational predictability. *Quick check:* How does the kernel’s eigenvalue decay affect the convergence rate of the KRR estimator?
- **Inverse propensity weighting (IPW):** Technique to correct sampling bias by reweighting observations by the inverse probability of being selected. *Why needed:* Makes the off-policy bandit data appear as if from a fixed design, crucial for consistent f estimation. *Quick check:* What happens if the propensity scores are very small in some regions?
- **Stein’s method & estimating equations:** Uses derivative information (score) to construct unbiased moment conditions for β. *Why needed:* Provides a tractable way to estimate the low-dimensional parameter without estimating the high-dimensional nuisance f. *Quick check:* Why does E[Y S(X)] = µ β hold under the single-index model?
- **Martingale central limit theorem (CLT)::** Asymptotic tool for dependent data from adaptive sampling. *Why needed:* Underlies the asymptotic normality claims for β and f under the bandit’s sequential, dependent observations. *Quick check:* What conditions on the martingale difference array are needed for the CLT to hold?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Raw data (context X_t, reward Y_t, chosen arm A_t) -> Compute propensity score π_t(A_t|X_t) -> IPW correction: reweight Y_t -> Kernel matrix K -> Solve IPW-KRR for f_hat; Also, compute score S(X_t) (or its estimate) -> Solve Stein equation for β_hat; Then, for new context X, compute index Xᵀβ_hat, predict reward f_hat(Xᵀβ_hat) for each arm -> Arm selection via ε-greedy.

**Critical path:**  
1. Accurate propensity estimation (from ε-greedy policy).  
2. Stable IPW-KRR solution (regularization balances bias/variance).  
3. Consistent score estimation if S unknown (currently assumed known).  
4. Combine β_hat and f_hat for decision.  
Failure in any step degrades both regret and inference.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Kernel choice: Universal kernels (e.g., Gaussian) offer flexibility but may require careful bandwidth tuning; misspecification induces bias in f_hat.  
- Exploration rate ε: Too small → poor coverage for β; too large → higher regret. Theoretical analysis uses a decaying schedule.  
- Regularization λ in KRR: Balances approximation error (RKHS norm) and estimation error (sample variance); cross-validation is impractical online.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Inferred confidence intervals for β or f have empirical coverage significantly below nominal (e.g., 80% aimed, 60% observed).  
- Regret grows super-linearly (e.g., O(T^{2/3})) in practice, violating Õ(√T).  
- β_hat destabilizes or oscillates when the score function S is estimated poorly.  
- Computational latency per round increases quadratically with arm count due to IPW-KRR per arm.

**First three experiments to run:**  
1. **Synthetic validation:** Generate data from a known single-index model (e.g., f(u)=sin(u), β true, Gaussian X). Run K-SIEGE, check that (a) regret curve matches Õ(√T) slope, (b) 95% CIs for β cover true β ~95% of the time at final T, (c) pointwise CIs for f have ~95% coverage at a grid of u values.  
2. **Ablation on score estimation:** Replace true score S with a kernel density estimator or a neural net plug-in. Measure coverage degradation for β and increase in regret. Vary the sample size used to estimate S.  
3. **Comparison to benchmarks:** Against (a) a random exploration policy, (b) a non-IPW KRR (oracle off-policy learner), (c) a fully nonparametric bandit (no single-index restriction). Compare regret and, where possible, inference validity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
- **Question:** How to handle an unknown score function in the Stein-based index estimator?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Remark 1 states: "In practice, when the covariate distribution is unknown, the score function may be replaced by a suitable estimator. We leave the analysis of such plug-in approaches to future work."  
- **Why unresolved:** The moment equation E[Y S(X)] = µ β assumes S is known. When S is replaced by an estimator ˆS, the resulting estimating equation may be biased and the martingale CLT may fail without controlling the estimation error of S.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive the asymptotic distribution of ˆβ under a plug-in score estimator with a known rate. Characterize the additional variance or bias terms. Propose a consistent (and possibly adaptive) score estimator for the bandit setting and validate coverage via simulations.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question:** Can the methodology be extended to multi-index or partially linear reward structures while retaining inference guarantees?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion lists "multi-index or partially linear reward structures" as a future direction.  
- **Why unresolved:** The Stein-based identification relies on a single low-dimensional projection. In multi-index models, β is not uniquely defined without additional constraints. Partially linear models mix a parametric component with a nonparametric component, requiring different estimation and inference strategies.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** For partially linear models, adapt IPW-KRR to estimate the nonparametric part and derive a Stein-type equation for the linear coefficients. For multi-index, impose structure (e.g., additive projections) to achieve identifiability. Extend the CLT arguments to these settings and demonstrate finite-sample performance through simulation.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question:** How to construct anytime-valid (uniform) confidence bands for the index directions and reward functions that hold simultaneously for all stopping times?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion mentions "uniform or anytime-valid confidence bands for sequential monitoring" as a needed extension.  
- **Why unresolved:** The current confidence intervals are pointwise and asymptotic for a fixed inference time. They do not account for multiplicity when examining results repeatedly or stopping early based on interim outcomes, leading to inflated type I error.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop time-uniform exponential concentration for the martingale arrays appearing in the CLT proofs. Use self-normalized or supermartingale techniques to construct confidence sequences. Prove coverage holds uniformly over all stopping rules and compare the widths to the fixed-time intervals in simulations.

**Open Question 4**  
- **Question:** What is the robustness of the proposed inference under mild violations of the single-index model?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion advocates studying "robustness to model misspecification" as a future direction.  
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical guarantees require exact adherence to Y = f(X^T β) + ε. In practice, small departures (e.g., omitted interactions, slightly different links, or heterogeneous residual variance) may bias estimators and degrade coverage.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Introduce mis-specification metrics (e.g., the distance from the model manifold). Study asymptotic bias of β and f estimators under local alternatives. Quantify the coverage failure rate as a function of the deviation. Develop robust adjustments (e.g., weighted estimating equations, flexible kernels) and evaluate via simulations with controlled misspecification.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis imposes strong conditions: bounded contexts/arms, Lipschitz link, correctly specified kernel, and eigenvalue conditions that may not hold in real datasets.
- Inference results are asymptotic and pointwise; finite-sample coverage may be poor, and no uniform confidence sequences are provided.
- The Õ(√T) regret bound is under idealized assumptions; practical performance is sensitive to kernel choice, regularization, and the exploration schedule.
- Computational cost per round is high due to solving kernel ridge regression, especially with large action spaces.
