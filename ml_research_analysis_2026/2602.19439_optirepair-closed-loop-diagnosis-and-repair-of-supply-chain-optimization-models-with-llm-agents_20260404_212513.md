# OptiRepair: Closed-Loop Diagnosis and Repair of Supply Chain Optimization Models with LLM Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19439  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19439  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Trained 8B-parameter models achieve 81.7% Rational Recovery Rate on 976 multi-echelon problems, far surpassing the best API model's 42.2%.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents OptiRepair, a framework that uses LLM agents to automatically diagnose and repair infeasible supply chain optimization models. The approach employs a two-phase closed-loop process: Phase 1 iteratively repairs any linear program using solver Irreducible Inconsistent Subsystem (IIS) feedback, while Phase 2 validates operational rationality through five inventory-theory checks. Training two 8B-parameter models with self-taught reasoning and solver-verified rewards yields a Rational Recovery Rate (RRR) of 81.7%, compared to 42.2% for the best API model. The performance gap is concentrated in Phase 1 (solver interaction), where trained models achieve 97.2% recovery versus 27.6% for APIs, while Phase 2 rationality pass rates remain high for APIs (76.3%). The results indicate that domain-specialized training is crucial for solver-facing tasks, and explicit rationality specification proves more impactful than scaling model size.

## Method Summary
OptiRepair operates in two phases. Phase 1: Given an infeasible linear program, an LLM agent proposes modifications based on IIS feedback from a solver, iterating until feasibility is restored. Phase 2: The repaired model is checked against five inventory-theory rationality criteria (e.g., non-negative inventory, sensible order quantities). Two 8B-parameter models are trained using a self-taught reasoning paradigm, where the model generates reasoning traces, and rewards are derived from solver-verified outcomes (feasibility + rationality pass). The training emphasizes explicit rationality specification to ensure repaired models are not only feasible but also operationally sound.

## Key Results
- Trained models achieve 81.7% Rational Recovery Rate (RRR) vs. 42.2% for the best API model.
- Phase 1 repair: 97.2% success for trained models vs. 27.6% for API models.
- Phase 2 rationality pass rates are high for APIs (76.3%), suggesting operational reasoning is easier than solver interaction.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The closed-loop design leverages precise solver IIS feedback to guide targeted repairs, avoiding random or heuristic modifications. Explicit rationality checks enforce domain knowledge, filtering out feasible but impractical solutions. Self-taught reasoning enables the model to learn from its own repair attempts without extensive human annotations, while solver-verified rewards align training directly with true repair success, bridging the gap between natural language understanding and mathematical optimization.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Closed-Loop Diagnosis & Repair**  
   *Why needed*: Enables iterative refinement using solver feedback, ensuring repairs address root causes of infeasibility.  
   *Quick check*: Does the agent incorporate solver feedback after each modification attempt?

2. **Solver IIS Feedback**  
   *Why needed*: Identifies minimal constraint subsets causing infeasibility, allowing precise edits.  
   *Quick check*: Is IIS output parsed and explicitly referenced in the repair prompt?

3. **Inventory-Theory Rationality Checks**  
   *Why needed*: Ensures repaired models are operationally viable, not just mathematically feasible.  
   *Quick check*: Are all five inventory checks (e.g., non-negative inventory) enforced before final acceptance?

4. **Self-Taught Reasoning**  
   *Why needed*: Generates domain-specific reasoning traces without human annotation, adapting to optimization syntax.  
   *Quick check*: Does training use model-generated reasoning evaluated by solver outcomes?

5. **Solver-Verified Rewards**  
   *Why needed*: Grounds rewards in actual solver behavior, preventing reward hacking and misalignment.  
   *Quick check*: Are rewards computed from binary feasibility and rationality pass/fail signals?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map**: LLM Agent → IIS Feedback Parser → Repair Module → Solver → Rationality Checker → Reward Calculator → Training Loop  
- **Critical Path**: IIS feedback drives iterative repair; rationality checks validate final model; solver-verified rewards close the training loop.  
- **Design Tradeoffs**: Repair accuracy vs. iteration count; explicit rationality constraints vs. model flexibility; training complexity vs. generalization to new problem structures.  
- **Failure Signatures**: Persistent infeasibility after multiple iterations; rationality check failures; excessive iteration counts; degradation on problems with different echelon counts or constraint types.  
- **First Experiments**:  
  1. Test on held-out problems with varying echelon counts (2–10) and constraint types to probe generalization.  
  2. Ablate self-taught reasoning and solver-verified rewards to isolate their contributions.  
  3. Measure wall-clock repair time and compare with human expert repairs on a representative sample.

## Limitations
- Benchmark restricted to 976 multi-echelon LPs; generalizability to larger, real-world, or nonlinear models is uncertain.
- Operational rationality relies on only five inventory checks, omitting capacity, lead times, and demand uncertainty.
- Solver-specific IIS feedback may not transfer across solvers or alternative LP formulations.

## Confidence
- Trained model RRR (81.7%) vs. API RRR (42.2%): Medium  
- Phase 1 repair gap (97.2% vs. 27.6%): Medium  
- Domain-specialized training closes performance gaps: Medium  
- Explicit rationality specification > scaling model size: Low (only 8B scale tested)

## Next Checks
1. Test on diverse, held-out supply chain problems (varying echelons, constraints, solvers) to evaluate generalization and solver independence.
2. Ablate training components (self-taught reasoning, solver-verified rewards, explicit rationality) and scale beyond 8B to isolate contributions and scaling effects.
3. Measure repair efficiency (iterations, wall time) and compare with human experts on a subset to assess practical utility and cost.
