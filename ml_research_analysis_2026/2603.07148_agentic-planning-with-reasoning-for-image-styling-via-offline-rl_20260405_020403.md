# Agentic Planning with Reasoning for Image Styling via Offline RL
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.07148
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07148
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The proposed agentic planning framework with offline RL improves multi-step image styling by up to 7.3 points over the Edit-Only baseline and outperforms GPT-4o zero-shot on image quality in 10 out of 11 configurations.

## Executive Summary
Direct prompt-based image editing fails on complex multi-step transformations due to vague prompts. This paper introduces a structured agentic planning framework that decomposes the task into four stages: extracting a 10‑dimensional structured context from the input image, generating an action sequence with per‑step chain‑of‑thought reasoning, synthesizing precise editing instructions, and executing them with a frozen image editor. The planner is trained via offline reinforcement learning on synthetic trajectories generated by a teacher model (Qwen3‑VL), using reward‑weighted methods (RW, SW) to emphasize high‑quality plans. Three datasets of ~10K trajectories each were created. Results show significant gains over the baseline and superior performance compared to GPT‑4o zero‑shot. Human validation of the synthetic data yields a 77% pass rate, demonstrating the pipeline’s effectiveness.

## Method Summary
A teacher model (Qwen3‑VL‑8B‑Instruct) generates synthetic training trajectories. For each image‑prompt pair, the teacher first extracts a structured 10‑dimensional context describing visual attributes (e.g., time of day, architecture). It then produces a sequence of tool‑based actions from a library of orthogonal editing primitives, accompanied by chain‑of‑thought reasoning. These actions are converted into a natural‑language instruction for a frozen image editor (Qwen‑Image‑Edit), which modifies the image. The teacher scores the trajectory on multiple quality dimensions (0–5). To train a smaller student planner (4B/8B parameters), the authors apply offline RL techniques: standard supervised learning (SL), filtering by high reward (R), reward‑weighted regression (RW), standardized reward‑weighted (SW), and DPO. RW weights each trajectory’s gradient by `max(r_i−3.0,0)`; SW standardizes rewards per batch. The student uses LoRA (rank 16) on the language model, optionally with cached visual embeddings.

## Key Results
- RW and SW improve over the Edit‑Only baseline by up to 7.3 points on combined visual quality and instruction‑following metrics.
- Student models outperform GPT‑4o zero‑shot planning on image quality in 10 of 11 tested configurations.
- Human evaluation of teacher‑generated trajectories reports a 77% pass rate, confirming synthetic data quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Structured Decomposition Enables Reliable Multi‑Step Planning**  
The framework breaks down complex styling into four stages with a structured context and a library of orthogonal editing tools. Each tool controls a distinct visual aspect (e.g., `time_of_day` affects lighting but not architecture), enabling predictable composition. The planner generates actions with explicit chain‑of‑thought reasoning, grounding decisions in the current context and goal. This explicit planning succeeds where direct prompting fails. A break condition occurs if context extraction misses a key attribute, leading to semantic drift. Orthogonality is critical; non‑orthogonal tools could interfere and break compositionality.

**Mechanism 2: Reward‑Weighted Offline RL Distills High‑Quality Behaviors**  
Reward‑weighted methods (RW, SW) effectively utilize the teacher’s scalar quality scores. RW weights each trajectory’s gradient by `max(r_i−3.0,0)`, upweighting high‑reward samples while retaining all data. SW standardizes rewards within a batch to handle varying difficulty across inputs. These approaches provide a denser training signal than binary filtering or pairwise preferences, preserving diversity and emphasizing quality distinctions. Consistent improvements over SL and filtering are observed. However, if the teacher’s reward is systematically biased, RW/SW may amplify those errors.

**Mechanism 3: Synthetic Data Pipeline Provides Scalable High‑Quality Trajectories**  
The teacher model generates full trajectories (context, actions, reasoning, edited image, reward) at scale. Human validation of 3,000 samples yields a 77% pass rate, indicating that synthetic plans are generally faithful and executable. This shows that a strong teacher can produce data sufficient to train compact student planners without costly human annotation. The pass rate, while above the hypothesized threshold (~70%), leaves limited margin, suggesting potential fragility. Success hinges on teacher quality and alignment of its reward with human preferences.

## Foundational Learning
- **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) Reasoning**  
  *Why needed:* The planner must generate interpretable, step‑by‑step justifications for each tool call to ensure coherent multi‑step planning and enable reward‑weighted learning. Without explicit reasoning, the student cannot learn *why* actions are chosen.  
  *Quick check:* Given the goal "Transform desert to moss garden", design a 3‑step plan where each step's reasoning explicitly references the current visual state and the transformation goal.

- **Offline Reinforcement Learning (RL)**  
  *Why needed:* Training requires distilling high‑quality behaviors from a fixed dataset of teacher trajectories without further interaction with the environment. The reward signal (quality score) must be leveraged without online policy updates.  
  *Quick check:* How does reward‑weighted regression relate to importance sampling, and why is it suitable for offline learning from heterogeneous‑quality data?

- **Compositional Tool Libraries**  
  *Why needed:* Complex styling is achieved by combining a finite set of orthogonal primitives (e.g., `time_of_day`, `season`, `mood_lighting`). Orthogonality ensures that tool effects compose predictably, avoiding interference.  
  *Quick check:* Why is orthogonality critical for planning? What would happen if `mood_lighting` also affected `color_grading`?

- **Structured Context Representation**  
  *Why needed:* The planner must be state‑aware; a 10‑dimensional symbolic encoding of the current image grounds planning in explicit attributes rather than implicit visual features, enabling reasoning like "currently midday, need sunset".  
  *Quick check:* Is a frozen text‑only model sufficient for planning if given the extracted context? What does adding visual features (vision‑language) buy you?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Context Extraction (Stage 1, frozen Qwen3‑VL) -> Action Planning (Stage 2, student model with LoRA) -> Instruction Synthesis (Stage 3, rule‑based or small model) -> Frozen Editor (Stage 4, Qwen‑Image‑Edit)

**Critical path**  
Input image and prompt → structured context → action plan with reasoning → natural‑language editing instruction → final image. During data generation, a teacher assigns a quality reward; at inference, no reward is used.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Frozen editor vs. end‑to‑end:* Separating planning from execution avoids expensive image model gradients but limits adaptation to editor quirks.  
- *Synthetic data vs. human annotation:* Scales to large datasets but depends on teacher quality and may introduce distribution skew.  
- *RW vs. SW:* RW uses absolute reward weighting; SW uses within‑batch standardization. SW adapts to varying difficulty but may downweight truly exceptional trajectories in an easy batch.

**Failure signatures**  
- Low validation reward (avg <3.5): indicates poor plan quality; check for missing context attributes or degenerate actions.  
- Human validation rejections concentrated in specific configurations (e.g., preservation constraints) suggest tool library or reward model limitations.  
- DPO collapse: if chosen‑rejected pairs have tiny reward differences (Δr <0.5), DPO may not learn meaningful preferences.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Train Standard SL on Simple dataset; verify it beats Edit‑Only on planning metrics (expected SW win by ~2–3 points overall).  
2. Ablate RW vs. SL on Regular dataset; compute validation reward trajectory and confirm RW improvement, especially on semantic accuracy.  
3. Pilot human validation: manually label 100 samples from each dataset; expect >70% pass rate. If lower, audit teacher generations for systematic artifacts.

## Limitations
- Missing implementation specifics: exact tool parameter schemas and reward rubric details are not fully provided, hindering faithful replication.
- Modest human validation pass rate (77%) leaves little margin; synthetic data quality may be fragile, especially for complex preservation constraints.
- Teacher dependency: student performance is bounded by teacher quality and potential reward bias; no analysis of failure modes per tool.
