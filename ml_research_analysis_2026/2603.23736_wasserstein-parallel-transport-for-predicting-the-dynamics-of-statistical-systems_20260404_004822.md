# Wasserstein Parallel Transport for Predicting the Dynamics of Statistical Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23736
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23736
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces Wasserstein Parallel Trends (WPT), a method for counterfactual prediction of probability distribution dynamics using parallel transport on the Wasserstein manifold, with theoretical stability guarantees and empirical superiority over baselines in low-to-moderate dimensions.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of predicting counterfactual dynamics for systems described by evolving probability distributions—a setting where standard vector space methods fail. The authors propose Wasserstein Parallel Transport (WPT), which transfers temporal dynamics across different initial distributions by parallel transporting tangent velocity fields along optimal transport geodesics. The core innovation is a tractable "fanning scheme" for approximating parallel transport on the Wasserstein manifold, accompanied by the first theoretical stability guarantees for this setting. WPT generalizes the classical parallel trends assumption (common in causal inference) from means to entire distributions and yields closed-form expressions for Gaussian systems. Experiments on synthetic Gaussian systems and single-cell RNA sequencing data show lower Wasserstein distance errors compared to mean-shift and non-parallel Brenier baselines, particularly when distributional shape differences matter.

## Method Summary
WPT predicts the future state of a counterfactual distribution by transferring tangent velocity fields from a control trajectory. For a sequence of control measures \(\{\nu_i\}\) and a counterfactual initial measure \(\mu_0^*\), the algorithm iteratively: (1) estimates the control tangent velocity \(\nabla\phi_i\) between \(\nu_i\) and \(\nu_{i+1}\) via discrete optimal transport (barycentric displacements); (2) approximates the parallel transport of \(\nabla\phi_i\) from \(\nu_i\) to the current counterfactual state \(\mu_i^*\) using a fanning scheme that propagates Jacobi fields along the Wasserstein geodesic and projects via an RKHS-based Helmholtz–Hodge decomposition; (3) exponentiates the transported field to obtain \(\mu_{i+1}^*\). The fanning scheme discretizes the geodesic into \(N\) steps, and the RKHS regression enforces the tangent space constraint. The method recovers the classical parallel trends for averages as a special case and admits closed-form Gaussian PT.

## Key Results
- WPT achieves lower \(W_2\) prediction errors than mean-shift and non-parallel Brenier baselines on synthetic Gaussian trajectories with non-stationary covariances and on two scRNA-seq datasets.
- The fanning approximation enjoys an \(O(1/N)\) error bound under strong regularity (uniformly Lipschitz velocity, smooth densities), and the accumulated trajectory error grows at most exponentially with the number of steps.
- WPT without the RKHS projection (WPT⁻) often performs best in higher dimensions due to reduced estimation error, while the full WPT with projection is more robust in lower dimensions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Jacobi-based Parallel Transport Approximation
- **Claim:** Wasserstein Parallel Transport approximates Levi-Civita parallel transport on the Wasserstein manifold by locally transporting along perturbed Lagrangian paths using Jacobi fields, enabling tractable counterfactual prediction.
- **Mechanism:** For a regular Wasserstein geodesic \(\mu_t\) with velocity \(\nabla\phi_t\), the parallel transport of a tangent vector \(v \in T_\nu\mathcal{P}_2(\mathcal{M})\) to \(\mu_s\) is approximated as \(v_s \approx \Pi_{\mu_s}(s^{-1} j_{\nu,u}(0,v)(s))\), where \(j_{\nu,u}\) is a Jacobi field on the base manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) associated with the infinitesimal variation from the ODE \(\dot{x}=u(x)\) generating the geodesic. The projection \(\Pi_{\mu_s}\) enforces the tangent space constraint.
- **Core assumption:** The Wasserstein geodesic between reference distributions is *strongly regular* (uniformly Lipschitz velocity field, finite \(L^2\) norm). The base space \(\mathcal{M}\) admits stable Jacobi field approximations (e.g., via the fanning scheme) and has adequate injectivity radius.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract introduces the "fanning scheme" and provides "first theoretical stability guarantees." Section 3.2 derives the Jacobi-based approximation and cites Gigli (2012) for the connection between Wasserstein and base-manifold parallel transport along regular curves.
- **Break condition:** If the Wasserstein geodesic is not strongly regular (velocity field not uniformly Lipschitz), the Jacobi field approximation and stability bounds may fail. For \(\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^d\) with non-convex domains or non-smooth densities, \(C^4\) boundary and bounded density assumptions (Proposition 3.7) can be violated, breaking existence.

### Mechanism 2: Generalization of Parallel Trends
- **Claim:** The Wasserstein Parallel Trends (WPT) assumption generalizes the classical parallel trends assumption for averages, requiring that tangent velocities (distributional trends) are parallel-transported across initial measures rather than simply their means being parallel.
- **Mechanism:** For a control trajectory \(\{\nu_i\}\) and a counterfactual treatment trajectory \(\{\mu_i^*\}\), WPT requires \(\nabla\phi_i^* = \text{PT}_{\nu_i\to\mu_i^*}(\nabla\phi_i)\), where \(\nabla\phi_i = \log_{\nu_i}(\nu_{i+1})\) is the control velocity. This constructs a "parallel" curve in Wasserstein space.
- **Core assumption:** The control and counterfactual trajectories can be represented by regular Wasserstein geodesics between consecutive time points; the initial discrepancy \(\nu_i\) vs. \(\mu_i^*\) can be viewed as a stable transformation along time.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states that WPT "recovers the classical parallel trends assumption for averages as a special case." Section 4.2 and Theorem 4.6 prove that if tangent velocities are parallel in Wasserstein space, then their means evolve identically. Section 5.2 applies the method to scRNA-seq data where control and treated populations share time-varying technical effects, making parallel dynamics plausible.
- **Break condition:** If the underlying system's dynamics for control vs. treated groups are not "parallel" in the Wasserstein geometric sense (e.g., treatment induces distributional changes not simply a rotated/shifted version of control), the WPT assumption fails. The method then imputes a counterfactual that diverges from the true treated evolution.

### Mechanism 3: RKHS-based Helmholtz–Hodge Projection
- **Claim:** The Helmholtz–Hodge projection \(\Pi_\mu: L^2(\mu;\mathbb{R}^d) \to T_\mu\mathcal{P}_2(\mathcal{M})\) onto Wasserstein tangent vectors (gradient fields) is consistently estimated via RKHS gradient regression, enabling practical implementation of the parallel transport PDE discretization.
- **Mechanism:** Given a vector field \(z\) and sampled supports, solve \(\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|\nabla f(x_i)-z(x_i)\|^2 + \lambda\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2\) in a scalar RKHS \(\mathcal{H}\). By the Gradient Representer Theorem, the solution is \(\hat{f}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^n\langle c_i,\nabla^2 K(x_i,\cdot)\rangle\), yielding the projected field \(\Pi_\mu z \approx \nabla\hat{f}\).
- **Core assumption:** The RKHS induced by the kernel is dense in the \(L^2(\mu)\)-closure of smooth gradients; the measure \(\mu\) has a uniformly bounded density; the vector fields \(z\) are in \(H^1(\mu)\).
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.4 derives the gradient RKHS formulation and provides Theorem 3.12 (consistency) and Proposition 3.13 (density condition for Sobolev-equivalent kernels). The appendix details the empirical objective and linear system.
- **Break condition:** If the kernel’s RKHS does not contain all gradients of interest (e.g., non-Mercer kernels, or kernels with spectral decay too slow to capture \(H^1\) functions), the density condition fails and the estimator is inconsistent. High dimensions inflate the RKHS norm penalty, leading to over-smoothing and large projection errors.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Wasserstein geometry and tangent space structure.**
  - *Why needed:* The entire method operates on the manifold \(\mathcal{P}_2(\mathcal{M})\); understanding tangent vectors as gradient fields \(\nabla\phi\) and the metric \(\langle \nabla\phi_1,\nabla\phi_2\rangle_\mu = \int \langle \nabla\phi_1,\nabla\phi_2\rangle_g d\mu\) is essential to define parallel transport.
  - *Quick check:* Given a measure \(\mu\), describe the natural embedding of a Euclidean vector field \(v\) into \(T_\mu\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)\) and the orthogonal complement (divergence-free fields).
- **Concept: Parallel transport on Riemannian manifolds.**
  - *Why needed:* The core innovation is moving dynamics across initial distributions by “keeping the vector field unchanged” along the geodesic, formalized by \(\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}} v \equiv 0\) with respect to the Wasserstein connection.
  - *Quick check:* For a curve \(\gamma(t)\) in a manifold with connection \(\nabla\), write the PDE defining a parallel vector field \(v(t)\) along \(\gamma\).
- **Concept: Helmholtz–Hodge decomposition.**
  - *Why needed:* The projection \(\Pi_\mu\) discards the divergence-free component of a vector field to restrict it to the tangent space of \(\mathcal{P}_2\). Implementing this via RKHS regression is the practical step enabling Algorithm 1.
  - *Quick check:* In \(\mathbb{R}^d\) with Lebesgue measure, what is the orthogonal complement of gradient fields in \(L^2(\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}^d)\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - OT Map Estimator -> Base-Jacobi Solver -> RKHS Helmholtz Projector -> WPT Core Loop
  - The OT Map Estimator computes discrete optimal transport plans between empirical measures to derive tangent velocities (barycentric displacements) and generate Wasserstein geodesic steps.
  - The Base-Jacobi Solver computes Jacobi fields along geodesics on the base manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) (for \(\mathbb{R}^d\) this is trivial; for curved spaces requires ODE integration).
  - The RKHS Helmholtz Projector projects a vector field onto the gradient subspace via kernel ridge regression in a scalar RKHS.
  - The WPT Core Loop iterates: compute control velocity; invoke parallel transport; push the counterfactual forward.

- **Critical path:**
  1. Obtain a regular Wasserstein geodesic between pairs of distributions.
  2. Compute the tangent velocity of the control trajectory.
  3. Parallel transport that velocity along the geodesic from control to counterfactual state using the fanning scheme.
  4. Exponentiate the transported field to update the counterfactual distribution.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Fanning resolution \(N\) vs. time: higher \(N\) reduces approximation error \(O(1/N)\) but increases computation linearly.
  - RKHS vs. simple projection: full WPT with Helmholtz projection theoretically enforces tangent space membership but introduces nonparametric estimation error; omitting projection (WPT⁻) often performs better in high dimensions.
  - Manifold choice: Theory uses \(\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{T}^d\) for stability proofs; using \(\mathbb{R}^d\) requires careful handling of boundary/unboundedness not covered by guarantees.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Numerical instability in Jacobi propagation when OT maps are near-singular.
  - Mass leakage in RKHS projection when densities have sharp peaks/troughs violating uniform bounds.
  - Error compounding in long trajectories due to exponential growth bound.
  - High-dimensional instability (diverging errors for \(d > 10\)).

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Gaussian fluid simulation: validates on known parallel Gaussian trajectories, showing WPT captures mean and covariance evolution while baselines fail.
  2. scRNA-seq organoid alignment: imputes chimp organoid dynamics onto human initial conditions; WPT⁻ outperforms mean-shift, especially at higher PCA dimensions.
  3. Dimension scaling study: synthetic Gaussians in \(d=\{2,4,8,12,16\}\); WPT without projection remains best for \(d<10\), but mean-shift/estimated-Gaussian become competitive as \(d\) grows.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Statistical properties of finite-sample estimators:** What are the convergence rates, asymptotic distributions, and optimality guarantees for empirical versions of Wasserstein parallel transport? The paper provides only population-level bounds.
- **Empirical validation of the WPT assumption:** How can one test the plausibility of the parallel trends assumption using pre-intervention data, especially in Difference-in-Differences applications?
- **Extension to unbalanced transport:** Can parallel transport be defined on spaces of measures with varying total mass (e.g., using Hellinger–Kantorovich geometry)? This would better suit applications like cellular dynamics where cell counts change.

## Limitations
- **Regularity dependence:** Stability guarantees require strongly regular Wasserstein geodesics (uniformly Lipschitz velocity, smooth densities), which real data (e.g., scRNA-seq) often violate due to sparsity and multimodality.
- **Curse of dimensionality:** Performance degrades sharply beyond \(d \approx 10\); aggressive dimensionality reduction (PCA) is necessary for practical use.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** No guidelines for fanning steps \(N\), RKHS regularization \(\lambda\), Sinkhorn entropy \(\varepsilon\), or kernel lengthscale; defaults may be unstable.

## Confidence
- **Fanning approximation error bound (Theorem 4.4):** Medium. Theory is solid but empirical performance suggests assumptions are brittle in practice, especially in higher dimensions.
- **WPT assumption and Gaussian PT (Theorem 4.6):** High for Gaussian systems; Medium for general distributions due to limited validation on only two scRNA-seq datasets.
- **RKHS projection consistency (Theorem 3.12):** Medium. Consistency holds under kernel density conditions, but high-dimensional performance is poor and hyperparameter sensitivity remains uncharacterized.

3. **Projection ablation:** Compare full WPT (with RKHS projection), WPT⁻ (no projection), and Euclidean parallel transport (identity Jacobi) across dimensions and sample sizes. Quantify when the tangent-space enforcement improves or harms accuracy under finite-sample noise.
