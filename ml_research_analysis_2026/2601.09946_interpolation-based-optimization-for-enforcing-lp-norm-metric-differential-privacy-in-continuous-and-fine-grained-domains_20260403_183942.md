# Interpolation-Based Optimization for Enforcing lp-Norm Metric Differential Privacy in Continuous and Fine-Grained Domains

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.09946  
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09946  
- **References:** 40  
- **Key result:** AIPO achieves strictly zero metric differential privacy violations while reducing expected extra travel distance by ~60% and cutting runtime by 30–70% compared to LP‑based baselines.

## Executive Summary
Metric differential privacy (mDP) extends differential privacy to continuous or fine‑grained domains by limiting the ratio of output probabilities based on a distance metric. Existing linear programming (LP) approaches for optimizing mDP mechanisms are computationally heavy and weaken privacy through domain discretization. This paper presents an interpolation‑based optimization framework (AIPO) that optimizes perturbation distributions only at sparse anchor points (the corners of axis‑aligned hyperrectangles) and then interpolates for all other records using a log‑convex, dimension‑wise process. Per‑dimension privacy budgets are allocated to satisfy an ℓₚ‑norm composition rule (∑εℓ^{q} ≤ ε^{q}, q = p/(p‑1)), which guarantees global (ε,dₚ)‑mDP. An approximate anchor perturbation optimization (Approx‑APO) replaces a non‑convex objective with a linear surrogate, yielding an efficiently solvable LP. Evaluated on real‑world road network data under ℓ₁ and ℓ₂ metrics, AIPO exhibits zero mDP violations, improves utility by ~60% compared to pre‑defined mechanisms, and runs 30–70% faster than LP baselines while achieving comparable utility loss.

## Method Summary
The AIPO mechanism proceeds in three stages. First, the continuous domain is partitioned into axis‑aligned hyperrectangles (orthotopes). For each orthotope, its 2^N corner points are designated as anchors. Second, an Anchor Perturbation Optimization (APO) problem jointly determines per‑coordinate privacy budgets {ε_ℓ} and the perturbation distribution at each anchor to minimize expected utility loss, subject to mDP constraints between ℓ‑neighbor anchors. To overcome the non‑convexity of the original objective, Approx‑APO introduces a linear surrogate based on a geometric mean approximation of the interpolated probabilities, reducing the problem to a linear program. Third, for any query point, the mechanism interpolates the log‑probabilities separately along each coordinate (convex combination of anchor log‑probs), multiplies the results, and normalizes to obtain the output distribution. This dimension‑wise interpolation, combined with the ℓₚ‑norm composition condition, ensures (ε,dₚ)‑mDP by design.

## Key Results
- AIPO yields **zero** mDP violations across all datasets and metrics, whereas LP‑based mechanisms exhibit up to 2.3% violations due to discretization errors.
- In utility (expected extra travel distance), AIPO reduces loss by approximately **60%** compared to pre‑defined mechanisms (EM, Laplace, TEM) and performs on par with LP baselines (≤0.5 km higher loss).
- Runtime is **30–70% lower** than LP and COPT methods thanks to sparse anchor selection; ablation studies show asymmetric budget allocation improves utility by up to 7.8%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Dimension‑Wise Log‑Convex Interpolation
- **Claim:** Log‑convex interpolation along each coordinate axis, with per‑dimension privacy budgets satisfying a composition rule (∑ε_ℓ^{q} ≤ ε^{q}), preserves (ε,dₚ)‑metric differential privacy globally.
- **Mechanism:** For a one‑dimensional interval between anchor points, the log‑probability for an intermediate input is a linear interpolation (convex combination) of the anchor log‑probabilities. This ensures Lipschitz bounds (|f(x)−f(x′)| ≤ ε_ℓ|x−x′|) hold exactly within and across intervals (Propositions 1‑2). In N‑dimensions, the mechanism applies these one‑dimensional interpolations sequentially along each axis and composes them multiplicatively. Theorem 1 proves that if per‑axis bounds hold and budgets satisfy the ℓₚ‑composition condition, the composed function satisfies (ε,dₚ)‑Lipschitz continuity globally.
- **Core assumption:** The ℓₚ‑norm distance metric induces convexity, making naive multi‑dimensional log‑convex interpolation insufficient (Jensen’s inequality enlarges effective distance). The fix requires axis‑aligned partitioning and coordinate‑wise composition.
- **Break condition:** If the domain is not partitioned into axis‑aligned orthotopes, or if anchor neighbors are not constrained to differ in only one coordinate, the per‑axis Lipschitz bounds cannot be cleanly chained to the global bound. The composition rule also fails if the per‑dimension budgets violate Eq. (17)‑(18).

### Mechanism 2: Anchor Perturbation Optimization (APO)
- **Claim:** Optimizing perturbation distributions at a sparse set of anchor points (cube corners) and interpolating yields lower expected utility loss than pre‑defined mechanisms while maintaining strict (ε,dₚ)‑mDP.
- **Mechanism:** The secret domain X is partitioned into N‑orthotopes. For each orthotope, the 2^N corner points are anchors. APO jointly chooses (i) per‑coordinate privacy budgets ε_ℓ (subject to the composition constraint) and (ii) disturbance probabilities z(y_k|𝑥̂) at anchors to minimize expected utility loss L(Z) = ∫_{C_m} f_int(x,y_k,Z)p(x)L(x,y_k)dx, where f_int is the unnormalized interpolant. Constraints enforce (ε_ℓ,d₁)‑mDP between ℓ‑neighbor anchors and probability normalization.
- **Core assumption:** The interpolation function f_int accurately represents the distribution at non‑anchor points; optimizing at anchors is sufficient because the interpolation is smooth and Lipschitz.
- **Break condition:** The coupling between ε_ℓ and Z_𝑋̂ makes APO non‑convex and computationally heavy in higher dimensions or with many anchors. If the utility loss function L(x,y) is highly non‑linear or discontinuous, the linear approximation in Approx‑APO may yield a poor solution.

### Mechanism 3: Linear Approximation (Approx‑APO) for Tractable Optimization
- **Claim:** Replacing the true expected utility loss with a linear surrogate based on geometric interpolation weights yields a solvable linear program (LP) whose solution approximates the true APO optimum with a bounded optimality gap.
- **Mechanism:** The exact cell loss L(Z_𝑋̂_m) involves an integral of ratios of products of exponentials, which is analytically intractable. Proposition 4 approximates the perturbation probability at a non‑anchor x by a weighted geometric mean of anchor probabilities, where weights depend on x’s position within the orthotope. This converts the cell loss into a linear inner product ⟨𝐶̃_𝑋̂_m, Z_𝑋̂_m⟩, where 𝐶̃ is constant and computable from the prior p(x) and utility loss L(x,y). The resulting Approx‑APO is an LP with the same constraints as APO but a linear objective.
- **Core assumption:** The geometric mean approximation is sufficiently accurate for the utility objective. A universal lower bound LB (Appendix C) allows quantifying how close Approx‑APO comes to the true optimum.
- **Break condition:** The approximation error grows if the true probability f_int(x,y) varies highly non‑linearly within a cell (e.g., sharp utility cliffs). The bound LB may be loose for certain priors or utility functions.

- **Why needed:** Theorem 1 uses Hölder’s inequality to sum per‑coordinate privacy losses ∑ε_ℓ|x_ℓ−x′_ℓ| into a global ε·dₚ bound. The exponent p/(p‑1) is the dual norm q.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Domain partitioning → Anchor optimization (Approx‑APO solver) → Interpolation engine → Privacy verifier

**Critical path:**  
Partition → Optimize anchors & budgets → Interpolate → Verify

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Anchor sparsity vs. accuracy: Fewer anchors reduce APO runtime but increase interpolation error; diminishing returns beyond ~8–10 horizontal cells.
- Utility vs. privacy: Tighter mDP (smaller ε) increases noise; asymmetric ε_ℓ allocation can improve utility by matching directional sensitivity but requires searching the feasible budget manifold.
- Approximation tightness: Approx‑APO’s linear surrogate enables LP solving but introduces an optimality gap; the lower bound LB helps assess quality.

**Failure signatures:**
- **mDP violation:** PPR > ε for some (x,x′,y_k). Causes: unenforced constraints between ℓ‑neighbor anchors, non‑axis‑aligned anchors, or normalization increasing Lipschitz constant beyond 2ε (Prop. 3).
- **High utility loss:** Overly conservative ε_ℓ (e.g., equal split when anisotropy exists), coarse partitioning (too few anchors), or poor prior/utility specification.
- **Solver failure/timeout:** APO LP too large (many anchors or large output alphabet). Mitigation: reduce grid resolution or prune anchors.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Privacy compliance test:** Sample 1000 random location pairs, compute max_{y_k} PPR(x,x′,y_k). Expect ~0% violations for AIPO; non‑zero for LP/COPT.
2. **Utility comparison:** Compute expected extra travel distance against a baseline LP mechanism. Expect AIPO loss within 0.5 km of LP but with zero violations.
3. **Scalability sweep:** Vary grid resolution (number of orthotopes) and measure runtime and utility loss. Expect super‑linear runtime growth and diminishing utility returns beyond a dataset‑specific threshold.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1: High‑Dimensional Scaling
- **Question:** How can the anchor interpolation framework be scaled to high‑dimensional continuous data (e.g., embeddings with hundreds of dimensions) while maintaining rigorous ℓₚ‑mDP guarantees and avoiding exponential anchor blowup?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion explicitly states: “While our method is highly effective in low‑dimensional settings, its scalability is challenged in high‑dimensional domains due to the exponential growth in anchor points... These limitations... motivate future work on bilevel programming, optimization decomposition, or adaptive partitioning.”
- **Why unresolved:** The current method anchors each N‑orthotope at its 2^N corners; this is computationally feasible only for small N (e.g., 2D spatial data). For higher dimensions, both memory and runtime become prohibitive.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A scalable extension that reduces anchor cardinality (e.g., via adaptively selected sparse anchors, hierarchical sampling) with a provable guarantee that the interpolated mechanism still satisfies (ε,d_p)‑mDP; empirical validation on high‑dimensional continuous datasets (e.g., text or image embeddings).

### Open Question 2: Efficient Budget Allocation in High Dimensions
- **Question:** What efficient algorithms can compute near‑optimal per‑dimension privacy budget allocations {ε_ℓ} under the ℓₚ‑mDP composition rule when N ≫ 2, avoiding the exponential grid search used here?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper notes that for N>2, “such brute‑force grid search becomes computationally expensive... as the search space grows exponentially with N.” It also identifies “joint budget allocation strategies” as needed for high‑dimensional scaling.
- **Why unresolved:** The joint optimization over {ε_ℓ} and Z is a non‑convex bi‑level problem; direct search is exponential in N. No scalable solver is provided.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A practical algorithm (e.g., based on convex relaxations, meta‑learning, or coordinate descent) that finds allocations with provable approximation guarantees and empirically outperforms equal‑split in higher dimensions (N≥10) with acceptable runtime overhead.

### Open Question 3: Adaptive Partitioning Beyond Axis‑Aligned Hyperrectangles
- **Question:** Can adaptive, data‑dependent partitioning (beyond axis‑aligned hyperrectangles) further improve utility or reduce anchor count while preserving the theoretical ℓₚ‑mDP guarantees of the interpolation framework?
- **Basis in paper:** The future work explicitly mentions “adaptive partitioning” as a direction. The paper also lists “anchor pruning... and hierarchical grid partitioning” as potential remedies.
- **Why unresolved:** The correctness proof relies heavily on axis‑aligned neighbor anchors for one‑dimensional Lipschitz preservation. Arbitrary partitions would break that structure; new interpolation rules and proofs are needed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A modified partitioning scheme (e.g., based on data density, utility gradients, or hierarchical trees) with a corresponding interpolation function that is proven to satisfy (ε,dₚ)‑mDP; empirical demonstrations of improved utility or reduced anchors on non‑uniform datasets.

### Open Question 4: Tighter Normalization with Slack < 2
- **Question:** Can the normalization step after log‑convex interpolation be redesigned to achieve a tighter privacy slack than the current factor‑2 blowup (i.e., (cε,dₚ)-mDP with c<2) without sacrificing utility?
- **Basis in paper:** Proposition 3 shows that normalization leads to a (2ε,dₚ) guarantee instead of the desired (ε,dₚ). The discussion notes: “normalization can increase the effective Lipschitz bound... by up to a factor of 2.” This limitation is acknowledged but not addressed; no attempt is made to tighten it.
- **Why unresolved:** The proof that normalization multiplies the bound by ≤2 arises from bounding the partition function’s ratio. Alternative combinations (e.g., additive in probability space) likely break convexity or mDP. A construction achieving c<2 is nontrivial.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A re‑designed interpolation+normalization mechanism that provably satisfies (cε,dₚ)‑mDP with c<2 (e.g., via scaled interpolation, subsampling, or deterministic rounding) and empirical demonstrations of improved privacy‑utility trade‑offs (i.e., same utility with smaller ε or same ε with lower loss).

## Limitations
- The framework requires axis‑aligned partitioning and coordinate‑wise composition to preserve mDP; it is unclear whether correlated dimensions or non‑axis‑aligned regions would break the guarantee.
- The linear surrogate in Approx‑APO may be loose for certain priors or utility functions, leading to sub‑optimal solutions.
- Extensive evaluations are provided only for three road networks under ℓ₁/ℓ₂ metrics; generalization to other domains (e.g., images) or more complex metrics remains open.
