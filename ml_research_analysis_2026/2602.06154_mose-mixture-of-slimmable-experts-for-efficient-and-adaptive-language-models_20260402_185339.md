# MoSE: Mixture of Slimmable Experts for Efficient and Adaptive Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06154
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06154
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: MoSE achieves up to 30% fewer inference FLOPs while matching or improving perplexity and zero-shot accuracy over standard MoE.

## Executive Summary
MoSE (Mixture of Slimmable Experts) introduces a novel approach to Mixture-of-Experts modeling that enables fine-grained control over computational effort per token. By structuring each expert as a slimmable subnetwork with nested weights, MoSE decouples the decision of which experts to activate from how much of each expert to use. This allows adaptive computation at the level of individual experts within a token. The method combines multi-width pre-training—where each batch computes losses at full and a random width—with standard MoE auxiliary losses to maintain stable routing and robust representations across widths. At inference, a lightweight test‑time training phase optimizes a sharpness parameter that maps router confidence to per-expert widths under a fixed FLOP budget. Evaluations on GPT models show MoSE matches or exceeds standard MoE at full width and shifts the accuracy-FLOPs Pareto frontier, achieving up to 20.3% FLOP reduction for GPT2-Small while lowering OpenWebText perplexity by over 1 point.

## Method Summary
MoSE modifies the standard MoE layer by making its feed‑forward experts slimmable. Each expert’s up-projection matrix is independently sliced along the channel dimension according to a width multiplier \(w \in \mathcal{A}\) (e.g., 0.25, 0.3, …, 1.0), and the down‑projection correspondingly selects the same intermediate channels. All width variants share a single set of parameters in a nested structure, so no retraining is needed when switching widths. Pre‑training proceeds with a two‑loss regime: for every mini‑batch, the model computes the language modeling loss once with all experts at full width and again with all experts at a uniformly sampled width in \([0.25, 1.0]\). The average loss is taken along with standard MoE auxiliary losses (load balancing and router z‑loss) to encourage balanced expert usage and prevent router collapse. After pre‑training, for a given compute budget \(\Gamma\), a scalar sharpness parameter \(\gamma\) (either shared across layers or per‑layer) is optimized via gradient descent on a small calibration dataset while keeping all model weights frozen. The learned \(\gamma\) determines how the router’s output probabilities for the selected experts are transformed into discrete width allocations that respect the budget. At inference, for each token, the router first picks top‑k experts; then each selected expert is assigned a width \(w_i \in \mathcal{A}\) according to the budget‑constrained policy derived from \(\gamma\) and the router confidence; the expert runs with the sliced weights; finally, the expert outputs are combined with router weights to produce the layer output.

## Key Results
- MoSE matches or improves standard MoE accuracy at full width, demonstrating that the slimmable nesting does not harm maximum capacity.
- The proposed test‑time training (TTT) consistently shifts the Pareto frontier: for a fixed FLOP budget, MoSE+TTT yields lower perplexity than uniform‑width or router‑probability baselines.
- Up to 30% fewer inference FLOPs can be achieved while maintaining comparable or better perplexity and zero‑shot accuracy; for example, on GPT2‑Small, MoSE reduces FLOPs by 20.3% and improves OpenWebText perplexity by over 1 point.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Slimmable Expert Nesting Enables Variable Compute per Expert
- **Claim**: Each expert in MoSE is a slimmable subnetwork via nested weight slicing, allowing execution at discrete widths without retraining.
- **Mechanism**: For a width multiplier \(w\), the intermediate dimension of the expert’s feed‑forward network is set to \(m(w) = \lceil w \cdot 4d \rceil\) (where \(d\) is model hidden size). The up‑projection weight matrix \(W_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 4d}\) is sliced to its first \(m(w)\) columns, and the down‑projection weight matrix is sliced to its first \(m(w)\) rows; biases are sliced accordingly. All width variants share the full parameter set, so switching widths merely selects a subset of channels.
- **Supporting evidence**: The paper formalizes this in Section 2.2 (Equation 4, Figure 2). The concept builds on prior work on slimmable networks (Yu et al., 2019) but applies it within a sparse MoE routing context, which is novel.
- **Potential failure**: If the width set \(\mathcal{A}\) is too coarse (e.g., only full width) or if the router’s selection and the width assignment interfere with learned expert specializations, the conditional compute axis provides no benefit or may harm optimization.

### Mechanism 2: Multi‑Width Pre‑Training Stabilizes Nested Representations Under Sparse Routing
- **Claim**: Pre‑training with stochastic width sampling (full and a random width per batch) ensures all experts remain competent across the width spectrum while preserving stable MoE routing dynamics.
- **Mechanism**: Each mini‑batch yields two forward passes: one with all experts at \(w_{\max}=1.0\) and another with all experts at \(w \sim \text{Uniform}(w_{\min}, w_{\max})\). The total language modeling loss is the average of the two losses. Standard MoE auxiliary losses (load balancing, router z‑loss) are added to prevent expert collapse and encourage balanced usage.
- **Supporting evidence**: The paper’s Section 2.3 defines the loss \(L_{\text{MoSE}}^{\text{LM}}\). Figure 3 shows training loss trajectories for MoSE and standard MoE track closely, indicating no destabilization. The use of random width draws extends slimmable network training (Tastan et al., 2025a) to the MoE setting.
- **Potential failure**: If the random width distribution heavily favors narrow widths, expert representations at full width may degrade. The method assumes \(w_{\max}\) is always included (each batch includes a full‑width pass), guaranteeing sufficient full‑width exposure.

### Mechanism 3: Test‑Time Learning Maps Router Confidence to Widths Under a Fixed Budget
- **Claim**: A lightweight test‑time training procedure optimizes a sharpness parameter \(\gamma\) that maps router probabilities to per‑expert widths while respecting a global compute budget \(\Gamma\).
- **Mechanism**: After pre‑training, a calibration dataset \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{calib}}\) is processed. Only \(\gamma\) (shared or layer‑specific) is updated via gradient descent to minimize the language modeling loss under the router‑conditioned width policy (Equations 7‑10). The budget constraint is enforced by scaling the normalized router probabilities to sum to \(\Gamma\) and discretizing to the valid width set. The learned \(\gamma^\star\) is then fixed for deployment.
- **Supporting evidence**: The paper’s Section 2.4 details the optimization. Figures 4, 5, 6, and 10 demonstrate that TTT yields consistent Pareto improvements over uniform‑width and normalized‑probability (\(\gamma=1\)) baselines.
- **Potential failure**: If the calibration data is small or mismatched to the test distribution, \(\gamma\) may overfit, degrading performance. The method also requires the budget \(\Gamma\) to be known during calibration. The sharpness parameterization might be insufficient to capture complex routing‑width relationships.

## Foundational Learning

- **Slimmable / Nested Networks**  
  *Why needed*: MoSE’s core innovation is applying width‑adaptive execution to individual MoE experts; understanding nested slicing is key.  
  *Quick check*: For an expert’s up‑projection weight \(W_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 4d}\) and width \(w=0.5\), how many columns are kept? Answer: \(\lceil 0.5 \times 4d \rceil = 2d\) columns (the first \(2d\)).

- **Sparse Routing in Mixture‑of‑Experts**  
  *Why needed*: MoSE extends standard MoE; one must know that a router selects \(k\) experts per token and only those are computed. MoSE adds a width degree of freedom within each selected expert.  
  *Quick check*: In a standard top‑2 MoE with 8 experts, how many experts are computed per token on average? Answer: 2. In MoSE, the number of selected experts remains 2, but each can run at a variable width.

- **Test‑Time Adaptation / Calibration**  
  *Why needed*: MoSE’s best performance relies on a light post‑pre‑training step (TTT) that learns how to map router confidence to widths under a fixed budget.  
  *Quick check*: During TTT, which parameters are trained? Answer: Only the sharpness parameter(s) \(\gamma\) (shared or per layer); all model weights remain frozen; training occurs on a short calibration stream.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**  
  Router → (selects top‑k experts) → Slimmable Expert (executes at assigned width) → aggregation. Width Allocator uses router probabilities and \(\gamma\) to compute per‑expert widths; Calibration Module optimizes \(\gamma\) during TTT.

- **Critical path** (inference with TTT)  
  1. Token \(x\) enters a MoE layer.  
  2. Router computes scores; selects top‑k experts \(S(x)\).  
  3. For each selected expert \(i\), take router probability \(p_i(x)\) (normalized over \(S(x)\)).  
  4. Using \(\gamma\), compute allocation weights \(q_i(\gamma)\) (e.g., \(q_i = p_i^\gamma / \sum_j p_j^\gamma\)).  
  5. Budget \(\Gamma\) is allocated: raw width \(\tilde{w}_i = \Gamma \cdot q_i\).  
  6. Discretize \(\tilde{w}_i\) to nearest valid width \(w_i \in \mathcal{A}\).  
  7. Execute expert \(i\) with width \(w_i\) by slicing its nested weights.  
  8. Combine expert outputs weighted by the original router scores to produce layer output.

- **Design tradeoffs**  
  - *Width set granularity*: Finer discrete width steps (e.g., step 0.01) allow smoother compute‑accuracy trade‑offs but may complicate training and increase cost of width assignment; MoSE uses step 0.05.  
  - *\(\gamma\) expressivity*: A single shared \(\gamma\) is cheap to calibrate but cannot capture layer‑specific optimal sharpness; per‑layer \(\gamma\) adds \(L\) parameters and modest calibration cost but yields better Pareto curves.  
  - *TTT reliance*: TTT requires representative calibration data; if unavailable, fallback to uniform‑width or normalized‑probability (\(\gamma=1\)) is possible but uniformly dominated by TTT in the paper.

- **Failure signatures**  
  - Training divergence or exploding loss when sampling random widths, often due to insufficient auxiliary loss weighting or too wide random width range.  
  - Invalid width assignments: width multiplier leads to an intermediate dimension outside the valid range (< \(d\) or > \(4d\)); can be caught by assertions.  
  - TTT overfitting: calibration loss decreases but validation perplexity worsens, indicating \(\gamma\) has overfitted; mitigate via early stopping or smaller learning rate.  
  - FLOPs‑perplexity curves not matching paper: discrepancy in FLOP counting (e.g., counting multiply‑adds as 1 vs. 2) or missing components (e.g., attention overhead) leads to shifted curves; careful accounting needed.

- **First 3 experiments**  
  1. **Stability of multi‑width pre‑training**: Train MoSE (e.g., GPT2‑Small with 8 experts) and plot training loss for both full‑width and random‑width passes; they should track closely and not diverge over 3B tokens.  
  2. **Full‑width accuracy parity**: After pre‑training, evaluate MoSE at full width (\(w=1.0\) for all experts) on OpenWebText validation perplexity and compare to a standard MoE checkpoint; MoSE should match or beat it.  
  3. **Pareto shift with TTT**: For a trained MoSE model, vary compute budget \(\Gamma\) and collect perplexity vs. FLOPs for (a) uniform width, (b) router‑probability normalized (\(\gamma=1\)), (c) TTT (shared \(\gamma\)). Plot the three curves; TTT should dominate (lower perplexity at same FLOPs). If not, inspect calibration procedure and \(\gamma\) initialization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Post‑training instillation of slimmability**: Can we retrofit slimmable capability into a pre‑trained standard MoE without full retraining?
2. **Scalability to massive MoE models**: Does MoSE maintain efficiency gains at billion‑parameter scales with hundreds of experts?
3. **Practical overhead of TTT**: What is the real‑world cost (latency, memory) and robustness of test‑time training for \(\gamma\) to distribution shifts?
4. **Generalization from uniform pre‑training**: Why does training with independent uniform widths enable effective non‑uniform, router‑conditioned width allocation at inference? What are the limits?
5. **Orthogonality to routing mechanisms**: Are MoSE’s gains independent of the specific MoE routing algorithm (top‑k, switch, etc.)?

## Limitations
- Independent validation absent: All experiments are from the authors’ implementation; no third‑party replications exist.
- TTT calibration assumptions: Uses a small, domain‑specific calibration set; generalization to out‑of‑distribution data is unverified.
- Theoretical FLOP counts: Reported reductions rely on idealized FLOP calculations; actual hardware speedups may be smaller due to memory bandwidth, kernel launch overheads, or non‑linear scaling.
