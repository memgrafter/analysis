# Vendi Novelty Scores for Out-of-Distribution Detection
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10062
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10062
- Reference count: 33
- Primary result: Introduces Vendi Novelty Score (VNS), a diversity‑based OOD detector that replaces density modeling, achieving state‑of‑the‑art on CIFAR and ImageNet‑1K with 13% lower average FPR@95 on ImageNet‑1K and strong performance using only 1% of training data.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents the Vendi Novelty Score (VNS), a novel approach for out‑of‑distribution (OOD) detection that shifts paradigm from density modeling to measuring feature diversity. VNS quantifies how much a test sample increases the Vendi Score—a diversity metric based on effective counting—of the in‑distribution class features. For efficiency, it approximates the score change using only the leading eigenvalue and eigenvector of each class’s covariance matrix. The final score adaptively aggregates class‑conditional novelty signals weighted by the classifier’s softmax probabilities and includes a global dataset‑level correction. Extensive experiments on CIFAR and ImageNet‑1K benchmarks show VNS attaining state‑of‑the‑art performance, notably reducing average false positive rate at 95% true positive rate (FPR@95) by 13% on ImageNet‑1K versus prior methods. Remarkably, VNS remains effective even when trained with merely 1% of the training data, highlighting its data efficiency.

## Method Summary
VNS operates post‑hoc with a pretrained image classifier. For each class *c*, it constructs a density matrix ρ_c from ℓ2‑normalized penultimate‑layer features. The Vendi Score of order 2 (VS2) equals 1 / Tr(ρ_c²). The novelty of a test sample *h(x)* is the log‑change in VS2 when adding *h(x)* to the class feature set: Δ_c(x) = log VS2(ρ_c′) – log VS2(ρ_c). To avoid costly eigendecompositions, VNS assumes the top eigenvalue λ_c dominates the spectrum and keeps only its eigenvector *u_c*, yielding a closed‑form Δ_c(x) that depends on the projection α_c(x) = (u_cᵀ h(x))². This rank‑1 approximation reduces per‑class cost to O(*D*).

A sample’s overall OOD score aggregates Δ_c over the *K* classes with highest classifier probability, weighting by class size *N_c* and probability raised to power γ: S_local = Σ_{c∈top‑K} *N_c* *p_c*(x)^γ Δ_c(x). A global correction based on VS∞ (≈ 1/λ_max of the overall covariance) is computed similarly and subtracted (optionally) to separate local from dataset‑wide novelty: VNS(x) = S_local – *g* · (*N*·Δ_global). The entire pipeline runs in O(*C D*) time, storing only O(*C D*) statistics (λ_c, u_c, N_c and global equivalents).

## Key Results
- VNS achieves state‑of‑the‑art OOD detection on CIFAR‑10/100 and ImageNet‑1K, reducing average FPR@95 by 13% on ImageNet‑1K compared to prior methods (e.g., from 38.99% to ~34%).
- The method works effectively with only 1% of training data, demonstrating robustness to limited in‑distribution samples.
- Ablations confirm the rank‑1 approximation incurs negligible performance drop while drastically cutting computational cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Rank-1 Vendi Score Approximation
- **Claim:** VNS measures the incremental change in VS2 of a class’s feature set when adding a test sample, using only the leading eigenspace for efficiency.
- **Mechanism:** For each class *c*, compute class density matrix ρ_c = X_c^⊤ X_c / N_c (ℓ2‑normalized features). VS2(ρ_c) = 1 / Tr(ρ_c²). Adding sample *h(x)* gives ρ_c′ = (N_c ρ_c + h(x)h(x)^⊤) / (N_c+1). The change Δ_c(x) = log VS2(ρ_c′) – log VS2(ρ_c) is approximated by keeping only the top eigenvalue λ_c and eigenvector u_c, leading to Δ_c(x) = –log((N_c² λ_c² + 2 N_c λ_c α_c(x) + 1)/(N_c+1)²) + log(λ_c²), where α_c(x) = (u_c^⊤ h(x))².
- **Core assumption:** The spectrum of ρ_c has a large spectral gap so that the top eigenvalue dominates VS2; higher eigenvalues contribute negligibly and are noise‑sensitive.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract]: “VNS quantifies how much a test sample increases the VS of the in‑distribution feature set, providing a principled notion of novelty that does not require density modeling.”
  - [section 3, Page 4]: “We achieve the strongest results if we only maintain the top eigenvalue.”
  - [corpus]: Evidence is weak; no corpus paper directly validates the rank‑1 approximation for VS2 in OOD detection. The Vendi Score papers focus on diversity measurement, not OOD.
- **Break condition:** If the class feature matrix has an isotropic spectrum (no dominant eigenvalue), the rank‑1 approximation fails to capture the true VS2 change, producing inaccurate novelty scores.

### Mechanism 2: Probability‑Weighted Aggregation of Class‑Conditional Novelty
- **Claim:** VNS aggregates class‑conditional novelty scores using a probability‑weighted top‑K scheme to adaptively combine local signals based on model confidence.
- **Mechanism:** Given predictive distribution *p(x)* from classifier, compute S_local‑OOD(x) = Σ_{c∈T_K(x)} N_c p_c(x)^γ Δ_c(x), where T_K(x) are the *K* classes with highest *p_c*, γ≥0 controls sharpness, and *N_c* normalizes for class size.
- **Core assumption:** The classifier’s predicted probabilities reflect the relevance of each class to the sample; thus, weighting by *p_c*^γ emphasizes classes where the sample is more likely to belong, improving signal‑to‑noise.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “It aggregates class‑conditional (local) and dataset‑level (global) novelty signals.”
  - [section 3, Page 5]: “Rather than relying on the novelty with respect to a single predicted class, we aggregate class‑conditional novelty scores using a probability‑weighted scheme.”
  - [corpus]: Weak; while GEN (Liu et al., 2023) uses probability‑weighted aggregation of logits, VNS weights class‑conditional novelty scores, which is different. No corpus paper directly studies this aggregation for diversity‑based novelty.
- **Break condition:** If the classifier’s probabilities are miscalibrated (e.g., overconfident on OOD samples) or if the true class is not in the top‑*K* (common for OOD), the aggregation may omit informative novelty signals or include noise.

### Mechanism 3: Global Correction via VS∞ Approximation
- **Claim:** VNS incorporates a global diversity correction by approximating the change in VS∞ of the entire dataset, subtracting it to remove global effects and sharpen OOD separation.
- **Mechanism:** Compute global density matrix ρ_global from all training features. The change in VS∞ (which is 1/λ_max) when adding *h(x)* is approximated via first‑order perturbation: Δ_global(x) ≈ –log((N λ_max(ρ_global) + α(x))/(N+1)) + log λ_max(ρ_global), where α(x) = (u_max^⊤ h(x))². Scale by *N*: S_global‑OOD(x) = –N log((N λ_max + α(x))/(N+1)) + N log λ_max. Final score VNS(x) = S_local‑OOD(x) – *g* · S_global‑OOD(x), with *g*∈{0,1}.
- **Core assumption:** The global VS∞ is dominated by the largest eigenvalue of ρ_global, and the first‑order expansion accurately captures the effect of adding one sample (error O(1/N²)). Additionally, subtracting global novelty disentangles local class‑specific effects from dataset‑wide shifts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “and includes a global correction.”
  - [section 3, Page 6]: Proposition 3.1 and Equation 10.
  - [section 4, Page 11]: “VNS only utilizes the largest eigenvalue from each class, which can be reliably estimated from small sample sizes.” (Supports robustness of eigenvalue‑based approximations.)
  - [corpus]: Weak; prior OOD methods like Mahalanobis‑based ones also use global correction, but the specific VS∞ approximation is novel. No corpus paper covers this exact mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If the global feature space has multiple large eigenvalues (e.g., flat spectrum), the VS∞ ≈ 1/λ_max assumption breaks. Also, if OOD samples affect global diversity in a way that correlates with local novelty, subtraction may hurt performance.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept:** Vendi Score (VS)  
  **Why needed:** VS is the core diversity metric that VNS builds upon to quantify novelty as an increase in effective sample count. Understanding VS clarifies how a single sample affects set diversity.  
  **Quick check:** For ℓ2‑normalized features with cosine similarity, what is the Vendi Score of order 2 in terms of the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix? (Answer: VS2 = 1 / Tr(ρ²), where ρ is the normalized covariance matrix.)

- **Concept:** Density matrix and eigendecomposition  
  **Why needed:** VNS represents feature distributions via density matrices (ρ = X⊤X/N) and exploits eigenvalue decay to enable the rank‑1 approximation, drastically reducing computational cost.  
  **Quick check:** Given ℓ2‑normalized features, show that the eigenvalues of ρ = X⊤X/N coincide with the non‑zero eigenvalues of K = XX⊤/N.

- **Concept:** Probability‑weighted aggregation for OOD detection  
  **Why needed:** VNS combines class‑conditional novelty scores using the classifier’s predictive probabilities to adaptively emphasize classes that the model deems plausible, balancing confidence and diversity signal.  
  **Quick check:** In S_local(x) = Σ_{c∈top‑K} N_c p_c(x)^γ Δ_c(x), what is the effect of increasing γ? It makes the weighting more confident (higher probabilities dominate); decreasing γ flattens the weighting to consider more classes more equally.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**:
Feature extractor (penultimate layer) → h(x)  
Classifier → p(x)  
Per‑class storage (λ_c, u_c, N_c) → Δ_c(x)  
Global storage (λ_max, u_max, N) → Δ_global(x)  
Scoring function (aggregation with K, γ, g) → VNS(x)

**Critical path**:
1. Extract feature h(x) and ℓ2‑normalize.
2. For each class c, compute α_c(x) = (u_cᵀ h(x))² and then Δ_c(x) via the rank‑1 formula (Eq. 6).
3. Obtain p(x) from classifier; identify the *K* classes with highest probabilities.
4. Compute S_local‑OOD(x) = Σ_{c∈top‑K} N_c p_c(x)^γ Δ_c(x).
5. Compute α(x) = (u_maxᵀ h(x))² and Δ_global(x) via Eq. 10.
6. Compute S_global‑OOD(x) = *N* · Δ_global(x).
7. VNS(x) = S_local‑OOD(x) – *g* · S_global‑OOD(x).
8. Compare VNS(x) against threshold *T* (set at 95% TPR on validation) to label OOD.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **Accuracy vs. compute:** Using only top eigenvalue (rank‑1) reduces complexity to O(CD) but discards spectral information; full VS2 would be O(CD²) or more.
- **Aggregation:** Larger *K* includes more classes but may add noise; smaller *K* is faster but may miss relevant classes. γ balances high‑confidence (large γ) vs. broader consideration (small γ).
- **Global correction:** *g*=1 may improve robustness but adds compute; *g*=0 simpler but may underperform on some datasets.
- **Data usage:** VNS works well even with 1% of training data because it relies on top eigenvalues, which are stable with few samples.

**Failure signatures**:
- High false positive rate on near‑OOD datasets if class‑conditional novelty is not sufficiently discriminative.
- Performance drop on architectures where features are not well‑separated by class (e.g., poor classifier).
- If the top eigenvalue of a class is estimated from too few samples, Δ_c may be noisy; monitor class sizes.
- Global correction may increase FPR if the global eigenvalue λ_max is not stable (e.g., due to dataset imbalance).
- Hyperparameter sensitivity: Poor choice of *K*, γ, *g* on validation may not transfer to test OOD sets (since validation uses synthetic noise).

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Rank approximation ablation:** Compute VNS using full VS2 (by computing all eigenvalues of ρ_c′) vs. rank‑1 approximation. Measure change in FPR@95 and AUROC to quantify accuracy loss and confirm spectral dominance.
2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Sweep *K* and γ on validation set (synthetic noise) and evaluate on test OOD sets to identify robust ranges; compare with/without global correction.
3. **Data efficiency:** Train VNS with varying percentages (0.1%, 1%, 10%, 100%) of training data. Plot FPR@95 vs. % data to verify stability even with very few samples.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**  
  Can VNS be generalized to other similarity kernels (e.g., learned kernels, RBF) and does this improve OOD detection performance?  
  *Basis in paper:* Explicitly mentioned in the conclusion as a future direction.  
  *Why unresolved:* Only cosine kernel is evaluated; alternative kernels may better capture domain-specific similarities.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic experiments comparing VNS with various kernels on standard OOD benchmarks, measuring FPR@95 and AUROC.

- **Open Question 2**  
  Does VNS maintain its effectiveness for OOD detection in non‑image domains (e.g., text, audio, tabular data)?  
  *Basis in paper:* Proposed as future work beyond image classification.  
  *Why unresolved:* Validation is limited to image tasks; feature diversity may behave differently in other modalities.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Apply VNS to text (e.g., BERT embeddings), audio (e.g., spectrogram features), and tabular datasets with appropriate kernels and compare to modality‑specific detectors.

- **Open Question 3**  
  Under what conditions (e.g., model architecture, dataset characteristics) does the rank‑1 approximation become insufficient, and would higher‑rank approximations yield gains?  
  *Basis in paper:* Section 4.3 shows rank‑1 works well due to eigenvalue decay, but no universal guarantee.  
  *Why unresolved:* Not tested on diverse architectures/datasets; sub‑dominant eigenvalues might carry discriminative signal.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Ablate rank from 1 to full across many models/datasets; correlate eigenvalue decay with performance drop when truncating.

- **Open Question 4**  
  How does the choice of hyperparameters *K*, γ, and *g* affect VNS across diverse OOD scenarios, and can a theoretically grounded, data‑free selection criterion replace the current noise‑based validation?  
  *Basis in paper:* Hyperparameters are tuned on synthetic Gaussian noise; limitation acknowledged.  
  *Why unresolved:* Synthetic validation may not transfer to real OOD; sensitivity not characterized.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Comprehensive sensitivity analysis on multiple OOD benchmarks; develop a selection method using only in‑distribution statistics.

- **Open Question 5**  
  What is the adversarial robustness of VNS against common OOD detection attacks (e.g., adversarial examples that mimic ID features), and how does it compare to baselines?  
  *Basis in paper:* No adversarial evaluation; impact statement mentions failures under adversarial conditions.  
  *Why unresolved:* OOD detectors are known vulnerable; VNS’s reliance on feature diversity might be susceptible to attacks manipulating eigenvalue structure.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Evaluate VNS under adversarial attacks (e.g., PGD to minimize VNS score); compare attack success rates to baselines; possibly provide certified bounds.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter selection uses a synthetic validation set (Gaussian noise), risking overfitting and poor generalization to real OOD distributions.
- The rank‑1 approximation assumes a large spectral gap in class feature covariances; this may fail for isotropic spectra or certain architectures, degrading performance.
- Probability‑weighted aggregation depends on classifier confidence, which can be miscalibrated on OOD inputs; no explicit calibration is applied, potentially harming robustness.

## Confidence
- **Effectiveness:** Medium – SOTA results are compelling but lack independent replication and synthetic validation may bias results.
- **Efficiency:** High – Linear O(CD) complexity and mathematical justification for rank‑1 approximation are solid, supported by ablations.
- **Mechanistic soundness:** Medium – Proposed mechanisms are plausible and empirically motivated, but core assumptions (spectral gap, aggregation weighting, global correction) are not fully validated and could break under some distribution shifts.

## Next Checks
1. **Full‑VS2 ablation:** Compute VNS with the exact VS2 change (using all eigenvalues) on a held‑out subset; compare FPR@95/AUROC against the rank‑1 version to quantify accuracy loss and confirm spectral dominance.
2. **Spectral gap quantification:** For each dataset, compute eigenvalue decay of class covariance matrices; report the ratio of the top eigenvalue to the sum of all eigenvalues. Correlate this ratio with VNS performance to test the rank‑1 assumption.
3. **Real‑OOD hyperparameter transfer:** Tune hyperparameters on the synthetic Gaussian validation set, then evaluate VNS on multiple real OOD benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet‑O, Textures, Places365). Measure performance drops to assess robustness of the validation strategy.
