# Attention in Space: Functional Roles of VLM Heads for Spatial Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.20662  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20662  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** VLMs rely on a sparse subset of attention heads for cognitive functions; spatial heads are especially scarce, and targeted interventions (e.g., masking, SHA) can significantly improve spatial reasoning performance.

## Executive Summary
Vision-language models (VLMs) often struggle with spatial reasoning. This work investigates how attention heads within VLMs support these capabilities. The authors introduce CogVSR, a dataset that breaks complex spatial questions into stepwise subquestions, each labeled with one of eight cognitive functions (e.g., spatial perception, relational reasoning). Using CogVSR, they develop a probing framework to identify attention heads specialized for each function across multiple VLM architectures. Analysis reveals that functional heads are universally sparse—fewer than 9% of heads are highly important—and that spatial heads are particularly scarce. Intervention experiments confirm their causal roles: masking identified cognitive heads drastically reduces corresponding task performance, while Spatial Head Activation (SHA), which augments images with bounding boxes and masks to reduce high-level visual cues, boosts spatial perception accuracy by up to 10.5% on smaller models. Positive interventions that steer head activations along functional directions also yield consistent gains. The study demonstrates that targeted manipulation of attention heads can enhance VLMs' spatial reasoning, underscoring the need for cognitively informed model design.

## Method Summary
The authors construct CogVSR by decomposing 1,142 complex spatial questions into 3,759 subquestions, each annotated with multi-label cognitive functions. A probing pipeline is applied to several VLMs (InternVL3, Qwen2.5-VL, Llama3.2-Vision). For correctly answered subquestions, they extract activations from all attention heads for the top‑3 “important” tokens (selected by an external LLM proxy). A multi‑layer perceptron classifier is trained on these features to predict the cognitive function labels. Head importance is computed as the gradient of the classification loss with respect to the head output, multiplied by the activation (averaged across samples). Heads with importance above an elbow threshold per function are designated as “cognitive heads.” Interventions include: (1) masking—scaling the output of identified heads by a small factor ε=0.001; (2) positive steering—shifting head activations along a function‑specific direction (mean activation difference between correct and incorrect samples) with strength α=0.1; and (3) SHA—overlaying images with bounding boxes and instance masks to attenuate object‑level cues and thereby engage latent spatial reasoning pathways. All interventions are evaluated on the CogVSR test set and external spatial benchmarks (VSR, Spatial457, SpatialEval, 3DSRBench).

## Key Results
- Functional attention heads are universally sparse: fewer than 9% of all heads achieve high importance scores across any cognitive function.
- Spatially specialized heads (for spatial perception and relational reasoning) are noticeably scarcer than heads for other functions, correlating with weaker reasoning performance.
- Spatial Head Activation (SHA) yields substantial absolute gains: spatial perception accuracy on InternVL3‑2B increases from 61.37% to 71.82% (+10.5%), with smaller but consistent improvements observed in larger models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Sparse functional specialization
**Claim:** VLMs rely on a sparse subset of specialized attention heads for distinct cognitive functions.  
**Mechanism:** A probing classifier is trained on head activations from correctly answered subquestions. Importance scores (gradient × activation) are computed for each head; only a small fraction exceed a threshold.  
**Core assumption:** Correct reasoning steps produce distinguishable activation patterns in functionally relevant heads.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 6.1 report that fewer than 9% of heads are highly important, consistent across architectures.  
**Break condition:** If probing accuracy is near random (e.g., chance level for multi‑label classification), the identified heads may not be functionally specialized.

### Mechanism 2: Scarcity of spatial heads
**Claim:** Spatially specialized attention heads are particularly scarce compared to other functions.  
**Mechanism:** After ranking heads by importance per function, heatmaps show fewer high‑importance heads for spatial perception and relational reasoning, along with lower peak scores.  
**Core assumption:** The number and amplitude of high‑importance heads directly reflect a function’s representation capacity.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 6.3 explicitly state that spatial/relational heads are scarcer and that this scarcity impacts performance.  
**Break condition:** If spatial reasoning performance does not correlate with the count or strength of identified spatial heads across different models, scarcity may not be the primary bottleneck.

### Mechanism 3: SHA activates latent spatial heads
**Claim:** Latent spatial heads can be activated to improve spatial reasoning via input augmentation that reduces high‑level visual cues.  
**Mechanism:** SHA adds bounding box and mask overlays to images, forcing the model to rely more on spatial layout than object recognition. This increases the number and activation strength of spatial/relational heads.  
**Core assumption:** Disentangling spatial from high‑level visual processing engages latent spatial reasoning pathways.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 6.4 and Table 3 show accuracy improvements with +BBox+Mask on InternVL3‑2B (~10.5% gain) and Llama3.2‑90B‑Vision (~5% gain).  
**Break condition:** If SHA fails to increase spatial head activations (measured via re‑probing) or degrades performance on tasks requiring high‑level visual perception, the method may introduce harmful interference.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Probing via activation extraction and linear classification**  
   *Why needed:* The entire analysis hinges on extracting attention head outputs and training a probe to associate heads with cognitive functions. Without probing, identifying “cognitive heads” would be impossible.  
   *Quick check:* If a linear probe trained on head activations achieves high accuracy on cognitive function labels, what does that imply about the information contained in those activations?

2. **Sparse functional specialization in transformers**  
   *Why needed:* The paper’s central finding is that only a small subset of heads are important for any given function; this challenges assumptions of distributed, redundant representations.  
   *Quick check:* If specialization is universal, what might be the training dynamics or architectural inductive biases that produce such sparsity?

3. **Intervention as causal validation**  
   *Why needed:* The paper uses masking (negative) and activation steering (positive) to test whether identified heads causally affect performance, moving beyond correlation.  
   *Quick check:* What is the key difference between observing a head’s activation correlate with a task and demonstrating its functional role via intervention?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Visual encoder (InternViT/CLIP) → Projector → LLM backbone (transformer with L layers; each layer has M attention heads and an MLP) → Output layer  
- **Critical path:** For a subquestion, text+image tokens flow through the LLM layers. During generation, the output of each attention head `x^m_l(i)` is extracted for a selected set of “important” tokens `i` (top‑k). These per‑head activations are aggregated (e.g., mean) to form a head feature vector `¯x^m'_l`. The feature vector feeds into the probing classifier or importance computation. Interventions modify the head output before it is added back to the residual stream.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Token selection: Using top‑k important tokens (via external LLM) yields cleaner functional signals but introduces dependency on the selector. Using all or only the last token is simpler but noisier.  
  - Importance metric: Gradient×activation captures learned relevance but can be noisy; alternatives like activation magnitude or variance are simpler but may not reflect functional importance.  
  - Intervention strength: Masking factor ε must be small enough to avoid disrupting overall computation yet large enough to nullify the head’s contribution (chosen as 0.001). Steering strength α is fixed at 0.1 across models.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Probe accuracy on CogVSR test set is near random (multi‑label chance level).  
  - Masking random heads causes similar performance drops as masking cognitive heads (indicating poor head identification).  
  - SHA degrades performance on high‑level visual perception tasks (e.g., object recognition) while improving spatial tasks (expected trade‑off).  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Functional head identification: Train the MLP probe on CogVSR training set, compute importance scores for all heads, generate per‑function heatmaps (e.g., Fig. 2, 5‑9).  
  2. Negative intervention: Mask top cognitive heads (vs. random heads) and evaluate on CogVSR test subquestions and four external spatial benchmarks (200 samples each).  
  3. Spatial Head Activation (SHA): Generate object masks/bounding boxes for test images, apply the augmentation, re‑probe to count activated spatial heads, and measure accuracy gain (Table 3, Fig. 4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **What functional roles do MLP (feed‑forward) layers play in spatial reasoning?** The study focuses exclusively on attention heads, leaving MLPs completely unexplored.  
- **What advanced probing methods can provide deeper and more reliable insights?** The current linear probe approach may be limited; alternative techniques (e.g., causal mediation, activation patching) could yield richer interpretations.  
- **How can VLMs be architecturally or training‑wise modified to promote the development of spatially specialized attention heads?** The paper identifies the scarcity of spatial heads but offers no concrete modifications to increase their prevalence or effectiveness.

## Limitations
- **Probing framework validity** is uncertain due to moderate probe accuracy (~78‑89%) and unspecified details (token‑selection heuristic, elbow threshold), which could introduce noise or arbitrariness into head identification.  
- **SHA mechanism** lacks thorough ablation; it is unclear whether gains stem specifically from engaging latent spatial heads or from simpler visual simplification effects. Potential cross‑task interference (e.g., harm to object recognition) remains untested.  
- **Dataset quality** may be compromised by automated subquestion generation with GPT‑4o‑mini, which could contain logical noise despite human verification of a subset.

## Confidence
- Sparse functional specialization: **Medium**  
- Spatial head scarcity: **Medium**  
- Spatial Head Activation (SHA) effectiveness: **Medium**  
- Probe‑based head identification: **Low**

## Next Checks
- **Probe generalization stress‑test:** Train probes on shuffled cognitive function labels; if accuracy remains high, head identification may be spurious. Also, check whether probe accuracy scales with model size in line with spatial reasoning improvements.  
- **SHA cross‑task interference evaluation:** Apply SHA to non‑spatial benchmarks (e.g., object classification, captioning). Significant degradation would confirm a trade‑off; negligible impact would suggest SHA benefits are specific to spatial reasoning.  
- **Head count vs. performance correlation across models:** For each VLM, plot spatial head count (or average importance) against spatial benchmark accuracy (e.g., VSR, Spatial457). A strong positive correlation would strengthen the scarcity bottleneck claim; weak/no correlation would point to other limiting factors.
