# On the Width Scaling of Neural Optimizers Under Matrix Operator Norms I: Row/Column Normalization and Hyperparameter Transfer

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.09952  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09952  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Introducing mean‑normalized operator norms (p,mean)→(q,mean) yields the MOGA optimizer, which achieves near‑constant optimal learning rates across model widths and converges faster than AdamW and Muon under large‑token, low‑loss training regimes.

## Executive Summary
Modern neural optimizers like AdamW and Muon suffer from poor hyperparameter transferability when scaling network width. This work unifies these optimizers as instances of steepest descent under matrix operator norms and shows that standard p→q norms fail to produce width‑independent Lipschitz and smoothness bounds due to layer incompatibility. The solution is to enforce cross‑layer compatibility via mean‑normalized operator norms, which guarantee width‑independent Lipschitz control; smoothness becomes width‑independent when q≥2p. This theoretical insight leads to MOGA, a practical row/column normalization method with explicit width‑aware learning‑rate scaling. Large‑scale pre‑training experiments on GPT‑2 and LLaMA families confirm that MOGA (especially row normalization) maintains stable optimal learning rates across widths from small to billion‑parameter models and attains faster convergence than AdamW/Muon under extended token budgets.

## Method Summary
The authors treat gradient‑based optimizers as steepest‑descent updates under matrix norms. Standard norms introduce per‑layer scaling factors (e.g., √(d_in/d_out) in Muon) that depend on layer dimensions, causing Lipschitz constants to grow with width unless norm parameters are tuned per layer. To achieve true width‑independence, they define mean‑normalized norms: (p,mean) norms normalize by mean magnitude across rows/columns, while (q,mean) norms define step size. This enforces uniform sensitivity across layers. The resulting MOGA optimizer applies a scaling factor η / (d_in)^{1/p} combined with a row‑ or column‑normalized gradient (e.g., rownorm_p(grad)). Empirically, they train GPT‑2 (Small/Medium/Large/XL) and LLaMA‑130M on OpenWebText and C4 under 1× and 8× Chinchilla token budgets, sweeping learning rates for AdamW, Muon, and MOGA to compare final training loss curves. Warmup is linear (10% for standard, 2.5% for large‑token runs) followed by cosine decay.

## Key Results
- MOGA with row normalization achieves nearly constant optimal learning rate across model widths (from 124M to 1.5B parameters), confirming width‑independent step‑size scaling.
- Under 8× Chinchilla token budgets, MOGA(row) converges faster and reaches lower training loss than AdamW and Muon, particularly in the late training phase when models approach the low‑loss regime.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Standard operator norms (e.g., p→q) assign a scaling factor to each weight matrix that depends on its input/output dimensions (e.g., √(d_in/d_out) for q=2). This scaling is layer‑specific and incompatible across widths; as width increases, the Lipschitz constant of the preconditioner grows unless the learning rate is adjusted per width. Mean‑normalized norms replace raw dimensions with mean activations (or gradient statistics) across rows/columns, effectively standardizing the scale of weight updates relative to typical activation magnitude. This enforces cross‑layer compatibility: each layer’s update magnitude becomes O(1) in a width‑independent sense when p and q satisfy q≥2p for smoothness. Consequently, a single global learning rate suffices across widths. Row normalization (normalizing over output dimension) aligns with the inductive bias of back‑propagated gradients and performed best empirically.

## Foundational Learning
- **Operator norms for optimizers**: Norms on weight matrices define the steepest‑descent metric; choice of p→q determines scaling behavior. *Why needed*: Provides theoretical unification of AdamW/Muon. *Quick check*: Verify that an update is f(W) = argmin∥ΔW∥_p such that loss(W+ΔW) ≤ loss(W) + ⟨∇L, ΔW⟩.
- **Mean‑normalized norms**: (p,mean) norm uses (∑|g_{ij}|^p / m)^{1/p} per row/column instead of (∑|g_{ij}|^p)^{1/p}. *Why needed*: Removes dependence on width d_in/d_out. *Quick check*: For a random matrix, verify that row‑normalized value has variance not growing with d_in.
- **Lipschitz and smoothness constants**: Bounding ∥∇L∥_p and Hessian in operator norms ensures stable updates. *Why needed*: Guarantees convergence under fixed learning rate. *Quick check*: For a linear model, compute theoretical constants with/without mean‑normalization.
- **Hyperparameter transfer**: The property that optimal hyperparameters (e.g., LR) are invariant to model width. *Why needed*: Reduces tuning cost for large models. *Quick check*: Train two widths with same LR; compare training loss curves.
- **Row vs column normalization**: Row‑norm operates along output dimension; column‑norm along input. *Why needed*: Aligns with gradient flow direction. *Quick check*: Inspect gradient distributions across layers; row‑norm typically yields tighter per‑layer scale homogeneity.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Data → Tokenizer → Model → Forward pass → Loss → Backward gradient g → Row/column normalization (mean‑p norm) → Learning‑rate scaling (η / d_in^{1/p}) → Weight update (e.g., sign‑SGD variant). For embeddings, sign‑SGD or column‑norm may be used.
- **Critical path**:  
  Gradient computation → Normalization per layer (compute row‑wise ℓ_p norms, scale) → Global LR application → Parameter update. The normalization step is crucial; it must be width‑aware (divide by d_in^{1/p}) and use the correct p.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  Row vs column: Row normalization generally more stable for back‑prop gradients; column may better suit certain architectures. Choice of p: Larger p increases robustness to outliers but may under‑normalize; p=2 is a balanced default. Warmup fraction: Larger warmup (10%) safer for standard runs; reduced warmup (2.5%) accelerates large‑token runs but risks instability.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Optimal LR varies widely with width → missing d_in^{−1/p} scaling or incorrect norm implementation.  
  - Divergence in large‑token runs → LR too high or warmup insufficient; check per‑layer update magnitudes.  
  - MOGA underperforms Muon → verify sign‑SGD step (sign(g) instead of g/∥g∥) and exact rownorm formula; ensure no momentum if using pure steepest descent.
- **First three experiments**:  
  1. **Width‑transfer test**: Train GPT‑2 Small/Medium/Large/XL with MOGA(row, p=2) for 3B tokens each, sweeping peak LR ∈ [4e−3, 4e−2]. Plot final loss vs LR; expect optimal LR within 20% variation across widths.  
  2. **Large‑token advantage**: For GPT‑2 Small and LLaMA‑130M, train AdamW, Muon, and MOGA(row) at their 1× optimal LRs for 8× Chinchilla tokens. Compare loss curves; MOGA should gain advantage after ~1× tokens.  
  3. **Per‑layer norm sanity**: For a trained GPT‑2 Small with MOGA, log ∥ΔW∥_p per layer across steps. Values should be O(1) and width‑independent; also repeat with different widths to confirm consistency.

## Limitations
- Token budget definitions for “1×” and “8×” Chinchilla are approximate (exact multiplier may differ from 20× parameters).
- Empirical results are sensitive to implementation details: weight tying for GPT‑2, gradient clipping, mixed precision, and embedding layer handling.
- Muon’s scaling factor (√(d_in/d_out)) is discussed conceptually; repo implementation may differ, affecting baseline performance.
