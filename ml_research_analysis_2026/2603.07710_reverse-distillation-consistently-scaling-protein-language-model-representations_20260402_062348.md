# Reverse Distillation: Consistently Scaling Protein Language Model Representations
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07710
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07710
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** Reverse distillation yields nested embeddings that restore monotonic scaling in protein language models, with rd.15B achieving a Spearman correlation of 0.904 on the ProteinGym DMS benchmark.

## Executive Summary
Protein language models (PLMs) often exhibit non‑monotonic scaling: larger models sometimes underperform smaller ones on tasks such as fitness prediction. This work introduces *reverse distillation*, a method that decomposes a larger model’s representation into an orthogonal residual added to the complete smaller model’s embedding. The resulting nested, Matryoshka‑style embeddings ensure that the smaller model’s representation is fully preserved. On the ProteinGym deep mutational scanning benchmark, reverse‑distilled variants (rd.650M, rd.3B, rd.15B) consistently outperform their baseline counterparts at identical dimensionality, with rd.15B achieving the strongest average Spearman correlation (0.904) on single‑mutation datasets. Larger reverse‑distilled models also more frequently beat smaller ones across all mutation counts, restoring monotonic scaling. Improvements extend to secondary structure and protein localization tasks, demonstrating the broad utility of the approach.

## Method Summary
Reverse distillation operates on a pair of PLMs: a smaller base model and a larger target model. First, a set of held‑out sequences (e.g., 1 000 UniRef50 sequences with low identity to test sets) is embedded by both models. A linear mapping **W*** from the base to the target space is learned via principal component regression (PCR), discarding noise‑dominated principal components using the Johnstone threshold. The residual matrix **R** = **Hₚ** − **Hᵣ****W*** captures information unique to the larger model. An SVD of **R** yields right singular vectors **V**<sub>res</sub> that form an orthogonal basis for this residual. At inference, the representation is constructed as the concatenation **H**<sub>rd</sub> = [**H**<sub>r</sub>, (**H**<sub>p</sub>−**H**<sub>r</sub>**W***)**V**<sub>res</sub>]. For a family of models, the process is chained: e.g., an 8 M model is reverse‑distilled into a 35 M model, whose output then serves as the base for a 150 M model, and so on. This produces a sequence of nested embeddings where each larger model’s representation begins with the full smaller model’s embedding followed by a complementary residual.

## Key Results
- Reverse‑distilled ESM‑2 variants (rd.650M, rd.3B, rd.15B) outperform their baseline counterparts at the same embedding dimensionality on the ProteinGym DMS benchmark.
- The rd.15B model achieves the highest average Spearman correlation (0.904) on single‑mutation datasets and the best performance on multi‑mutation sets among all 15 B‑scale models.
- Reverse distillation restores monotonic scaling: larger reverse‑distilled models more consistently outperform smaller ones across mutation counts, and gains also hold on secondary structure and localization tasks (rd.15B attains top AUPR in 4 of 5 benchmarks).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Reverse distillation restores monotonic scaling by preserving the entire representation of the smaller model and supplementing it with an orthogonal residual that captures the larger model’s unique information. The orthogonalization ensures the residual does not redundantly encode content already present in the base embedding, allowing increased capacity without interference. Because the smaller model’s embedding forms a prefix of the larger one, any downstream task using only the first *k* dimensions automatically inherits the smaller model’s performance—this guarantees that scaling up cannot degrade results and that improvements are additive.

## Foundational Learning
- **Protein language models (PLMs):** Neural networks trained on massive protein sequence corpora to produce contextual embeddings. *Why needed:* Provide generic representations that can be adapted to downstream biology tasks. *Quick check:* Does the PLM accurately predict masked amino acids in held‑out protein sequences?
- **Scaling laws:** Empirical relationship where model performance improves with size, compute, or data. *Why needed:* Guide efficient resource allocation. *Quick check:* Plot validation loss vs. model parameters for a family of PLMs; expect a monotonic decrease.
- **Non‑monotonic scaling:** Phenomenon where larger models sometimes underperform smaller ones. *Why needed:* Highlights gaps in current scaling theory. *Quick check:* Train or evaluate models of increasing size on a fixed task; identify size points where performance drops.
- **Orthogonal decomposition:** Representing a vector as a sum of components lying in orthogonal subspaces. *Why needed:* Enables separation of shared vs. unique information between models. *Quick check:* Given two random matrices, perform QR or SVD to obtain orthogonal bases; verify dot products between bases are near zero.
- **Principal component regression (PCR):** Regression performed after dimensionality reduction via PCA. *Why needed:* Denoises the mapping between model embeddings by discarding low‑variance directions. *Quick check:* Apply PCR to a synthetic dataset with added noise; compare reconstruction error against ordinary least squares.
- **Nested embeddings:** Sequence of representations where each larger container includes all lower‑dimensional ones as prefixes. *Why needed:* Guarantees consistent performance when truncating to a fixed size. *Quick check:* For two embedded vectors of different lengths, truncate the longer to the shorter’s dimension and compute similarity; should match the shorter’s self‑similarity.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Training sequences → embeddings from base (**Hᵣ**) and target (**Hₚ**) → PCR learning **W*** → residual **R** = **Hₚ**−**Hᵣ****W*** → SVD → **V**<sub>res</sub> → inference concatenation **H**<sub>rd</sub> = [**Hᵣ**, (**Hₚ**−**Hᵣ****W***)**V**<sub>res</sub>]. Chaining: output of one reverse‑distilled model becomes base for next.

**Critical path:**  
1. Gather and filter a diverse, held‑out sequence set.  
2. Compute and store embeddings for both models.  
3. Solve PCR (ridge + eigenvalue threshold) to obtain **W***.  
4. Compute residual matrix and run SVD to extract top (kₚ−kᵣ) right singular vectors.  
5. At inference, load base model, compute embeddings, apply linear map, subtract, project onto **V**<sub>res</sub>, and concatenate.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *PCR threshold (Johnstone):* Too aggressive discards useful signal; too conservative retains noise.  
- *Ridge regularization (α):* Balances over‑/under‑fitting of **W***; depends on data size and model correlation.  
- *Residual dimensionality:* More dimensions increase capacity but risk overfitting; must match downstream task.  
- *Linearity assumption:* The mapping **W*** is linear; if base‑target relationship is highly non‑linear, performance may suffer.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Reconstruction error ∥**Hₚ** − [**Hᵣ**, **H**<sub>res</sub>]∥<sub>F</sub> high on validation → poor **W*** or **V**<sub>res</sub>.  
- Large gap between single‑mutation and multi‑mutation correlations → overfitting to local effects.  
- rd.*k* embedding does not match rd.*k−1* prefix on first *k* dimensions → chaining error.  
- Singular values of **R** decay slowly → residual mostly noise, indicating ineffective PCR.

**First experiments:**  
1. **Nesting verification:** For 100 random sequences, compute rd.650M and rd.3B embeddings; confirm first 1280 dimensions of rd.3B equal rd.650M within 1e‑5 RMS.  
2. **Ridge sensitivity:** Perform LOOCV on a single‑mutation DMS dataset, sweeping α over {1e‑4, 1e‑3, 1e‑2, 1e‑1, 1}. Record Spearman; choose α maximizing validation.  
3. **Scaling check:** Evaluate baseline and reverse‑distilled models (8 M, 35 M, 150 M, 650 M, 3 B, 15 B) on a held‑out DMS dataset; plot Spearman vs. size and verify monotonic increase for reverse‑distilled models.

## Limitations
- The exact procedure for constructing the 1 000‑sequence training set (UniRef50 snapshot, clustering tool, length thresholds) is not disclosed, risking dataset shift and affecting decomposition quality.  
- Ridge regression hyperparameters (α) and the precise Johnstone threshold computation for PCR are omitted; these critically influence the residual basis and downstream performance.  
- Handling of variable‑length sequences during stacking (padding, masking, or bucketing) is unspecified, which could alter the residual matrix and singular vectors.

## Confidence

- **Monotonic scaling restoration:** Medium  
- **Transfer to other tasks (secondary structure, localization):** Low
