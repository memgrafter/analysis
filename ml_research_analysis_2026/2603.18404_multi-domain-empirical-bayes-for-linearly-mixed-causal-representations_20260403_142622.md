# Multi-Domain Empirical Bayes for Linearly-Mixed Causal Representations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18404
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18404
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Achieves relative MSE 0.106 and Frobenius error 0.534 on synthetic data, significantly outperforming competing empirical Bayes methods.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles multi-domain causal representation learning, where latent causal variables generate high-dimensional observations across experimental conditions via interventions. Under a linear Gaussian measurement model and an acyclic structural causal model (SCM) prior with known graph and intervention targets, the authors develop an empirical Bayes f-modeling algorithm. The EM procedure uses Tweedie’s formula for posterior updates, with the score function estimated via causal score matching that respects the graph structure (using cubic splines). Updates for the mixing matrix and noise variance are analytical given the posteriors. Orthogonal mixing reduces the problem to a normal means model. On synthetic data, the method substantially improves latent recovery and mixing matrix estimation compared to baselines and shows robustness to variations in dimension and sample size.

## Method Summary
The model assumes a linear Gaussian measurement process: \(x = A z + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)\), where \(z\) are latent causal variables following an acyclic SCM with known directed acyclic graph (DAG) and interventions indicated by binary vectors \(a\). The mixing matrix \(A\) is constrained to have orthogonal columns (\(A^\top A = D^2\) diagonal). An EM algorithm alternates between: an E‑step that computes posterior moments of \(z\) via Tweedie’s formula, requiring the score of the marginal distribution of \(y = O^\top x\) (where \(A = O D\)); and an M‑step with closed‑form updates for \(A\) (via Procrustes solution from SVD of cross‑moments) and \(\sigma^2\). The score is estimated nonparametrically by cubic splines that respect the DAG factorization—each component depends only on its parents and intervention indicator—trained by decoupled score matching losses with ridge regularization.

## Key Results
- Relative MSE of latents: 0.106 (vs. 0.414–0.592 for competing EB methods)
- Frobenius error of mixing matrix: 0.534 (vs. 1.12–1.57)
- Robustness to varying latent dimension and sample size

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Orthogonal Projection to Normal Means
- **Claim:** Under orthogonality of the mixing matrix, the model reduces to a multivariate normal means problem, enabling the use of Tweedie’s formula for posterior estimation.
- **Mechanism:** The linear Gaussian measurement model \(x = A z + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)\) and \(A^\top A = D^2\) (diagonal) admits the reparameterization \(A = O D\) with \(O\) orthonormal. Multiplying by \(O^\top\) yields \(y = O^\top x = D z + \zeta\), \(\zeta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)\). This is a normal means model with means \(Dz\) and known noise variance \(\sigma^2\).
- **Core assumption:** The true mixing matrix satisfies \(A^\top A = \text{diag}(\cdot)\) (orthogonal columns, possibly different norms). This is Assumption 4.1.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Orthogonal mixing assures reduction to a normal means model."
  - [section 4.3] details the reduction and parametrization \(A = O D\).
- **Break condition:** If the columns of \(A\) are not orthogonal, the projected sufficient statistics no longer correspond to a normal means model, and Tweedie’s update (4.4) becomes biased; the EM algorithm may not converge to the true parameters.

### Mechanism 2: Causal Score Matching
- **Claim:** The score of the marginal distribution of \(y\) (needed for Tweedie) is estimated by a sparse, causally structured function that respects the known DAG, allowing efficient learning and stable posterior updates.
- **Mechanism:** The score \(\nabla \log f_a(y)\) is approximated by a vector field \(s(y,a)\) with component \(s_j(y_j, y_{\text{pa}(j)}, a_j)\) (Eq. 5.2). This restricts dependence to the causal parents and the local intervention indicator, drastically reducing the number of parameters and aligning with the generative factorization. The parameters are learned by minimizing the Fisher divergence (score matching loss) which decouples across nodes (Theorem 5.1). Cubic splines provide a flexible nonparametric fit.
- **Core assumption:** The causal graph \(G^\star\) (parents structure) is known and correctly specified; the true score decomposes according to the Markov blanket (Eq. 5.1). The approximation becomes exact when \(G^\star\) is used and the noise is small.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5] introduces the causal score function and its decoupled estimation.
  - [abstract] "causal score matching that respects the graph structure (using cubic splines)."
- **Break condition:** Misspecification of the graph (e.g., using an empty graph when strong parental effects exist) leads to a biased score estimate, which propagates to erroneous posterior means and mixing matrix updates. Additionally, if the sample size is too small relative to the spline basis complexity, the score estimate becomes high‑variance.

### Mechanism 3: EM with Analytical Updates
- **Claim:** An EM‑style algorithm alternating between posterior updates (via Tweedie) and M‑step updates (closed‑form for \(A\) and \(\sigma^2\)) yields stable and accurate estimation of the measurement parameters and latent causal variables.
- **Mechanism:** The E‑step computes the first and second moments of the latent posterior using the current score estimate and the normal means model (Eqs. 4.4–4.5). The M‑step maximizes the expected complete‑data log‑likelihood; because the measurement model is linear Gaussian, the updates for \(A\) (via Procrustes solution) and \(\sigma^2\) (via MLE) are analytic given the posterior summaries (Eqs. 4.8–4.9). This avoids costly numerical optimization and leverages the conjugate structure.
- **Core assumption:** The model class is correctly specified (linear Gaussian measurements, SCM priors, known graph and interventions). The posterior approximations from Tweedie’s formula are accurate under the current parameter estimates.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.1] outlines the EM procedure and Algorithm 1.
  - [section 4.5] derives the analytical M‑step updates.
- **Break condition:** If the posterior moments from the E‑step are poor (e.g., due to a bad score estimate or wrong noise variance), the M‑step may converge to a suboptimal or degenerate solution (e.g., \(A\) with collinear columns). The algorithm may also get stuck in local optima if initialized poorly.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Empirical Bayes (EB) Shrinkage
- **Why needed here:** The method pools information across the many latent variables (across samples and domains) via an implicit prior borrowed from the interventional SCM. EB shrinkage improves estimation accuracy for individual latent variables by leveraging the shared structure.
- **Quick check question:** In EB f‑modeling, how does Tweedie’s formula relate the score of the marginal distribution to the posterior mean of the latent variable?

### Concept: Score Matching for Density Estimation
- **Why needed here:** The marginal density \(f_a(y)\) is intractable due to the latent variables and interventional priors. Score matching bypasses density estimation by directly learning the score function, which is sufficient for Tweedie updates.
- **Quick check question:** Why does the score matching loss decouple across components when the score is constrained to respect a DAG structure?

### Concept: Linear Gaussian Measurement with SCM Priors
- **Why needed here:** This specific model class yields tractable posterior updates via Tweedie and analytical M‑steps, while still capturing the causal invariances across domains (via interventions on the SCM). It forms the foundation for the identifiability claims and algorithm design.
- **Quick check question:** What role does the orthogonality condition on the mixing matrix play, and how does it relate to independent component analysis?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Inputs: Multi‑domain data \(((x_i^e, a^e))_{e=1}^M\).  
Modules:  
1. Orthogonal projection via current \(O\): compute \(y_i^e = O^\top x_i^e\).  
2. Causal score estimator: fit spline functions \(s_j(y_j, y_{\text{pa}(j)}, a^ej)\) by solving decoupled ridge regressions (Eq. A.1).  
3. Tweedie updates: compute posterior means \(\tilde{z}_i^e = D^{-1}(y_i^e + \eta \hat{\sigma}^2 \hat{s}(y_i^e, a^e))\) and element‑wise second moments using derivative of splines.  
4. M‑step: form matrix \(M = \sum_{e,i} x_i^e \tilde{z}_i^{e\top}\), perform SVD \(M = U\Sigma V^\top\) to get \(O = UV^\top\), \(D_{jj} = \Sigma_{jj} / \sum_{e,i} \tilde{z}_{ij}^{2}\), and update \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) via Eq. 4.9.  
Outputs: Estimated latents \(\tilde{z}_i^e\), mixing matrix \(\hat{A} = \hat{O}\hat{D}\), noise variance \(\hat{\sigma}^2\).

**Critical path:**  
Initialization: PCA or random orthogonal \(O\), diagonal \(D\). Then repeat until convergence:  
- Score estimation (most expensive, scales with spline basis and parent set sizes).  
- Tweedie updates (cheap, element‑wise).  
- Analytical M‑step (SVD of \(M\), cheap if \(d_Z\) small).  
- Damping \(\eta\) optionally reduced over iterations.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Orthogonality vs generality: Relaxing \(A^\top A = D^2\) to \(A = O\Lambda\) adds a full \(d_Z \times d_Z\) PSD matrix, making Tweedie update require matrix inversion (more costly, less stable).  
- Graph sparsity: Using the true DAG yields smaller spline inputs (small \(|pa(j)|\)) → lower variance, but risks bias if graph wrong; the complete DAG (over the causal order) is more flexible but computationally expensive (exponential in \(|pa(j)|\)).  
- Spline flexibility: More knots/basis functions increase approximation capacity but risk overfitting; ridge regularization mitigates but requires tuning.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Relative MSE > 0.5 or mixing matrix Frobenius error > 1.0 (vs true ~0.5) indicates poor learning.  
- High variance across random seeds suggests instability (e.g., from over‑splining or bad initialization).  
- Diverging \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) or columns of \(\hat{A}\) becoming collinear signal violation of orthogonality or faulty posterior moments.  
- If the empirical per‑environment performance varies wildly, it may indicate misspecified intervention targets or graph.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline comparison (Fig. 3 left):** Run 40 independent seeds on a chain SCM (4 latents, 100 observables, 20 single‑node interventions, \(N_e=2000\)). Compare CRL f‑modeling with true DAG, empty DAG, complete DAG, pooled PCA, and no‑shrinkage (\(\eta=0\)). Metrics: relative MSE of latents and Frobenius error of \(A\).  
2. **Scaling with latent dimension \(d_Z\) (Fig. 3 middle‑top):** Vary \(d_Z \in \{2,5,10,20,35\}\) (with \(d_X=100\), \(N_e=200\)), plot median relative MSE over 20 runs. Expect ~\(\sqrt{d_Z}\) growth.  
3. **Scaling with sample size \(N_e\) (Fig. 3 middle‑bottom):** Fix \(d_Z=4\) but vary \(N_e \in \{100,200,400,800,1500,3000\}\), plot median relative MSE over 20 runs. Expect error to stabilize beyond \(N_e\ge200\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the empirical Bayes f‑modeling framework be extended to settings where the causal graph and intervention targets are unknown, as opposed to the known‑graph assumption in this paper?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "Extensions include ... relaxing the assumption of known graphs or targets by using the optimal EB objectives for graph/target discovery."
- **Why unresolved:** The current algorithm requires the true DAG and intervention targets as input; joint learning of graph structure and representation remains an open problem.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A concrete EM extension that alternates between graph hypothesis updates (e.g., via score‑based structure learning) and the current latent/parameter updates, with theoretical guarantees and empirical validation.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the proposed EB f‑modeling be generalized to richer intervention structures beyond binary intervention targets, such as soft interventions (e.g., changing mechanism parameters continuously) or additive shifts in latent variables?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors mention "richer intervention structures \(p(z|a)\) with non‑binary a (e.g., additive shifts)" as a desired extension.
- **Why unresolved:** The model assumes binary \(a_e\) indicating intervention targets; the score parameterization and Tweedie updates may not directly apply to more complex intervention variables.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derivation of score‑matching objectives for continuous or parametric intervention regimes, combined with modifications to the posterior updates, and experiments showing improved recovery for relevant data types (e.g., single‑cell perturbations with dosage effects).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** The algorithm relies on the orthogonality condition \(A^\top A = D^2\) (diagonal) to reduce to a normal means model. How does violating strict orthogonality affect the accuracy and convergence of the EM algorithm, and does the proposed relaxation to \(A = O\Lambda\) (\(\Lambda\) positive semi‑definite) consistently recover the true parameters?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper introduces the orthogonality assumption (Asm. 4.1) and mentions a relaxation in §4.6, but all experiments use orthogonal \(A^\star\) and no analysis or tests of the relaxed case are provided.
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical guarantees and empirical robustness of the relaxation are unknown; non‑orthogonal mixing matrices are common in practice, and the impact on the Tweedie updates and second‑order corrections is unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical study of identifiability and estimator properties under the relaxed parametrization, plus simulation experiments comparing orthogonal vs. non‑orthogonal ground truth and evaluating convergence and error metrics.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** For nonlinear measurement models, Tweedie's formula does not directly apply. Can f‑modeling be extended to such settings, perhaps via local approximations or score‑based methods, while retaining its advantages over g‑modeling?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors state: "For nonlinear measurement models, f‑modeling is less directly applicable because of the difficulty to adapt Tweedie's formula. On the other hand, g‑modeling becomes more natural." This suggests a gap: can f‑modeling be made viable for nonlinear observations?
- **Why unresolved:** The f‑modeling approach hinges on the linear‑Gaussian structure to obtain closed‑form posterior updates (Tweedie). Nonlinear measurements break this, leaving an open challenge to develop analogous EB schemes without explicit priors.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A methodology that extends f‑modeling—e.g., by using score matching to directly estimate the marginal score of \(x\) (rather than \(y\)) under nonlinearity, or by developing approximate Tweedie formulas for specific nonlinear families—and demonstrating superior sample efficiency or accuracy compared to g‑modeling in identified nonlinear CRL settings.

## Limitations
- Orthogonal mixing assumption (\(A^\top A\) diagonal) is strong; relaxing it complicates updates and may degrade performance.
- Requires fully known causal DAG and intervention targets; misspecification can cause significant bias.
- Spline hyperparameters (knots, ridge \(\lambda\)) and damping factor \(\eta\) are not fully specified, hindering faithful reproduction.

## Confidence
- EM recovery under orthogonal mixing: Medium
- Causal score matching: Medium
- Robustness to dimension/sample size: Low
