# Tide: A Customisable Dataset Generator for Anti-Money Laundering Research

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01863  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01863  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Open-source, customizable generator that produces graph-based financial networks with injected laundering patterns; released two benchmark datasets (0.10% and 0.19% illicit ratios) and demonstrated condition-dependent model rankings (LightGBM PR-AUC 78.05 in low illicit, XGBoost 85.12 in high illicit).

## Executive Summary
Tide addresses the critical shortage of accessible, realistic transactional data for Anti-Money Laundering (AML) machine learning research, where privacy concerns prevent sharing of real data and existing synthetic generators neglect temporal dynamics. It is an open-source, highly configurable generator that constructs heterogeneous financial transaction graphs by injecting laundering patterns defined through combined structural (topology) and temporal (timing/frequency) components. The paper releases two benchmark datasets with illicit ratios of 0.10% (low) and 0.19% (high), and shows that model performance is condition‑dependent: LightGBM achieves the highest PR‑AUC (78.05) under the low‑illicit condition, while XGBoost performs best (85.12) under the high‑illicit condition. Scalability analysis demonstrates near‑linear time complexity (O(N^1.164)), enabling the production of large‑scale, reproducible benchmarks that differentiate model robustness across operational scenarios.

## Method Summary
Tide generates synthetic financial transaction graphs via a highly configurable pipeline driven by YAML files specifying graph size, simulation timeframe, and pattern parameters. The process begins with an Entity Factory that creates nodes (individuals, businesses, accounts) populated with attributes such as risk scores and countries. A Clusterer then groups entities into risk‑based clusters (e.g., high‑risk jurisdictions, cash‑intensive businesses). Fraudulent laundering patterns—each defined by a structural topology *S* and a temporal signature *T*—are injected by selecting entities from appropriate clusters and generating transaction sequences that obey pattern‑specific rules (e.g., U‑turn chains with periodic transfers). Concurrently, a Background Generator produces legitimate activity, including *counter‑leakage* patterns that mimic fraud signatures (e.g., legitimate bursts, chains) to prevent trivial detection. An Aggregator merges all fraudulent subgraphs and background activity into a single heterogeneous directed graph. Finally, the Exporter serializes the data (CSV, PyTorch Geometric), applies a temporal split (training ≤ *t*₁, validation ≤ *t*₂, test > *t*₁), and excludes leakage‑prone features like internal risk scores.

For evaluation, Tide provides two benchmark datasets (illicit ratios 0.10% and 0.19%). Baseline models include graph neural networks (GIN, PNA) and gradient‑boosted trees (LightGBM, XGBoost), trained on either graph representations or tabular features with handcrafted temporal aggregates. Due to extreme class imbalance, PR‑AUC is the primary metric; thresholds are set via Youden’s J. Results confirm condition‑dependent model rankings and the generator’s scalability (O(N^1.164) across 1k–12k individuals), supporting the creation of large‑scale, reproducible AML benchmarks.

## Key Results
- Released two benchmark datasets with illicit ratios of 0.10% (low) and 0.19% (high).  
- Model performance is condition‑dependent: LightGBM achieves highest PR‑AUC (78.05) under low illicit conditions; XGBoost performs best (85.12) under high illicit conditions.  
- Generator scales near‑linearly with graph size (time complexity O(N^1.164)), enabling efficient production of large‑scale graphs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Combined structural and temporal pattern injection
- **Claim:** Tide distinguishes laundering from legitimate activity by injecting subgraphs that combine specific topological shapes with precise temporal signatures.  
- **Mechanism:** The generator pre‑defines laundering pattern tuples *P* = (*S*, *T*). The structural component *S* selects clustered entities (e.g., high‑risk individuals, overseas businesses) to form a topology (e.g., U‑turn chain). The temporal component *T* enforces timing rules (e.g., periodic transfers every 7 days, bursts within 1 hour) on the transaction sequence. The co‑occurrence of a rare topology and a rare temporal profile yields a stronger fraud signal than either alone.  
- **Core assumption:** Real laundering schemes exhibit such conjunctions of structure and time; synthetic patterns based on regulatory typologies will mimic this.  
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract describes the combined approach; Section 4.4 explicitly defines five laundering patterns with structural and temporal parameters (e.g., “Periodic (7/14/30d)” and “1→n overseas”); neighboring literature provides limited direct corroboration.  
- **Break condition:** If temporal parameters are too loose (e.g., periodic window too wide) or structural clustering too broad, patterns become statistically indistinguishable from background, degrading PR‑AUC (observed during calibration, Section 3.1).

### Mechanism 2: Counter‑leakage via overlapping legitimate patterns
- **Claim:** Tide prevents trivial detection (data leakage) by ensuring background legitimate activity overlaps with fraud patterns across structural, temporal, and feature dimensions.  
- **Mechanism:** Alongside baseline random payments/salaries, the generator injects *counter‑leakage* patterns (Section 4.5.7). For every fraud signature (e.g., cash deposits, multi‑hop chains, near‑threshold amounts), a legitimate pattern is generated that shares that signature (e.g., “legitimate bursts” create tight 5–15 transaction clusters; “legitimate chains” create 2–5 hop paths). This forces models to learn the *contextual combination* of features rather than any single artefact.  
- **Core assumption:** In real data, legitimate activity exhibits similar micro‑features (timing bursts, multi‑hop payments) but with different macro semantics; a robust detector must capture the latter.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.5.7 lists seven counter‑leakage patterns explicitly designed to overlap with fraud signatures; Section 5.2.3 notes that models still achieve only modest precision (2–12%) despite strong PR‑AUC, suggesting the overlap successfully prevents trivial solutions.  
- **Break condition:** If counter‑leakage patterns are omitted or mis‑calibrated (e.g., legitimate bursts are rarer or have different amount distributions), a single feature (e.g., “transaction count in 1 hour”) becomes a near‑perfect fraud predictor, inflating metrics unrealistically.

### Mechanism 3: Scalable, deterministic, and temporally valid benchmarking
- **Claim:** The generator’s near‑linear time complexity (O(N^1.164)) and deterministic output enable reproducible benchmarking of AML models across graph sizes and temporal splits.  
- **Mechanism:** Tide uses a clustered entity selection (Section 4.2.1) that operates in near‑constant time relative to total graph size. Pattern instances are generated in isolation then merged, avoiding full‑graph recomputation. Reproducibility is enforced via seed‑based RNGs at every step. The temporal split strategy (Section 4.7) constructs training/validation/test graphs that respect chronology, simulating operational deployment where only past transactions are visible.  
- **Core assumption:** Model performance should be comparable across different graph scales and data splits if the generator’s statistical properties (fraud ratio, pattern density) are held constant.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 6.6 reports scaling exponent α=1.164 and R²=0.976 across 1k–12k individuals, demonstrating predictable near‑linear growth; Section 4.7 details the temporal split process; neighboring papers (e.g., AMLSim, SynthAML) are cited for scalability/reproducibility, but Tide’s specific scaling and temporal validation are novel.  
- **Break condition:** If the clustering index becomes a bottleneck (e.g., too many clusters, complex queries) or memory usage grows super‑linearly (O(N²)), generation time explodes for large graphs, breaking the ability to produce large‑scale benchmarks efficiently.

## Foundational Learning
- **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) & Message Passing**  
  *Why needed:* Tide’s output is a graph. Understanding GNNs (e.g., GIN, PNA) is essential to grasp why some models capture injected structural patterns (e.g., U‑turn chains) while others do not.  
  *Quick check:* What does the sum aggregator in GIN allow it to capture that average aggregation (GCN) might miss in a fan‑out pattern?

- **Extreme Class Imbalance & PR‑AUC**  
  *Why needed:* AML data has fraud ratios far below 1%. ROC‑AUC is misleading; PR‑AUC is the primary metric. Engineers must understand why precision‑recall curves matter more than ROC here.  
  *Quick check:* Why would a model with 99% ROC‑AUC still have only 2% precision in a 0.1% fraud rate scenario?

- **Temporal Graph Splitting**  
  *Why needed:* Random train/test splits leak future information. The temporal split (Section 4.7) is crucial for realistic evaluation; misunderstanding it leads to invalid benchmarks.  
  *Quick check:* If you use all transactions up to *t*<sub>max</sub> to compute node features for a test transaction at time *t*<sub>test</sub> < *t*<sub>max</sub>, what type of leakage occurs?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Entity Factory → Clusterer → Pattern Injector & Background Generator → Aggregator → Exporter

**Component descriptions:**  
- *Entity Factory:* Creates nodes (individuals, businesses, accounts) with attributes (risk_score, country, etc.).  
- *Clusterer:* Groups entities into risk‑based clusters (high‑risk jurisdiction, cash‑intensive business).  
- *Pattern Injector:* For each configured pattern (e.g., U‑Turn), uses clusters to select entities and generates timed transaction sequences adhering to pattern‑specific structural and temporal rules.  
- *Background Generator:* Produces legitimate activity, including counter‑leakage patterns that overlap with fraud signatures.  
- *Aggregator:* Merges all fraudulent subgraphs and background into a single transaction graph.  
- *Exporter:* Serializes to CSV/PyTorch Geometric, applies temporal splits, and excludes leakage‑prone features.

**Critical path:** Entity Factory → Clusterer → (multiple Pattern Injector instances + Background Generator) → Aggregator → Exporter.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Customisability vs. Complexity:* More pattern parameters increase flexibility but require careful calibration to avoid leakage or undetectability.  
- *Realism vs. Determinism:* Calibrating to real‑world distributions improves realism but deterministic seeds may oversimplify stochasticity.  
- *Graph Size vs. Memory:* Near‑linear scaling holds, but absolute memory (~3.7 GB for 10 M edges on 8 GB RAM) limits practical scale.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Trivial detection:* PR‑AUC > 0.90 early in calibration indicates patterns too distinct from background (reduce temporal variance, increase background overlap).  
- *Undetectable patterns:* PR‑AUC ≈ random baseline (equal to fraud ratio) suggests patterns too subtle or camouflaged (increase signal strength, adjust layering).  
- *Memory explosion:* Peak memory grows super‑linearly → likely inefficient clustering or full‑graph operations; profile Clusterer and Aggregator.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Run a minimal generation: Use default config with 1,000 individuals, 1 fraud pattern, 1‑month timeline. Verify output graph size and basic statistics.  
2. Validate a single pattern: Inject only “Overseas Transfers” with burst timing. Use structural validation (Section 5.1) to programmatically check entity selection and transaction timing constraints.  
3. Check counter‑leakage: Generate dataset with and without counter‑leakage patterns. Train a simple decision tree; if accuracy jumps from ~50% to >95% without counter‑leakage, leakage artifact is present.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Pattern‑type impact on model architectures:** How do specific laundering pattern types (e.g., U‑Turn vs. Rapid Fund Movement) differentially impact the performance of various model architectures (GNNs vs. GBTs)? The paper did not disaggregate performance by pattern type.  
- **Statistical resemblance to real laundering:** To what extent do Tide’s synthetic patterns reflect the structural‑temporal signatures of actual money laundering schemes? The absence of ground‑truth validation against real financial data prevents direct verification.  
- **Extending pattern library:** Can adding more complex/diverse laundering typologies (e.g., trade‑based laundering, cryptocurrency mixing) maintain scalability and discriminative power? Current library covers only five typologies.  
- **Generalisation to non‑U.S. systems:** How well do the calibrated background activity distributions (amounts, frequencies) generalise to non‑U.S. financial systems with different transaction profiles? Calibrations are derived primarily from U.S. sources.  
- **Temporal Graph Networks (TGNs):** Do TGNs significantly outperform static GNNs and GBTs on Tide datasets, thereby validating the generator’s emphasis on temporal dynamics? Current evaluation uses static GNNs and tabular GBTs with limited temporal features.

## Limitations
- Exact YAML configurations for the released Low Illicit (0.10%) and High Illicit (0.19%) datasets are not specified in the paper; reproduction depends on unreferenced repository files.  
- The Graph Feature Preprocessor (+GFP models) is described only at a high level; its exact implementation and integration rely on external pipeline code not detailed in the paper.  
- Community validation appears limited: neighboring papers show low average citations (0.0) and weak alignment with Tide’s specific S/T injection mechanism, suggesting minimal external scrutiny.
