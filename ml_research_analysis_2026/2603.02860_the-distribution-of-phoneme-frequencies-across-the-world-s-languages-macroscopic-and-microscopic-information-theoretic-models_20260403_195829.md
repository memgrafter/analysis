# The Distribution of Phoneme Frequencies across the World's Languages: Macroscopic and Microscopic Information-Theoretic Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02860
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02860
- **Reference count:** 19
- **Primary result:** Phoneme frequency distributions are captured by a two-level model: a macroscopic Dirichlet scaling law with inventory size and a microscopic Maximum Entropy model using three constraints, revealing a systematic compensation effect where larger inventories have more even distributions.

## Executive Summary
This study addresses the cross-linguistic distribution of phoneme frequencies. At the **macroscopic level**, it finds that rank-frequency patterns across languages follow the order statistics of a symmetric Dirichlet distribution. The Dirichlet concentration parameter scales predictably and negatively with phonemic inventory size (β ≈ –0.95 on log–log). This allows reconstruction of rank-frequencies from inventory size alone. At the **microscopic level**, a Maximum Entropy model incorporating physical cost (cross-linguistic incidence), phonotactic surprisal, and lexical information gain accurately predicts language-specific phoneme probabilities. The two levels are linked: the compensation effect—where larger inventories exhibit lower relative entropy (down to ~71% vs ~91% for smallest)—emerges from systematic relations between inventory size and the average values of the three constraints.

## Method Summary
The analysis uses three datasets: curated inventories (Sigurd 1968; Macklin-Cordes & Round 2020) and UDHR transcriptions via XPF (53 languages). **Macroscopic modeling**: For each language, compute inventory size *n* and smoothed entropy *Ĥ*. Solve *Ĥ* = ψ(*nα*+1) – ψ(*α*+1) for concentration *α*, then regress log *α* on log *n* to obtain a scaling law. Expected rank-frequencies are derived from order statistics of Dirichlet(*α*). **Microscopic modeling**: For each phoneme *p*, compute three features: (1) physical cost = –log(incidence) from PHOIBLE, (2) phonotactic surprisal *I<sub>s</sub>(p)* from word-form contexts, (3) lexical information gain *H(W|p)* from words containing *p*. For each language, compute empirical expectations *c<sub>i</sub>* = Σ *p<sub>obs</sub>(p) f<sub>i</sub>(p)*. Solve a constrained optimization to maximize entropy subject to Σ *p* = 1 and Σ *p f<sub>i</sub>(p)* = *c<sub>i</sub>*, yielding predicted probabilities *p<sub>pred</sub>(p)* ∝ exp(λ<sub>i</sub> f<sub>i</sub>(p)).

## Key Results
- Phoneme rank-frequency distributions are accurately modeled by the order statistics of a symmetric Dirichlet distribution, with its concentration parameter *α* scaling as *α* ≈ 19.47 × *n*<sup>–0.95</sup>.
- The Maximum Entropy model with physical cost, phonotactic surprisal, and lexical information gain predicts observed phoneme probabilities with high correlation; predicted and observed relative entropies closely match.
- Larger phonemic inventories exhibit lower relative entropy in their phoneme frequency distributions—a "compensation effect" where increased contrastive complexity is offset by greater distributional evenness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Cross-linguistic phoneme rank-frequency distributions follow the order statistics of a symmetric Dirichlet distribution, with its concentration parameter *α* scaling systematically and negatively with inventory size *n*.
- **Mechanism**: For a language with *n* phonemes, *α* is estimated from the smoothed entropy *Ĥ* via *Ĥ* = ψ(*nα*+1) – ψ(*α*+1). The negative power-law relationship (*α* ∝ *n*<sup>–0.95</sup>) allows *α* to be predicted from *n* alone. Expected rank-frequencies are then the order statistics of Dirichlet(*α*).
- **Core assumption**: At the macroscopic level, phoneme identities are abstracted away; no phoneme is a priori more probable, justifying a symmetric Dirichlet prior.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: rank–frequency patterns match order statistics; Section 4.1 and Figure 3b show β ≈ –0.95 scaling; high reconstruction accuracy across datasets.
- **Break condition**: Systematic non-monotonic deviations from order statistics in rank-frequency plots, or failure of the *α*–*n* scaling in inventories that are not closed sets.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Language-specific phoneme probabilities are accurately predicted by a Maximum Entropy model incorporating three constraints: physical cost (cross-linguistic incidence), phonotactic surprisal (segmental information), and lexical information gain.
- **Mechanism**: For each language, the empirical expectations *c<sub>i</sub>* of the three feature functions are computed. The MaxEnt distribution is found by solving max –Σ *p* log *p* subject to Σ *p* = 1 and Σ *p f<sub>i</sub>(p)* = *c<sub>i</sub>*. The solution is *p(p)* ∝ exp(Σ λ<sub>i</sub> f<sub>i</sub>(p)*), with λ<sub>i</sub> set to satisfy constraints.
- **Core assumption**: The three constraints capture the major factors causing deviation from uniformity; per Jaynes' Entropy Concentration Theorem, the observed entropy should be near the maximum possible under these constraints.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: model accurately predicts probabilities; Section 5.3.2: predicted vs. observed log probabilities show near-identity regression; relative entropies closely match (Figure 4d).
- **Break condition**: Consistent underprediction of entropy across languages, indicating persistent unmodelled constraints; high residuals for specific phonemes suggesting feature miscalibration.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Larger phonemic inventories exhibit lower relative entropy—a "compensation effect" where increased contrastive complexity is offset by greater distributional evenness.
- **Mechanism**: The macroscopic scaling (*α* ∝ *n*<sup>–0.95</sup>) implies expected relative entropy *E[H]/log(n)* decreases with *n*. Microscopically, inventory size correlates with the average values of the three constraints, embedding the compensation into the MaxEnt expectations.
- **Core assumption**: The Compensation Hypothesis: system-wide trade-offs balance complexity across linguistic domains.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract: relative entropy ranges from 71% (large inventories) to 91% (small); Section 4.2 and 5.3.3 show correlations between *n* and constraint averages.
- **Break condition**: Failure to replicate the negative correlation between inventory size and relative entropy in new, typologically diverse samples, especially from underdocumented families.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Maximum Entropy Principle**
  - *Why needed*: The microscopic model relies on Jaynes' principle to generate the least-structured distribution consistent with observed constraints, ensuring predictions are data-driven and not overfitted.
  - *Quick check*: According to Jaynes' Entropy Concentration Theorem, what does it imply when an observed distribution's entropy is close to the maximum possible under known constraints?
- **Concept: Dirichlet Distribution and Order Statistics**
  - *Why needed*: The macroscopic model uses the symmetric Dirichlet as a prior over phoneme distributions; its order statistics yield expected rank-frequencies.
  - *Quick check*: How does the concentration parameter *α* in a symmetric Dirichlet distribution affect the expected skewness of sampled multinomials?
- **Concept: Cross-linguistic Feature Engineering**
  - *Why needed*: The microscopic constraints (cost, surprisal, lexical gain) must be operationalized from cross-linguistic data (PHOIBLE, UDHR corpora), requiring understanding of phonological typology and corpus extraction.
  - *Quick check*: How is "phonotactic surprisal" (segment information) computed from word-form corpora, and why might it be inversely related to phoneme frequency?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  Data ingestion -> Macroscopic module: estimates α from n and entropy -> rank-frequencies
  Data ingestion -> Microscopic module: computes constraints c_i -> solves MaxEnt -> phoneme probabilities
  Both modules -> Validation: compares predictions to observed ranks/probabilities and entropies
- **Critical path**:
  - Macroscopic: *n* → predicted *α* → Dirichlet order statistics → reconstructed rank-frequencies.
  - Microscopic: Raw corpus → constraint features (*f<sub>i</sub>*) → empirical expectations *c<sub>i</sub>* → MaxEnt λ<sub>i</sub> → predicted *p(p)*.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Accuracy vs. data availability*: Curated inventories yield better fits but limited diversity; automatic UDHR transcriptions increase diversity but introduce noise.
  - *Simplicity vs. fit*: Symmetric Dirichlet (1 parameter) vs. MaxEnt (3 constraints). Adding constraints may improve fit but risks overfitting on small inventories.
  - *Generality vs. specificity*: Macroscopic model requires only *n*, but microscopic needs language-specific feature computation (e.g., lexical diversity estimation).
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Macroscopic: Poor reconstruction (high residuals in rank-frequency fits) for languages with extreme skew (e.g., one phoneme >80% frequency); unstable *α* estimates for very small/large *n*.
  - Microscopic: Systematic underprediction of entropy (points below identity in entropy comparison); optimization failures or invalid probabilities (negative or sum ≠1); NaNs in *I<sub>s</sub>(p)* due to unsmoothed zero counts.
  - Data: Low phoneme coverage after PHOIBLE/XPF mapping; inconsistent transcription schemes causing feature computation failures.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Replication**: For a new language (e.g., from PHOIBLE), predict *α* from *n* via the scaling law, compute order statistics, and compare to observed rank-frequencies. Validate macroscopic reconstruction outside the paper's datasets.
  2. **Constraint ablation**: Remove each of the three constraints from the MaxEnt model in turn; re-run on UDHR languages; measure change in correlation (predicted vs. observed log probs) and entropy match. Quantify each constraint's contribution.
  3. **Model comparison**: Fit alternative power-law models (Yule-Simon, Zipf-Mandelbrot) to the same phoneme distributions; compare goodness-of-fit (e.g., Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance) against the Dirichlet-based model. Test the claim that power-law models are unsuitable for closed inventories.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: What additional linguistic or cognitive constraints beyond physical cost, phonotactic surprisal, and lexical information gain are necessary to fully account for the observed phoneme frequency distributions, as indicated by the slight entropy deficit in the Maximum Entropy model's predictions?
- **Open Question 2**: What is the causal mechanism linking phonemic inventory size to the average values of the constraints (physical cost, phonotactic surprisal, lexical information gain), thereby producing the compensation effect where larger inventories exhibit lower relative entropy?
- **Open Question 3**: How reliably do the macroscopic Dirichlet-based findings (scaling of concentration parameter with inventory size) generalize to spoken-language data, given that the primary cross-linguistic dataset (UDHR) derives from written texts potentially biased by genre and formality?

## Limitations
- Smoothing methods for phonotactic surprisal *I<sub>s</sub>(p)* and the precise entropy estimator for *H(W|p)* are unspecified, potentially affecting MaxEnt predictions.
- Mapping between XPF and PHOIBLE transcriptions is heuristic; mismatches could bias constraint values, especially for rare phonemes.
- Macroscopic scaling may be unstable for very small or very large inventories; the symmetric Dirichlet assumption might break for highly skewed distributions.
- Analysis relies on specific corpora (UDHR) and curated inventories; typological coverage remains limited relative to global diversity.

## Confidence
- **Macroscopic Dirichlet scaling**: High – strong empirical fit across datasets, consistent β ≈ –0.95, high reconstruction accuracy.
- **Microscopic MaxEnt with three constraints**: Medium – good predictive performance, but implementation ambiguities (smoothing, entropy estimation) and slight entropy underprediction leave room for variation.
- **Compensation effect**: Medium – emergent from both models and observed entropy trends, but dependent on validity of microscopic constraints and macroscopic *α*-*n* relationship; needs cross-validation on independent samples.
