# A Multi-Dimensional Quality Scoring Framework for Decentralized LLM Inference with Proof of Quality

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04028
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04028
- **Reference count:** 37
- **Primary result:** Systematic reliability auditing and calibration of a multi-dimensional scoring framework (removing misaligned dimensions and re-normalizing weights) yields a composite score that matches or exceeds the best single evaluator and consensus baselines (calibrated Pearson 0.760 vs. best single 0.754). When integrated into Proof of Quality, this calibrated composite improves reward ranking alignment and, combined with robust aggregation and adaptive trust weighting, enhances resilience against adversarial evaluators.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the reliability problem in decentralized LLM inference networks, where single-evaluator quality signals can be gamed or misaligned. It proposes a modular multi-dimensional scoring framework that breaks quality into interpretable components: priors, structure, semantic quality, query-output alignment, and agreement/uncertainty. The default composite underperforms the best single semantic evaluator due to task-dependent negative correlations from alignment and agreement dimensions. However, systematic per-task reliability auditing—removing misaligned dimensions and re-normalizing weights—yields a calibrated composite that matches or exceeds single-evaluator and consensus baselines. Integrated into the existing Proof of Quality (PoQ) incentive protocol, this calibrated signal improves reward ranking alignment to reference quality and, when combined with robust aggregation and adaptive trust weighting, increases resilience against adversarial evaluators.

## Method Summary
The framework defines five normalized dimension scorers (priors, structure, semantic, alignment, agreement) whose outputs are combined via a weighted sum into a composite quality score. For a given task family (e.g., QA, summarization), the reliability auditor computes each dimension’s correlation with a reference signal (human or strong judge). Dimensions with negative or low correlation are removed, and remaining weights are re-normalized. The calibrated composite then replaces a single evaluator within the PoQ protocol: a cost-aware sampler selects evaluator nodes, their composite scores are aggregated robustly (e.g., median, trimmed mean, or adaptive trust-weighted mean), and rewards are allocated based on the consensus score. This two-layer approach separates signal validity (via calibrated composite) from source reliability (via robust aggregation/trust).

## Key Results
- The default composite underperforms the best single semantic evaluator (Pearson 0.513 vs. 0.754) because alignment and agreement dimensions exhibit negative correlations with ground truth on QA tasks.
- After removing alignment and agreement and re-normalizing weights, the calibrated composite achieves Pearson 0.760, matching or exceeding the best single evaluator and consensus baselines.
- In PoQ simulations, the calibrated composite improves reward ranking alignment with reference quality and, when combined with robust aggregation and adaptive trust weighting, shows greater resilience against adversarial evaluators compared to the default composite.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Multi-dimensional scoring can degrade quality signal alignment unless dimensions are audited for task-specific reliability.
- **Mechanism:** The framework decomposes quality into interpretable modules (priors, structure, semantics, alignment, agreement). However, dimensions like alignment and agreement exhibit task-dependent directionality—they can correlate negatively with ground-truth quality on some tasks (e.g., QA) while being weakly positive on others (e.g., summarization). Naïve inclusion of such dimensions drags down the composite score's correlation with reference signals.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "dimensions like alignment and agreement can be task-dependent or negatively correlated with ground truth."
  - [section 5.2] Table 7 shows alignment and agreement have strongly negative Pearson correlations on QA (-0.571, -0.553) but weakly positive on summarization (0.102, 0.188).
  - [corpus] Corpus neighbors discuss related PoQ and evaluation frameworks but do not provide direct evidence for this mechanism; evidence is contained within the paper's own empirical analysis.
- **Break condition:** If all dimensions consistently show positive correlation across all target tasks and distributions, the calibration step of removing dimensions may be unnecessary or harmful.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Systematic reliability auditing and weight re-normalization (calibration) yields a composite score that matches or exceeds the best single evaluator and consensus baselines.
- **Mechanism:** By computing per-dimension correlation with reference signals on task-specific subsets, unreliable dimensions (those with negative or low correlation) are identified and removed from the composite. The remaining weights are re-normalized to sum to 1. This prevents negative contributors from degrading the composite, allowing the remaining reliable signals (e.g., semantic quality) to dominate.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "systematic reliability auditing and calibration—removing misaligned dimensions (alignment and agreement) and re-normalizing weights—yields a calibrated composite that matches or exceeds single-evaluator and consensus baselines (calibrated Pearson 0.760)."
  - [section 5.4] Table 8 shows the "Calibrated (remove alignment+agreement)" variant achieves Pearson 0.760, surpassing the default composite (0.513) and matching the best single evaluator (0.754).
  - [section 5.3] Figure 8 contrasts the default composite underperforming the best single evaluator versus the calibrated composite matching it.
- **Break condition:** If the removed dimensions contain unique, irreplaceable signals not captured by the remaining dimensions (e.g., a critical aspect of quality only measured by alignment), calibration could reduce alignment on tasks where that dimension was reliable.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The calibrated composite quality signal integrates effectively with Proof of Quality (PoQ) incentive mechanisms, and its benefits are complementary to robust aggregation and adaptive trust weighting.
- **Mechanism:** The composite score acts as a drop-in replacement for a single evaluator score within the existing PoQ protocol (cost-aware sampling, consensus aggregation, reward allocation). Because the composite is pre-calibrated for alignment, the reward ranking it produces better reflects true quality. Robust aggregation (e.g., median, trimmed mean) and adaptive trust weighting then operate on these improved signals, further mitigating adversarial evaluators. The two layers address orthogonal failure modes: calibration fixes signal validity; robust aggregation/trust fixes source reliability.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Integrated into Proof of Quality, this calibrated composite improves reward ranking alignment and, combined with robust aggregation and adaptive trust weighting, enhances resilience against adversarial evaluators."
  - [section 6] "Composite scoring with adaptive trust... multi-dimensional scoring and adaptive trust weighting address different failure modes: the composite focuses on measurement design... while trust weighting focuses on source reliability."
  - [section 6.3] Figure 14 shows that using the calibrated composite under robust defenses leads to better reward consistency under attack compared to the default composite.
- **Break condition:** If the calibration process itself introduces a systematic bias or if the remaining dimensions are all highly correlated with a spurious feature that adversaries can easily manipulate, the composite signal could become more vulnerable to certain attacks despite robustness defenses.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Byzantine-Robust Aggregation (e.g., median, trimmed mean, adaptive trust weighting)
**Why needed here:** PoQ aggregates scores from potentially adversarial or noisy evaluator nodes. Robust aggregation principles from distributed systems and machine learning (cited in [2, 4, 24, 7]) are essential to prevent a minority of malicious evaluators from distorting the consensus quality signal and reward allocations.
**Quick check question:** Why might the mean be a poor choice for aggregating evaluator scores in a decentralized setting with potential adversaries, and what is a simple robust alternative?

### Concept: Task-Dependent Evaluation Metric Validity
**Why needed here:** The paper's core finding is that automatic evaluation dimensions are not universally reliable; their correlation with ground-truth quality can invert across tasks (e.g., QA vs. summarization). Understanding that metric performance is distribution- and task-dependent is crucial to avoid incentive misalignment.
**Quick check question:** What does it mean for an evaluation dimension to have "negative correlation" with ground truth quality, and why is this particularly problematic for incentive mechanisms?

### Concept: Modular System Design for Auditability
**Why needed here:** The scoring framework's modularity (separate dimension scorers) enables isolated auditing (computing correlation per dimension) and surgical calibration (removing/weighting). This is a design principle that makes the system interpretable and maintainable in a dynamic decentralized environment.
**Quick check question:** How does decomposing a quality score into independent, normalized modules facilitate reliability analysis compared to using a single monolithic black-box evaluator?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Dimension Scorer Modules (Priors, Structure, Semantic, Alignment, Agreement) -> Composite Scorer (with calibrated weights) -> PoQ Integration Layer (Cost-Aware Sampler, Robust Aggregator, Reward Allocator). Reliability Auditor (offline) computes per-dimension correlations and outputs task-specific weight sets.

### Critical path
Raw outputs + queries -> [Dimension Scorers] -> Normalized dimension scores -> [Composite Scorer (with calibrated weights)] -> Composite score per evaluator -> [PoQ Robust Aggregator (median/trimmed mean/trust-weighted mean)] -> Consensus score -> Reward allocation.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Interpretability vs. Automation:** Modular dimensions provide clear failure modes (e.g., "structure score failed") but require manual weight calibration. A single neural evaluator might be more accurate but is a black box and harder to audit.
- **Generality vs. Performance:** Using a single weight set for all tasks is simple but risks misalignment on tasks where some dimensions are unreliable. Task-specific calibration improves alignment but requires maintaining multiple weight sets and performing per-task auditing.
- **Evaluation Cost vs. Signal Quality:** Semantic and alignment dimensions are more expensive. The framework allows cheap priors/structure to filter obvious failures, but the calibration process itself requires reference data for each task, which may be costly to obtain.

### Failure signatures
- **Negative Composite Correlation:** Overall Pearson correlation with reference is significantly lower than the best single semantic dimension (as in default composite 0.513 vs. 0.754).
- **Task-Inverted Dimensions:** Specific dimensions (alignment, agreement) show strongly negative correlation on one task (QA) and positive on another (summarization).
- **Reward Misalignment:** PoQ reward rankings based on the composite score diverge from rankings based on a trusted reference signal, especially after adversarial attacks.
- **Weight Sensitivity:** Large swings in composite correlation when a single dimension weight is perturbed, indicating over-reliance on an unreliable signal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can dimension selection and weighting be automatically adapted to specific tasks or prompt distributions in a decentralized inference network?
2. How can the composite quality score be calibrated without relying on expensive or imperfect reference signals (e.g., human judgments or strong LLM-as-a-judge)?
3. Does moving beyond a simple weighted sum to a learned, non-linear composition of dimension scores improve robustness and alignment across diverse tasks?
4. How can the multi-dimensional scoring framework continuously adapt to changes in the evaluator pool, model outputs, and task distributions over time in a live system?

## Limitations
- Exact implementations and normalization of dimension scorers (e.g., specific NLI model, semantic metric) are not detailed.
- Default and calibrated weight values are only partially reported; calibration relies on task-specific reference data, limiting portability.
- Framework assumes reference signals are perfect ground truth; any bias will mislead calibration.
- Evaluation restricted to QA and summarization; performance on other tasks is unknown.
- No protection against poisoning of individual dimension modules.

## Confidence
- Claim 1: Multi-dimensional scoring can degrade quality signal alignment unless dimensions are audited for task-specific reliability. **Medium** (evidence from two tasks only).
- Claim 2: Systematic reliability auditing and weight re-normalization yields a composite score that matches or exceeds the best single evaluator and consensus baselines. **High** (consistent empirical results).
- Claim 3: The calibrated composite quality signal integrates effectively with Proof of Quality and its benefits are complementary to robust aggregation and adaptive trust weighting. **Medium** (simulation only, no live deployment).
