# Quantitative Rule-Based Strategy modeling in Classic Indian Rummy: A Metric Optimization Approach
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.00024
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00024
- Reference count: 8
- Primary result: MinDist-based agents, especially with opponent modeling, achieve statistically significant win-rate improvements over MinScore and random strategies (p<0.05).

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles strategic decision-making in Classic Indian Rummy by proposing a rule-based framework centered on a novel hand-evaluation metric called MinDist. MinDist measures the minimal number of card replacements needed to transform a hand into a valid declaration, offering finer discrimination than the traditional MinScore metric. The authors develop an efficient dynamic programming algorithm with bit-masking and a "super joker" trick to compute MinDist exactly during gameplay. They also incorporate a simple opponent modeling heuristic that uses observed discards to avoid giving cards beneficial to the opponent. Empirical evaluations via 1,000-game simulations against various baselines (random, MinScore, etc.) show that MinDist-based agents, particularly with opponent modeling (MinDistOpp), achieve statistically significant improvements in win rates after adjusting for first-mover advantage. The ranking of strategies by skill is MinScore < MinDist < MinDistScore < MinDistOpp (all p < 0.05). Additionally, skilled strategies yield shorter game durations. The work establishes a formal, interpretable baseline for algorithmic Rummy strategy.

## Method Summary
The authors implement rule-based agents that select moves by evaluating the current hand using either MinScore or MinDist. MinDist computation involves representing the 13-card hand with bit masks and solving a dynamic program that searches for the minimum number of "super jokers" (wildcard replacements) required to partition the hand into a pure sequence plus additional sequences/sets. The algorithm is optimized to run in under 100ms. During play, the agent considers drawing from the pile or deck, then discards the card that most reduces MinDist (or MinScore). The MinDistOpp variant adds an opponent model that, when no discard improves MinDist, chooses discards that are unlikely to combine with the opponent's recent discards to form melds. Simulations are conducted in a two-player zero-sum environment with a single deck and two printed jokers, over 1,000 games per matchup. Performance is measured by win rate, median gain, and game duration, with statistical significance assessed via adjusted win probabilities accounting for first-mover advantage.

## Key Results
- MinDist-based agents outperform MinScore and random baselines with statistical significance (p<0.05) after adjusting for first-mover advantage.
- The skill gradient is clear: MinScore < MinDist < MinDistScore < MinDistOpp.
- Skilled strategies also produce shorter game durations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: MinDist provides a more discriminative hand evaluation than MinScore by measuring structural proximity (edit distance) to a valid declaration.
- **Mechanism**: MinDist calculates the minimum number of card replacements (modeled as "super jokers") needed to transform the current hand into any valid configuration. This directly quantifies how many "turns" the hand is from winning, whereas MinScore aggregates deadwood points and saturates at 80 for many unplayable hands.
- **Core assumption**: A hand requiring fewer card replacements (lower MinDist) is closer to a winning state and can be improved more predictably through draws.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "MinDist measures the minimal number of card replacements needed to transform a hand into a valid configuration, capturing structural proximity better than the existing MinScore metric."
  - [section IV.B] "MinDist... allows discrimination between near-valid hands... The key motivation behind MinDist as a metric is that MinScore... does not have much discriminative power between hands."
  - [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; this is a novel metric proposed in the paper.
- **Break condition**: The metric becomes misleading if the required replacement cards are already visible in the discard pile (unavailable to be drawn) or held by a skilled opponent—MinDist treats all replacement cards as equally obtainable "super jokers."

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Simple opponent modeling, based on observed discards, improves decision-making by reducing the chance of discarding cards that benefit the opponent.
- **Mechanism**: The agent tracks opponent discards and, when a non-improving move is necessary, preferentially discards cards that can combine with the opponent's recent discards to form a meld (e.g., discarding a 5♣ after opponent discards 4♣ or 6♣). This heuristic aims to "void" potential meld combinations for the opponent.
- **Core assumption**: The opponent is playing a strategy where they will pick up useful cards from the discard pile; avoiding giving them such cards slows their progress.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section VI] "we would wish not to drop a card for the opponent to take, which can create a meld with the picked up cards by the opponent; and also we would wish to drop a card which creates a meld with the dropped cards by the opponent."
  - [section VIII] "Min-dist based improved by Opponent play... consistently achieves the highest win rates."
  - [corpus] Weak; corpus neighbors discuss game design/ML but not this specific heuristic.
- **Break condition**: The heuristic fails if the opponent employs a deceptive strategy (e.g., purposeful "misdiscards"), uses a different evaluation metric (like pure MinScore), or is a random player where discard-reading provides no predictive signal.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The dynamic programming algorithm with bit-masking and the "super joker" trick makes exact MinDist computation tractable in real-time gameplay.
- **Mechanism**: Hand subsets are represented as 13-bit masks, enabling efficient subset operations (AND/XOR) to enumerate and evaluate valid melds. The "super joker" trick converts the problem of finding minimal replacements into a series of MinScore-like decisions by adding dummy "universal" jokers to the hand until a valid partition exists.
- **Core assumption**: The number of possible valid meld subsets is manageable with aggressive pruning and caching, and the super joker abstraction correctly models the edit-distance problem.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section V] "bit-masking... representing any subset of the hand with just 13 bits – a massive improvement... This drastically improves the computation time... from being intractable to under 100 ms."
  - [section V] "The unobserved cards can be viewed as a separate card that can replace itself with some remaining card... hence considering itself as a joker is a natural idea... This special type cards are called as Super Joker cards."
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; this is a specific algorithmic contribution of the paper.
- **Break condition**: The algorithm's complexity grows exponentially with hand size; it is explicitly designed for 13-card Rummy and may not scale to variants with larger hands or different meld requirements without significant modification.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Bit-masking for subset enumeration**  
  - *Why needed here*: The core DP algorithm must efficiently test and combine subsets of 13 cards to form valid melds. Bit-masks provide a compact, constant-time representation for subset operations.
  - *Quick check question*: How does a bitwise AND operation between a meld mask and a hand mask determine if the meld is still available in the current hand state?

- **Concept: Dynamic Programming on set partitions**  
  - *Why needed here*: Both MinScore and MinDist are optimal partition problems: dividing the hand into disjoint valid melds while minimizing a cost (deadwood points or replacements). DP avoids recomputation by storing subproblem solutions.
  - *Quick check question*: What does the `dp[mask][state]` table entry represent in the algorithm, and how does the state variable enforce the sequence requirement (pure sequence first)?

- **Concept: Heuristic opponent modeling in imperfect-information games**  
  - *Why needed here*: Rummy has hidden information. Without a full Bayesian model, the paper uses a lightweight, rule-based model to exploit publicly observable opponent actions (discards).
  - *Quick check question*: Why is the chosen heuristic ("discard cards that could combine with opponent's discards") a reasonable proxy for reducing opponent hand strength, and what is its key limitation?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  1.  **Hand Evaluator**: Computes MinDist (and optionally MinScore) for a given 13-card hand and joker. Uses the DP+bit-mask+super-joker algorithm.
  2.  **Decision Engine**: For the current hand and pile card, evaluates the outcome of picking the pile card vs. drawing a deck card. For each option, it computes the resulting hand's MinDist (and possibly MinScore) to decide the action. If discarding, it selects the card whose removal most improves the metric (or follows the opponent heuristic).
  3.  **Opponent Modeler (optional)**: A module that maintains a record of opponent discards. When the Decision Engine must discard without improving MinDist, this module suggests discarding cards that are "close" to opponent's recent discards (sequential or same-rank).
  4.  **Drop Policy (optional)**: A simple threshold check (e.g., MinDist > 4 in first round) that triggers a fold.

- **Critical path**: `Draw Card (Pile/Deck)` → `Update Hand` → `Hand Evaluator (compute MinDist)` → `Decision Engine (if Pile improves MinDist? else evaluate discard options)` → `[Opponent Modeler check if applicable]` → `Select Discard`.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Accuracy vs. Speed**: The exact MinDist DP is ~100ms per evaluation. Using a smaller hand subset (e.g., best 11/12 cards) or caching frequent hand patterns could speed up play at some cost to optimality.
  - **Static vs. Dynamic Metric**: MinDist is a static snapshot of the hand. It does not incorporate card removal (discards seen) or opponent card density beliefs, which a more advanced Bayesian model would. The tradeoff is computational simplicity vs. situational awareness.
  - **Generalization**: The algorithm is hard-coded for 13-card Indian Rummy rules (pure seq first, then seq/set). Adapting to other Rummy variants requires changing the `state` machine in the DP and meld validation logic.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - **High MinDist, long game duration**: The agent is stuck in a loop of marginal improvements without a clear path to declaration, suggesting the metric may be insufficiently directional or the agent is unlucky with draws.
  - **Opponent modeling backfires**: Win rate drops when the opponent is random or uses a strategy that doesn't value common meld cards (e.g., focuses on high deadwood). This indicates the heuristic is opponent-dependent.
  - **Consistent loss in late-game**: The agent fails to recognize when it should abandon the current MinDist-optimal path for a riskier, higher-variance declaration line, as the metric is myopic to game stage.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1.  **Metric Sanity Check**: Generate 10,000 random 13-card hands. Compute and plot the distribution of MinDist (should peak at 2-4, max ~6) and compare against the paper's Figure 3. Validate the algorithm's correctness by checking known hands (e.g., a hand with one pure sequence and the rest in sets should have MinDist=0).
  2.  **Super Joker Validation**: For a hand with known MinDist `k` (e.g., by manual construction), run the algorithm with `max_dist` limits. Confirm it returns `k` and that for any `j < k`, no valid declaration is found even with `j` super jokers.
  3.  **Opponent Heuristic A/B Test**: Run 1,000-game simulations: MinDist agent vs. MinDist+OpponentModel agent (both as first player against a fixed MinDist baseline). Confirm the win rate improvement (~5-10% as per Table I) is replicated. Then test the opponent model against a `DefeatSeeking` agent; the heuristic should provide no benefit (or slight harm) if the opponent discards randomly.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How can MinDist be extended to incorporate the unavailability of cards seen in the discard pile?
  - *Basis in paper*: The paper states that MinDist "does not incorporate the fact that cards on the visible pile, are not available," calling it a serious limitation.
  - *Why unresolved*: The current "super joker" approach assumes any replacement card can be any missing card, ignoring that some cards are already known to be out of play, which may overestimate hand quality.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: A revised algorithm that tracks discarded cards and restricts the replacement set to unseen cards; evaluate its impact on win rates in simulation against the original MinDist.

- **Open Question 2**: Does a dynamic version of MinDist that incorporates game history (discards, opponent draws) improve performance?
  - *Basis in paper*: The authors explicitly say that the metrics are static and "can be enhanced to make them dynamic (or evolving) with respect to a game (i.e., incorporating discards and/or incorporate accumulative belief regarding opponent hand)."
  - *Why unresolved*: The paper's strategies rely solely on static snapshots; no empirical comparison with a dynamic variant is provided.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Implement a dynamic metric (e.g., using discard counts to compute probabilities for missing cards) and measure its win rate against static MinDist in head-to-head simulations.

- **Open Question 3**: What is the optimal drop strategy (threshold and timing) for maximizing expected gain in Rummy?
  - *Basis in paper*: The paper's drop adherence experiment showed no significant improvement with a simple rule, and the authors speculate that "dropping more frequently might increase the skill gap, but it then hampers the performance against other strategies."
  - *Why unresolved*: The drop policy was not systematically tuned; the trade-off between risk reduction and strategic disadvantage remains unclear.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Perform a grid search over drop thresholds (based on MinDist or MinScore) and permissible rounds, then test the best policies against a variety of opponent skill levels to identify conditions where dropping yields net benefit.

- **Open Question 4**: Can Bayesian opponent modeling, instead of the simple heuristic, further improve MinDist-based agents?
  - *Basis in paper*: The authors note that "while a sophisticated Bayesian hand modeling could have delivered much more promising results," they chose simple rules to avoid hyperparameter tuning.
  - *Why unresolved*: The potential benefit of probabilistic opponent modeling is unexplored; the current heuristic only avoids discarding cards that might help the opponent.
  - *What evidence would resolve it*: Build a Bayesian model that estimates the opponent's hand distribution from observed actions, integrate it with MinDist decision-making, and compare its win rate against the heuristic model in simulation.

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details for key parameters (drop threshold, opponent heuristic specifics) hinder exact replication and may lead to divergent agent behaviors.
- The super-joker abstraction assumes all replacement cards are equally obtainable, ignoring discards and opponent holdings, which can overestimate hand quality and misguide decisions.
- The opponent modeling heuristic is narrow and context-dependent; its effectiveness against adaptive or deceptive opponents is uncertain.
- Simulations are restricted to 2-player single-deck Indian Rummy; findings may not generalize to other Rummy variants or player counts.
