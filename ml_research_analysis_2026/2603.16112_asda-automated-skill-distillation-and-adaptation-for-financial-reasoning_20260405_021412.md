# ASDA: Automated Skill Distillation and Adaptation for Financial Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16112
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16112
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** Achieves up to +17.33% arithmetic and +5.95% non-arithmetic improvement on the FAMMA benchmark without model weight updates, far exceeding training-free baselines.

## Executive Summary
ASDA tackles the high cost and model-locking of financial LLM fine‑tuning by automatically generating structured, executable skill artifacts from error analysis, all without updating model weights. A teacher model clusters a student’s failures and synthesizes modular skill files for dynamic prompt injection. On the FAMMA benchmark, ASDA yields absolute accuracy gains of up to 17.33% on arithmetic and 5.95% on non‑arithmetic questions. Ablation studies indicate that 73% of the improvement stems from the distillation process itself, not from superior supervision. Skills are model‑specific but inexpensive to regenerate (~$13, 6 hours), offering a practical training‑free adaptation pathway for financial reasoning.

## Method Summary
ASDA operates in two phases: warm‑up and iterative refinement. During warm‑up, a teacher model analyzes the student’s failures on the training set, clusters them by a predefined 10‑category error taxonomy, and synthesizes initial skill files (each containing a description, “when to use” conditions, step‑by‑step procedures, and code templates). A top‑level `SKILL.md` acts as a navigation router. In iterative refinement, the current skill library is applied to all training questions to partition outcomes into correctly answered (`Q+`), regressed (`Q‑`), and still failing (`Q_gap`). Attribution identifies the single skill file most responsible for each outcome; coverage updates address `Q_gap` while safety repairs fix `Q‑` regressions. Proposed updates are verified against correctness thresholds (`τ_cov`, `τ_safe`) before commitment. At inference, an LLM‑based selector injects relevant skills into the student’s prompt. The entire process avoids weight updates and requires only API calls to teacher and student models.

## Key Results
- **Arithmetic improvement:** +17.33% absolute gain on FAMMA test set.
- **Non‑arithmetic improvement:** +5.95% absolute gain.
- **Distillation dominance:** Self‑teaching (student as teacher) recovers 73% of the full arithmetic gain, confirming that the process itself externalizes latent knowledge.
- **Model‑specificity:** Skills transferred from Haiku 3.5 to Haiku 4.5 cause a net regression of –2.33% overall (–6.21% on open‑ended questions), underscoring the need for per‑model regeneration.
- **Cost efficiency:** Regenerating a full skill library costs approximately $13 and 6 hours of compute.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

1. **Distillation externalizes latent knowledge:** The primary improvement comes from the distillation process, not from a superior teacher. The teacher analyzes failures, clusters them by subfield and error type, and synthesizes modular skill files containing reasoning procedures and code templates. This forces the student to consistently apply domain knowledge it already implicitly possesses but cannot reliably access during single‑pass inference. *Evidence:* Self‑teaching ablation recovers 73% of arithmetic gain (+6.33pp vs. +8.67pp with Sonnet teacher) [Section 5.3]. *Break condition:* If the student lacks the underlying knowledge entirely, distillation would fail to produce meaningful skills.

2. **Skills are model‑specific remedies:** Skill files encode heuristics tailored to a specific model’s error distribution and failure modes. A stronger model has different blind spots; applying another model’s corrective heuristics misaligns with its internal representations, introducing over‑reasoning or irrelevant constraints that degrade performance. *Evidence:* Haiku 4.5 with Haiku 3.5’s skills regresses by –2.33pp overall, driven by –6.21pp on open‑ended questions [Section 5.4]. *Break condition:* If two models share nearly identical failure distributions (e.g., different checkpoints of the same run), cross‑transfer might not regress.

3. **Iterative refinement with verification gates:** Dual‑phase (coverage then safety) cycles systematically reduce residual failures while controlling regressions. After warm‑up, the skill library is iteratively improved: coverage phase adds patterns to solve remaining failures (`Q_gap`); safety phase repairs skills causing regressions (`Q‑`) while preserving correct behavior (`Q+`). Each candidate update is verified against a correctness threshold `τ`. *Evidence:* Arithmetic gains improve from warm‑up (+8.67pp) to epoch 2 (+17.33pp), then regress at epoch 3, demonstrating benefit and limit [Section 5.1]. *Break condition:* If failures do not cluster cleanly by subfield/error type, attribution becomes unreliable and refinement may introduce more regressions than fixes, especially on non‑arithmetic tasks.

## Foundational Learning
- **Failure Pattern Taxonomy:** A predefined set of 10 error types (e.g., “wrong method selection”, “concept confusion”) used to cluster failures. Needed to give the skill library a coherent structure for routing and refinement. *Quick check:* What are the ten error types? How does “wrong method selection” differ from “concept confusion” in terms of root cause?
- **Skill Artifact Structure:** Each skill pattern includes executable components: “when to use” conditions, step‑by‑step procedures, and worked examples/code templates. This makes skills machine‑readable and dynamically injectable. *Quick check:* For the `fixed_income/wrong_method_selection.md` pattern, what are the three key components (besides description) that enable the student model to execute the correct procedure?
- **Attribution Analysis:** Determines which loaded skill file is “most responsible” for a question’s outcome to drive per‑file refinement. Needed to isolate each file’s contribution and enable targeted updates rather than noisy library‑wide changes. *Quick check:* How does the teacher model determine which loaded skill file is most responsible during the attribution step?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Teacher model -> Failure analysis & skill synthesis -> Student model + Skill library -> Selector -> Inference. More concretely: Teacher diagnoses failures and generates skill files; Student executes with dynamically injected skills; Skill library is a two‑level hierarchy (subfield/error_type files) plus `SKILL.md` navigation router; Selector reads the question and `SKILL.md` to choose relevant files.

**Critical path:**
1. Warm‑up: Run student on training set → collect failures → teacher analyzes each failure → cluster by `(subfield, error_type)` → synthesize initial skill files → build `SKILL.md`.
2. Iterative refinement (per epoch): Evaluate all training questions with/without current skill library → partition into `Q+`, `Q‑`, `Q_gap` → attribute each question to a specific skill file → for each file with `Q_gap`, propose coverage update → verify against `τ_cov` → commit if passed → re‑evaluate to update `Q+`/`Q‑` → for each file with regressions (`Q‑`), propose safety repair → verify against `τ_safe` → commit if passed → repeat for `T` epochs.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Skill specificity vs. generality: Narrow patterns reduce regressions but may miss edge cases; broad patterns risk over‑reasoning. Threshold `τ` trades off innovation vs. stability.
- Cost vs. coverage: More refinement epochs improve coverage but increase compute and risk overfitting.
- Selector precision vs. recall: Loading too many skills increases regression risk; too few leaves coverage gaps.

**Failure signatures:**
- Overfitting: Performance declines after too many refinement epochs (e.g., arithmetic regresses at epoch 3).
- Selector misrouting: Irrelevant skills injected → over‑reasoning → correct baseline answers become wrong (`Q‑` cases).
- Skill bloat: Excessive narrowly‑scoped files increase maintenance burden and routing complexity.
- Attribution noise: If multiple skills contribute equally to an outcome, per‑file updates may destabilize other files.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Warm‑up baseline: Run student on training set with no skills to establish failure distribution. Verify that failure clusters correspond to meaningful error types.
2. Cross‑transfer test: Apply skills distilled from Model A to Model B (same family). Expect net regression if skills are model‑specific; confirms need for per‑model distillation.
3. Self‑teaching ablation: Use student as its own teacher for warm‑up. Compare gain to full ASDA with superior teacher. If gain comparable (~73%), distillation process is dominant, not teacher quality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Cross‑domain transfer:** Does ASDA generalize to other knowledge‑intensive domains like legal or tax reasoning? The paper explicitly leaves this as future work; only FAMMA with Claude models was evaluated. Resolving requires applying ASDA to legal/tax benchmarks (e.g., LawBench, TaxBench) and comparing skill library composition and gains.
2. **Self‑teaching limits:** How far can self‑teaching (student as teacher) substitute for a stronger teacher model? The self‑teaching ablation recovered 73% of arithmetic gain but only modest non‑arithmetic improvement, and only on Haiku 3.5. Resolving requires systematic comparisons across a spectrum of student models (weak to strong), multiple domains, and task types.
3. **Cross‑model transfer factors:** What factors determine the failure of cross‑model skill transfer, and can skills be made portable under any conditions? Only one model pair (Haiku 3.5 → 4.5) was tested; underlying causes (capability gap, error distribution mismatch, overfitting) are not isolated. Resolving requires varying student model pairs, analyzing failure pattern overlap, and testing interventions (e.g., selective injection) to identify predictors and enablers of successful transfer.

## Limitations
- High sensitivity to verification thresholds (`τ_cov`, `τ_safe`) and refinement epochs; optimal hyperparameters are dataset‑dependent.
- Skills are model‑specific, requiring per‑model regeneration and limiting reuse across model versions.
- Overfitting risk after too many refinement cycles, as evidenced by performance regression at epoch 3.

1. **Generalizability test:** Run ASDA on a different financial benchmark (e.g., FinQA) to verify whether reported gains extend beyond FAMMA.

3. **Threshold sensitivity analysis:** Systematically vary `τ_cov` and `τ_safe`, measuring effects on final accuracy and overfitting to identify robust hyperparameter ranges.
