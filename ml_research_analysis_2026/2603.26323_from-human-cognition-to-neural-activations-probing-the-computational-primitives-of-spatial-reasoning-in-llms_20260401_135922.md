# From Human Cognition to Neural Activations: Probing the Computational Primitives of Spatial Reasoning in LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.26323  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26323  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: LLMs encode spatial information transiently and in a fragmented manner, with weak integration, indicating limited spatial reasoning.

## Executive Summary
This paper probes the internal representations of spatial reasoning in large language models (LLMs) using computational primitives derived from human cognition. The authors create three families of controlled tasks—relational composition, representational transformation, and stateful updating—across English, Chinese, and Arabic. Through linear probing, sparse autoencoders, and causal interventions on Qwen2.5‑7B and Llama3‑8B models, they find that spatial information is transiently encoded in intermediate layers (peak R² ≈ 0.40) and then declines. Representations are highly fragmented across task families with minimal overlap (12–18%), and cross‑linguistic analyses reveal mechanistic degeneracy—similar performance arises from distinct internal pathways. Behavioral accuracy varies widely, with program execution up to 62% and orientation reasoning near chance. Interventions confirm that identified spatial features have a causal effect but are weakly integrated into final predictions. Overall, LLMs do not possess robust, general‑purpose spatial reasoning; instead, they rely on context‑dependent, fragile spatial encodings.

## Method Summary
The study constructs three controlled task families grounded in cognitive primitives: relational composition (reasoning about paths/mazes), representational transformation (egocentric to allocentric orientation), and stateful updating (temporal spatial changes). Each family is instantiated in English, Chinese, and Arabic via deterministic algorithms that ensure multi‑step reasoning requirements. Datasets of 2000 training and 200 test instances per language are used. Two instruct‑tuned LLMs (Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct and Llama3‑8B‑Instruct) are evaluated in a single‑pass greedy decoding setup. To investigate internal representations, the authors perform layer‑wise linear probing to linearly decode spatial variables (e.g., coordinates, direction vectors) from hidden states, identifying layers where spatial information is most accessible. Sparse autoencoders (expansion 32×, L1 = 0.001) are then trained on these peak layers to disentangle monosemantic features, measuring overlap and selectivity across tasks. Finally, causal interventions—including activation patching with counterfactual states and ablation of top spatial features—quantify the contribution of spatial encodings to model behavior. Cross‑linguistic analyses compare internal pathways across languages while controlling for task structure.

## Key Results
- Spatial information peaks in intermediate layers (R² up to 0.40) and declines sharply thereafter, indicating that spatial structure is built but not preserved for token prediction.  
- Spatial representations are fragmented across task families, with minimal feature overlap (12–18%) and strong language‑specific patterns (mechanistic degeneracy), suggesting no unified spatial module.  
- Behavioral accuracy shows large task‑dependent variation (spatial program execution ~62%, orientation reasoning ~chance), and causal interventions demonstrate that spatial features influence output but are only weakly integrated (accuracy drops 14–29% when removed).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Spatial information is transiently encoded in intermediate layers but declines sharply in final layers.  
- **Mechanism:** Linear probes are trained on hidden states from each layer to decode task‑relevant spatial variables (relative positions, orientation vectors, coordinates). Probe performance (R²) peaks at mid‑layers and drops toward output layers, indicating that spatial structure is built during intermediate processing but not preserved for token prediction.  
- **Core assumption:** Spatial information is linearly decodable from activations.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “spatial information is transiently encoded in intermediate layers (peak probe R² up to 0.40) but declines sharply in final layers.”  
  - [section 4.2] “Across models and tasks, spatial information consistently peaks in intermediate layers and declines sharply toward the final layers.”  
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers on probing (e.g., *Beyond Pattern Recognition: Probing Mental Representations of LMs*) support the methodological approach but do not directly confirm the claim.  
- **Break condition:** If probe R² remains at chance (~0) across all layers for all spatial variables, the claim fails; spatial information would not be linearly accessible.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Spatial representations are fragmented across task families, with minimal feature overlap and mechanistic degeneracy across languages.  
- **Mechanism:** Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are trained on layers with peak probe performance to extract interpretable features. Feature selectivity (gradient × activation) and overlap between tasks are quantified. Limited overlap (12–18%) and language‑specific feature sets indicate fragmented, context‑dependent representations.  
- **Core assumption:** SAEs can disentangle monosemantic features from the superposition in dense activations.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “representations are fragmented across task families with minimal feature overlap” and “Cross‑linguistic analysis reveals mechanistic degeneracy—similar performance arises from distinct internal pathways.”  
  - [section 4.3] “Feature overlap across task families is limited (12–18%)” and “SAE analysis reveals partial feature sharing based on activation frequency, but substantially lower overlap when features are ranked by causal attribution.”  
  - [corpus] Limited; related work on feature decomposition (e.g., *Truth as a Trajectory*) provides context but not direct support.  
- **Break condition:** If SAE features show high overlap (>50%) across task families or lack clear spatial selectivity, the fragmentation claim weakens significantly.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Encoded spatial features causally influence model behavior but are weakly integrated into final predictions.  
- **Mechanism:** Activation patching (replacing mid‑layer activations with counterfactual spatial states) and feature ablation (zeroing top spatial features) are performed. Output shifts and accuracy drops are measured to establish causality.  
- **Core assumption:** Targeted interventions on specific activations/features can isolate their causal contribution to behavior.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “Causal interventions confirm identified spatial features functionally influence behavior but are weakly integrated.”  
  - [section 4.4] “patching intermediate‑layer activations with counterfactual spatial states systematically shifts model outputs” and “Removing top spatial features reduces accuracy by 29% in orientation tasks and 14.5% in spatial program execution.”  
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers (e.g., *Catching rationalization in the act*) use similar intervention techniques, lending methodological credibility.  
- **Break condition:** If interventions produce no statistically significant change in outputs, the causal role of spatial features is not supported; representations may be epiphenomenal.

## Foundational Learning

#### Linear Probing
- **Why needed here:** To detect whether spatial variables (positions, orientations) are represented in a linearly decodable manner within hidden states, establishing the presence of spatial information.  
- **Quick check:** Given activations from a specific layer and true spatial labels, can a linear model achieve above‑chance R²? A peak in mid‑layers suggests transient encoding.

#### Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)
- **Why needed here:** To decompose dense activations into potentially monosemantic features and assess whether spatial computations rely on shared or task‑specific components.  
- **Quick check:** Do SAE features exhibit clear spatial selectivity (e.g., fire for specific directions or coordinate ranges) and are they sparse (few active per sample)? High sparsity and interpretability indicate successful decomposition.

## Architecture Onboarding

#### Component map
Task generators (rule‑based dataset creation) → Inference engine (single‑pass activation collection) → Probing module (layer‑wise linear probes) → SAE trainer (sparse autoencoders) → Intervention module (activation patching & feature ablation) → Analysis suite (metrics aggregation)

#### Critical path
Baseline behavioral evaluation → layer‑wise probing → SAE training on peak layers → causal interventions. Probing identifies peak layers; interventions target those layers to maximize effect.

#### Design tradeoffs
- Single‑pass inference avoids explicit reasoning traces but may underestimate latent competence; chain‑of‑thought could stabilize representations but introduces verbalized reasoning confounds.  
- Linear probes are fast and interpretable but may miss nonlinear structure; SAEs reveal features but require careful hyperparameter tuning (e.g., L1 coefficient).  
- Multilingual design controls linguistic confounds but multiplies experimental matrix; trade‑off between generality and compute.  
- Model scale (7B/8B) balances cost and representational capacity; larger models may show different patterns.

#### Failure signatures
- Probe R² flatlines near zero → data generation error or misaligned probe targets.  
- SAE reconstruction error high (>1.0) or features not sparse → SAE hyperparameters need adjustment.  
- Interventions show no effect → either spatial features not causal or intervention target layers incorrect.  
- Behavioral accuracy near random → model checkpoint corrupted or prompt template mismatch.

#### First 3 experiments
1. **Behavioral baseline:** Run all models on all tasks/languages; verify program execution > relational > orientation pattern, and note cross‑linguistic variations.  
2. **Layer‑probing sweep:** For each task‑language pair, train linear probes on every layer; plot R² curves; confirm inverted‑U shape and identify peak layers.  
3. **SAE feature analysis:** Train SAE on peak layer for each task; compute feature selectivity and overlap between tasks; expect low overlap (~15%) and spatially selective features.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Do LLMs use structured spatial representations or linguistic heuristics?  
2. Why do spatial representations peak in intermediate layers and decline in final layers?  
3. What causes mechanistic degeneracy across languages?

## Limitations
- Probe linearity may miss nonlinear spatial representations; nonlinear probes could reveal different patterns.  
- Sparse autoencoder decomposition relies on strong assumptions (monosemanticity, sparsity) that may not fully capture the true feature space.  
- Reproduction details (dataset hyperparameters, SAE convergence, intervention magnitudes) are underspecified, introducing variability and uncertainty.

## Confidence
- Transient spatial encoding: Medium  
- Fragmented, degenerate representations: Medium  
- Causal influence with weak integration: Medium  

## Next Checks
1. Re‑run probing with nonlinear classifiers (e.g., MLPs) to test whether spatial information is truly linearly accessible or if probes underestimate latent structure.  
2. Systematically vary SAE expansion factors and L1 regularization to measure robustness of the low feature overlap finding; high overlap under different hyperparameters would challenge fragmentation claims.  
3. Perform interventions using multiple perturbation strengths and layer ranges to establish effect‑size stability; vanishing effects outside reported layer windows would indicate narrow integration.
