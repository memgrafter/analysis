# Architecting Agentic Communities using Design Patterns
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2601.03624
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03624
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** Introduces a 46-pattern catalogue across three tiers (LLM Agents, Agentic AI, Agentic Communities) grounded in ODP-EL formalism to enable provable governance and accountability in multi-agent human-AI systems, validated in a clinical trial matching case study.

## Executive Summary
This paper provides a systematic architectural framework for building production-grade, governed agentic AI systems in regulated enterprises. It presents a catalogue of 46 design patterns organized into three tiers of increasing complexity and autonomy: task-specific LLM Agents, adaptive goal-seeking Agentic AI, and multi-participant Agentic Communities. The core innovation is grounding the highest tier in the ODP-EL formalism, using deontic tokens (burden, permit, embargo) to formally specify and verify governance properties like safety, authority, and prohibition. A three-step methodology (assess, compose, scope) guides practitioners in selecting and combining patterns. The framework is empirically validated through a clinical trial matching case study that composed 15 patterns across all tiers, demonstrating traceable responsibility and provable compliance.

## Method Summary
The paper's methodology for constructing a governed agentic system involves three steps. First, assess the use case's characteristics across autonomy, regulatory, data, and learning dimensions to identify relevant patterns. Second, compose selected patterns using defined relationships: layered (vertical stacking), complementary (horizontal combination), or alternative (choice between options). Third, scope the implementation into incremental tiers (Simple Automation, Departmental, Enterprise) based on complexity and risk. For systems requiring verifiable governance (Agentic Communities), patterns are grounded in ODP-EL specifications that define roles, deontic tokens representing normative constraints, and contracts binding roles, enabling formal property verification.

## Key Results
*   A validated three-tier pattern classification (LLM Agents, Agentic AI, Agentic Communities) matches architectural solutions to required autonomy and coordination levels.
*   ODP-EL grounding with deontic tokens enables formal verification of critical governance properties (safety, authority, prohibition, accountability) in multi-agent human-AI coordination.
*   A clinical trial matching case study successfully composed 15 patterns across all tiers, proving the framework's ability to deliver verifiable compliance and traceable responsibility in a regulated domain.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** A three-tier classification (LLM Agents, Agentic AI, Agentic Communities) systematically matches architectural patterns to required autonomy and coordination levels.
*   **Mechanism:** The classification distinguishes systems by autonomy scope (task execution vs. goal pursuit) and participant count (single vs. multi-agent/human). This allows architects to select patterns appropriate to the problem's inherent complexity—using LLM Agent patterns for narrow automation, Agentic AI patterns for adaptive reasoning, and Agentic Community patterns for governed multi-participant coordination.
*   **Core assumption:** Autonomy and coordination requirements are the primary drivers for architectural pattern selection in production systems.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] classification definition; [section 2.1] Table 1 defining tiers and characteristics.
*   **Break condition:** If a use case's requirements span multiple tiers, the classification alone is insufficient; pattern composition across tiers becomes necessary, as validated in the case study.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Grounding Agentic Community patterns in ODP-EL formalism enables verifiable governance and accountability through deontic tokens (burden, permit, embargo).
*   **Mechanism:** ODP-EL communities specify roles (for AI agents or humans), normative constraints as deontic tokens, and contracts binding roles. Tokens track obligations, permissions, and prohibitions across agent interactions, creating audit trails and enabling formal verification of properties like safety ("no action without consent"), authority ("only physicians decide enrollment"), and prohibition ("AI cannot auto-enroll").
*   **Core assumption:** Formal semantics are required to prove governance properties in regulated environments where testing alone is inadequate for assurance.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "grounding... in the ODP-EL formalism for formal verification"; [section 6.5] lists verifiable properties with formal logical expressions.
*   **Break condition:** If regulatory acceptance does not require formal proofs (e.g., in low-risk domains), the overhead of ODP-EL specification may not be justified.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** A three-step methodology (assess characteristics, compose patterns, scope tiers) guides the practical construction of enterprise agentic systems from the pattern catalogue.
*   **Mechanism:** Step 1 maps use case dimensions to candidate patterns. Step 2 composes these using layered (vertical), parallel (horizontal), and system-wide (cross-cutting) relationships. Step 3 scopes implementation into incremental tiers (Simple, Departmental, Enterprise) based on complexity and risk. This transforms abstract patterns into actionable architecture plans.
*   **Core assumption:** Pattern relationships (layered, complementary, alternative) are stable and can be codified into composition strategies that manage complexity and risk.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [section 4] methodology description; [section 5] case study demonstrating all three steps.
*   **Break condition:** If pattern relationships are not well-understood for a novel domain, composition may produce incoherent or unverifiable architectures.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** Three-Tier Classification (LLM Agent, Agentic AI, Agentic Community). *Why needed:* Primary organizing principle for the pattern catalogue; determines relevant patterns based on autonomy and coordination needs. *Quick check:* Does your use case require a single autonomous agent making strategic decisions (Agentic AI) or multiple participants (AI + humans) coordinating under governance (Agentic Community)?
*   **Concept:** Deontic Tokens (Burden, Permit, Embargo). *Why needed:* Operational mechanism in ODP-EL for representing and tracking governance rules (obligations, permissions, prohibitions) to enable accountability chains. *Quick check:* Can you formally specify who is *obligated* to act, who is *permitted* to access data, and what is *prohibited* for all agents?
*   **Concept:** Pattern Composition Relationships (Layered, Complementary, Alternative). *Why needed:* Patterns are rarely used in isolation; understanding these relationships is essential for building complete systems. *Quick check:* When combining ReAct and Memory-Augmented, is the relationship layered (Memory augments ReAct) or complementary (independent benefits)?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** FHIR Foundation (LLM Agent patterns) -> Matching Workflow (Agentic AI patterns) -> Negotiation Community (Agentic Community patterns)
*   **Critical path:** Data Access (Layer 1: Extraction + Access Control + Compliance) -> Core Reasoning (Layer 2: ReAct + Validation + Human-in-the-Loop) + Audit Trail (cross-cutting). Skipping Layer 1's governance or Layer 2's human authority breaks compliance.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   Autonomy vs. Governance: More agentic autonomy requires more governance patterns and human oversight.
    *   Simplicity vs. Completeness: Simple Automation tier delivers fast value but lacks verifiable governance; Enterprise tier provides compliance at higher cost.
    *   Model-Agnostic vs. Optimization: Patterns are model-agnostic for longevity but may forgo provider-specific optimizations.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   Governance Gaps: Missing `burden` or `embargo` tokens for high-risk actions (e.g., no `burden(make decision, physician)`).
    *   Coordination Breakdown: Incompatible compositions (e.g., `Orchestration` without `Inter-Agent Communication`).
    *   Emergent Misalignment: Multi-agent systems where harmful strategies are not prevented by `Constitutional AI` or `Compliance`.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Tier 1 MVP:** Implement Simple Automation with `Structured Extraction`, `ReAct`, and `Validation` on non-sensitive data. Goal: Prove pattern utility without governance.
    2.  **Add Governance Cluster:** To Tier 1, add `Access Control`, `Compliance/Governance`, and `Audit Trail`. Use synthetic data to verify `embargo` blocks unauthorized actions and `permit` is logged. Goal: Validate deontic token enforcement.
    3.  **Introduce Human-AI Coordination:** Extend to Departmental tier with `Human-in-the-Loop` and `Explanation`. Test low-confidence escalations. Goal: Verify `burden` token assignment and explanation coherence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** How can usage patterns for progressive autonomy escalation (e.g., advisory → supervised → autonomous modes) be systematically developed and validated for agentic AI systems in safety-critical domains? *Basis:* [explicit] Section 8.2 identifies "Usage Patterns and Operational Practices" as future work, calling for patterns for "progressive autonomy introduction."
*   **Open Question 2:** What domain-specific languages (DSLs) and tooling can enable non-expert practitioners to express verifiable governance policies (e.g., deontic constraints) without requiring formal methods expertise? *Basis:* [explicit] Section 8.2 lists "Formal Verification and Tooling," noting the need for DSLs to "bridge formal specifications with domain-expert expressibility."
*   **Open Question 3:** How can the pattern catalogue be specialized and validated for high-regulation domains (e.g., clinical healthcare, algorithmic trading) while preserving formal verifiability across multi-agent human-AI coordination? *Basis:* [explicit] Section 8.2 identifies "Domain-Specific Instantiations," calling for specialized collections for healthcare, finance, etc.
*   **Open Question 4:** What validation criteria and peer-review processes should govern contributions to the living pattern catalogue to ensure new patterns align with the three-tier classification and maintain formal foundations without stifling innovation? *Basis:* [inferred] Section 8.2 discusses "Community Evolution and Contribution," noting plans for a GitHub repository with "clear validation criteria," but specifics are absent.

## Limitations
*   ODP-EL token runtime enforcement details are opaque—tooling (e.g., XMPRO MAGS) is cited but not openly specified, blocking replication of formal verification.
*   The full pattern catalogue (46 patterns) and detailed composition rules are unavailable; only examples are provided, limiting compositional validation.
*   Generalizability beyond clinical trial matching is unproven; other regulated domains (finance, aviation) may reveal tier-classification gaps.

## Confidence
*   **Three-tier classification utility:** Medium—logic is clear and case-supported, but break condition (multi-tier scenarios) suggests context-dependent validity.
*   **ODP-EL grounding for verification:** Low—formal claims rely on internal tooling; corpus shows no independent validation of deontic token enforcement in production.
*   **Pattern methodology and composition:** Medium—case study demonstrates feasibility, but stability of pattern relationships across domains is assumed, not evidenced.
