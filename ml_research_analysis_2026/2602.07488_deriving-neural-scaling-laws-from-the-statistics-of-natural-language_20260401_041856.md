# Deriving Neural Scaling Laws from the statistics of natural language

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.07488  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07488  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: The paper predicts neural scaling exponents from natural language statistics via αD = γ/(2β), where γ is the decay of next-token conditional entropy with context length and β is the decay of token-token correlations with temporal separation; predictions match experiments on GPT-2 and LLaMA for TinyStories (αD ≈ 0.19) and WikiText (αD ≈ 0.14).

## Executive Summary
Predicting how neural language model performance scales with training data size—the neural scaling exponent—from first principles has been an open challenge. This work shows that two dataset-specific exponents suffice: γ (the rate at which next-token conditional entropy decreases with context length) and β (the rate at which token-token correlations decay with temporal separation). Combining them yields the data-limited scaling exponent αD = γ/(2β), under the assumption that modern transformers learn predictions efficiently within any usable horizon. Empirical validation on GPT‑2 and LLaMA trained on TinyStories and WikiText confirms the predicted exponents. The theory further reveals that increasing data primarily extends the usable prediction horizon rather than improving accuracy within a fixed horizon, a mechanism corroborated by a collapse of rescaled n‑gram loss curves.

## Method Summary
The methodology comprises four stages. First, datasets are tokenized with byte‑pair encoding (vocab size 8192) and concatenated into a single stream. Second, β is estimated by computing the token‑token covariance matrix C(n) for lags n, extracting its operator norm ∥C(n)∥op (largest singular value), and fitting the power‑law decay ∥C(n)∥op ∝ n⁻ᵝ over the initial regime. Third, γ is estimated by training a sufficiently large autoregressive transformer (≈100 M parameters) on the maximum available token count Pₘₐₓ; the n‑gram losses Lₙ(Pₘₐₓ) for small n are then fitted to Lₙ - H∞ ∝ n⁻ᵞ. Fourth, the predicted exponent αD = γ/(2β) is compared to the measured autoregressive scaling exponent α_exp obtained by training models across a range of P and context lengths T. An additional validation checks whether the rescaled quantities n^γ Lₙ(P) and P / n^{2β} collapse onto a single curve for all P and n, confirming the mechanism.

## Key Results
- Predicted scaling exponents match empirical measurements: αD ≈ 0.19 for TinyStories and αD ≈ 0.14 for WikiText‑103.
- Rescaled n‑gram loss curves (n^γ Lₙ vs P/n^{2β}) collapse onto a master curve, demonstrating that increasing data extends the usable prediction horizon as n*(P) ∝ P^{1/(2β)}.
- The autoregressive loss L(P) exhibits a power‑law decay with exponent α_exp in good agreement with γ/(2β), confirming the theory’s central formula.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Data‑dependent prediction horizon extension via detectable correlations
- **Claim**: Increasing training data primarily extends the usable prediction horizon n*(P) rather than improving predictions within a fixed horizon, with n*(P) scaling as P^{1/(2β)}.
- **Mechanism**: The model can only beneficially use tokens with temporal separation n when the top singular value of the token‑token covariance matrix ∥C(n)∥op exceeds the statistical sampling noise O(P^{-1/2}). This signal‑to‑noise threshold ∥C(n)∥op ∝ P^{-1/2} defines a data threshold P*ₙ for each horizon. The correlation strength is empirically observed to decay with separation n as ∥C(n)∥op ∝ n^{-β}, yielding P*ₙ ∝ n^{2β}. Thus the maximal usable horizon grows as n*(P) ∝ P^{1/(2β)}.
- **Core assumption**: The operator norm decay ∥C(n)∥op ∝ n^{-β} holds over the relevant horizon range and provides a reliable estimate of the strongest statistical signal.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [abstract] “increasing data primarily extends the usable prediction horizon”
  - [section 3] Eq. 4: threshold condition ∥C(n)∥op = O(1/√P) and definition of P*ₙ; Eq. 7: ∥C(n)∥op ∝ n^{-β}; Eq. 29: n*(P) ∝ P^{1/(2β)}
  - [section 4.1.2] Empirical confirmation of β from power‑law fits to ∥C(n)∥op vs n in Fig. 3 for both datasets
- **Break condition**: The mechanism fails if the correlation decay is not a simple power law (e.g., multi‑scale structure that breaks scaling), if the operator norm does not dominate the spectrum (many comparable singular values), or if the signal detection threshold is not well‑approximated by O(P^{-1/2}) due to estimation bias or non‑Gaussian noise.

### Mechanism 2: Horizon‑limited loss from conditional entropy decay
- **Claim**: The autoregressive loss is dominated by the next‑token conditional entropy at the data‑dependent prediction horizon, H_{n*(P)} - H∞ ∝ P^{-γ/(2β)}.
- **Mechanism**: The n‑gram loss Lₙ(P) is lower‑bounded by the conditional entropy Hₙ. When n*(P) is the maximal usable horizon, the loss L_AR(P) contains a boundary term H_{n*(P)}. The empirical observation Hₙ - H∞ ∝ n^{-γ} then gives H_{n*(P)} - H∞ ∝ n*(P)^{-γ} ∝ P^{-γ/(2β)}.
- **Core assumption**: The conditional entropy Hₙ decays as a power law with context length n, Hₙ - H∞ ∝ n^{-γ}, and this decay property is intrinsic to the dataset and independent of architecture (validated by convergence of Lₙ(P) across model families).
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [abstract] “decay of next‑token conditional entropy with the length of the conditioning context”
  - [section 4.1.1] Eq. 6: Hₙ - H∞ ∝ n^{-γ}; Fig. 2 shows Lₙ(P) for varying P converges to limiting curve for large P, allowing γ estimation; consistency across GPT‑2 APE, GPT‑2 RoPE, and LLaMA in Fig. 2 (right)
  - [corpus] Weak: Direct measurement of Hₙ from counts is infeasible; relies on the assumption that Lₙ(P) for large P and sufficiently expressive models is a tight upper bound that approaches Hₙ. The paper provides cross‑architecture convergence as evidence.
- **Break condition**: The mechanism fails if Hₙ does not follow a power‑law decay (e.g., plateaus abruptly) or if the exponent γ cannot be stably estimated (e.g., due to limited horizon range or poor model expressivity causing Lₙ(P) not to converge to Hₙ even at largest P).

### Mechanism 3: Fast learning within the usable horizon
- **Claim**: The excess loss Eₙ(P) (the suboptimal prediction within horizon n) decays faster than P^{-γ/(2β)} (i.e., δₙ > γ/(2β)), so the total loss reduction is dominated by horizon extension.
- **Mechanism**: For a given horizon n, as P increases beyond P*ₙ, the n‑gram loss Lₙ(P) approaches its asymptote Hₙ. The rate of this approach is governed by an exponent δₙ. If δₙ > γ/(2β) for all n ≤ n*(P), then the sum of excess losses Σ_{n≤n*} Eₙ(P) decays no slower than H_{n*(P)} - H∞, and the total loss scaling exponent α_D = γ/(2β) is determined solely by dataset statistics.
- **Core assumption**: Modern deep autoregressive transformers achieve near‑optimal conditional prediction within any usable horizon n before needing to extend the horizon further. This is an architecture‑specific assumption (does not hold for kernel methods or shallow networks).
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [section 3] “assuming that the Eₙ(P) decay sufficiently fast with P” and Eq. 38: α_D = min(δ, γ/(2β))
  - [section 5] Direct measurement of δₙ for n ≤ 12 in GPT‑2 on TinyStories (Fig. 6), showing δₙ > γ/(2β) for all n
  - [section 6] Discussion of universality class: “a broad class of deep architectures… do fall into the universality class” while “kernel methods or shallow networks” lie outside.
- **Break condition**: The mechanism fails if for some n ≤ n*(P), δₙ ≤ γ/(2β). This would mean that within‑horizon learning is the bottleneck, and the scaling exponent α_D would be lower (equal to minₙ δₙ instead of γ/(2β)). This could occur in architectures lacking sufficient inductive bias (e.g., lack of attention for long‑range dependencies) or with suboptimal optimization.

## Foundational Learning
### Autoregressive loss decomposition
- **Why needed here**: The total loss L_AR(P,T) = (1/T) Σ_{n=1}^T Lₙ(P) is decomposed into contributions from increasing usable horizon (H_{n*}) and suboptimal predictions within horizon (Eₙ). This decomposition is the foundation of the theory.
- **Quick check question**: If you double the training data, what two mechanisms can reduce L_AR, and which is assumed to dominate?

### Conditional entropy and n‑gram loss
- **Why needed here**: The n‑gram loss Lₙ(P) = -E[log p̂_θ(X_{n+1} | X_{1:n})] is an upper bound on the conditional entropy Hₙ = -E[log P(X_{n+1} | X_{1:n})]. Understanding their relationship and the decay of Hₙ with n is central to the horizon‑limited mechanism.
- **Quick check question**: Why is Hₙ a lower bound on Lₙ(P) for any model? What happens to Lₙ(P) as P → ∞ for a sufficiently expressive model?

### Token‑token covariance and correlation decay
- **Why needed here**: The covariance matrix C(n) quantifies statistical dependencies at temporal separation n. Its operator norm decay determines the data required to detect those dependencies, setting P*ₙ.
- **Quick check question**: If you observe ∥C(5)∥op ≈ 10⁻³ and ∥C(10)∥op ≈ 10⁻⁴, what would you expect for β? How would P*₁₀ compare to P*₅?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
1. **Dataset statistics measurement module**: Computes γ and β from raw text.
   - For β: compute C(n) from co‑occurrence counts on full dataset; fit power law to ∥C(n)∥op vs n.
   - For γ: train large autoregressive model (e.g., GPT‑2 ~100 M params) on largest feasible P; extract Lₙ(P) for all n; fit power law to Lₙ vs n for small n (where Lₙ ≈ Hₙ due to large P).
2. **Theory computation module**: Takes γ, β → predicts α_D = γ/(2β).
3. **Validation module**: 
   - Train models across range of P and multiple T.
   - For each T, fit L_AR(P) exponent α_exp (excluding region where T clips n*).
   - Test n‑gram collapse: rescale Lₙ(P) by n^γ horizontally by P/n^(2β); check collapse onto single curve.
   - Compare α_exp to α_D.
4. **Control experiments**: Repeat with different architectures (GPT‑2 APE, GPT‑2 RoPE, LLaMA) to verify γ architecture‑independence; vary model size to ensure Lₙ(P) converges.

### Critical path
1. Choose dataset (TinyStories or WikiText). Tokenize with BPE (vocab ~8k).
2. Compute β: For n=1 to max_horizon (e.g., 512), compute empirical token‑token co‑occurrence matrix C(n) from all training tokens. Compute operator norm (largest singular value). Fit log(∥C(n)∥op) = const - β log n over range where decay is power‑law (discard initial peak/break).
3. Estimate γ: Train a sufficiently large autoregressive model (e.g., 100 M+ params) on the largest available P (e.g., 2²⁸ tokens). For n=1 to T, compute Lₙ(P) on a held‑out set. For small n (where Lₙ(P) ≈ Hₙ), fit log Lₙ = const - γ log n. Validate by training smaller models at smaller P to see Lₙ curves converge to this limiting curve.
4. Compute α_D = γ/(2β).
5. Validation scaling: For multiple T (e.g., 64,128,256,512), train model family (same architecture as used for γ) across P from small (2²⁰) to large (2²⁸). For each T, record L_AR(P). Plot log L_AR vs log P. Identify scaling region where curve is linear and T does not clip n* (i.e., slope stable across T). Fit slope α_exp.
6. Compare α_exp to α_D. Also perform n‑gram collapse: for each P and n, plot n^γ Lₙ(P) vs P/n^(2β). Expect collapse.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Vocabulary size**: Smaller BPE vocab (8k) reduces C(n) size (V²) and sparsity, improving β estimation. Larger vocab may require more P for reliable covariance.
- **Model size for γ**: Too small may not converge to Hₙ even at large P; too large risks overfitting or longer training times. Paper uses ~100 M for TinyStories; up to 600 M for WikiText. Must ensure Lₙ(P) decreases with P and stabilizes.
- **Horizon range for β fit**: The decay may have breaks (e.g., peak around n≈10 in WikiText). Fit β over the initial power‑law regime (short lags) before any break, as that regime is relevant for n*.
- **P range for scaling validation**: Need enough decades of P (at least ~2 orders) to fit power law reliably. Must ensure max P is large enough that n*(P_max) is a non‑trivial fraction of T to see scaling but still less than T to avoid clipping.
- **Context length T**: Must be large enough that n*(P) << T for the P range of interest, otherwise loss saturates due to context limit, not data limit. Use multiple T to verify scaling region is T‑independent.

### Failure signatures
- β estimation: No clear power law in ∥C(n)∥op; high variance; multi‑exponent behavior; operator norm not representative (e.g., many singular values contribute equally). → Report best‑fit β but note uncertainty; theory may not apply.
- γ estimation: Lₙ(P) curves for different P do not converge; Lₙ does not decrease with n; poor fit to power law. → Model capacity insufficient or optimization failed; repeat with larger model.
- n‑gram collapse: Rescaled curves do not collapse; systematic offsets remain. → Either fast‑learning assumption violated (δₙ comparable to γ/(2β)) or exponents mis‑measured; re‑check β, γ fits.
- Autoregressive loss exponent deviates from α_D and is smaller. → Likely δ < γ/(2β): within‑horizon learning is slow. Could be due to architecture (test other architectures) or optimization (learning rate, epochs).
- Context clipping: L_AR(P) curves for different T converge at large P, then diverge? Actually, if T is too small, L_AR(P) will saturate earlier for smaller T, causing apparent exponent reduction. Verify that for the fitted scaling region, increasing T does not change the slope.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Dataset statistics extraction**: For TinyStories, train GPT‑2 APE (12 layers, 768 dim, 98 M params) on P=2²⁸ tokens. Compute Lₙ(P) on validation for n=1..512. Fit γ from n=1..32 range. Separately, compute C(n) from all training tokens for n=1..512; compute ∥C(n)∥op; fit β to n=1..128 range (excluding n≈10 peak if present). Document γ≈0.34, β≈0.88. Repeat with LLaMA and RoPE to confirm γ consistency.
2. **N‑gram collapse verification**: Using same GPT‑2 APE architecture, train models on P in {2²⁰, 2²², 2²⁴, 2²⁶, 2²⁸}. For each P, compute Lₙ(P) for n=1..128. For each n, rescale: horizontal axis P' = P / n^(2β), vertical axis L' = n^γ Lₙ. Plot all (P', L') on same axes. Expect single collapsing curve. Also plot unscaled to show separation.
3. **Autoregressive scaling prediction**: For GPT‑2 APE, train models across same P range for T ∈ {64,128,256,512}. For each T, compute L_AR(P). On log‑log plot, identify region where curves for different T overlap (i.e., n*(P) << T). Fit slope α_exp in that region using linear regression on log L_AR vs log P (with offset for H∞). Compare to α_D = 0.19. Repeat for WikiText (α_D≈0.14) and for RoPE/LLaMA variants. Also test that α_exp is stable across T in scaling region.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: Which model architectures and training dynamics yield the predicted data‑limited scaling exponent αD = γ/(2β)?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors explicitly ask this in the conclusions: “which model architectures and training dynamics give rise to the scaling exponent and behavior we propose?”
- **Why unresolved**: Only GPT‑2 and LLaMA transformers were tested on two English datasets; the performance of other architectures (e.g., state‑space models, CNNs) and the effect of training details remain unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Train a diverse set of architectures on standard datasets, measure γ and β from data statistics, check for n‑gram loss collapse and alignment of the autoregressive exponent with γ/(2β); identify which architectural or algorithmic features are necessary and sufficient.

### Open Question 2: Does the theory hold at the much larger dataset sizes (trillions of tokens) and context lengths (≥10⁵) used in industrial large language models?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors state that “it would be interesting to determine whether our conclusions extend to the larger maximal context lengths and dataset sizes accessible in industrial settings.”
- **Why unresolved**: Experiments used ≤10⁸ tokens and context windows ≤512; statistical assumptions (e.g., power‑law decay regimes) may break or new phenomena may arise at industrial scales.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Conduct scaling experiments with massive corpora and very long‑context models; estimate γ and β over the effective horizon; verify whether n‑gram collapse and the predicted power‑law exponent persist.

### Open Question 3: Are the two exponents γ (conditional entropy decay) and β (token‑token correlation decay) sufficient to predict αD for all natural language corpora, across languages and domains?
- **Basis in paper**: The theory claims sufficiency, yet only two English datasets were tested, and even those show a broken power law with a local peak (n≈10) that was ignored.
- **Why unresolved**: Additional dataset characteristics (e.g., long‑range dependencies, multilingual vocabularies, code‑switching) might introduce corrections to the exponent not captured by a single (γ, β) pair.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Apply the theory to a wide range of corpora (multiple languages, code, informal text); independently measure γ and β; compare predicted αD with measured scaling exponents; if mismatches occur, identify missing statistical descriptors.

## Limitations
- Tokenization specifics (BPE training data, pre‑tokenization) unspecified.
- Computing ∥C(n)∥op for V=8192 is costly; approximations may bias β.
- Exact data sizes P and context lengths T not listed; scaling fits sensitive to clipping.
- γ estimation relies on large models approximating Hₙ; smaller models may under‑estimate.
- Power‑law decays can break (e.g., WikiText peak); fitting regime choices affect exponents.
- Fast‑learning assumption (δₙ > γ/(2β)) only verified for n≤12.

## Confidence
- **Core formula αD = γ/(2β)**: *Medium*. Dataset matches promising but depend on accurate γ,β and fast‑learning.
- **Mechanism 1 (horizon extension)**: *Medium‑High*. β decay clear, but threshold and operator norm dominance need stronger evidence.
- **Mechanism 2 (entropy decay)**: *Medium*. Cross‑architecture Lₙ convergence suggests dataset‑γ, but Hₙ not directly measured.
- **Mechanism 3 (fast learning)**: *Medium*. δₙ > γ/(2β) for small n only; larger n unknown.
- **Overall empirical validation**: *High* for studied models, but generalizability uncertain.
