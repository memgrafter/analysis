# JetFormer: A Scalable and Efficient Transformer for Jet Tagging from Offline Analysis to FPGA Triggers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17215
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17215
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: JetFormer matches state-of-the-art ParT accuracy (within 0.7%) on the JETCLASS dataset using 37.4% fewer FLOPs and achieves sub-microsecond FPGA inference latency for compressed variants.

## Executive Summary
Deploying high-performance jet tagging models for both offline analysis and ultra-low-latency Level-1 triggers at the LHC presents significant computational challenges. JetFormer addresses this with a scalable encoder-only Transformer that processes variable-length particle sets. Hardware-friendly modifications—Batch Normalization replacing Layer Normalization and ReLU replacing SiLU—simplify inference. A comprehensive hardware-aware pipeline combines multi-objective hyperparameter search, structured pruning, and 1-bit quantization to produce compact models. On the large-scale JETCLASS dataset, JetFormer attains accuracy within 0.7% of the ParT baseline while reducing FLOPs by 37.4%. It also improves accuracy by 3–4% on HLS4ML benchmarks. Pruning reduces FLOPs by ~47% with <0.5% accuracy loss; 1-bit quantization shrinks model size by 82–92% at an accuracy cost of 1.5–3.5%. FPGA implementations via the Allo framework achieve sub-microsecond latencies (e.g., 0.404 ms) with low resource usage, demonstrating a practical pathway from high-accuracy modeling to real-time trigger deployment.

## Method Summary
JetFormer is an encoder-only Transformer that processes variable-length sets of jet constituents. To enable efficient hardware deployment, it replaces Layer Normalization with Batch Normalization and SiLU with ReLU, reducing per-sample computation and simplifying operations. The authors then employ a hardware-aware optimization pipeline: multi-objective hyperparameter search (Optuna NSGAIISampler) over depth, embedding dimension, and head counts, constrained by validation accuracy, to identify Pareto-optimal compact models. Structured pruning (Taylor importance) and 1-bit quantization-aware training (BitLinear) further compress the models. Finally, the compressed models are converted using the Allo framework and synthesized with Vitis HLS for FPGA deployment. This end-to-end approach bridges high-accuracy offline analysis with low-latency real-time trigger systems.

## Key Results
- On the JETCLASS dataset, JetFormer matches the ParT baseline’s accuracy within 0.7% while reducing FLOPs by 37.4%.
- Structured pruning cuts FLOPs by approximately 47% with negligible accuracy loss (<0.5%); 1-bit quantization shrinks model size by 82–92% at an accuracy cost of 1.5–3.5%.
- FPGA implementations achieve sub-microsecond inference latencies (e.g., 0.404 ms for a pruned variant) with low resource utilization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: JetFormer reduces hardware implementation complexity and maintains accuracy by replacing Layer Normalization with Batch Normalization and SiLU with ReLU.
- **Mechanism**: LayerNorm requires per-sample mean/variance computation at inference, which is costly; BatchNorm uses precomputed running statistics, enabling simpler fixed operations. SiLU involves sigmoid and multiplication; ReLU is a simple threshold, reducing arithmetic operations. Both changes are applied throughout the transformer blocks.
- **Core assumption**: These architectural substitutions do not degrade model performance significantly for the jet tagging task, as the model learns to compensate.
- **Evidence anchors**:

- **Break condition**: If the jet tagging task required sample-specific normalization, BatchNorm's fixed statistics could cause accuracy degradation. However, the paper reports no significant drop, suggesting the assumption holds for their datasets.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: JetFormer identifies Pareto-optimal compact models through automated multi-objective hyperparameter optimization, balancing accuracy and computational cost (FLOPs).
- **Mechanism**: Optuna with NSGAIISampler explores combinations of depth, embedding dimension, and heads. Trials are evaluated on validation accuracy and FLOPs; a constraint (accuracy > 0.65) filters infeasible models. The Pareto front yields models like JetFormer-tiny with minimal FLOPs while meeting performance thresholds.
- **Core assumption**: The hyperparameter space contains models that achieve both high accuracy and low FLOPs, and multi-objective search can efficiently find them.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Abstract]: "hardware-aware optimization pipeline based on multi-objective hyperparameter search"
  - [Section 3.3]: Full description of HPO workflow, samplers, and constraints.
  - [corpus: Limited; while HPO is common in ML, the corpus neighbors focus on hardware deployment rather than HPO methods.]
- **Break condition**: If the constraint is too strict for very small models, the search may yield no feasible solutions. Also, if the search space is poorly defined, Pareto-optimal models may be suboptimal.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: JetFormer achieves aggressive compression with minimal accuracy loss via structured pruning and 1-bit quantization-aware training.
- **Mechanism**: Structured pruning uses Taylor importance to remove entire channels across dependent layers, followed by fine-tuning. 1-bit quantization (BitLinear) binarizes weights to ±1 and uses 8-bit activations, trained with straight-through estimator. Pruning reduces FLOPs ~47% with <0.5% accuracy loss; quantization reduces model size 82-92% with 1.5-3.5% accuracy drop.
- **Core assumption**: Structured pruning preserves more accuracy than unstructured for hardware, and 1-bit QAT can maintain performance despite extreme weight binarization.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Abstract]: "pruning reduces FLOPs by ~47% with negligible accuracy loss (<0.5%), while 1-bit quantization shrinks model size by 82–92% at a 1.5–3.5% accuracy cost."
  - [Section 3.4 and 3.5]: Detailed procedures and results.
  - [corpus: Supporting evidence from neighboring works on quantization for FPGAs also apply quantization to reduce resource usage.]
- **Break condition**: Aggressive pruning may remove critical features; if the model is already small, further pruning could cause accuracy collapse. 1-bit quantization may fail for deeper models or more complex datasets, as accuracy drop increases with constituent count.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
The system consists of (1) data loading and preprocessing (normalization, truncation), (2) model definition (JetFormer with BatchNorm/ReLU), (3) training pipeline (AdamW, OneCycleLR), (4) hyperparameter optimization (Optuna), (5) compression (pruning via torch-pruning, quantization via BitNet), (6) hardware deployment (Allo conversion, Vitis HLS synthesis). Each component is scriptable and configurable.

### Critical Path
New data → preprocess → train baseline → run HPO → select compact model → apply pruning/quantization → convert to Allo → compile for FPGA → test latency/resources. Iterative feedback: if FPGA compilation fails, revisit model size or Allo extensions.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Accuracy vs. efficiency: Larger models achieve higher accuracy but exceed latency budgets; compression techniques trade accuracy for size/latency.
- Pruning aggressiveness: Higher pruning ratios yield lower FLOPs but risk accuracy loss; fine-tuning steps can mitigate.
- Quantization bit-width: 1-bit maximizes compression but accuracy drop may be unacceptable; higher bits (e.g., 8-bit) offer better accuracy but larger size.
- Batch size: Smaller batch sizes reduce FPGA resource usage but increase per-sample latency; tradeoff depends on target throughput.
- Allo support: Using custom operations may require manual dialect extensions, increasing development time.

### Failure Signatures
- Training instability: Loss diverges → check learning rate, normalization, activation.
- HPO stagnation: Hypervolume plateaus → widen hyperparameter search space or adjust constraint.
- Pruning collapse: Accuracy drop >1% after pruning → reduce pruning ratio or increase fine-tuning epochs.
- Quantization degradation: Accuracy drop >5% → consider mixed precision or adjust quantization parameters.
- Allo compilation errors: Missing operation support → implement custom dialects (e.g., log_softmax, batchnorm3d) as in Section 4.2.
- FPGA resource overflow: Utilization >100% → reduce model size (more pruning/quantization), lower batch size, or apply more aggressive scheduling.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question**: How can hardware optimizations be applied to JetFormer to achieve sub-microsecond inference latencies on FPGAs for Level-1 trigger systems?  
**Basis in paper**: Section 6 identifies "further hardware optimization to reduce the inference latency of FPGA-based JetFormer implementation" as future work.  
**Why unresolved**: Current FPGA implementations without dedicated optimizations show latencies in milliseconds (e.g., 4.767 ms for the original model), and while tools like Allo allow for potential improvements, specific strategies for transformer models (e.g., pipelining, parallelism) are not explored.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Implementing advanced HLS techniques such as deep pipelining or spatial replication for JetFormer's transformer operations, measuring latency and resource usage on FPGAs, and demonstrating compliance with sub-microsecond trigger budgets.

### Open Question 2
**Question**: What extensions to HLS frameworks are required to deploy quantized transformer models, such as 1-bit quantized JetFormer, on FPGAs?  
**Basis in paper**: Section 4.1 states that "Allo currently lacks support for quantized model deployment on the Vitis HLS backend," preventing the deployment of quantized variants despite their significant size reduction.  
**Why unresolved**: 1-bit quantization reduces model size by 82–92% with minor accuracy loss, but deployment barriers prevent evaluating real hardware performance and trade-offs for compressed models.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Enhancing Allo or hls4ml to support bit-level operations and quantization-aware training outputs, successfully synthesizing and testing quantized JetFormer on FPGA, and reporting metrics like latency, resource utilization, and accuracy retention.

### Open Question 3
**Question**: Can JetFormer's encoder-only transformer architecture be effectively adapted to other high-energy physics tasks beyond jet tagging, and what modifications are needed?  
**Basis in paper**: Section 6 lists "extending JetFormer to other HEP tasks" as a key future direction.  
**Why unresolved**: JetFormer is tailored for variable-length particle sets in jet tagging; other tasks (e.g., particle tracking, event classification) may involve different data structures (graphs, sequences), and its transferability is untested.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Applying JetFormer or its variants to benchmark HEP datasets for tasks like tracking or classification, comparing performance against task-specific models, and documenting architectural changes or training adjustments required.

## Limitations
- Generalization beyond LHC jet tagging datasets is untested.
- Long-term stability and drift of 1-bit quantized models under varying detector conditions.
- Potential accuracy degradation in extreme pruning scenarios not covered in the paper’s 47% FLOP reduction test.

- Multi-objective HPO efficiency: **Medium** – Methodology described but limited external validation; depends on constraint definition.
- Compression viability (pruning/quantization): **High** – Detailed results with break conditions; corroborated by quantization trends in related works.
