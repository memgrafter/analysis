# The Language of Bargaining: Linguistic Effects in LLM Negotiations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04387
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04387
- **Reference count:** 8
- **Primary result:** Language choice shifts LLM negotiation outcomes more strongly than model architecture, with Indic languages reversing advantages and altering stability and exploration patterns.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how linguistic framing influences negotiation behavior in large language models (LLMs). Through controlled multi-agent simulations across three canonical negotiation games (Ultimatum, Buy-Sell, Resource Exchange) in English and four Indic languages (Hindi, Punjabi, Gujarati, Marwadi), the authors hold game rules, incentives, and model architectures constant. They find that language alone can significantly shift key outcomes—such as seller win rates and acceptance stability—often more dramatically than swapping the underlying model. In the Buy-Sell game, Marwadi completely reverses the English baseline's buyer favoritism. Effects are task-contingent: Indic languages reduce stability in distributive (zero-sum) games but increase integrative exploration in positive-sum settings. The work demonstrates that evaluating LLMs solely in English yields an incomplete and potentially misleading picture of their negotiation tendencies.

## Method Summary
The authors leveraged the NegotiationArena framework to simulate structured negotiations. Four LLM families (GPT-4o, GPT-3.5, Claude-3-Haiku, Claude-3.5-Haiku) played repeated rounds of Ultimatum, Buy-Sell, and Resource Exchange games. The only manipulation was the language specified in a system prompt (e.g., "You speak and bargain only in Hindi"), with all other parameters—game rules, incentive payoffs, temperature—held fixed. Each language-model-game combination was run 10 times to capture distributional behavior. Outcomes were parsed from dialogues to compute metrics like acceptance rates, role-specific advantages (seller/buyer), and trade volumes, then compared across conditions.

## Key Results
- Language choice shifts outcome metrics more strongly than changing the underlying model architecture.
- In the Buy-Sell game, Marwadi reverses the English baseline's buyer advantage, producing a 60.5% seller win rate versus 42.0% in English and a seller advantage of 12.3 versus 6.9.
- Indic languages reduce stability in distributive games (e.g., Hindi acceptance rate 87.6% vs. English 98.2%) but increase integrative exploration, with trade volumes 18–20% higher in non-English settings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Language activates culturally-conditioned negotiation scripts from training data, shifting strategic priorities (e.g., cooperation vs. extraction).
- **Mechanism:** The LLM's policy network retrieves and reinforces behavior patterns associated with a linguistic context during training (e.g., English corpora encoding Western consumerism, Indic corpora encoding bazaar dynamics). This acts as a latent prior that biases offer generation and acceptance thresholds.
- **Core assumption:** Training data contains statistically correlated patterns between language and negotiation behaviors (e.g., English texts often frame buyers as savvy, Indic texts emphasize seller respect).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "language choice can shift outcomes more strongly than changing the underlying model architecture... reversing proposer advantages and reallocating surplus."
  - [section 5.2.1] English baseline shows high buyer advantage (13.1) and low seller win rate (41.98%), while Marwadi reverses this (seller advantage 12.3, win rate 60.47%).
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., "Language of Bargaining") study human language effects in bargaining, providing a theoretical bridge but not direct LLM evidence.
- **Break condition:** Effects vanish when using controlled, language-agnostic synthetic negotiation prompts or when training data is forcibly balanced across cultural scripts.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Linguistic pragmatics (e.g., high-context indirectness in Indic languages) alters negotiation stability and concession patterns.
- **Mechanism:** Grammatical and pragmatic features (e.g., Hindi formal pronouns *aap/tum*) signal relational positioning, prompting the model to adopt more defensive or less explicit strategies, which can create offer-acceptance mismatches.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs internalize pragmatic language norms from exposure to multilingual text, and these norms influence turn-taking and phrasing in goal-directed dialogue.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.1.2] Hindi produces the lowest initial offers ($15.5 vs. English $21.7) and lowest acceptance (87.6% vs. 98.2%), a paradox suggesting misaligned expectations.
  - [section 3.1] "Indic languages as high-context (relational)" citing Hall (1976).
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring work focuses on human bargaining ("Language of Bargaining") or personality effects, not pragmatic stability.
- **Break condition:** Removing linguistic cues (e.g., translating all prompts to a structured, non-pragmatic template) while keeping the target language eliminates stability differences.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Stereotype activation from cultural labels (e.g., "Marwadi") overrides linguistic pragmatics and task structure, producing extreme role advantages.
- **Mechanism:** The label functions as a strong semantic prime, retrieving stereotypical commercial traits (shrewd trader) from model weights, which then dominate the negotiation policy even in integrative settings.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs have compartmentalized representations linking ethnic/regional labels to occupational stereotypes, and these are triggered by persona prompts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.2.4] "Marwadi completely reverses English baseline... this cannot be explained linguistically (Marwadi is similar to Hindi) and directly reflects stereotype-driven behavioral scripts."
  - [section 3.3] Explicit hypothesis P3 (Stereotype Activation) citing Timberg (1978) on Marwari trader stereotypes.
  - [corpus] No direct neighbor evidence; stereotype bias in LLMs is well-documented generally (Bolukbasi et al. cited) but not specifically for negotiation.
- **Break condition:** Using neutral geographic labels (e.g., "Region X") instead of culturally-associated ones removes the extreme reversal effect.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Negotiation Game Taxonomies (Distributive vs. Integrative)**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's core finding is task-contingency; understanding the structural difference between zero-sum (Ultimatum, Buy-Sell) and positive-sum (Resource Exchange) games is essential to interpret why language effects diverge.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might a language that reduces agreement in a distributive game increase trade volume in an integrative game?

- **Concept: LLM Persona Conditioning**
  - *Why needed here:* The experiment manipulates behavior via a simple "You speak only in [language]" prompt. Recognizing this as a lightweight behavioral prime (akin to role-play) is key to understanding the mechanism as "latent policy prior" activation.
  - *Quick check question:* How does a persona prompt differ from fine-tuning, and what does that imply about the nature of the observed effects?

- **Concept: Training Data Bias & Representation**
  - *Why needed here:* All mechanisms trace back to biased corpora. The learner must connect macro-level data composition (English-centric, stereotype-laden) to micro-level negotiation outputs.
  - *Quick check question:* If the effect is due to training data, what would you predict happens if you evaluate a model trained exclusively on balanced, culturally-neutral negotiation transcripts?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Game configurations -> Prompt templates -> NegotiationArena framework -> Model API wrappers -> Logging & parser -> Analysis module
- **Critical path:**
  1. Replicate baseline English condition for all three games to establish within-study reference.
  2. Introduce one non-English language (e.g., Hindi) and re-run all games, keeping everything else identical.
  3. Compare key metrics (acceptance rate in Ultimatum, seller advantage in Buy-Sell, trade volume in Resource Exchange) between English and Hindi runs.
  4. Expand to all four Indic languages and all model pairs, ensuring 10 runs per combination as in the paper.
  5. Generate heatmaps and statistical summaries to verify pattern consistency (e.g., Marwadi seller advantage > English).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Controlled abstraction vs. ecological validity:* Using simple games and persona prompts isolates language but loses real-world complexity (long-term relationships, code-switching).
  - *Multilingual cost:* Running thousands of API calls across 5 languages × 4 models × 3 games × 10 runs is expensive; must balance replication fidelity with budget.
  - *Metric selection:* The paper uses role-specific advantages (seller/buyer) which are game-dependent; onboarding must clarify metric definitions per game to avoid misanalysis.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - *Model refusal/degradation:* Weaker models (GPT-3.5) may fail to follow non-English prompts or produce incoherent dialogues, leading to high variance or missing data.
  - *No effect observed:* If language prompts do not change outcomes, likely causes are: (a) model is instruction-tuned to ignore persona cues, (b) prompts are not strong enough, (c) game incentive structure is too dominant.
  - *Opposite effect direction:* If Hindi increases acceptance instead of decreasing, it may indicate the specific model's training data has different cross-lingual biases.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **English Baseline Replication:** Run 10 iterations of each game (Ultimatum, Buy-Sell, Resource Exchange) with all model pairs using English prompts. Verify metrics match paper's baseline numbers (e.g., ~98% Ultimatum acceptance, ~6.9 seller advantage in Buy-Sell).
  2. **Single-Language Contrast:** Add Hindi prompts only. Check if Hindi reduces Ultimatum acceptance and lowers initial offers as predicted.
  3. **Cross-Model Consistency Check:** In Buy-Sell, verify that Marwadi increases seller advantage *for all model pairs*, not just one. If only GPT-4o shows the effect, it may be a model artifact, not a linguistic one.

## Limitations
- Ecological validity: Effects are elicited via minimal synthetic persona prompts; it's unclear if shifts persist in complex open-ended negotiations or with native speakers.
- Causal ambiguity in Marwadi effect: The reversal cannot be explained by linguistic pragmatics alone, but the study cannot cleanly disentangle "cultural stereotype" from "coded discourse patterns" specific to Marwadi-associated texts.
- Indic language heterogeneity: The four Indic languages are grouped analytically, but Marwadi (a dialect/regional identity) produces outlier effects, suggesting the "Indic" category may mask significant internal variation in training data representation.

## Confidence
- **Language > Model Architecture:** High
- **Task-contingent effects (destabilizing distributive, enhancing integrative):** High
- **Stereotype activation (Marwadi reversal):** Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Baseline Replication & Prompt Adherence Audit:** Run the exact English baseline for all three games to verify within-study reference metrics (e.g., ~98% Ultimatum acceptance). Concurrently, measure non-English prompt adherence rates (e.g., % non-English tokens generated) to confirm the manipulation was followed.
2. **Pragmatic vs. Stereotype Disambiguation:** For the Marwadi condition, replace the culturally-associated label with a neutral geographic label (e.g., "Region X") while maintaining the Marwadi language prompt. If the seller advantage effect disappears, it confirms stereotype activation; if it persists, it points to language-specific discourse patterns.
3. **Cross-Model Generalization of Hindi Effect:** Specifically verify the predicted Hindi destabilization pattern (lower Ultimatum acceptance, lower initial offers) across *all* tested model pairs (GPT-4o, GPT-3.5, Claude models). Failure for any model pair would indicate model-specific instruction-following or training-data biases that moderate the core linguistic effect.
