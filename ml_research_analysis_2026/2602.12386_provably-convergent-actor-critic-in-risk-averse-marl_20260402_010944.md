# Provably Convergent Actor-Critic in Risk-averse MARL

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.12386
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12386
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Introduces a two-timescale Actor-Critic algorithm that provably converges to Risk-averse Quantal-response Equilibria in infinite-horizon general-sum Markov games under (µ, λ)-strong monotonicity, with finite-sample guarantees and empirical superiority over risk-neutral baselines.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the computational intractability of Nash equilibria in infinite-horizon general-sum Markov games by proposing a risk-averse solution concept, the Risk-averse Quantal-response Equilibrium (RQE). It designs a two-timescale Actor-Critic algorithm—fast policy updates and slow critic updates—and proves global convergence with finite-sample bounds under a strong monotonicity assumption on the stage game. Experiments on an inspection game, a gridworld cooperation task, and Simple Tag show faster, more stable convergence and better final performance compared to risk-neutral methods.

## Method Summary
The method transforms each agent’s problem into a four-player zero-sum-like game by introducing adversarial opponents that perturb opponent actions. This recasting induces (µ, λ)-strong monotonicity, guaranteeing a unique RQE. The algorithm performs fast gradient updates on policies and adversaries (actor) and slow stochastic updates on Q-functions (critic), leveraging the contraction of the risk-averse Bellman operator. Practical implementation uses MLPs for policies and Q-networks, a replay buffer, target networks, and convex regularizers (e.g., KL-divergence, negative entropy) to satisfy theoretical assumptions. Two-timescale separation (α ≪ β) is critical for convergence.

## Key Results
- The algorithm achieves global convergence with finite-sample guarantees; constant stepsizes yield linear convergence, while diminishing stepsizes yield sublinear rates.
- On inspection, gridworld, and Simple Tag environments, the risk-averse approach converges faster and more stably across multiple seeds than risk-neutral baselines.
- Final performance is consistently better, demonstrating the practical benefit of risk-averse equilibrium computation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Recasting the two-player general-sum Markov game as a four-player zero-sum-like structure (original agents plus adversarial opponents) induces strong monotonicity in the stage game, ensuring a unique Risk-averse Quantal-Response Equilibrium (RQEq) that is tractable to learn.
- **Mechanism:** For each agent *i*, introduce an adversary choosing a distribution *p_i* over the opponent’s actions. The agent’s objective becomes  
  `min_{π_i} max_{p_i} [ -π_i^T R_i p_i - (1/τ_i) D_i(p_i, π_{-i}) + ϵ_i ν_i(π_i) ]`,  
  where `D_i` is a penalty and `ν_i` a convex regularizer (e.g., entropy). The four-player gradient operator `F` is (µ, λ)-strongly monotone under conditions on `D_i` and `ν_i` (Theorem 3.2). Nash equilibria of this four-player game bijectively correspond to RQEs of the original game (Proposition 2.2).
- **Core assumption:** The stage game (for every state) is (µ, λ)-strongly monotone (Assumption 4.1). For instance, with KL‑divergence and log‑barrier regularizers, this requires `16ϵ1ϵ2τ1τ2 > 1` (Theorem 3.2).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “We demonstrate that RQE possesses strong regularity conditions that make it uniquely amenable to learning in MGs.”  
  - [section 2.1] Equation (6) defines the four‑player game; Proposition 2.2 links its Nash equilibria to RQE.  
  - [section 3] Theorem 3.2 gives sufficient conditions for (µ, λ)-strong monotonicity.  
  - [corpus] Paper 152108 (Mazumdar et al., 2024) uses risk aversion to achieve tractable equilibrium computation in normal‑form games, supporting the idea that risk aversion regularizes the game. The current paper extends to infinite‑horizon MGs and provides a provably convergent actor‑critic.
- **Break condition:** If `ϵ_i` or `τ_i` are too small, the monotonicity condition fails (e.g., `16ϵ1ϵ2τ1τ2 ≤ 1` with KL/log‑barrier). This may lead to non‑unique RQE or loss of contraction, causing divergence or convergence to an incorrect equilibrium.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Under the strong monotonicity of the stage game, the risk‑averse quantal‑response Bellman optimality operator `T` is a contraction with respect to the max‑norm, enabling iterative solution.
- **Mechanism:** `T` (Definition 2.5) maps a joint Q‑function to another via `TQ = -r + γ E[RQE(-Q(·,·))]`. The proof (Proposition 4.2) bounds `∥TQ - TQ'∥` using the Lipschitz continuity of RQE (Theorem 3.1) and the monotonicity of the stage game. For KL‑divergence/log‑barrier, bounded Q is required; for Lipschitz metrics `D_i`, a simpler condition on γ suffices.
- **Core assumption:** Assumption 4.1 (stage game strongly monotone) and smoothness of `D_i`, `ν_i` (Assumption 4.3). Discount factor `γ` must be below a threshold `γ_0 < 1`.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 2.2] Proposition 2.6 shows that fixed points of `T` correspond to RQEs.  
  - [section 4.1] Proposition 4.2: “Under Assumption 4.1… the Bellman optimality operator `T` is a `γ_0`‑contraction mapping.”  
  - [section G.1 (Appendix)] Detailed proof and threshold conditions.
- **Break condition:** If `γ` exceeds the threshold, `T` may not be a contraction. Also, unbounded Q (for KL case) or non‑smooth regularizers violate the contraction guarantee.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A two‑timescale Actor‑Critic algorithm with a **fast** policy update and a **slow** Q‑function update converges to the RQE with finite‑sample guarantees under the stage‑game monotonicity, mixing assumptions, and smooth regularizers.
- **Mechanism:**  
  - Actor/adversary update: `z_{t+1} = Proj( z_t - β_t Λ F(z_t; -Q_t) )` (preconditioned gradient descent on the four‑player objective for each state, using current Q).  
  - Critic update: `Qt+1 = (1-α_t) Qt + α_t T_{z_{t+1}} Qt` (stochastic Bellman update from sampled transitions).  
  The separation `α_t << β_t` ensures the policy tracks the quasi‑stationary RQE of the four‑player game given the current Q, while Q converges to the fixed point of `T`. Coupled Lyapunov drift analysis (Theorem 4.7) yields linear convergence for constant stepsizes and sublinear for diminishing stepsizes.
- **Core assumption:** Assumptions 4.1, 4.3 (smoothness), 4.5 (Markov chain mixing and lower‑bounded stationary distribution), 4.6 (bounded exploration). Stepsizes satisfy `α_t << β_t` and `β_t ≤ min{1/µ, µ/L_F^2}`.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “We propose a novel two‑timescale Actor‑Critic algorithm… provably convergent.”  
  - [section 4.2] Deterministic iteration (17) and Theorem 4.4.  
  - [section 4.3] Stochastic Algorithm 1/2 and Theorem 4.7 with finite‑sample bounds.  
  - [section H (Appendix)] Lyapunov drift inequalities.
- **Break condition:** Violating `α_t << β_t` (e.g., `β_t` too small or `α_t` too large) breaks the analysis. Insufficient mixing (Assumption 4.5) or exploration (Assumption 4.6) biases stochastic estimates. Poorly chosen `τ_i`, `ϵ_i` may violate monotonicity, preventing convergence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Markov games (discounted infinite‑horizon general‑sum):** Why needed: The problem setting. Quick check: What distinguishes a Markov game from a Markov decision process? (Answer: Multiple agents with possibly misaligned incentives; the transition and reward depend on joint actions.)
- **Actor‑Critic methods:** Why needed: The algorithm is a two‑timescale actor‑critic. Quick check: In traditional actor‑critic, which component typically updates faster, and why? (Answer: Critic updates faster to provide accurate value estimates for policy improvement.)
- **Risk‑averse decision making (convex risk measures):** Why needed: RQE incorporates risk aversion against opponent’s actions via the penalty `D_i`. Quick check: How does a convex risk measure differ from expected utility? How is it expressed in the objective? (Answer: It is a minimax over a set of distributions, yielding an extra regularization term.)
- **Quantal Response Equilibrium (QRE) and bounded rationality:** Why needed: RQE is a risk‑averse QRE, combining risk aversion and bounded rationality (via `ν_i`). Quick check: What does the temperature parameter control in QRE? (Answer: The degree of rationality; lower temperature reduces stochasticity.)
- **Monotonicity in games (λ‑monotone, strongly monotone):** Why needed: Convergence relies on the stage game being (µ, λ)-strongly monotone (Assumption 4.1). Quick check: What does strong monotonicity imply about the game’s equilibrium and gradient dynamics? (Answer: Unique equilibrium and linear convergence of gradient‑based methods.)
- **Two‑timescale stochastic approximation:** Why needed: The algorithm uses two learning rates `α_t` (critic) and `β_t` (actor) with `α_t << β_t`. Quick check: Why is a two‑timescale separation necessary in this actor‑critic? (Answer: To exploit the contraction of the Bellman operator while tracking the moving RQE via fast policy updates.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Replay Buffer -> Critic Networks -> Actor Networks & Adversary Networks. Target Networks are updated from Critic Networks.

**Critical Path:**
1. Initialize all networks and replay buffer.
2. For each episode: interact with environment using current policies to collect K transitions.
3. Sample a batch B from replay buffer.
4. Update actors/adversaries by minimizing losses in Equation 25 (uses current Q and regularizers).
5. Update critics by minimizing loss in Equation 26 using target networks to compute Bellman targets (Equation 27).
6. Soft‑update target networks: `ϕ_i^{target} ← (1-α_t) ϕ_i^{target} + α_t ϕ_i`.
7. Repeat until convergence.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Fast actor / slow critic:** Contrary to standard actor‑critic, actor updates faster. This is necessary because the policy gradient is replaced by exploiting the Bellman operator’s contraction. The trade‑off is that actor steps must be large enough to track the moving RQE.
- **Regularizer choice:** Theory requires `D_i` to be a metric and `ν_i` strongly convex. The paper uses KL/negative entropy for numerical stability, though log‑barrier (for KL) is theoretically also covered but less stable.
- **On‑policy vs. off‑policy:** On‑policy is simpler but sample‑inefficient; off‑policy reuses data but requires mixing (Assumption 4.5) and bounded exploration (Assumption 4.6) to avoid bias.
- **Network architecture:** MLP with 2 hidden layers and ReLU activations – simple and aligns with theoretical bounds, but deeper networks might improve empirical performance at the cost of tighter analysis.

**Failure Signatures:**
- **Diverging Q values:** If `α_t` is too large or mixing is poor (Assumption 4.5 violated), critic may explode. Monitor max‑norm of Q.
- **Policy collapse:** If `ϵ_i` is too small or regularizer ineffective, policies become deterministic, breaking exploration assumptions. Check policy entropy.
- **High variance across seeds:** Indicates unstable training; possible causes: insufficient risk‑aversion (`τ_i` too small), step‑size mismatch (`α_t` not sufficiently smaller than `β_t`), or poor network initialization.
- **Failure to cooperate:** In gridworld or Simple Tag, risk‑neutral agents often converge to mutual defection; risk‑averse agents should learn cooperation. If not, verify `τ_i, ϵ_i` meet monotonicity condition (e.g., `16ϵ1ϵ2τ1τ2 > 1`).

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Inspection game** (normal‑form): Shows risk‑averse gradient descent converges while risk‑neutral does not, validating the equilibrium concept.
2. **Gridworld cooperation game:** 5×5 grid with two agents; mutual cooperation yields higher reward but defection is tempting. Risk‑averse training converges faster and more stably than risk‑neutral across 10 seeds.
3. **MPE Simple Tag:** Predator‑prey environment with 1 good (fixed) and 2 adversary agents. Risk‑averse training gives more consistent learning curves and comparable final performance to risk‑neutral.

## Limitations
- Theoretical convergence relies on restrictive (µ, λ)-strong monotonicity condition that may not hold in many practical MARL environments.
- Implementation details for deep RL (network architectures, projection stability) are under‑specified, hindering reproducibility.
- Empirical validation is limited to three simple tasks; generalizability to complex benchmarks is uncertain.

## Confidence
- Theoretical convergence under strong monotonicity: High
- Equivalence between four‑player Nash and RQE: High
- Contraction of Bellman operator T: High
- Empirical superiority over risk‑neutral baselines: Medium
- General applicability to arbitrary MARL: Low
