# The Rank and Gradient Lost in Non-stationarity: Sample Weight Decay for Mitigating Plasticity Loss in Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.01913
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01913
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Sample Weight Decay (SWD) consistently improves RL learning performance, achieving Interquartile Mean (IQM) gains of 13.7%–30.1% over uniform sampling and state‑of‑the‑art results on DeepMind Control Suite Humanoid tasks.

## Executive Summary
Plasticity loss—the decline in a neural network’s capacity to learn from new data—is a critical challenge in deep reinforcement learning (RL) due to non‑stationary data distributions and bootstrapping‑induced target shifts. This paper identifies two root causes: rank collapse in the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) Gram matrix and a Θ(1/k) decay in gradient magnitude as training progresses. To counteract gradient attenuation, the authors propose Sample Weight Decay (SWD), a lightweight wrapper around experience replay that assigns linearly decreasing weights to older samples, thereby preserving strong gradients from recent experiences.

Extensive experiments on MuJoCo, ALE, and DeepMind Control Suite with TD3, Double DQN, and SAC demonstrate that SWD yields substantial and consistent improvements. Across tasks it achieves Interquartile Mean (IQM) gains of 13.7%–30.1% over uniform sampling, attains state‑of‑the‑art results on DMC Humanoid, and reduces gradient sparsity as measured by the GraMa metric. The method is orthogonal to other plasticity‑preserving techniques and offers a practical, robust solution for off‑policy RL.

## Method Summary
Sample Weight Decay (SWD) modifies the sampling distribution of an experience replay buffer to prioritize recent transitions. Each stored transition is annotated with the timestep at which it was collected. When a batch is needed, the age of a sample is computed as the difference between the current global timestep and its stored timestep. The weight assigned to the sample is `w = max(w_min, 1 - age / T)`, where `T` is a decay horizon and `w_min` a minimum weight. The sampling probability is proportional to `w`. By default, samples with age >> T receive the minimum weight, while those within the horizon receive linearly decreasing weights from 1 (age 0) to 0 (age T). This schedule counters the Θ(1/k) gradient decay caused by uniform mixing of all past data. SWD is algorithm‑agnostic; for large buffers, an approximate bucket‑based sampler with median weights reduces complexity from O(N) to O(B).

## Key Results
- SWD improves interquartile mean (IQM) performance across MuJoCo, ALE, and DMC tasks by 13.7%–30.1% compared to uniform sampling.
- It achieves state‑of‑the‑art results on challenging DeepMind Control Suite Humanoid tasks.
- SWD reduces gradient sparsity (GraMa) and remains effective across different update‑to‑data (UTD) ratios and in combination with other plasticity‑preserving methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Rank Collapse of the NTK Gram Matrix
- **Claim**: Training data non‑stationarity and bootstrapping‑induced target shifts cause rank collapse in the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) Gram matrix.
- **Mechanism**: In RL, each optimization step starts from the previous iterate rather than a random initialization. This violates the random‑initialization assumption required for a full‑rank NTK with high probability. As training advances, the Gram matrix formed by NTK features becomes rank‑deficient, limiting the network’s expressive capacity to approximate new value functions.
- **Core assumption**: Over‑parameterized networks rely on a well‑conditioned NTK for efficient convergence; non‑stationary data distributions degrade this conditioning.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions rank collapse; Section 4.1 discusses the violation of random initialization; indirect corpus support (survey, representation collapse papers).
- **Break condition**: Stationary data or periodic random re‑initialization can restore NTK rank.

### Mechanism 2: Θ(1/k) Gradient Magnitude Decay
- **Claim**: The magnitude of the gradient decays as Θ(1/k) with training iteration k.
- **Mechanism**: The replay buffer contains data distributed according to a mixture of past policy distributions. The recursive mixing formula (Proposition 1) shows that the gradient at iteration k is proportional to 1/k times the gradient under the current policy distribution, plus higher‑order terms. As k grows, the contribution from the current distribution shrinks, leading to an attenuated learning signal.
- **Core assumption**: Off‑policy RL buffers accumulate all past transitions; the mixing induces the 1/k factor.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states Θ(1/k) decay; Section 4.2 (Theorem 3) derives the scaling.
- **Break condition**: Resetting the replay buffer or reweighting to favor recent samples (as SWD does) mitigates the decay.

### Mechanism 3: Sample Weight Decay Preserves Gradient Magnitude
- **Claim**: SWD counteracts gradient attenuation by assigning linearly decreasing weights to older samples, thus maintaining effective gradient magnitude.
- **Mechanism**: By increasing the sampling probability of recent transitions, SWD reduces the mixing of outdated data that contributes a 1/k factor. The weighted sampling effectively re‑weights the empirical distribution closer to the current policy’s distribution, preserving the signal‑to‑noise ratio of gradients.
- **Core assumption**: The gradient decay arises from uniform sampling of all past data; reweighting can restore the original scale.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract describes linearly increasing weights for recent samples; Section 5 (Algorithm 1) defines the linear weighting and claims it maintains gradient magnitude; empirical results support the claim.
- **Break condition**: If older data becomes more valuable (e.g., recurring tasks) or if the weight schedule is poorly tuned (e.g., T too small or w_min too low), performance may degrade.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Environment Interaction → Replay Buffer (with timestamps) → SWD Sampler (computes age, weight, sampling probabilities) → Training Update (TD3/DQN/SAC)

**Critical path**:
1. Collect transition (s, a, r, s′, done) at global timestep t; store in buffer with timestamp t.
2. When sampling, compute age = current_t − sample_t for each candidate.
3. Compute weight w = max(w_min, 1 − age / T).
4. Normalize to probabilities p_i = w_i / Σ w.
5. Sample batch according to p_i.
6. Pass batch to the RL algorithm’s update step.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **T (decay horizon)**: Larger T retains older data longer (more diversity) but weakens recency emphasis; smaller T accelerates forgetting but may discard valuable experience.
- **w_min (minimum weight)**: Prevents complete elimination of old samples. Too high → near‑uniform sampling; too low → high variance if recent samples are noisy.
- **Bucket approximation**: For large buffers, partition into B chronological buckets and use median bucket weight, reducing O(N) to O(B) cost at the expense of some accuracy.

**Failure signatures**:
- Performance drops on stationary/slowly varying tasks where past data remains valuable.
- High variance in returns when recent samples are noisy and w_min is too low.
- Instability if T is extremely small, effectively discarding most replay data.
- No improvement on tasks where NTK rank collapse is the dominant cause of plasticity loss.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Reverse weighting (SWA)**: Higher weights for older samples caused worse performance, lower gradient norms, and higher GraMa, confirming the benefit of recency.
2. **Comparison with PER**: PER (TD‑error based) underperformed SWD, indicating that temporal weighting specifically addresses gradient decay.
3. **UTD scaling**: Increasing update‑to‑data ratio (1→2→5) amplified SWD gains (up to +30.1% on Humanoid Run), showing robustness to frequent updates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Unified theory**: Can the framework be extended to jointly characterize NTK rank collapse and gradient attenuation, including their interaction?
- **Real‑world applicability**: Does SWD maintain benefits on complex real‑world tasks with sparse rewards and high‑dimensional observations?
- **Adaptive weighting**: Can adaptive schemes (e.g., based on gradient statistics or policy stability) improve upon the fixed linear decay schedule?
- **Combination guarantees**: What are the theoretical limits and guarantees when combining SWD with other plasticity‑preserving methods?

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (infinite‑width NTK, exact distribution mixing) may not hold for finite networks; NTK rank collapse is inferred indirectly.
- Omitted implementation details (SimBa specifics, GraMa threshold, evaluation protocol) hinder replication and may hide interactions with other methods.
- SWD’s fixed linear schedule may be suboptimal in non‑stationary environments; older data may be undervalued in recurring/lifelong tasks.
