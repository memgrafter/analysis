# Understanding the Dynamics of Demonstration Conflict in In-Context Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.04464
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04464
- Reference count: 27
- Primary result: Single-demonstration corruption in in-context rule inference causes up to 58 percentage point accuracy degradation, revealing systematic misleading and a two-phase internal structure where rule encoding and resolution occur in separate layer ranges.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) handle conflicting demonstrations within in-context learning (ICL). By corrupting a single demonstration among a majority of correct ones, the authors observe dramatic accuracy drops (up to 58 percentage points), indicating that models can be systematically misled by minority conflicts. Through linear probes and logit lens, they uncover a two-phase computational structure: early-to-middle layers encode both correct and corrupted rules without strong preference, while late layers develop confident predictions. They identify two classes of attention heads—Vulnerability Heads (early) with positional bias and high corruption sensitivity, and Susceptible Heads (late) that reduce support for the correct rule under corruption. Targeted ablation of a small number of these heads recovers substantial performance improvements (over 10% relative) and reduces positional bias. Moreover, Susceptible Heads show cross-task generalization, improving another task when ablated. Entropy analysis further shows that conflicts increase reasoning uncertainty, which these interventions partially alleviate.

## Method Summary
The study employs two synthetic tasks—Operator Induction (inferring a mathematical operator from digit calculations) and Fake Word Inference (mapping synthetic words to colors/objects)—across four small instructed LLMs: Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-4B, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. The core corruption framework replaces one demonstration’s output with a conflicting rule while keeping the majority correct, varying the number of demonstrations (4/6/8) and the position of the corrupted example. Analyses include: (1) linear probes on residual streams to detect rule representations layer by layer; (2) logit lens to decode token probabilities from intermediate layers; (3) scoring attention heads: Vulnerability Score = attention allocation from the query forerunner token to a demonstration position × sensitivity when that position is corrupted; Susceptibility Score = reduction in a head’s contribution to the correct rule’s logit under corruption; and (4) targeted ablation of top-scoring heads during corrupted inference to measure causal impact on accuracy and bias.

## Key Results
- Single-demonstration corruption yields accuracy degradation up to 58 percentage points, demonstrating strong sensitivity to conflicting evidence.
- Both correct and corrupted rules are encoded in mid-layer representations, but confident predictions for one rule emerge only in late layers, confirming a two-phase processing structure.
- Vulnerability Heads (early-to-middle) and Susceptible Heads (late) mediate the degradation; ablating a small number (5–10) recovers >10% relative performance, reduces positional bias, and Susceptible Heads generalize across tasks, improving another task by 3.80%.
- Reasoning entropy increases under corruption, partially alleviated by head ablation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The authors propose three interrelated mechanisms that explain how conflicting demonstrations lead to systematic misleading and how targeted interventions mitigate it.

**Mechanism 1: Two-phase computational structure.** The model processes conflicting demonstrations via a temporally separated two-phase process. In the first phase (early-to-middle layers), the residual stream encodes multiple competing rules (correct and corrupted) without strong preference; both are detectable by linear probes. In the second phase (late layers), the model develops confident predictions for specific rules, revealing the conflict resolution stage. The core assumption is that the separation between representation and decision indicates functional specialization across layers. Evidence includes abstract statements, linear probe confidence for both rules in mid-layers under corruption (Section 4.1), and logit lens showing minimal early confidence but sharp emergence of competing confidences in late layers (Section 4.2). The hypothesis would be falsified if future work showed simultaneous encoding and confidence across all layers.

**Mechanism 2: Vulnerability Heads create positional biases.** Early-to-middle attention heads, termed Vulnerability Heads, exhibit two key properties: they allocate disproportionate attention to certain demonstration positions (positional bias), and their outputs change dramatically when the heavily-attended position contains a corrupted rule. The product of attention allocation and corruption sensitivity defines a Vulnerability Score; heads with high scores cluster in early-to-middle layers. Causal ablation of these heads (top 5–10) improves performance by up to 11.12% relative, confirming their role in introducing vulnerability. Moreover, ablation reduces the variance of accuracy degradation across positions (Table 4), linking positional bias directly to corruption sensitivity. The mechanism assumes that positional bias causes non-uniform injection of corrupted information into subsequent processing. If the correlation between positional attention and degradation vanishes under position permutation, or if ablation does not improve robustness, this mechanism would be invalidated.

**Mechanism 3: Susceptible Heads drive resolution failure.** Late-layer attention heads, called Susceptible Heads, significantly reduce their contribution to the correct rule’s logit when a corrupted demonstration is present, despite majority support for the correct rule. The Susceptibility Score measures this drop; high-scoring heads concentrate in late layers. Ablating them yields up to 10.33% relative improvement, proving causal involvement. Cross-task analysis reveals overlap in top Susceptible Heads (10 out of 20 shared between tasks) and cross-task ablation benefits (3.80% improvement in another task), suggesting a general conflict-resolution mechanism. Ablating Vulnerability Heads also lowers susceptibility scores of Susceptible Heads, indicating an empirical synergy—likely that the biased inputs from Vulnerability Heads shape the context that Susceptible Heads process. If susceptibility scores do not causally affect degradation or if late-layer concentration is an artifact of the probing method, this mechanism would be challenged.

## Foundational Learning
- **Mechanistic Interpretability Probes (Linear Probing)**  
  Why needed here: To track when and where specific rule information (correct or corrupted) is encoded in the model’s internal representations across layers.  
  Quick check: If a linear probe trained to detect “operator +” shows high confidence on representations from layer 10 but low on layer 25 for the same input, what does that suggest about when the model encodes that information?

- **Logit Lens Analysis**  
  Why needed here: To decode the model’s internal predictions (token probabilities) from intermediate layers and observe the emergence of confidence for competing rules, distinguishing representation from decision.  
  Quick check: If logit lens shows strong probability for both rule A and rule B at layer 28 but only rule A at layer 35, which phase of the two-stage hypothesis does this illustrate?

- **Attention Head Ablation as Causal Test**  
  Why needed here: To go beyond correlation and establish that specific heads *cause* vulnerability or susceptibility. Masking a head’s output tests its necessity in the computational path.  
  Quick check: Why is it insufficient to only show that Vulnerability Heads attend heavily to position 3? What additional experiment is needed to claim they *cause* the corruption effect at that position?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Input (demonstrations + query) -> Embedding -> Early-to-Middle Layers (integrate evidence with positional bias; contain Vulnerability Heads) -> Late Layers (resolve conflict; contain Susceptible Heads) -> Residual Stream at Query Forerunner Token -> Unembedding Matrix -> Output probabilities.

**Critical Path**
1. **Input Processing:** Demonstrations and query are tokenized and embedded.
2. **Evidence Integration (Early/Middle):** Information from demonstrations is integrated via attention, including positional biases. Both correct and corrupted rules become encoded in the residual stream (probe-detectable).
3. **Conflict Resolution (Late):** The model’s final “opinion” on the rule is formed. Susceptible Heads’ contributions determine if the correct or corrupted rule dominates the final logits.
4. **Output Generation:** The model samples/predicts based on final-layer logits.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- Specialization vs Redundancy: A small subset of heads (Vulnerability and Susceptible) accounts for large effects, indicating functional specialization; however, other heads partially compensate, so ablation does not fully restore performance.
- Interpretability vs Performance: Identified heads are specific failure points under conflict, not the general ICL engine. Many heads remain necessary for overall capability; random ablation harms performance, highlighting that interpretability findings isolate only part of the system.
- Task-Specific vs General Mechanisms: Vulnerability Heads show low cross-task overlap, suggesting their positional biases are tied to task-specific formatting or content. Susceptible Heads show high overlap, pointing to a more general resolution mechanism.

**Failure Signatures**
- Systematic Misleading: The corrupted rule appears in the majority of wrong predictions (e.g., 81%) despite being in the minority.
- Positional Bias in Degradation: Corruption at different positions causes markedly different accuracy drops (e.g., 40% at position 1 vs 15% at position 4).
- High Next-Token Entropy: Under corruption, the model’s reasoning (chain-of-thought) becomes more uncertain, partially alleviated by head ablation.

**First 3 Experiments**
1. Replicate Corruption Framework: Run the single-position corruption on a held-out task (e.g., a new operator induction variant) to confirm the performance degradation pattern and positional bias hold for your target model.
2. Probe Validation: Train linear probes on your model’s representations for clean vs corruption scenarios to verify the “both rules encoded in mid-layers” finding. Ensure your prompts elicit rule representation, not just final answer calculation.
3. Head Identification Pipeline: For your model, compute Vulnerability Scores (attention allocation × sensitivity) and Susceptibility Scores (logit contribution change under corruption). Visualize their layer distributions to confirm the stratified pattern (early for vulnerability, late for susceptibility) before proceeding to ablation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why are Vulnerability Heads less cross-task generalizable than Susceptible Heads?** The paper finds only 4 of the top 20 Vulnerability Heads overlap between Operator Induction and Fake Word Inference, compared to 10/20 for Susceptible Heads. The underlying reason—such as whether early-layer positional attention patterns are more tied to task-specific formatting or semantic content—is not investigated.
2. **What is the causal mechanism linking Vulnerability Heads and Susceptible Heads?** Ablating Vulnerability Heads reduces susceptibility scores of Susceptible Heads, suggesting synergy, but the information flow (feedforward, feedback, or mediated by representations) remains unknown.
3. **How does removing Vulnerability Heads reduce positional bias in corruption sensitivity?** The paper attributes the bias to disproportionate attention from Vulnerability Heads but does not directly measure whether ablation equalizes attention distribution across heads or positions.
4. **Do the two-phase structure and identified head roles generalize to more complex tasks?** The study uses simple, modular demonstrations; it is unclear whether similar dynamics appear in tasks requiring multi-step reasoning, interdependent demonstrations, or longer contexts.

## Limitations
- Generalizability: Findings rely on 2–4B parameter instructed models and two synthetic tasks; it is uncertain whether they scale to larger LLMs or natural ICL scenarios. Neighboring literature offers only weak corroboration.
- Implementation ambiguity: Details of probe training (sample counts, regularization, optimizer), head scoring thresholds, and masking procedures are underspecified, risking reproduction drift and limiting confidence.

## Confidence
- Two-phase structure: Medium
- Vulnerability Heads: Medium
- Susceptible Heads: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Phase boundary intervention**: Patch mid-layer representations (where both rules are probe-detectable) into late layers of corrupted prompts; if prediction confidence shifts prematurely, it challenges strict temporal separation.
2. **Positional bias causality test**: Systematically permute demonstration positions while measuring corruption degradation and attention allocation per head; if degradation no longer correlates with attention bias after permutation, re-evaluate the positional mechanism.
3. **Cross-architecture transfer**: Replicate vulnerability/susceptibility head identification and ablation on a decoder-only model without instruction tuning (e.g., GPT-2) and on a reasoning-heavy task (e.g., GSM8K with misleading examples); failure to recover performance would limit scope of claimed generalizability.
