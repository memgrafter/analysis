# Duration Aware Scheduling for ASR Serving Under Workload Drift
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11273  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11273  
- **Reference count:** 21  
- **Primary result:** Audio duration is an accurate, zero-overhead proxy for Whisper job length; SJF reduces median latency up to 73% but increases tail up to 97%, while HRRN reduces median up to 28% and bounds tail degradation to ≤24% under drifted workloads.

## Executive Summary
First-come-first-served (FCFS) scheduling in ASR serving ignores request duration variability, leading to head-of-line blocking and increased end-to-end latency, especially under workload drift. This paper shows that audio duration is an accurate, zero-overhead proxy for processing time in Whisper models. By integrating two classical duration-aware schedulers—Shortest Job First (SJF) and Highest Response Ratio Next (HRRN)—into the vLLM engine, the authors achieve significant latency reductions: SJF drastically lowers median latency but causes severe tail degradation due to starvation, whereas HRRN provides a favorable trade-off with bounded tail impact. These gains persist under drifted workloads, incur no throughput penalty, and add negligible overhead.

## Method Summary
The method exploits the linear correlation between audio duration and output token count in Whisper’s decoder-dominated execution. The estimated output tokens are computed as `duration × κ`, where `κ` is a language-specific constant. This estimate is injected into vLLM’s request path at the API endpoint and propagated via `SamplingParams.extra_args`. Custom request queues implement SJF (min-heap by estimated time) and HRRN (priority = `(wait + est_time)/est_time`), with preemption logic adjusted accordingly. Evaluation uses LibriSpeech test-clean (right-skewed durations) and a synthetically created uniform-duration split, under Poisson arrivals (burstiness=1.0) at rates 1–25 req/s on a single A100 GPU with `whisper-large-v3`. Metrics include end-to-end latency (E2EL) percentiles and throughput.

## Key Results
- SJF reduced median E2EL by up to 73% but increased 90th-percentile tail latency by up to 97% due to starvation.
- HRRN reduced median E2EL by up to 28% while bounding tail-latency degradation to at most 24%.
- Gains persisted under drifted workloads with uniform duration distribution, incurred no throughput penalty, and added <0.1 ms scheduling overhead per request.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Audio duration serves as a reliable proxy for processing time because Whisper’s decoding cost scales linearly with output tokens, which in turn correlate linearly with input audio duration. This zero-overhead signal enables effective job-length ordering. SJF exploits this by always selecting the shortest estimated job, minimizing average wait times but causing long jobs to starve under heavy, skewed loads, thus exploding tail latency. HRRN mitigates starvation by dynamically aging waiting jobs: its response ratio (`(waiting time + estimated time)/estimated time`) ensures long-waiting jobs eventually gain priority, reducing median latency while bounding tail degradation. Both policies’ effectiveness hinges on the stability of the duration-token linear relationship and the presence of sufficient duration variability in the workload.

## Architecture Onboarding
Request arrival -> API handler computes audio duration -> Request object initialized with `estimated_output_tokens` -> Scheduler’s custom `RequestQueue` (SJF/HRRN) uses estimate for priority -> Worker selected for execution (preemption may occur).  
The critical path requires the duration estimate to flow seamlessly from the endpoint through the scheduler without format changes.  
**Design tradeoffs:** (1) Used duration (zero overhead) vs. learned predictor (accuracy vs. cost). (2) Median vs. tail: SJF optimizes median; HRRN balances via aging. (3) Preemption cost vs. queueing gain. (4) Fixed `κ` simplicity vs. multilingual adaptability.  
**Failure signatures:** 1) Starvation tail explosion (P90 ↑>50% vs. FCFS) → likely SJF on skewed workload. 2) No gain → duration correlation broken (check `R²`). 3) Throughput drop → scheduler lock contention or GPU underutilization. 4) Gains collapse under bursty arrivals → HRRN more robust.  
**First 3 experiments:** 1) Validate duration→token correlation: plot actual tokens vs. duration; ensure `R² ≥ 0.8`. 2) Baseline FCFS saturation: increase Poisson rate until P90 E2EL inflection point (e.g., 20–25 req/s). 3) Head-to-head: at high load, compare P50/P90 for SJF/HRRN; verify scheduling overhead <0.1 ms.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
How can we mitigate overestimation from non-speech (silence) segments in audio durations? (Suggested: VAD preprocessing).  
How can we automatically adapt the duration-to-token scaling factor (`κ`) for multilingual deployments with differing tokenization densities?  
Can a dynamic runtime controller that switches between scheduling policies based on real-time metrics (queue depth, tail percentiles, starvation indicators) achieve better trade-offs under varying loads?

## Limitations
- The linear duration-token proxy may fail for audio with high silence or for languages/models with non-linear decoding scaling.  
- Synthetic uniform-duration dataset construction is ambiguous (trimming vs. selection), risking selection bias.  
- Evaluation limited to a single GPU and Whisper-large-v3; generalizability to other hardware or ASR architectures is untested.

## Confidence
- **Duration proxy accuracy:** High (strong empirical `R²`, zero overhead).  
- **SJF median latency reduction:** High (consistent with theory and experiments).  
- **HRRN tail boundedness:** Medium (bounded in evaluated regimes but untested under extreme overload).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-model κ stability:** Measure `R²` across Whisper sizes and other ASR models on multilingual corpora; break if `R² < 0.8`.  
2. **HRRN stress test:** At λ=30–35 req/s, verify P90 degradation stays <30%; >30% indicates bound violation.  
3. **Synthetic split replication:** Construct uniform-duration set via controlled trimming, validate distribution with histogram/KS test, rerun experiments to exclude selection bias.
