# General Machine Learning: Theory for Learning Under Variable Regimes

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23220  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23220  
- **Reference count:** 20  
- **Primary result:** Introduces the General Machine Learning (GML) framework with admissible transport, protected-core operator, and admissibility certificate to provide a rigorous mathematical grammar for regime‑varying learning, recovering classical fixed‑regime theory as a boundary case.

## Executive Summary
This paper develops a formal framework for machine learning when the data source, evaluator, and memory rules—collectively termed *regimes*—change over time. It defines a learning system as a sequence of admissible regime transitions governed by a certification predicate, with a protected‐core operator preserving the identity of the learning problem across syntactic variations. Under four standing structural requirements, the authors establish a stability template, morphism theorems, and semantic alignment results, demonstrating that classical fixed‑regime learning is a degenerate case. The work provides a theoretical foundation for continual and multi‑task learning where learning environments are non‑stationary.

## Method Summary
The GML framework instantiates learning on a set of regimes \(R\), each with typed carriers for state \(S_r\), memory \(M_r\), observer \(O_r\), action \(A_r\), and an evaluator \(V_r\). Admissible transitions \(\tau: r \rightsquigarrow r'\) are typed maps whose components must be well‑defined and measurable, and must satisfy an admissibility certificate \(\Gamma\) that checks typing, realizability, evaluator compatibility, and protection of a core semantic operator \(\Phi\). The *protected stability template* (Theorem 6.6) shows that if a discrepancy functional \(W_t\) anchored in \(\Phi(V_{r_t})\) satisfies a contractive drift condition with a bounded regime‑variation cost \(d_T\), then learning guarantees persist across admissible trajectories. A convex quadratic witness (Proposition 6.7) demonstrates non‑vacuous instantiation: trust‑region constraints define \(\Phi\), a gradient step with clipping defines \(\tau\), and the squared distance to the local optimum yields \(W_t\) with explicit constants \(\alpha, \beta\).

Under four standing structural requirements—structural closure, dynamical stability, bounded statistical capacity, and evaluative invariance—the framework yields general convergence guarantees.

## Key Results
- Classical Mitchell‑style fixed‑regime learning is recovered as a boundary case when the protected core equals the full evaluator (Proposition 6.2).
- Structural obstructions exist for faithful fixed‑ontology reduction when memory‑mediated admissibility, quotient‑restricted comparability, or non‑aggregable protected cores are present (Proposition 6.4).
- A general protected stability template (Theorem 6.6) unifies convergence analyses across regime changes via a contractive drift plus bounded transport cost; the template is instantiated in a convex quadratic subclass (Proposition 6.7).
- Algebraic structure: morphism, factorization, composition, and synthesis theorems (Propositions 6.15–6.22) formalize how GML systems combine and compare.
- Kernel‑level semantic alignment across working theorem layers is established, along with a non‑vacuous symbolic witness (Proposition 7.8).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Admissible Transport as Semantic Gatekeeper
- **Claim:** Regime transitions are semantically legitimate only when certified as admissible, preserving type‑structure and evaluator compatibility.
- **Mechanism:** The framework introduces a typed transport \(\tau\) carrying state, memory, interface, and action components. Admissibility predicate \(\Gamma\) checks well‑typing, realizability, evaluator compatibility, and protected‑core preservation (Definitions 3.3–3.8). \(\Gamma\) acts as a gatekeeper preventing arbitrary regime changes from counting as learning evolution.
- **Core assumption:** All relevant semantic aspects can be encoded in a typed fashion and a non‑empty set of transitions satisfies all certificate clauses.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract highlights “admissible transport \(\tau\) … and admissibility certificate \(\Gamma\)”. Section 3 defines the typed transformation grammar and certification rule. Section 4 requires structural closure, ensuring \(\Gamma\)‑certified transitions map the GML instantiation to itself. The SMGI precursor (Osmani 2026) frames admissibility as part of a broader structural program.
- **Break condition:** \(\Gamma = 0\) for a transition that would otherwise be locally attractive; or transport maps fail to be well‑typed or realizable.

### Mechanism 2: Protected‑Core Operator Preserving Learning Identity
- **Claim:** The identity of a learning problem across regimes is captured by a protected‑core operator \(\Phi\), which extracts the evaluative component that must remain invariant (or equivalent) under admissible changes.
- **Mechanism:** \(\Phi(V_r)\) defines a semantic invariant; if two regimes are \(\sim_\Phi\)‑equivalent (Definition 3.6), cross‑regime comparison is meaningful. Admissibility requires \(\Phi\) to be preserved literally or up to \(\sim_\Phi\) along any transition (Definitions 3.5–3.6). This isolates precisely which aspects of the evaluator define the learning problem, preventing reduction to a fixed ontology when protected parts are regime‑indexed.
- **Core assumption:** The protected core is non‑trivial (i.e., \(\Phi\) does not collapse everything), and \(\sim_\Phi\) is well‑behaved (measurable, closed) on the subclasses considered.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract highlights “protected‑core operator \(\Phi\)”. Section 3.2 defines the protected evaluative core and induced equivalence. Proposition 6.2 recovers Mitchell’s case when \(\Phi = V_r\). Proposition 6.4 shows non‑aggregable protected cores obstruct fixed‑ontology reduction.
- **Break condition:** \(\Phi\) becomes vacuous (maps everything to a single class) or so restrictive that no non‑trivial admissible transition exists; or \(\sim_\Phi\) is not closed, causing semantic discontinuities.

### Mechanism 3: Stability Template with Contractive Drift and Regime‑Variation Cost
- **Claim:** Protected‑level guarantees persist across admissible regime changes under a general drift‑controlled stability template, where a discrepancy functional \(W_t\) contracts locally and absorbs bounded regime‑variation costs.
- **Mechanism:** Theorem 6.6 posits a nonnegative \(W_t\) anchored in \(\Phi(V_{r_t})\) and proves along an admissible trajectory:  
  \(\mathbb{E}[W_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] \leq (1 - \alpha) W_t + \delta + \beta\, d_T(r_t, r_{t+1})\).  
  Unrolling yields boundedness, asymptotic recovery, or exact convergence depending on the regime‑cost profile. The template provides a structural proof schema; existence of \(W_t\) and constants \(\alpha, \beta, \delta\) is verified in specific working layers (e.g., convex quadratic subclass, Proposition 6.7).
- **Core assumption:** There exists \(W_t\) satisfying the local drift condition; the regime‑variation cost \(d_T\) is finite and measurable; and bounded statistical capacity holds so that transport does not explode complexity.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions “protected stability template with contractive drift and regime‑variation cost”. Section 6.2 states the template theorem and gives the convex anchor witness. Section 4 includes “dynamical stability” and “bounded statistical capacity” as standing requirements. Proposition 6.9 shows persistence of guarantees under admissible transport.
- **Break condition:** \(\alpha = 0\) (no contraction), \(\beta\) or \(d_T\) infinite (unbounded transport overhead), or failure to construct \(W_t\) that respects the protected core and update dynamics.

## Foundational Learning
- **Typed Regime Transformation (\(\tau\))**  
  *Why needed:* Makes precise how learner state, memory, and interfaces evolve between different learning configurations; essential for any theory of variable regimes.  
  *Quick check:* Given \(r\) and \(r'\), are the transport maps \(\tau_S, \tau_M, \tau_O, \tau_A\) well‑defined and measurable between the corresponding carriers?

- **Protected Equivalence (\(\sim_\Phi\))**  
  *Why needed:* Defines when two evaluators describe the same learning problem at the protected level, enabling cross‑regime comparison despite local syntactic differences.  
  *Quick check:* Do \(V_r\) and \(V_{r'}\) have protected cores that fall into the same \(\sim_\Phi\) class, i.e., \(\Phi(V_r) \sim_\Phi \Phi(V_{r'})\)?

- **Admissibility Certificate (\(\Gamma\))**  
  *Why needed:* Gates whether a regime transition is a legitimate continuation of learning; without it, any change could be mistaken for progress.  
  *Quick check:* Does the proposed transition \(\tau: r \rightsquigarrow r'\) satisfy all \(\Gamma\) clauses (typing, realizability, evaluator compatibility, protected‑core preservation)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Regimes (R) -> Typed carriers (Sr, Mr, Or, Ar) -> Evaluators Vr -> Transformation class T (typed τ) -> Protected‑core operator Φ -> Admissibility predicate Γ -> Protected equivalence ∼Φ -> Update operators U

- **Critical path:** To apply GML to a concrete system:  
  (1) Identify regime decomposition and define typed carriers Sr, Mr, Or, Ar as measurable spaces (or appropriate categories) to support well‑typed transport.  
  (2) Specify regime‑local evaluators Vr (measurable maps from histories to scores).  
  (3) Choose a protected‑core operator Φ that captures invariant semantics; Φ should map evaluators to a core space where ∼Φ defines equivalence.  
  (4) Define admissibility certificate Γ (e.g., structural checks, proof‑carrying, memory‑mediated constraints); Γ must enforce typing, realizability, evaluator compatibility, and protected‑core preservation (Definitions 3.3–3.8).  
  (5) Verify the four standing structural requirements (structural closure, dynamical stability, bounded statistical capacity, evaluative invariance) for the intended working layer (Section 4).  
  (6) For stability analysis, construct a discrepancy functional Wt (nonnegative, anchored in Φ(V_{r_t})) and verify the drift condition of Theorem 6.6: there must exist constants α>0, β, δ such that \(\mathbb{E}[W_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] \leq (1-\alpha)W_t + \delta + \beta d_T(r_t, r_{t+1})\).  
  (7) Demonstrate non‑trivial admissible trajectories (e.g., the two‑regime convex witness, Proposition 6.7) to show that Γ is satisfiable and the stability template yields meaningful bounds.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Expressivity vs. tractability:* Richer Φ and Γ increase semantic fidelity but may make verification hard.  
  - *Choice of dT:* Finer‑grained cost yields tighter stability bounds but requires more elaborate modeling of transport overhead.  
  - *Memory‑mediated admissibility:* Adding retained‑competence constraints improves modeling of continual learning but complicates composition.  
  - *Symbolic vs. numerical layers:* Symbolic Φ (e.g., logical entailment) offers crisp non‑aggregability but may lack tractable Γ; numerical Φ (e.g., thresholds) may be easier to certify but risk soft aggregation pitfalls (Proposition 6.4).

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *Γ = 0* on a transition that appears locally beneficial – indicates loss of protected semantics or illegal transport.  
  - Inability to find Wt with α>0 – suggests drift cannot be controlled.  
  - Unbounded dT along a chain – may cause the stability residual β∑dT to diverge.  
  - Protected core violation (Φ(Vr) not preserved) even when local utility improves – signals learning identity destruction.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Boundary degeneration:** Encode a single‑regime system with Φ = Vr and verify that GML reduces to Mitchell’s definition (Proposition 6.2).  
  2. **Convex stability witness:** Implement the two‑regime linear regression/toy quadratic example from Section 8.1.2; compute Wt = ‖st – µ(r)‖², verify the contractive drift and the anchor‑shift transport cost.  
  3. **Memory‑mediated admissibility:** Build a continual learner with a retention floor (Section 8.2.1); show two updates with identical local scores but different Γ outcomes due to memory protection.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Can admissibility certificates Γ be computed tractably for deep neural networks?  
  *Basis:* Section 11 notes exact verification of protected‑core preservation or entailment is often intractable for expressive hypothesis spaces like deep over‑parameterized networks.  
  *Why unresolved:* Current methods (interval bound propagation, abstract interpretation) are incomplete; a general constructive theory ensuring efficient Γ computation is unknown.  
  *Resolution evidence:* A polynomial‑time algorithm or proof of tractability for a broad class of architectures, or a hardness result with clearly defined tractable islands. For instance, if the protected core is defined via a simple norm constraint, Γ might reduce to checking a set of linear inequalities; but for logical specifications, it may be undecidable.

- **Question 2:** What are necessary and sufficient admissibility criteria for memory‑mediated multi‑regime systems?  
  *Basis:* The paper explicitly states it does not provide a complete algorithmic test for admissibility; Proposition 6.11 shows memory can gate admissibility beyond local scores.  
  *Why unresolved:* General characterization of Γ when admissibility depends on dynamic retained‑competence obligations is lacking; no full decision procedure exists. The paper gives examples where memory constraints interact with admissibility, but a systematic theory is absent.  
  *Resolution evidence:* An exact axiomatization or decision procedure for Γ on a broad class of systems with protected memory retention constraints, together with complexity analysis. This might require extending the typed‑carrier formalism to include memory invariants as first‑class objects.

- **Question 3:** How can distinct working theorem layers (e.g., numerical vs. symbolic) be formally aligned beyond semantic kernel correspondence?  
  *Basis:* Section 7 leaves a meta‑theoretical account of layer comparison as future work and notes that full development of symbolic layers is incomplete.  
  *Why unresolved:* While semantic commensurability (Definition 7.4) gives a kernel‑level notion, there is no general theory for transporting arbitrary layer‑specific theorems, proofs, or constructions across heterogeneous layers. The paper shows alignment for a few examples but lacks a uniform morphism theory for layers.  
  *Resolution evidence:* A formal theory of layer morphisms with composition and transport theorems, validated on non‑monotonic and hybrid logic‑statistical GML instantiations. This would likely involve categorical constructions that relate the semantic kernels of different layers.

## Limitations
- The abstract typed‑carrier formulation makes concrete implementation for deep networks challenging; Φ and Γ choices are not algorithmic. The framework requires the designer to specify these components by hand, and for complex models (e.g., deep neural networks) there is no general method to derive them, leaving a gap between theory and practice.
- The four standing structural requirements (structural closure, dynamical stability, bounded statistical capacity, evaluative invariance) may be violated in many realistic continual‑learning scenarios. For instance, new regimes might introduce hypothesis classes not covered by the original GML instantiation, breaking structural closure; or the regime‑variation cost d_T might be unbounded in practice. Thus the guarantees of Theorem 6.6 may not apply.
- Instantiations are restricted to convex quadratic losses; applicability to non‑convex, high‑dimensional, or stochastic problems remains unclear. The convex quadratic witness (Proposition 6.7) is a sanity check, but extending the stability template to realistic deep learning settings is an open challenge.
- No empirical validation on standard benchmarks; computability and cost of admissibility certificates and regime‑variation metrics are unaddressed. The paper is purely theoretical, and it is unknown whether the proposed framework can be implemented efficiently or yields practical benefits over existing continual‑learning methods.

## Confidence
- Algebraic properties (Props 6.15–6.22) and boundary recovery (Prop 6.2): **High** (rigorous proofs).  
- Obstructions to fixed‑ontology reduction (Prop 6.4): **High**.  
- Protected stability template (Thm 6.6) and convex anchor witness (Prop 6.7): **High** (within convex subclass).  
- General applicability beyond toy convex settings: **Medium** at best (lack of evidence).  
- Practical feasibility of computing Γ: **Low** (definition is proof‑theoretic, not algorithmic).
