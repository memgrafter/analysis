# GAS: Enhancing Reward-Cost Balance of Generative Model-assisted Offline Safe RL

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.05323
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05323
- Reference count: 34
- Primary result: GAS achieves safe performance under tight constraints and delivers a 6% average reward improvement under loose thresholds, outperforming nine existing baselines on 12 tasks.

## Executive Summary
GAS addresses two key shortcomings in generative model-assisted offline safe RL: limited stitching from suboptimal trajectories and poor balancing of reward-cost trade-offs. It introduces transition-level augmentation and relabeling to enrich dataset diversity, trains separate reward and cost goal functions via expectile regression to estimate optimal achievable returns, and reshapes the dataset for a uniform reward-cost distribution. Empirical evaluations on 12 tasks across two benchmarks demonstrate that GAS maintains safety under stringent constraints and improves rewards by 6% under looser thresholds, surpassing nine baselines. The method also shows robust zero-shot adaptation across threshold settings and resilience to imbalanced reward-cost specifications.

## Method Summary
GAS processes offline data through three stages: first, it applies Temporal Segmented Return Augmentation and Transition-level Return Relabeling to create diverse sub-trajectories and perturb targets, enhancing stitching robustness. Second, it trains reward and cost goal functions using expectile regression on high quantiles of the augmented data, estimating the maximum achievable reward under cost constraints without Bellman backups. Third, it reshapes the dataset by resampling transitions with high rewards within cost bins, balancing the representation of desirable transitions. A constrained policy optimization then uses these goal functions to select actions that achieve the estimated returns while satisfying cost constraints, effectively stitching suboptimal segments into high-performing trajectories.

## Key Results
- GAS achieves safe performance even under tight cost constraints, with no violations.
- Under loose thresholds, it delivers a 6% average reward improvement over nine baselines.
- Demonstrates reliable zero-shot adaptation across varying threshold settings and robustness to imbalanced reward-cost specifications.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Transition-level augmentation and relabeling for enhanced stitching
- **Claim:** These techniques enrich the dataset with diverse sub-trajectory segments and make the policy robust to misaligned test targets, improving stitching.
- **Mechanism:** Temporal Segmented Return Augmentation expands each transition into multiple sub-trajectories of varying lengths, providing more segments for cross-trajectory stitching. Transition-level Return Relabeling perturbs the reward-cost targets during training (e.g., scaling returns by a factor within [1-δ, 1+δ]), so the policy learns under a wider distribution of targets.
- **Core assumption:** Suboptimal trajectories contain useful segments that can be recombined; perturbed targets teach the policy to handle imperfect specifications.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions augmentation and relabeling; Sections 5.2–5.3 formalize them; neighboring papers use different approaches, highlighting novelty.

### Mechanism 2: Expectile regression for goal functions
- **Claim:** Reward and cost goal functions, trained via expectile regression, estimate optimal achievable returns under constraints without Bellman backups, guiding policy to balance reward and safety.
- **Mechanism:** Goal functions `V_R` and `V_C` are regressed to upper expectiles (high α) of the reward-cost distribution. The policy is trained via constrained AWR to match actions achieving these goals under `V_C < ˆC`.
- **Core assumption:** Dataset contains near-optimal transitions identifiable via high-quantile regression; expectile regression approximates optimal returns without OOD-free Bellman backups.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract describes expectile regression; Sections 5.4 & B.1 provide loss functions and theoretical limit; corpus uses different estimation methods.

### Mechanism 3: Dataset reshaping for uniform reward-cost distribution
- **Claim:** Reshaping prevents overfitting to conservative transitions, improving training stability and performance under loose constraints.
- **Mechanism:** The dataset is resampled to include all transitions in the top q% of rewards for each cost bin, mixed with original data (probability ε), balancing representation of "ideal" transitions.
- **Core assumption:** Original dataset is imbalanced toward low-reward transitions, biasing behavior cloning toward conservatism.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions reshaping; Section 5.5 and Figure 4 illustrate; data imbalance recognized in OSRL literature.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Dataset Processor -> Goal Networks -> Policy Network
- **Critical path:**
  1. Preprocess dataset: apply augmentation & relabeling, then reshape distribution.
  2. Train goal networks (`V_R`, `V_C`) to convergence on processed data via expectile losses.
  3. Jointly train policy using fixed (or slowly updating) goal networks; iterate.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - MLP vs Transformer: GAS uses MLP to avoid attention's ineffective temporal modeling, sacrificing long-horizon capture for better stitching.
  - Expectile level α: Higher α (e.g., 0.9) focuses on optimal transitions but risks overfitting "lucky" samples; lower α (0.5) degrades to BC. Default 0.8 balances.
  - Reshape parameters (q, ε): Larger q increases ideal transition coverage but may include noise; ε controls mix of original vs. reshaped data.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Constraint violations in tight thresholds: Goal functions underestimated safe returns or policy ignored constraint.
  - Poor reward under loose thresholds: Reshaping insufficient (q too low) or augmentation not providing high-reward segments.
  - Training instability: Large δ or extreme α causing noisy gradients.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Stitching validation: Compare GAS vs. CDT on suboptimal datasets (e.g., AntRun); expect GAS to achieve higher reward at same/better safety.
  2. Ablation on TSRA & Expectile: Run `GAS w/o TSRA` and `GAS α=0.5`; expect significant drop in reward/safety.
  3. Imbalanced target robustness: Vary target reward-cost ratios; expect GAS with relabeling (δ>0) to maintain safety where `GAS w/o Relabel` fails.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can memory mechanisms be designed for GAS to capture long-term dependencies without harming stitching ability? Current MLP sacrifices memory for stitching; need architecture with refined attention or recurrence.
2. Can GAS be extended to construct novel safe trajectories by combining safe and unsafe transitions? Currently discards unsafe transitions, missing potential information about safety boundaries.
3. How does GAS handle infeasible constraint thresholds (no transition satisfies cost constraint)? The objective assumes existence of transitions with cost below threshold; may fail if none exist.
4. What is the theoretical relationship between expectile level α and dataset return distribution? Can α be chosen automatically? Currently hand-tuned; need principle linking α to quantile of cost-constrained distribution.

## Limitations
- Dataset dependence: Requires near-optimal transitions for expectile regression; lacking them leads to conservatism.
- Parameter sensitivity: Augmentation horizon Γ, relabeling range δ, expectile α, reshape parameters (q, ε) need careful tuning.
- Benchmark scope: Evaluated on only 12 tasks across two benchmarks; generalization to other domains unverified.
