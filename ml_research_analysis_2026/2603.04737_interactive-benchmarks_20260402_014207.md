# Interactive Benchmarks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04737
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04737
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Interactive evaluation outperforms budget-matched repeated sampling (pass@k) by 20–50% absolute accuracy on math, exposing capabilities unrevealed by static benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Interactive Benchmarks, a framework that evaluates model intelligence through budget-constrained, multi-turn interaction rather than static one-shot tests. Two complementary settings are proposed: Interactive Proofs, where models ask yes/no questions to deduce hidden truths in logic or math puzzles under a turn budget; and Interactive Games, where models adapt strategically against opponents in Texas Hold’em poker and iterated Trust Games. Across six frontier LLMs, significant performance gaps emerge, with top models solving only 17.4–30.4% of logic puzzles and 34.6–76.9% of math instances, and only some surpassing simple heuristic baselines in Trust Games. Under matched token budgets, interactive assessment yields substantially higher accuracy than pass@k, demonstrating that active information acquisition is critical for measuring real-world reasoning capability.

## Method Summary
The framework instantiates two interactive evaluation settings. In **Interactive Proofs**, a model (Player) queries a deterministic Judge with yes/no questions to uncover a hidden truth (e.g., a logic puzzle solution or math problem answer) within a 20-turn budget; success is binary accuracy and efficiency measured by average turns on solved instances. In **Interactive Games**, models act as agents in competitive or cooperative games: Texas Hold’em poker (observing private/public cards and betting history) and iterated Trust Games (mutual cooperation/defection with random continuation). Metrics include average profit per hand (poker) and average payoff per round (trust), with opponents varying across models. For fair comparison to static benchmarks, the token budget in interactive proofs is approximately matched to that used in repeated sampling (pass@k). All evaluations are zero-shot with fixed system prompts; the Judge for proofs is a fixed model (Grok-4.1-fast).

## Key Results
- Interactive Proofs: Top models achieve only 17.4–30.4% accuracy on logic puzzles and 34.6–76.9% on math; solved instances require 5–18 turns on average.
- Interactive Games: In poker, Gemini-3-flash leads profitability (31.8 chips/hand). In Trust Games, only Qwen3-max and GPT-5-mini surpass heuristic baselines (Grim Trigger, Tit-for-Tat).
- Budget-matched comparison: Interactive evaluation outperforms pass@k by 20–50% absolute accuracy on the math subset, highlighting the value of active information gathering.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Interactive evaluation exposes latent reasoning capability by forcing active information acquisition under budget constraints, which static benchmarks fail to capture.
- **Mechanism:** The model must sequentially decide which queries will maximally reduce uncertainty (Proofs) or increase long-term payoff (Games). Each action consumes a limited resource (turns/chips), creating a trade-off between exploration (asking informative questions) and exploitation (submitting an answer). This tests the model's ability to plan its own information-gathering strategy rather than responding to a fully specified prompt.
- **Core assumption:** A model's intelligence can be measured by its efficiency in a sequential decision process where it controls the information flow.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "evaluating model's ability to acquire information actively is important to assess model's intelligence"
  - [section 2.1] "the Player interacts with the Judge by posing yes-or-no-style questions to isolate critical facts and iteratively validate hypotheses"
  - [corpus] Weak direct corpus evidence; the related work discusses dynamic benchmarks but the paper's own framework is the primary source for this mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If a model can achieve near-perfect accuracy without using its full budget (e.g., solves puzzles in 1–2 turns consistently), the benchmark may be too easy or contaminated.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The budget-constrained interaction protocol creates a principled, comparable measure of reasoning efficiency across diverse tasks by normalizing for total computational effort (tokens).
- **Mechanism:** By capping total interaction turns or tokens, the evaluation measures not just binary success but also the *cost* of success. This allows fair comparison between models with different generation lengths and between interactive and non-interactive (pass@k) paradigms using matched token budgets.
- **Core assumption:** Intelligence includes resource awareness; a capable agent should achieve goals with minimal necessary steps.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2.2] "To make a fair comparison with repeated sampling, we approximately match the inference budget measured in total tokens."
  - [section 2] Equations (2.1) and (2.2) formalize the optimization problems with explicit budget/utility constraints.
  - [corpus] Corpus mentions agentic benchmarks but not this specific budgeting mechanism; evidence is paper-internal.
- **Break condition:** If budget matching is impossible due to extreme variance in per-turn token counts (e.g., one model uses 10x tokens per turn), the efficiency comparison becomes noisy.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The framework bifurcates reasoning into two fundamental regimes: **convergent** (Interactive Proofs, minimizing uncertainty toward a fixed truth) and **divergent** (Interactive Games, maximizing utility against adaptive opponents), which together probe complementary facets of intelligence.
- **Mechanism:** In Proofs, the environment (Judge) is deterministic and consistent, rewarding hypothesis testing that converges on a single correct answer. In Games, the environment is stochastic/adversarial and strategic, rewarding adaptation, opponent modeling, and long-horizon planning. This separation isolates skills like deductive logic from skills like theory of mind.
- **Core assumption:** General intelligence requires both truth-seeking and strategic utility maximization in dynamic multi-agent settings.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "two settings are instantiated: Interactive Proofs... and Interactive Games..."
  - [section 2, Figure 1] "Interactive benchmarks acts as a sequential decision process. Left: In Interactive Proofs... Right: In Interactive Games..."
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence for this specific bifurcation is absent; it is a novel categorization proposed in the paper.
- **Break condition:** If a single task domain exhibits properties of both regimes (e.g., a game with verifiable optimal strategies), the classification becomes ambiguous and may confound measurement.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Sequential Decision-Making under Partial Observability**
  - **Why needed here:** Both benchmark settings are POMDPs: the model does not have full knowledge of the state (hidden truth in Proofs, opponent's hand/strategy in Games) and must act based on history.
  - **Quick check question:** "In the Situation Puzzle, why can't the model simply ask 'What is the solution?' and what does this reveal about the information structure?"
- **Concept: Information-Theoretic Exploration (Value of Information)**
  - **Why needed here:** The optimal query in Interactive Proofs is one that maximally reduces the hypothesis space entropy per unit cost. Models must implicitly compute or approximate expected information gain.
  - **Quick check question:** "If a yes/no question splits the remaining plausible explanations into 90% and 10% groups, which is more informative and why?"
- **Concept: Repeated Game Equilibrium & Strategy Adaptation**
  - **Why needed here:** The Trust Game requires recognizing and responding to opponent patterns (e.g., Tit-for-Tat) over a random horizon, testing basic game-theoretic reasoning and online learning.
  - **Quick check question:** "In an iterated Prisoner's Dilemma with unknown horizon, why is always cooperating not a robustly optimal strategy?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Agent -> Environment/Judge -> Budget Tracker -> Metric Calculator; Environment returns observations to Agent; Budget Tracker enforces termination.
- **Critical path:** Initialize environment with instance -> Loop: Agent observes history, selects action -> Environment updates state, provides observation, checks budget -> If termination (correct answer/budget exhausted/hand ends), break -> Evaluate.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Judge consistency vs. cost: A more informative judge may leak solution; paper uses strict {yes/no/both/irrelevant}.
  - Budget size: Too small makes task unsolvable; too large enables brute-force. Paper uses 20 turns for Proofs and natural game horizons.
  - Opponent diversity in Games: Single strong opponent may not measure adaptability; paper uses round-robin across models.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Proofs: High irrelevant/both query rate → poor hypothesis generation; many turns on solved instances → inefficient search; budget exhaustion without correct answer → failure to converge.
  - Games: Negative average profit → strategic misunderstanding; very low VPIP + high fold rate → overly passive; high betrayal rate after cooperation → failure in reciprocal strategies.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Sanity check: Run a single Situation Puzzle with a small model (e.g., GPT-2) to verify interaction loop and judge logic.
  2. Baseline replication: Replicate pass@k vs. interactive comparison on math for one model (e.g., Grok-4.1-fast), expecting ~20% interactive gain.
  3. Pilot tournament: Run mini Trust Game tournament with 3 models for 100 matches each; verify top models outperform baselines.

## Limitations
- Exact prompts, dataset items (46 logic puzzles, 52 math instances), and Judge response logic are not publicly specified.
- Poker engine configuration details (stack sizes, blinds, number of tables/hands) and Trust Game parameters (δ, repeats, seat swapping) are incompletely described.
- Access to the Grok-4.1-fast Judge and all six frontier models is required; substitutions may alter relative performance.

## Confidence
- **Interactive evaluation outperforms static benchmarks:** Medium (strong internal evidence but reliant on unreproducible datasets and judge)
- **Bifurcation into convergent/divergent regimes:** Low (novel categorization with no external validation; risk of confounding)
- **Budget-matched efficiency comparison:** Medium (method clearly described, but token variance across models could skew comparisons)
