# Scaling Laws for Precision in High-Dimensional Linear Regression
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19241
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19241
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Quantization introduces an additive error and reduces effective data size; multiplicative (FP-like) quantization preserves effective model size, while additive (INT-like) quantization shrinks it, leading to a unified risk scaling law.

## Executive Summary
This paper provides a theoretical analysis of how low-precision quantization affects scaling laws in high-dimensional linear regression. Within a sketched linear regression framework trained by one-pass SGD, the authors study multiplicative (signal-dependent, akin to floating-point) and additive (signal-independent, akin to integer) quantization schemes. They derive a unified risk bound showing that both schemes add an irreducible error component and reduce the effective data size, but they differ critically in their impact on effective model size: multiplicative quantization maintains full model capacity, whereas additive quantization shrinks it due to spectral flattening. The theory is validated by numerical experiments matching predicted power-law exponents with R² > 0.99. These results offer a principled foundation for jointly optimizing compute, model scale, data volume, and numerical precision.

## Method Summary
The study uses a linear regression model y = x⊤ w* + ε, where data x has covariance H with power-law eigenvalue decay λ_i ∝ i^{-a} (a>1). A random Gaussian sketch matrix S (size M×p) reduces dimensionality; the effective parameter is v* = (S H S⊤)^{-1} S H w*. Training employs one-pass SGD with iterate averaging on quantized data, features, labels, parameters, and gradients. Quantization is modeled as stochastic operators: multiplicative (error proportional to signal) and additive (constant-variance error). The risk decomposition yields irreducible noise, approximation (due to finite M), and excess risk. The excess risk bound depends on effective data size N_eff and effective model size M_eff, with additive error terms ϵ₃. For multiplicative quantization, M_eff = M; for additive, M_eff < M due to spectral flattening. Experiments sweep N and M, fitting log-linear models to estimate scaling exponents.

## Key Results
- Both quantization types introduce an additive irreducible error term (ϵ₃) and reduce effective data size N_eff by amplifying noise and distorting the spectrum.
- Multiplicative quantization preserves full model capacity (M_eff = M), while additive quantization shrinks effective model size because constant-quantization noise flattens the eigenvalue spectrum.
- Theoretical scaling exponents (risk ∝ M^{-(a-1)} and N^{-(a-1)/a}) are empirically validated with R² > 0.99 across a range of precisions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Additive Error Floor from Feature Space Distortion
Quantization creates a mismatch between the quantized feature covariance and the full-precision covariance, introducing an additive irreducible error. For multiplicative quantization, the gap is captured by ϵ₃^{(M)} = 1 − 1/[(1+ϵ_d)(1+ϵ_f)(1+ϵ_s)]; for additive quantization by ϵ₃^{(A)} = [ϵ_f + (1+ϵ_{dp})ϵ_s + ϵ_d(p/M)] / [1 + ϵ_f + (1+ϵ_{dp})ϵ_s + ϵ_d(p/M)]. This error forms a floor in the risk decomposition independent of M and N scaling. The key assumption is the expectation conditioned on the sketch matrix and unbiased quantization, which isolates the gap.

### Mechanism 2: Effective Data Size Reduction via Noise Amplification and Spectral Distortion
Quantization decreases the effective number of samples N_eff by (1) amplifying gradient noise (coefficient ϵ₂) and (2) distorting the data spectrum (changes in eigenvalues λ_i). Both effects combine multiplicatively: N_eff = N / [1+ϵ₂/((1−ϵ₃)^{1/a})]^{-a/(a-1)}. The mechanism relies on power-law spectra, where tail dimensions with small eigenvalues contribute significantly to sample complexity; reducing their effective signal-to-noise ratio shrinks N_eff.

### Mechanism 3: Differential Capacity Preservation versus Flattening
The core dichotomy: multiplicative quantization’s error variance scales with signal (ϵ x x⊤), so in tail dimensions the absolute error weakens proportionally, preserving the relative eigenstructure and hence full model capacity (M_eff = M). Additive quantization’s fixed noise variance (σ² I) forms a constant floor that overwhelms exponentially decaying tail signals, effectively deleting those dimensions and reducing M_eff. Quantitatively, additive quantization introduces a denominator factor scaling with M^{-1/(a-1)} in the excess risk bound, while multiplicative does not.

## Foundational Learning
- **Quantization as stochastic operator**: The paper models quantization as a random function with bounded second moments, parameterized separately for data, sketch, features, etc. This abstraction allows quantifying error propagation. *Why needed*: Defines the multiplicative/additive error families and derives the ϵ₂, ϵ₃ coefficients. *Quick check*: If quantization satisfies E[Q(x)x⊤] = x x⊤ up to scaling, is it multiplicative? What if it satisfies E[Q(x)] = x and Cov[Q(x)] = σ² I?
- **Sketched linear regression**: Using a random Gaussian sketch matrix S reduces the effective dimension to M, enabling analysis of high-dimensional settings. *Why needed*: Connects original covariance H to the sketched covariance S H S⊤; eigen-concentration results are used to bound risks. *Quick check*: How does the smallest eigenvalue of S H S⊤ relate to the decay of eigenvalues of H? What probability bound guarantees it stays positive?
- **One-pass SGD with iterate averaging**: The training algorithm is a single pass over quantized data with a constant stepsize and averaging of iterates. *Why needed*: The bias-variance decomposition of the excess risk depends on the linear update structure and the iterate covariance evolution. *Quick check*: Why must the stepsize γ satisfy γ < 1/λ₁(H_f^q) for stability? How does the averaged iterate ṽ_N differ from the final parameter v_N?

## Architecture Onboarding
Data stream -> Sketch Matrix S (random Gaussian) -> Sketched features S x -> Quantization (Q_d, Q_s, Q_f, Q_l, Q_p, Q_a, Q_o) -> Quantized SGD update -> Parameter iterate (averaged) -> Test risk evaluation

Critical path:
1. Generate data with covariance H following power-law decay.
2. Quantization operators modify data, sketch, features, labels, parameters, activations, gradients according to multiplicative/additive schemes.
3. One-pass SGD computes quantized gradients and updates parameters; iterate averaging produces final estimator.
4. Risk decomposition isolates irreducible noise, approximation error (due to finite M), and excess error (due to quantization and finite N).
5. Analysis of eigenvalues of H_f^{(q)} and S H S⊤ yields bounds on excess risk in terms of N_eff and M_eff.

Design tradeoffs:
- Commutativity assumption H_f^{(q)} ≃ S H S⊤ simplifies bounds but may not always hold; without it, extra condition number penalties appear.
- Analysis uses worst-case constants; real quantization may have smaller constants, making bounds loose.
- Separate coefficients for each quantization target enable mixed-precision design but complicate the optimization landscape.
- The theory assumes well-specified linear models and stationary distribution; distribution shifts could break the mechanism.

Failure signatures:
- Stepsize γ ≥ 1/λ₁(H_f^q) leads to divergence, invalidating lower bounds.
- Non-Gaussian sketch or non-power-law eigenvalue decay violates concentration lemmas, causing theoretical mismatch.
- Heavy-tailed data may violate fourth-moment bounds, limiting applicability to sub-Gaussian regimes.
- Correlated quantization errors across tensors (e.g., hardware coupling) violate independence assumptions, leading to unexpected interactions.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Spectral flattening**: Compute eigenvalues of quantized feature covariance H_f^q for both quantization types. Verify additive quantization flattens the tail while multiplicative preserves power-law slope.
2. **Neff scaling**: Vary N with fixed M; estimate effective sample size from learning curves. Confirm multiplicative quantization yields larger N_eff than additive at the same bit width.
3. **Meff invariance**: Vary M with fixed N; test error should scale as M^{-(a-1)} for multiplicative quantization but slower for additive, indicating reduced effective model size.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Can matching lower and upper bounds be established that explicitly exhibit effective model size reduction under additive quantization? Current lower bounds do not cleanly separate the Meff contraction term due to technical coupling in the recursive covariance update.
- **Open Question 2**: How do the theoretical scaling laws extend to nonlinear neural networks? The linear analysis relies on closed-form gradient dynamics and Gaussian sketching; linking to overparameterized nonlinear models remains open.
- **Open Question 3**: What are the scaling laws for low-precision training when using other optimizers (e.g., Adam, multi-pass SGD) or learning rate schedules? The analysis is tailored to one-pass constant-stepsize SGD.
- **Open Question 4**: When the commutativity assumption between H_f^{(q)} and S H S⊤ is violated, what precise scaling laws hold? The general bounds contain condition number factors whose impact on exponents or constants is unclear.
- **Open Question 5**: For mixed-precision training (different bit-widths across tensors), what is the optimal combination of multiplicative/additive quantization to maximize effective model and data sizes under a fixed compute/memory budget? The paper treats quantization type globally but does not optimize heterogeneous precision.

## Limitations
- The analysis assumes a specific stochastic quantization model and power-law eigenvalue decay, which may not hold for real deep learning datasets.
- Stepsize selection relies on asymptotic bounds; finite-sample behavior and optimal constant choices remain heuristic.
- The theory applies only to linear regression with one-pass SGD; extension to deep networks is speculative.
- Commutativity condition is invoked for sharp bounds but is not guaranteed with arbitrary data.

## Confidence
- Additive irreducible error term (ϵ₃): High (proven in Theorems 4.1/4.2, validated experimentally)
- Effective data size reduction (Neff): Medium (theoretically sound but depends on accurate estimation of noise amplification and spectral distortion coefficients)
- Differential capacity preservation (Meff): High (clear mechanistic distinction and experimental support)
- Overall scaling exponent matching: High (log-log fits achieve R² > 0.99)
- Generalization to deep networks: Low (no theoretical or empirical evidence beyond linear setting)

## Next Checks
- **Spectral flattening measurement**: Directly compute eigenvalues of H_f^q for various bit widths; verify additive quantization flattens the tail and that the Meff reduction formula aligns with empirical loss decay.
- **Robustness to sketch distribution**: Replace the Gaussian sketch with a structured random projection (e.g., CountSketch) and confirm whether N_eff scaling predictions remain valid under modified concentration properties.
- **Mixed-precision regime**: Implement combinations of multiplicative and additive quantization (e.g., FP16 for activations, INT8 for weights) and test whether observed effective model size follows the theoretical interpolation or reveals new phenomena not captured by the binary dichotomy.
