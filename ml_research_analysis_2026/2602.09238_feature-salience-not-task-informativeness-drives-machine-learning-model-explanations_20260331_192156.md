# Feature salience - not task-informativeness - drives machine learning model explanations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09238
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09238
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** Feature attributions are overwhelmingly driven by low-level image salience (e.g., edges, brightness) rather than whether a feature is statistically informative for the prediction task.

## Executive Summary
Explainable AI (XAI) methods are widely assumed to highlight features that are informative and relevant to a model’s decision-making. This paper challenges that assumption through controlled experiments in binary image classification. By manipulating test images with salient but non-informative watermarks or global lightness changes—and varying whether those manipulations were predictive during training—the authors show that attribution outputs are primarily determined by the presence of visually salient structures, not by whether the model learned to use them. Across five popular attribution methods, the mere existence of a watermark explained 45–67% of the variance in importance metrics, while whether the watermark was class-predictive explained at most 3%. Models trained without ever seeing watermarks still attributed high importance to them at test time, behaving similarly to edge detectors. These findings suggest that current XAI techniques reflect general image salience more than model reasoning, casting doubt on their reliability for detecting shortcuts or interpreting learned behavior.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted two controlled studies using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) on binary image classification tasks (cats vs. dogs; animals vs. vehicles). In the **watermark study**, they overlaid translucent logos on images, creating three training conditions: confounded (watermark predicts class), balanced (watermark non-informative), and no-watermark. Test images included both watermarked and clean variants. In the **lightness study**, they globally manipulated image brightness via Beta distribution histograms under analogous conditions. After training CNNs with standard SGD, they applied five attribution methods (Deconvolution, Integrated Gradients, Gradient SHAP, LRP-ϵ, LRP-αβ) to test images. The primary metric was Relative Importance on Watermark (RIW) or Lightness (RIL)—the normalized average attribution inside the manipulated region versus the whole image. Linear mixed models quantified how much variance in RIW/RIL was explained by (a) test-time salience (watermark presence or brightness) versus (b) training informativeness (whether the manipulation was predictive during training).

## Key Results
- The presence of a salient watermark alone explained 45–67% of the variation in RIW (R² ≥ 0.45) across all attribution methods and training settings.
- Whether the watermark was class-dependent (informative) explained at most 3% of the variance (R² ≤ 0.03).
- Models trained without watermarks still attributed high importance to the watermark at test time, matching the pattern of a discrete Laplacian edge detector.
- Global lightness manipulations produced analogous results: overall brightness dominated attributions irrespective of training.
- Inverted color encoding revealed method-specific biases: Gradient SHAP and LRP-ϵ were sensitive to raw intensity, while Deconvolution and LRP-αβ were more gradient-sensitive.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Feature attribution methods primarily respond to low-level image salience (e.g., sharp edges, high intensity), not whether a feature is statistically informative for the prediction task.  
**Mechanism:** Attribution algorithms compute gradients or backpropagated signals that are sensitive to spatial derivatives or absolute pixel values. These are elevated at salient boundaries (like watermark edges) regardless of the feature’s relationship to the target label. Thus, visually prominent structures receive high importance even if they are non-informative or absent during training.  
**Core assumption:** Visual prominence in the test input, not learned utility, drives the attribution output.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Results show that the mere presence of the salient watermark explained 45–67% of the variation in RIW... while whether the watermark was class‑dependent (informative) explained at most 3%." [section 2.1.2] "RIW is mainly driven by low‑level image properties, such as highly salient edges and corners."

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Different attribution methods exhibit distinct sensitivities to raw pixel intensity versus spatial gradients, leading to inconsistent behavior under manipulations like color inversion.  
**Mechanism:** Methods like Deconvolution and LRP-αβ amplify gradient-like signals, while Gradient SHAP and LRP-ϵ rely more on absolute feature values. This is inferred from opposite RIW shifts when color encoding is inverted (bright ↔ dark).  
**Core assumption:** The mathematical formulation of each method determines its bias toward intensity or gradient information.  
**Evidence anchors:** [section 2.1.3] "Deconvolution, and LRP-αβ show the familiar RIW upward shift... GradientSHAP and LRP-ϵ... display a noticeable RIW downward shift... suggesting that GradientSHAP and LRP-ϵ are highly sensitive to raw feature values." [section 3] "it is primarily raw intensity rather than local spatial gradients... that most strongly correlates with importance attributions."

### Mechanism 3
**Assumption:** The observed primacy of salience reveals a fundamental misalignment: XAI methods are not designed to identify causal roles of features (informative vs. suppressor vs. confounder), only to highlight regions that are salient relative to the model’s internal representation.  
**Mechanism:** Because most attribution algorithms compute weighted sums of gradients or backpropagated signals, they amplify high-magnitude activations at edges or bright spots. This signal does not encode the data-generating process (e.g., confounding) and cannot distinguish a causal feature from a spurious correlate.  
**Core assumption:** Diagnosing shortcut learning requires causal inference, which post-hoc attributions do not perform.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "These findings indicate that low‑level image salience, not statistical associations learned by the model, is the dominant factor driving feature attribution." [section 3] "Distinguishing features with different causal roles... requires methods of causal inference that currently go beyond the capabilities of XAI methods."

## Foundational Learning

### Suppressor Variables
**Why needed here:** The paper contrasts *informative* features (statistically associated with the target) with *suppressors* (non-informative but used by optimal models to denoise). Understanding this distinction is critical to evaluate if attributions reflect true utility or just noise cancellation.  
**Quick check question:** Can a feature be highly attributed by a model yet be statistically independent of the target? (Yes, if it's a suppressor.)

### Causal vs. Statistical Association
**Why needed here:** The core critique is that XAI conflates statistical correlation (X↑ when Y↑) with causal relevance (X causes Y). Confounding (C→X, C→Y) creates spurious association; XAI cannot disentangle this.  
**Quick check question:** Does a watermark that is equally present in both classes (balanced setting) have a causal effect on the class label? (No, it's non-informative; any attribution to it is not task-relevant.)

### Feature Salience in Vision
**Why needed here:** The paper's evidence hinges on the idea that watermarks and lightness changes are perceptually salient (high contrast, edges). Understanding salience detection (e.g., edge detectors) is key to seeing why attribution mimics them.  
**Quick check question:** Would a uniform color patch (low salience) receive high attribution if it was informative? (According to this paper, likely no; salience dominates.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Data Generator → CNN Trainer → Attribution Engine → Quantifier → Statistical Analyzer

**Critical path:** Data manipulation (watermarks/lightness) → model training → attribution on test images (with/without manipulation) → RIW/RIL calculation → variance decomposition via linear models. The key insight comes from comparing RIW across training settings for the *same* test images.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Fixed vs. variable watermark position: tests whether spatial prior alone drives attribution vs. mere presence signal.  
- Standard vs. inverted color encoding: disentangles intensity vs. gradient sensitivity.  
- Default method parameters: chosen for consistency with prior work; no hyperparameter tuning to avoid "explanation optimization" bias.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High RIW on watermarked areas even for models never seen watermarks (no-watermark setting).  
- RIW strongly correlates with watermark presence (R² ≥ 0.45) but not with training setting (R² ≤ 0.03).  
- Inverted color encoding reverses RIW for intensity-sensitive methods but not gradient-sensitive ones.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Fixed-position watermark study: Train on confounded/balanced/no-watermark data; apply to test images with/without watermark; compute RIW histograms and R² by watermark presence vs. training setting.  
2. Inverted color encoding replication: Repeat Experiment 1 with RGB values inverted; observe which methods flip RIW sign for watermarked images.  
3. Lightness study: Replace local watermark with global lightness manipulation; compute RIL; verify that global brightness drives attribution regardless of training setting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How do neural network architectures affect the dominance of input salience in XAI attributions?  
*Basis:* Future studies may differentiate between model architectures.  
*Why unresolved:* Only a single CNN was tested; other architectures (e.g., ViT, MLP) might have different inductive biases.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate across multiple architectures; quantify whether salience-dependence varies.

### Open Question 2
Can hyperparameter settings of attribution methods (e.g., baselines, noise) mitigate the reliance on low-level salience?  
*Basis:* No parameter sweeps were performed; real-world applications typically use defaults.  
*Why unresolved:* All results used defaults; it is unknown if alternative choices could align attributions with true task informativeness.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Systematically vary key parameters; assess whether tuning reduces salience-driven bias.

### Open Question 3
Are new benchmarks needed where informative features are low-salience to properly evaluate XAI methods?  
*Basis:* Existing benchmarks often use highly salient artifacts, confounding salience with informativeness and overestimating XAI performance.  
*Why unresolved:* No such benchmarks currently exist.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Construct datasets with subtle, low-contrast true features; test whether XAI prioritizes them over salient distractors.

### Open Question 4
Can XAI methods incorporate causal reasoning to distinguish informative, confounded, and suppressor features?  
*Basis:* Current methods are purely associative; they cannot indicate whether a feature is causal, a confounder, or a suppressor.  
*Why unresolved:* No mainstream XAI technique estimates causal graphs or uses do-calculus.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Develop XAI techniques using causal graphs; validate on synthetic tasks with known causal structures.

### Open Question 5
How does the impact of feature salience on XAI change when salience and informativeness vary continuously?  
*Basis:* The study examined only binary conditions; the functional relationship is unknown.  
*Why unresolved:* No parametric exploration of graded salience vs. informativeness.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Generate datasets with graded salience (e.g., opacity) and informativeness (label-conditional probability); fit models to predict attribution metrics from these factors.

## Limitations
- **Domain generality:** Experiments are confined to controlled binary image classification; unknown if salience dominance extends to multi-class, segmentation, or NLP tasks.
- **Artifact dependence:** Watermark and lightness manipulations may inadvertently create statistical dependencies beyond pure salience; disentanglement from all possible learned cues is not exhaustive.
- **Causal leap:** The conclusion that XAI "cannot reveal model reasoning" overreaches; the paper shows salience bias but does not disprove that some attributions can be informative under specific conditions.

## Confidence
- **High:** The empirical finding that test-time salience (watermark presence/global lightness) explains a large portion of variance in RIW/RIL (R² ≥ 0.45) across all settings, while training informativeness explains ≤ 3%.
- **Medium:** The claim that different attribution methods have distinct sensitivities to intensity vs. gradients, based on inverted-color experiments.
- **Low:** The broad assertion that XAI methods are fundamentally incapable of identifying causal feature roles; this is a theoretical interpretation supported only by the presented experiments.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-architecture & modality replication:** Repeat core experiments with transformer vision models (ViT), graph neural networks, and language models using salient perturbation tokens (e.g., rare words). Test whether salience dominance is modality/architecture-agnostic.
2. **Causal benchmarking:** Apply the same design but include a causal feature attribution method (e.g., causal SHAP, invariant risk minimization) as a comparator. Check if such methods systematically ignore salient non-informative features and highlight non-salient but causally necessary ones.
3. **Natural shortcut detection:** Evaluate salience bias with real-world shortcuts (e.g., background correlations, timestamps in medical images). Train models with/without the shortcut, then apply attributions to images containing the shortcut but no label information. If attributions still highlight the shortcut, it confirms the effect in ecological settings.
