# Decentralized Ranking Aggregation: Gossip Algorithms for Borda and Copeland Consensus

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22847
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22847
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** Decentralized Borda and Copeland consensus via gossip algorithms with convergence rate \(O(e^{-ct/2})\) and improved robustness through local Kemenization.

## Executive Summary
The paper studies decentralized ranking aggregation in networks without central coordination, focusing on Borda and Copeland consensus methods. It proposes random gossip algorithms where agents locally average score estimates (ranks for Borda, pairwise preferences for Copeland) via pairwise communication over connected graphs. Theoretical analysis shows convergence to the global average at a rate \(O(e^{-ct/2})\) where \(c\) is the spectral gap of the graph Laplacian. Empirical validation on synthetic Mallows data and real Sushi/Debian datasets demonstrates exponential decay in mean squared error and Kendall-tau distance across various graph topologies. Additional robustness analysis reveals that Copeland and Kemeny methods (via local Kemenization post-processing) resist outliers and spam better than plain Borda.

## Method Summary
Each agent starts with a local ranking over \(m\) items. For **Borda**, agents convert their ranking into a rank vector \(\mathbf{x}_v \in \mathbb{R}^m\). For **Copeland**, they initialize a vector of pairwise preference indicators \(\mathbf{x}_v \in \mathbb{R}^{m(m-1)/2}\). Agents then repeatedly perform **pairwise gossip**: a random edge \((u,v)\) is selected (with probabilities proportional to \(1/d_u + 1/d_v\)), and both endpoints replace their vectors with the element‑wise average. After sufficient iterations, each agent sorts its converged estimate to produce a consensus ranking (ascending for Borda; descending for Copeland scores computed from pairwise estimates). To improve robustness, **local Kemenization** can be applied: each agent iteratively swaps adjacent items in its ranking if a majority (inferred from its pairwise estimates) prefers the reverse order. The process terminates when no swaps are needed. The theoretical convergence rate depends on the spectral gap \(c\) of the underlying graph: the expected squared error decays as \((1 - c/2)^t\).

## Key Results
- **Convergence rate:** Gossip averaging converges to the global average of initial score estimates at \(O(e^{-ct/2})\) with \(c\) the spectral gap.
- **Empirical validation:** Across complete, geometric, Watts‑Strogatz, and grid graphs, mean squared error and Kendall‑tau distance decay exponentially, typically converging within thousands of iterations on synthetic and real datasets.
- **Robustness:** Local Kemenization applied to Borda or Copeland outputs consistently approximates the Kemeny consensus and shows stronger resistance to adversarial/spam data compared to plain Borda.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Random pairwise gossip averaging converges to the global average of initial score estimates (Borda ranks or Copeland pairwise preferences) at a rate governed by the graph's spectral gap.
- **Mechanism:** Each agent holds a local vector of score estimates. At each step, a random edge \((u,v)\) is selected; both endpoints replace their vectors with the element‑wise average. This is a distributed implementation of the classic randomized gossip algorithm for vector averaging.
- **Core assumption:** The communication graph is connected and non‑bipartite, and edge selection probabilities are positive.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract states the core method: “random gossip algorithms where agents locally average score estimates (ranks for Borda, pairwise preferences for Copeland) via pairwise communication.”
  - Section 3.1 & 2.2 formalizes Algorithm 1 and Lemma 1, proving \(\mathbb{E}\|X_i(t)-\bar{x}_i\mathbf{1}_n\|^2 \le (1-c/2)^t \|X_i(0)-\bar{x}_i\mathbf{1}_n\|^2\).
  - Corpus shows foundational work like Boyd et al. [6] on gossip convergence.
- **Break condition:** Guarantee fails if the graph is disconnected or bipartite (violates Lemma 3 assumptions), or if edge probabilities are zero for critical links; the rate constant \(c\) becomes zero.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Once score estimates have sufficiently converged, locally sorting them at each agent recovers the correct global consensus ranking (Borda or Copeland), with high probability.
- **Mechanism:** After gossip iterations, each agent sorts its estimated score vector (ascending for Borda, descending for Copeland scores) to produce a permutation. Correctness hinges on the gap between true scores being larger than the aggregate estimation error.
- **Core assumption:** The true consensus scores are distinct (unique minimum gap \(\Delta_B\) or \(\Delta_C\)), ensuring a single correct ordering.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Section 3.1 Proof of Proposition 1 derives the condition: if \(\varepsilon_{v,i}(t)+\varepsilon_{v,j}(t) < |s_i-s_j|\), then the ordering between items \(i\) and \(j\) is correct at node \(v\). Summing over pairs bounds Kendall‑\(\tau\) error.
  - Section 3.2 mirrors this logic for Copeland scores.
- **Break condition:** Guarantee degrades when score gaps are very small relative to estimation error; tied scores cause non‑unique orderings; local sorting may yield inconsistent rankings across nodes even with converged scores.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Applying decentralized local Kemenization as a post‑processing step to a rough consensus (e.g., from Borda or Copeland) yields a locally Kemeny‑optimal ranking that approximates the true Kemeny consensus and improves robustness to spam/outliers.
- **Mechanism:** Each agent, using its locally converged pairwise preference estimates \(\hat{x}_{v,(i,j)}\), iteratively scans its current ranking. For each adjacent pair \((i,j)\), if a majority of agents (inferred from \(\hat{x}_{v,(i,j)}\le 1/2\)) prefer \(j\) over \(i\), the pair is swapped. This repeats until no adjacent swaps are needed.
- **Core assumption:** The initial ranking is a valid permutation, and the pairwise majority preferences derived from converged estimates are consistent enough that the local swap process terminates and inherits the extended Condorcet property.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Section 2.1 defines local Kemenization and its properties.
  - Section 3.3 describes Algorithm 4 for its decentralized implementation.
  - Section 4.1 Tables 1 & 2 show empirical evidence: applying local Kemenization consistently recovers the Kemeny consensus and reduces excess loss under contamination.
- **Break condition:** The local swap process may not terminate if the pairwise estimates are too noisy or contradictory (creating cycles). Robustness guarantee fails if the majority preference estimates are themselves corrupted beyond repair by adversarial nodes.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Agent State (x_v) → Graph Interface (sample_edge) → Gossip Kernel ((x_u + x_v)/2) → Consensus Layer (sorting/local Kemenization) → Evaluation Monitor (∥x_v − global_avg∥, Kendall‑τ)
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Initialize x_v(0) from local ranking.  
  2. Main loop (T iterations): sample edge → gossip update.  
  3. Termination: after T or when score convergence (∥x_v − x_u∥ small for neighbors) is detected.  
  4. Finalization: run local sorting or local Kemenization on x_v(T) to produce final ranking.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Communication vs. Accuracy: More iterations improve accuracy but increase message exchanges. Tradeoff determined by acceptable Kendall‑τ error and graph’s spectral gap.  
  - Memory vs. Method: Borda stores O(m) floats/agent; Copeland stores O(m²)—costly for large m.  
  - Robustness vs. Simplicity: Borda simplest but least robust. Adding Local Kemenization adds O(m²) pairwise checks per iteration post‑gossip but greatly improves spam resistance.  
  - Centralized vs. Decentralized Eval: In real deployment, no ground truth; monitor pairwise disagreement between neighboring agents as a proxy for convergence.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - No convergence: ∥x_v − x_u∥ remains large after many iterations. Likely cause: graph not connected/non‑bipartite (check c≈0), or edge sampling biased/inactive.  
  - Inconsistent final rankings: Different agents output different rankings after termination. Likely cause: score gaps too small relative to final error, or premature termination.  
  - Poor robustness: Performance collapses with few corrupt agents. Likely cause: using plain Borda without Local Kemenization; corrupt agents’ initial ranks poison the average.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Convergence on Complete Graph:** Run on small complete graph (n=10, m=5) with synthetic Mallows data. Plot average ∥x_v − true_scores∥ vs. iteration. Should see exponential decay matching (1−c/2)^t with c computed from graph.  
  2. **Sensitivity to Score Gap:** With fixed graph and n,m, generate Mallows data with varying dispersion ϕ. Smaller ϕ → larger score gaps Δ. Measure iterations to reach Kendall‑τ < 0.5. Expect fewer iterations as ϕ decreases (larger Δ).  
  3. **Local Kemenization Robustness:** Introduce ε fraction of adversarial agents (e.g., reversed rankings). Compare Kendall‑τ to true consensus for: (a) plain Borda, (b) Borda + Local Kemenization, (c) Copeland. Expect (b) and (c) to match Kemeny’s lower error, per Tables 1 & 2.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Theoretical robustness guarantees against adversarial nodes?** The paper provides only empirical results; no theory exists on how Byzantine nodes affect the spectral gap or error bounds of the gossip process.
- **Can communication per iteration be reduced to scale to large item sets?** Current Borda and Copeland protocols exchange full vectors (O(m) and O(m²)); no compression or approximate methods are considered.
- **How to handle ties and non‑strict transitivity in Copeland consensus?** The analysis assumes strict stochastic transitivity and distinct Copeland scores; real data often produce ties, leading to undefined or multiple consensus rankings.
- **Convergence and robustness properties of decentralized local Kemenization?** The paper offers a decentralized implementation but lacks theoretical analysis of its convergence or error propagation under noisy estimates.

## Limitations
- Convergence rate guarantee assumes a connected, non‑bipartite graph with proper edge sampling; the spectral gap \(c\) may be very small in sparse topologies, leading to slow practical convergence.
- Ranking recovery via sorting depends on distinct score gaps; real‑world rankings often contain near‑ties, which can cause inconsistent local rankings even if scores converge.
- The robustness claim for local Kemenization lacks a formal theoretical foundation for the decentralized swap procedure; its convergence and sensitivity to noisy pairwise estimates remain unproven.

2. **Score Gap Sensitivity Curve:** Fix a graph and \(n,m\). Systematically vary the Mallows dispersion parameter \(\varphi\) to create a spectrum of true score gaps (Δ). Measure the iterations required for Kendall‑τ error to drop below 0.5. Plot error vs. iteration for multiple Δ values to empirically confirm the predicted threshold effect: recovery time should increase sharply as Δ → 0.
3. **Local Kemenization Swap Equivalence:** Implement both the paper’s decentralized swap procedure (using local pairwise estimates) and the centralized definition of local Kemenization (using global pairwise majority). On converged gossip states from clean and contaminated data, compare final rankings and swap iteration counts. Discrepancies would indicate the decentralized process does not perfectly replicate the centralized fixed point, challenging the robustness claim.
