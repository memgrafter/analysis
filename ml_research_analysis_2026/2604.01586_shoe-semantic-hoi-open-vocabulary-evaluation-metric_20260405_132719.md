# SHOE: Semantic HOI Open-Vocabulary Evaluation Metric

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01586
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01586
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** SHOE achieves 85.73% agreement with human similarity judgments, surpassing inter-annotator agreement (78.61%), and enables large-vocabulary HOI evaluation with soft matching.

## Executive Summary
Standard HOI detection metrics like mAP require exact label matches, which penalizes semantically equivalent but lexically different predictions and fails to evaluate open-vocabulary models fairly. SHOE introduces a soft, semantically-aware metric that decomposes HOI predictions into verb and object components, maps them to WordNet synsets, and estimates similarity via multi-LLM consensus. The verb and object scores are averaged to compute overall similarity, allowing partial credit. SHOE supports both confidence-ranked mAP for detectors and a confidence-free mode for generative models. It achieves human-aligned evaluation and shows significant gains for vision-language models on benchmarks like HICO-DET, while scaling to over 38 million HOI concepts efficiently.

## Method Summary
SHOE replaces exact HOI class matching with a continuous semantic similarity score. For each predicted and ground-truth human-object pair that passes an IoU threshold, the verb and object labels are lemmatized and mapped to WordNet synsets for sense disambiguation. Pairwise verb-verb and object-object similarities are precomputed by querying multiple LLMs to rate synset pairs on a 0–4 scale using their glosses; the scores are averaged across LLMs to form lookup tables. The final HOI similarity is the arithmetic mean of the verb and object scores (typically weighted equally). For confidence-based detectors, SHOE computes a soft average precision (Soft-AP) by sorting predictions and integrating the precision-recall curve using these similarity scores. For generative models without reliable confidence, a confidence-free macro F1 (mF1) is computed by thresholding similarity values. This decomposition reduces computational complexity from quadratic in the full HOI vocabulary to additive in the verb and object vocabularies, enabling open-vocabulary scaling.

## Key Results
- SHOE achieves 85.73% agreement with average human similarity judgments, exceeding the 78.61% average inter-annotator agreement.
- On HICO-DET, SHOE mAP consistently outperforms standard mAP, with VLMs showing large gains (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-32B: 66.03 vs 34.83).
- The method scales to over 38 million HOI concepts while remaining efficient via precomputed lookup tables.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Compositional Decomposition for Scalable Semantic Matching
- **Claim:** Decomposing HOI labels into separate verb and object components avoids combinatorial explosion when computing pairwise semantic similarities across a vast open-vocabulary space.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of directly comparing full “verb-object” phrases (which would grow quadratically with vocabulary size, i.e., (V×O)²), SHOE precomputes verb-verb (V²) and object-object (O²) similarity scores. For any new HOI prediction (v_p, o_p) and ground truth (v_g, o_g), the final similarity is a function f(sim_v(v_p, v_g), sim_o(o_p, o_g)). This reduces the computational burden from ~10¹² comparisons (for 38M HOIs) to ~10⁷ verb and ~10⁸ object comparisons.
- **Core assumption:** Semantic similarity of a compound HOI phrase can be adequately captured by a weighted combination of its atomic verb and object similarities.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "decomposes each HOI prediction into its verb and object components... This enables flexible and scalable evaluation... supporting significantly larger HOI vocabularies."  
  [section 3.1] "Instead of scoring every possible verb-object pair combination... we only need to compute pairwise similarities among verbs and among objects... This design drastically increases the HOI vocabulary... while maintaining computational efficiency."
- **Break condition:** If verb-object interactions exhibit strong non-compositional semantics (e.g., "kick the bucket" ≠ "kick" + "bucket"), the additive model fails to capture idiomatic meanings.

### Mechanism 2: Multi-LLM Consensus for Human-Aligned Similarity
- **Claim:** Averaging similarity scores from multiple large language models (LLMs) produces a semantic similarity metric that aligns better with human judgment than single-model or embedding-based approaches.
- **Mechanism:** For each candidate verb/object synset pair, the system queries 5 different LLMs (DeepSeek-V3, Gemini, Llama, Qwen, Yi) to rate similarity on a 5-point scale using WordNet glosses. The final score is the mean of these ratings. This aggregates diverse model biases to approximate a consensus closer to human perception.
- **Core assumption:** Disagreements among capable LLMs reflect model-specific noise or bias; averaging mitigates this to yield a more robust, human-like score.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section 4.3] "Our study shows... SHOE achieves 85.73% agreement with the average human ratings, surpassing the 78.61% average agreement among human annotators."  
  [section 4.4] "Our proposed SHOE metric... achieved the highest agreement with annotators (85.73%)... By contrast, instance-based direct-matching [mAP] has the lowest agreement (38.90%)."  
  [supplementary, section 1] LLM ablation shows agreement is stable across model subsets (85.12%–85.82%), supporting the robustness of the consensus approach.
- **Break condition:** If all LLMs share a systematic bias (e.g., due to common training data), the consensus will amplify rather than reduce error. Performance degrades on domain-specific HOIs where LLMs lack exposure (e.g., niche laboratory actions).

### Mechanism 3: WordNet Synset Mapping for Sense Disambiguation
- **Claim:** Mapping lexical verb/object labels to specific WordNet synsets resolves polysemy, ensuring similarity comparisons operate on distinct conceptual senses rather than ambiguous string forms.
- **Mechanism:** Predicted and ground-truth labels are lemmatized and matched to the most appropriate WordNet sense. For example, "bank" (financial institution) vs. "bank" (river edge) are treated as different synsets. This prevents false high similarity scores from homonyms and enables structured graph traversals (hypernyms, hyponyms) to expand the evaluation vocabulary.
- **Core assumption:** WordNet’s sense inventory provides a sufficiently fine-grained and stable semantic framework for general HOI evaluation.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section 3] "We map each verb and object... to its corresponding WordNet synset... This disambiguation step mitigates lexical ambiguity, ensuring that semantic comparisons reflect intended meanings."  
  [section 4.1] "Using the WordNet graph structure, we expand each label to include semantically related nodes... resulting in a significantly broader set of candidate HOI classes while maintaining semantic alignment."
- **Break condition:** WordNet’s coverage is incomplete for emerging, technical, or culturally specific verbs/objects. Polysemous words with many rare senses may map incorrectly, corrupting the similarity score.

## Foundational Learning
- **WordNet Synsets & Lexical Semantics**  
  *Why needed:* The entire SHOE pipeline depends on accurate sense disambiguation. Understanding synsets, hypernymy/hyponymy, and glosses is essential to grasp how verbs/objects are compared.  
  *Quick check:* If "keyboard" appears in a prediction, how would you determine whether it refers to the computer peripheral (keyboard.n.01) or the set of keys in a musical instrument (keyboard.n.02)? What WordNet properties would you use?

- **Graded Similarity vs. Exact Matching**  
  *Why needed:* SHOE’s core innovation is replacing binary HOI class matching with a continuous similarity score. This requires understanding precision/recall trade-offs in a soft setting.  
  *Quick check:* In standard mAP, a prediction "ride motorcycle" for GT "drive motorcycle" is a false positive. In SHOE, it might get a partial score. How does this change the interpretation of a model’s "error"? Does it encourage or discourage lexical diversity?

- **Bounding Box Matching Pre-filter**  
  *Why needed:* SHOE only computes semantic similarity for human-object pairs that pass an IoU threshold. This spatial pre-filter is a prerequisite to avoid semantic scoring on mismatched spatial locations.  
  *Quick check:* Given a prediction with high semantic similarity but low IoU on the object box, should it contribute to the score? Why does SHOE’s design exclude it from the semantic similarity calculation?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Processor -> Similarity Matrix Builder -> Matcher & Scorer -> Aggregator  
- **Critical path:** Similarity Matrix Builder → Matcher & Scorer → Aggregator. The matrix is built once per dataset; evaluation repeatedly queries it.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *LLM cost vs. coverage:* Exhaustive LLM scoring for all 850k verb pairs is expensive. Paper uses Qwen3-32B for initial filtering (sparsification) then averages 4 other LLMs on non-zero pairs. This trades some recall for massive cost savings.  
  - *Aggregation function:* Paper uses arithmetic mean (w=0.5) after MAE tuning showed category-specific weights. Simpler than learned weighting but may not capture all HOI semantics.  
  - *Confidence handling:* Provides two modes (mAP-style with token probs, and confidence-free mF1) to accommodate VLMs' poorly calibrated confidence.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *High FN rate:* The GT miss rate (unmatched GTs) indicates poor spatial localization or very low similarity scores. Check IoU thresholds and synonym coverage in WordNet.  
  - *High FP rate:* The prediction miss rate suggests models generate HOIs outside the expanded WordNet vocabulary or with truly low semantic alignment. Inspect unmatched predictions' synset mappings.  
  - *Low per-class SHOE-AP:* Indicates a specific HOI class (verb-object pair) is semantically mismatched. Debug by inspecting the verb/object similarity lookup values for that class's neighbors.  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline Reproduction:** Run SHOE on a standard HOI detector (e.g., HOLA) using the provided lookup tables. Verify SHOE mAP is slightly higher than standard mAP, as in Table 2. Confirms the pipeline integrates correctly.  
  2. **Ablation - Similarity Source:** Replace the multi-LLM similarity scores with a single LLM (e.g., DeepSeek-V3) or CLIP gloss embeddings. Measure change in human agreement (Table 1) or model rankings. Validates the consensus design.  
  3. **Vocabulary Stress Test:** Evaluate a model on a *synthetic* open-vocabulary set: take HICO-DET GTs, replace object "bicycle" with hypernym "vehicle" or hyponym "mountain bike". SHOE should give partial credit; mAP should drop to zero. Tests robustness to lexical variation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** What architectural changes or training paradigms can enable real-time, open-vocabulary HOI detection without relying on separate object detectors, as required for practical deployment?  
  *Basis:* [explicit] "This highlights the need to develop true open-vocabulary, real-time HOI predictors that combine strong localization with true semantic understanding." (Section: Future Work)  
  *Why unresolved:* Current VLMs and MLLMs are large (1.5B–400B parameters) and depend on external detectors like DETR, making them inefficient and unsuitable for real-time or video applications.  
  *Resolution evidence:* Development of a model that jointly performs object detection and interaction classification with competitive accuracy on standard benchmarks (e.g., HICO-DET) while achieving real-time inference speeds (<30ms per image) on commodity hardware.

- **Question 2:** How should annotation guidelines be standardized to ensure exhaustive and fine-grained HOI coverage in future benchmarks, thereby enabling reliable evaluation of open-vocabulary models?  
  *Basis:* [explicit] "As the field moves toward open-vocabulary HOI recognition, it will be essential to establish clear annotation guidelines and to develop more comprehensive datasets." (Section: Future Work)  
  *Why unresolved:* Existing benchmarks like HICO-DET and SWIG-HOI either have limited interaction sets, use coarse action labels, or miss multiple interactions per image, which penalizes valid open-vocabulary predictions.  
  *Resolution evidence:* Release of a new benchmark with exhaustive, fine-grained HOI annotations for all humans and objects in each image, accompanied by a detailed annotation protocol that demonstrates high inter-annotator agreement (>0.8 Cohen’s κ).

- **Question 3:** How can SHOE be modified to handle domain-specific interactions where WordNet coverage is insufficient, and how can biases from LLM-based similarity scoring be mitigated?  
  *Basis:* [explicit] "For building highly domain-specific HOI datasets with low WordNet coverage (e.g., medical or laboratory settings) we suggest using definitions from common ontologies in the field such as UMLS and reducing risks of domain biases by conducting an expert user study, similar to the methodology adopted in this work." (Section: Limitations)  
  *Why unresolved:* The current SHOE implementation uses WordNet and generalist LLMs, which may not capture specialized terminology or may reflect cultural biases from pretraining data.  
  *Resolution evidence:* A case study applying SHOE to a domain-specific HOI dataset (e.g., surgical instruments) using a tailored ontology and expert validation, showing improved alignment with domain expert judgments compared to the original SHOE.

- **Question 4:** What methods can better capture verb similarity for HOI evaluation, given that LLM agreements on verb pairs are significantly lower than for object pairs?  
  *Basis:* [inferred] The reported Pearson correlations between LLMs are lower for verbs (range 0.50–0.72) than for objects (up to 0.84), and the authors note "the broader semantic range and polysemy of actions complicate verb similarity scoring." (Section 4.2, Figure 3)  
  *Why unresolved:* Verb similarity is crucial to SHOE’s accuracy, but current LLM-based ratings show substantial disagreement, potentially harming metric reliability.  
  *Resolution evidence:* Introduction of a dedicated verb similarity model or dataset (e.g., verb embeddings trained on action-oriented corpora) that significantly improves inter-model agreement (e.g., >0.9 Pearson) and better correlates with human judgments than existing LLM ensembles.

## Limitations
- Exact LLM prompting template is undisclosed, critical for reproducibility and score fidelity.
- Sense disambiguation algorithm for WordNet mapping (handling polysemy) is unspecified.
- Compositionality assumption (additive verb+object similarity) may fail for idiomatic/non-compositional HOIs.
- Evaluation primarily on HICO-DET; robustness on diverse open-vocabulary distributions remains untested.
- Omitted details: δ threshold for FP assignment, score normalization from 0–4 to 0–1.

## Confidence
- **Scalability via decomposition:** High. Theoretical reduction from O((V·O)²) to O(V²+O²) is sound; break condition possible but unverified.
- **Multi-LLM consensus yields human-aligned similarity:** Medium. 85.73% agreement vs. 78.61% human is strong, but prompt/model sensitivity unknown.
- **WordNet sense disambiguation effectiveness:** Medium. Relies on WordNet coverage and disambiguation accuracy, neither quantified.
- **SHOE as a superior metric for open-vocabulary HOI:** Medium. Empirical VLM gains are compelling but may be benchmark-specific.
