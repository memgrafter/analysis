# Improving Diffusion Planners by Self-Supervised Action Gating with Energies

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02650  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02650  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** SAGE improves strong diffusion planners on D4RL benchmarks (e.g., MuJoCo average from 82.9 to 84.4, Kitchen Partial from 90.0 to 96.6).

## Executive Summary
Diffusion planners in offline reinforcement learning often select trajectories with high value scores but local dynamic inconsistencies, leading to brittle execution. SAGE addresses this by introducing an inference‑time feasibility re‑ranker that penalizes such inconsistencies. It trains a JEPA encoder and an action‑conditioned latent predictor offline on state sequences, then at test time assigns each candidate plan a latent prediction error energy over its early prefix. This energy is combined with the planner’s value score for selection, requiring no environment interaction or policy retraining.

Across D4RL benchmarks, SAGE yields consistent gains: MuJoCo average rises from 82.9 to 84.4, Kitchen Partial from 90.0 to 96.6, AntMaze from 81.6 to 84.5, and Maze2D from 161.6 to 163.1. Controlled corruption tests show the energy sharply spikes when actions are locally perturbed, confirming it acts as a feasibility signal. The added computational overhead is modest (~6.8% relative to the baseline).

## Method Summary
SAGE operates in two offline training stages followed by a lightweight inference procedure. First, a JEPA encoder is trained on masked state windows using self‑supervision with an EMA teacher and VICReg regularization, producing a frozen embedding function. Second, an action‑conditioned Transformer predictor is trained on latent‑action windows using the frozen encoder; it predicts the next latent given the current latent and action, using a combined one‑step loss, short‑horizon rollout loss (H_ro=4), and an action‑usage hinge. At test time, given a pre‑trained diffusion planner that generates C candidate trajectories, SAGE computes for each candidate an energy E(τ) equal to the mean L1 prediction error of the latent predictor over the first K transitions. It keeps the lowest‑energy P fraction of candidates and selects the one maximizing J(τ) – λE(τ), where J(τ) is the value critic score. No environment rollouts or policy retraining are needed.

## Key Results
- SAGE yields consistent improvements across D4RL benchmarks: MuJoCo +1.5, Kitchen Partial +6.6, AntMaze +2.9, Maze2D +1.5 in normalized score.
- The feasibility energy spikes sharply on locally corrupted actions (AUROC >0.94), successfully localizing dynamic infeasibility.
- Inference overhead is modest (~6.8% relative to the MCSS baseline).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Latent Consistency Energy Localizes Dynamic Infeasibility
- **Claim:** The latent consistency energy computed by an action‑conditioned predictor localises dynamic infeasibility in candidate plan prefixes.
- **Mechanism:** A frozen JEPA encoder embeds state windows into a latent space. An action‑conditioned Transformer predictor takes [z_t, a_t] tokens and predicts z_{t+1}. The per‑step ℓ1 error between predicted and actual latent is averaged over the first K steps to produce an energy E(τ). Low energy indicates the prefix aligns with dataset‑supported dynamics.
- **Core assumption:** The JEPA latent space preserves local transition structure such that prediction error correlates with feasibility; actions meaningfully influence next‑state predictions.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states SAGE assigns energy by latent prediction error; Section 4.2 shows that corrupting a short action window induces a sharp, localised energy spike (AUROC >0.94 across tasks).
- **Break condition:** If the predictor overfits to the offline distribution or test state distribution drifts significantly, energy may not correlate with true feasibility.

### Mechanism 2: Decoupling Feasibility from Value at Inference Time
- **Claim:** Decoupling feasibility from value at inference time prevents selection of high‑value but locally infeasible plans, improving robustness without retraining.
- **Mechanism:** The base diffusion planner generates candidates and scores them with a learned value critic J(τ). SAGE additionally computes E(τ) on the early prefix, filters the lowest‑energy P fraction, and selects the maximiser of J(τ) – λE(τ). This soft gating penalises infeasible prefixes while preserving the original value ranking.
- **Core assumption:** High‑value trajectories exist among the candidate set and are often accompanied by locally inconsistent prefixes; a linear combination of value and feasibility signals yields a better trade‑off than value alone.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions explicit separation of feasibility from value; Section 1 notes value optimisation encourages extrapolation while feasibility requires conservatism; Section 4.3 reports performance gains (e.g., MuJoCo from 82.9 to 84.4, Kitchen Partial from 90.0 to 96.6).
- **Break condition:** If value and feasibility signals conflict or λ is poorly tuned, the combined score may underperform the baseline.

### Mechanism 3: Inference‑Time Modularity and Low Overhead
- **Claim:** SAGE’s inference‑time operation makes it a modular plug‑in that adds minimal computational overhead while leveraging any strong diffusion planner.
- **Mechanism:** SAGE requires only a pre‑trained JEPA encoder and latent predictor; at each decision step it evaluates E(τ) for each candidate’s first K transitions (O(C·K) evaluations). No environment interaction, generator retraining, or policy distillation is needed. The base diffusion generator and critic remain untouched.
- **Core assumption:** The base diffusion planner already produces diverse candidates and has a reliable value scorer; SAGE’s additional evaluation is sufficiently lightweight.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states SAGE integrates without environment rollouts or policy re‑training; Section 4.2 reports overhead ~6.8% latency relative to MCSS baseline.
- **Break condition:** If the base planner generates too few candidates (small C), re‑ranking may not find a feasible high‑value plan; the extra O(C·K) cost may become prohibitive for very large candidate pools or long K.

## Foundational Learning
### Offline Reinforcement Learning
- **Why needed:** SAGE addresses the distribution‑shift problem in offline RL, where a policy must act solely from a fixed dataset without further environment interaction. The feasibility problem stems from candidates that drift off the dataset’s dynamical support.
- **Quick check:** What is the core challenge in offline RL that makes value‑based selection prone to selecting brittle plans?

### Diffusion Models for Trajectory Planning
- **Why needed:** Diffusion planners model a distribution over state‑action trajectories, generating diverse candidates via iterative denoising. SAGE assumes such a generator exists and focuses on improving candidate selection.
- **Quick check:** How does a diffusion planner typically act in a receding‑horizon loop?

### Joint‑Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)
- **Why needed:** JEPA provides a self‑supervised way to learn latent representations where predictable future states are close in embedding space, without reconstructing observations. SAGE uses a frozen JEPA encoder to embed states for the feasibility predictor.
- **Quick check:** What objective does a JEPA encoder optimise, and what does low prediction error indicate about a transition?

### Energy‑Based Reranking
- **Why needed:** SAGE defines an energy as latent prediction error and uses it to penalise infeasible candidates during selection. This is a lightweight, test‑time only mechanism that does not alter the generator.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Stage I JEPA Encoder → Stage II Latent Predictor → Base Diffusion Planner → SAGE Inference

**Critical path:**
1. Collect offline trajectories D.
2. Train Stage I JEPA on state windows (Algorithm 1) → obtain frozen encoder.
3. Train Stage II action‑conditioned predictor with frozen encoder (Eq. 10).
4. At each test decision step:
   - Sample C candidates from diffusion planner.
   - Compute energy E(τ) for each candidate on first K transitions (Eq. 11).
   - Filter to set I_t of lowest‑energy P fraction.
   - Choose τ* = argmax_{i∈I_t} (J(τ_i) – λE(τ_i)).
   - Execute first action of τ*.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Prefix length K:** Longer K provides more context but risks compounding prediction error; moderate K≈10 optimal.
- **Keep rate P:** Aggressive filtering (small P) removes infeasible candidates but may discard feasible high‑value plans; conservative (P≈1) weakens feasibility signal.
- **Penalty λ:** Balances feasibility and value; too large biases to easy‑to‑predict plans, too small leaves infeasible candidates unchecked. Paper uses λ=0.1 after offline calibration.
- **Representation choice:** JEPA (vs. random latents or state‑space forward models) yields stronger feasibility discrimination (AUROC≈0.98) thanks to offline predictive pre‑training.

**Failure signatures:**
- No performance gain over baseline: Possibly λ too low, P too high, or base planner already generates mostly feasible plans.
- Overly conservative behaviour: If P very low or λ very high, selected plans may be feasible but low value.
- Energy not spiking on corruptions: Indicates failed Stage II training or representation mismatch; check predictor loss and corruption diagnostic.
- High computational overhead: If C or K are too large, O(C·K) evaluations may dominate; monitor latency.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Corruption localisation test:** Take a clean dataset trajectory, shuffle actions within a short window, compute per‑step energy. Expect a sharp spike aligned with the corrupted window (AUROC >0.9).
2. **Ablation over K, P, λ:** Vary each hyperparameter individually while holding others fixed, evaluate on a subset of D4RL tasks. Confirm performance peaks at moderate values (e.g., K=10, P=0.8, λ=0.1) and degrades at extremes.
3. **Representation comparison:** Train alternative feasibility models (state‑space ridge/MLP, random‑latent ridge) on the same data and compare discrimination AUROC on action‑shuffled negatives. Expect JEPA+AC predictor to outperform, justifying the design.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: Does SAGE improve guided diffusion planners (e.g., with classifier‑free guidance)?
- **Basis in paper:** Evaluated only on generate‑and‑rank (DV); guided methods not tested.
- **Why unresolved:** Guidance alters candidate distribution; SAGE’s post‑hoc energy may behave differently.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply SAGE to guided planners (e.g., Diffuser+guidance, IDQL) on D4RL; compare scores and energy patterns.

### Open Question 2: Can SAGE work with image observations using a convolutional encoder?
- **Basis in paper:** Experiments used state vectors and MLP; images need different architecture.
- **Why unresolved:** Feasibility energy relies on latent prediction; images may degrade representation quality.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Test on visual offline RL (e.g., D4RL images, Atari) with CNN JEPA; compare performance and energy.

### Open Question 3: Can hyperparameters (K, P, λ) be automatically tuned per task?
- **Basis in paper:** Paper uses fixed values; ablations show sensitivity.
- **Why unresolved:** Different domains may need different settings; fixed defaults could be suboptimal.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform per‑task hyperparameter search on validation data; compare to fixed and test adaptive rules.

## Limitations
- Depends on a pre‑trained diffusion planner; gains may not transfer to weaker or differently trained generators.
- Hyperparameters (K, P, λ) need per‑task tuning; no principled automatic selection method provided.
- JEPA latent space may overfit to offline data distribution; under test‑time distribution shift, energy may cease to correlate with true feasibility.

## Confidence
