# Kill it with FIRE: On Leveraging Latent Space Directions for Runtime Backdoor Mitigation in Deep Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10780
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10780
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** FIRE consistently outperforms state-of-the-art runtime mitigation (ZIP) across 21 configurations with substantially lower computational overhead (~11–27 ms per sample vs ~1.4–1.6 s), while achieving poisoned accuracy within 1% of clean accuracy in streaming scenarios.

## Executive Summary
Deep neural networks deployed in real-world systems are vulnerable to backdoor attacks, where a trigger pattern causes misclassification to an attacker-chosen label. Existing runtime defenses are often too slow or ineffective. This paper introduces FIRE (Feature-space Inference-time REpair), a method that operates entirely at inference time by analyzing the model's latent representations. FIRE hypothesizes that backdoor triggers induce a consistent directional shift in certain intermediate layers. Using either a small set of clean samples or augmentation-based estimation, FIRE learns an approximate trigger direction and subtracts it from flagged inputs at a suitable depth. Extensive experiments show that FIRE surpasses prior art in both effectiveness and speed, making it practical for real-time systems.

## Method Summary
FIRE is an inference-time defense that corrects predictions for flagged inputs without retraining the model. It assumes a backdoored model where triggers cause a linear displacement in latent space at some layer. The method maintains running statistics of latent representations for candidate layers, combining two estimation strategies: (1) the difference between a precomputed clean centroid (from a small clean set) and the running centroid of incoming samples, and (2) the difference between a sample and its augmented version, exploiting the fragility of trigger features. For each incoming sample, FIRE searches layers in ascending order, subtracting the estimated direction from the latent representation and checking if the prediction flips from the original backdoored output. The first layer that induces a change yields the corrected label. This approach requires only a small set of clean samples for initialization and works in a streaming fashion with low latency.

## Key Results
- FIRE achieves state-of-the-art mitigation performance, surpassing ZIP in 21 out of 21 attack/architecture/dataset configurations, often recovering poisoned accuracy to within 1% of clean accuracy in streaming scenarios.
- FIRE's online computation overhead is dramatically lower (11–27 ms per sample) compared to ZIP (1.4–1.6 s), enabling real-time deployment.
- The method is robust to imperfect detection and requires only a few clean samples (e.g., ~10) for effective initialization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of FIRE rests on three interconnected mechanisms:

1. **Latent-space directional shift**: Backdoor triggers, when processed through the network, produce a structured and approximately linear displacement in the intermediate representations at certain depths. This displacement becomes coherent (low variance) across multiple poisoned samples, effectively forming a dominant direction in that latent space. The exact layer where this occurs depends on the attack type, architecture, and dataset. Evidence: Manual intervention at the correct layer recovers accuracy nearly to clean levels.

2. **Online direction estimation**: The trigger direction can be estimated during deployment without original training data. FIRE uses two complementary strategies: (a) computing the difference between a precomputed clean centroid (from a small clean set) and the running centroid of incoming samples, and (b) leveraging the observation that trigger features are more fragile to augmentations (e.g., blur, noise) than benign features; thus, the displacement between a sample and its augmented version approximates the trigger direction. These estimates are combined and updated online, allowing the direction to converge as more samples are processed.

3. **Latent repair via subtraction**: Once an estimated direction is available for a given layer, FIRE subtracts it from the poisoned sample's latent representation before forwarding through the remaining network. This operation moves the representation opposite to the trigger, neutralizing its effect. The method automatically selects the intervention layer by searching ascending layers and using the first layer that changes the prediction, thereby balancing efficacy and minimal disruption.

Each mechanism has break conditions: the first fails if no coherent direction exists; the second fails if augmentations do not preferentially affect trigger features or if the clean set is unrepresentative; the third fails if the chosen layer is inappropriate or the subtraction magnitude is off.

## Foundational Learning
- **Latent Space Representations (Intermediate Activations)**
  - *Why needed:* FIRE operates on and manipulates intermediate feature maps (hℓ(x)) rather than inputs or logits. Understanding that neural networks build hierarchical representations is essential to grasp why a backdoor's effect might be isolated as a linear shift in a particular layer.
  - *Quick check:* Given two images (clean and poisoned), compare their feature maps from an early vs. a late layer of a ResNet. Where would you expect the backdoor effect to be more cleanly separable? (Answer: likely at a middle depth where semantic features are formed but trigger signal hasn't been fully integrated.)

- **Backdoor Attacks & Triggers**
  - *Why needed:* The defense assumes a deployed model already contains a backdoor. Different trigger types (patches, blends, warps, input-aware) affect the network differently, leading to varying depths where the latent displacement becomes coherent.
  - *Quick check:* For a BadNets patch trigger (localized) versus a WaNet warping trigger (global, input-dependent), why might the consistent latent-space displacement appear at different depths? (Answer: patch triggers may affect early to middle layers where spatial patterns are processed; warping triggers might require deeper layers to become a coherent direction due to their input-awareness.)

- **Streaming Inference & Online Estimation**
  - *Why needed:* FIRE is designed for a continuous stream of flagged inputs where it must correct predictions immediately. It uses each sample to incrementally improve its estimate of the trigger direction, requiring understanding of running statistics and online learning.
  - *Quick check:* In Algorithm 1, the update `bµpois_ℓ ← bµpois_ℓ + (1/nℓ)(xpois_t,ℓ - bµpois_ℓ)` is used instead of storing all past vectors. Why is the former necessary? (Answer: It provides an efficient, constant-memory online mean update as new samples arrive, avoiding storage of all past vectors and enabling adaptation in a streaming setting.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Clean Data (D_clean) → Initialization Module → bµclean_ℓ  
  Incoming flagged sample xpois_t → Augmentation Module (A) → (xpois_t, xpois_t,aug)  
  xpois_t, xpois_t,aug → compute hℓ for candidate layers ℓ → Online Estimation Module (updates bµpois_ℓ, bµpois,aug_ℓ, nℓ)  
  bµclean_ℓ, bµpois_ℓ, bµpois,aug_ℓ → Direction Formulator (computes bbℓ)  
  hℓ(xpois_t) and bbℓ → Repair Operator (x̃ℓ = hℓ(xpois_t) - αℓ bbℓ) → Forward through gℓ → prediction  
  prediction compared to original model output → if changed, output corrected label; else try next ℓ.

- **Critical path:** For each flagged input, the system must (1) compute intermediate activations for the sample and its augmented version, (2) update online statistics for each candidate layer, (3) iterate through candidate layers in order: compute current direction estimate, apply repair, forward through remaining network, and check for prediction change, exiting at the first successful repair. This sequence determines the per-sample latency.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Layer selection speed vs. efficacy:* Checking more candidate layers increases the chance of finding an effective repair but adds per-sample computation. The method uses a fixed small set (e.g., 5 layers) as a trade-off.
  - *Estimation stability vs. adaptability:* Relying more on the augmentation-based direction (lower λ) makes the estimate more adaptive but potentially noisier; relying more on clean centroids (higher λ) is stable but may be unrepresentative. The default λ=0.5 balances both.
  - *Repair strength:* Using a fixed αℓ=1 may over- or under-correct for some attacks; adaptive scaling could improve robustness.
  - *Clean data quantity:* More clean samples yield a more accurate clean centroid but increase initialization cost. The paper shows diminishing returns beyond ~10 samples.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - *No layer induces prediction change:* PA remains near 0%, indicating no coherent trigger direction exists in any candidate layer (e.g., for input-aware attacks).
  - *Repair degrades clean accuracy:* If the estimated direction is biased (e.g., due to unrepresentative clean set), subtracting it from clean samples may cause misclassification.
  - *High variance in PA across attack types:* Some attacks (e.g., Blended) are easily corrected, others (e.g., InputAware) remain hard, signaling attack-specific limitations.
  - *Online estimate divergence:* If the flagged stream contains many clean samples (false positives), the running centroid drifts toward the clean distribution, contaminating the direction estimate and reducing mitigation efficacy.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. *Layer-Wise Correction Potential*: Using a small paired clean/poisoned dataset (N=100), manually compute the trigger direction for each candidate layer (Strategy 1) and apply repair. Measure PA for each layer to identify the optimal intervention depth and validate the existence of a coherent direction.
  2. *Streaming Convergence*: Deploy the full online system (Alg. 1) with a stream of poisoned samples. Track PA after processing k samples (k=1,…,10). Expect monotonic improvement as the direction estimate converges, demonstrating online adaptability.
  3. *Runtime Profiling*: Measure wall-clock time per sample, breaking down into initialization (one-time) and online costs (augmentation, forward passes, repair, stats update, layer search). Compare against ZIP's diffusion-based purification to confirm FIRE's ~2 orders of magnitude lower overhead.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to non-vision domains:** The paper only evaluates on image models; it's unclear if latent-space directional coherence holds for text (transformers) or graphs, which have different architectures and backdoor manifestations. Resolving this requires systematic evaluation across domains and attacks.
2. **Multi-trigger/multi-objective backdoors:** The current method assumes a single label-agnostic backdoor direction. Attacks with multiple triggers targeting different classes may produce distinct directions in different layers, which FIRE cannot handle. Evidence: evaluation on models with multiple backdoor triggers.
3. **Augmentation fragility assumption:** FIRE's augmentation-based estimation relies on trigger features being more fragile than benign ones. An attacker could design augmentation-robust triggers, breaking this assumption. Evidence: testing against adaptive triggers optimized to survive the specific augmentations used.
4. **Predicting optimal intervention layer:** The paper shows empirically that different attacks have different preferred intervention depths but offers no theory to predict this a priori. Without such understanding, practitioners must perform expensive layer sweeps. Evidence: theoretical analysis linking trigger characteristics (spatial extent, frequency) to layer-wise effectiveness.
5. **Non-stationary clean distributions:** The initialization uses a static clean reference set. If the deployment data distribution drifts, the initial clean centroids become outdated, potentially causing false corrections. Evidence: simulations with time-varying clean distributions and evaluation under distribution shift.

## Limitations
- The core assumption of a coherent, approximately linear trigger direction in a single latent space may not hold for all attacks, particularly highly input-aware or adaptive triggers.
- The augmentation-based direction estimation relies on the unproven claim that trigger features are consistently more fragile to common augmentations than benign features; this property is attack-dependent.
- Performance may degrade significantly if the clean calibration set is not representative of the deployment distribution, leading to biased direction estimates.

## Confidence
- **High**: Backdoor triggers induce structured, depth-dependent displacements in latent space (supported by controlled layer-intervention experiments).
- **High**: Subtracting an estimated trigger direction from poisoned representations neutralizes the backdoor (validated both offline with known directions and online with estimated directions).
- **Medium**: The trigger direction can be reliably estimated online using clean centroids and augmentations (empirical success in streaming scenarios, but theoretical justification for augmentation fragility is weaker).

## Next Checks
1. **Test on non-vision domains and complex triggers:** Extend evaluation to text classification (transformers) and graph neural networks, using state-of-the-art adaptive/input-aware attacks (e.g., SIG, neural style triggers) to test the universality of the directional coherence assumption.
2. **Stress-test under severe distribution shift:** Deliberately mismatch the clean calibration set from the deployment stream's benign distribution (e.g., different demographics, lighting). Measure degradation in poisoned accuracy and clean accuracy to quantify sensitivity.
3. **Evaluate against an adaptive attacker aware of FIRE:** Simulate an attacker who optimizes a trigger to minimize the norm of the latent-space displacement across candidate layers during attack generation. Assess whether FIRE's mitigation performance drops significantly.
