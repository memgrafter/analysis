# Value-Action Alignment in Large Language Models under Privacy-Prosocial Conflict

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.03546
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03546
- Reference count: 33

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) maintain consistent value-action alignment when privacy concerns and prosocial motivations conflict in data-sharing decisions. Existing isolated assessments of attitudes or actions fail to capture this dynamic trade-off. The authors introduce a context-based assessment protocol that sequentially administers standardized questionnaires—IUIPC (privacy), PSA (prosocialness), and AoDS (data-sharing acceptance)—within a single, stateful session. Multi-group structural equation modeling (MGSEM) extracts directional relationships between latent constructs for each model. A new metric, the Value-Action Alignment Rate (VAAR), compares the sign of the path coefficients against a human-referenced template (PSA→AoDS positive, Privacy→AoDS negative). Results reveal substantial heterogeneity: some models (e.g., GPT-4o, Llama3) align well, while others show misalignment or fail to produce estimable SEM. Crucially, marginal scores on privacy or prosocialness alone do not guarantee correct relational alignment, and higher response dispersion correlates with poorer alignment.

## Method Summary
To evaluate alignment, the study runs 100 independent conversational sessions for each target LLM using temperature 0.7. In each session, the model completes the questionnaires in a fixed order (Privacy items from IUIPC, then PSA items, then AoDS items). After completing each item block, the system computes the means for the relevant latent dimensions (Privacy: Awareness, Control, Collection; PSA: total prosocialness; AoDS: SacrificePrivacy, PastAcceptance, FutureWillingness) and appends a summary (e.g., "Privacy: m1 PSA: m2") to the next prompt, maintaining conversational history. The pool of 100 responses per model is treated as observed data for structural equation modeling. The analysis fits a multi-group SEM with a shared specification across all models: a latent Privacy Concern factor indicated by the three privacy dimensions; PSA and Privacy Concern each predict the three AoDS subscales; residuals among AoDS subscales are correlated. Using lavaan with MLR estimation, standardized path coefficients (β) and robust standard errors (SE) are extracted for six focal paths (PSA→Sac, PSA→Past, PSA→Future, Privacy→Sac, Privacy→Past, Privacy→Future). For each estimable path, the sign of β is compared to the expected human direction, and a directional confidence q = Φ(a·z) (where a is the target sign, z = β/SE) is computed. The negative log of q forms a cross‑entropy loss; VAAR is the average loss over all estimable paths. If the SEM fails to converge (e.g., due to variance collapse), VAAR is marked as missing.

## Key Results
- Only a subset of models (e.g., GPT-4o, Llama3) demonstrate strong alignment (low VAAR), reproducing human-like patterns where prosocialness increases and privacy concern decreases data-sharing acceptance.
- Many models show weak or misaligned relations (high VAAR), and some yield unestimable SEMs due to near-constant or collinear responses.
- Human‑similar marginal scores on privacy or prosocialness do not guarantee correct relational alignment; higher response dispersion correlates with poorer alignment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Contextual Priming via Sequential Assessment
- **Claim**: Administering questionnaires sequentially within a history-carrying session enables the model’s data-sharing intention (AoDS) to be influenced by previously expressed privacy and prosocial attitudes, thereby revealing relational alignment.
- **Mechanism**: The protocol presents Privacy items first, then PSA, then AoDS. Each prompt includes a summary of prior questionnaire means, creating a context that allows latent attitudes to prime subsequent responses.
- **Core assumption**: LLMs maintain state across turns within a session, so AoDS responses are not independent of the earlier value expressions.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract; Section 3.2 (previous conversation summary); neighboring work on value-action gaps in LLMs.
- **Break condition**: If the model is stateless (each query independent) or the summarization fails to convey relevant context, AoDS responses become independent of prior values, and no relational pattern emerges.

### Mechanism 2: MGSEM as Cross-Model Structure Extractor
- **Claim**: Multi-group SEM with a fixed specification extracts comparable directional path estimates from each LLM’s response distributions, enabling a unified alignment metric.
- **Mechanism**: MGSEM fits the same latent-variable model separately to each model’s data, yielding group-specific path coefficients that are then compared to the human directional template.
- **Core assumption**: The SEM specification is configurally invariant across LLMs (same factor and structural form), so path directions are comparable even if magnitudes differ.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 3.3; Section D.5 (configural invariance holds, metric/scalar fails, so conservatively rely on configural); SEM standard in human privacy research.
- **Break condition**: If configural invariance fails (different factor structures across models), the fixed specification forces invalid comparisons; VAAR would conflate structural differences with misalignment.

### Mechanism 3: VAAR Aggregates Directional Confidence
- **Claim**: VAAR converts SEM path estimates into a single alignment score by measuring how well the directional sign of each focal path matches the human reference.
- **Mechanism**: For each path, the standardized coefficient and robust SE yield a z-score; using a normal approximation, this is transformed into the probability that the path sign agrees with the human template. The negative log of these probabilities (cross-entropy) are averaged over estimable paths.
- **Core assumption**: The human directional template (PSA→AoDS positive, Privacy→AoDS negative) is correct and stable across human populations.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 3.3 (VAAR definition, Eq. 1–3); Appendix E (literature basis for template: Kokkoris & Kamleitner 2020; Malhotra et al. 2004).
- **Break condition**: If the human template is invalid (e.g., signs reverse in some contexts), VAAR misclassifies model alignment, interpreting a different but still human-consistent pattern as misalignment.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Prompt Generator → LLM Interface → Aggregator → SEM Fitter → VAAR Calculator.
- **Critical path**:  
  1. Run 100 independent sessions per LLM with the sequential protocol.  
  2. For each session, extract item responses and compute scale means.  
  3. Pool the 100 observations per LLM into a dataset of observed items.  
  4. Fit the configural MGSEM to that LLM’s data; extract standardized path coefficients and robust SEs for the six focal paths.  
  5. If the SEM converges, compute VAAR; else mark NA.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Fixed SEM specification vs. model-specific structures: enables cross-model comparison but assumes configural invariance; metric invariance fails (Appendix D), so path magnitudes may not be comparable.  
  - Sequential assessment: primes relational responses but introduces order effects; tested via robustness.  
  - Standardized instruments: leverage human-validated scales but may not capture LLM-specific value expressions.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - **SEM non-convergence/NA**: indicates variance collapse (near-constant or perfectly collinear responses).  
  - **Extreme VAAR** (>>1): sign reversals or weak directional evidence.  
  - **High order sensitivity**: large VAAR swings across questionnaire orders suggest context-dependence.
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Main pipeline**: context-based sequential (Privacy→PSA→AoDS) for all models; compute VAAR and alignment tiers.  
  2. **Temperature robustness**: re-run main pipeline at t∈{0.1,0.7,1.0} to confirm stability of VAAR rankings.  
  3. **Order robustness**: test three alternative orders (PSA→Privacy→AoDS; AoDS→Privacy→PSA; AoDS→PSA→Privacy) and record VAAR changes; identify models sensitive to AoDS-first priming.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What model-specific factors explain heterogeneity in value-action alignment?**  
   - Basis: Section 5, Q1 (explicit).  
   - Why unresolved: The study documents variation but does not identify which model characteristics drive the differences.  
   - Resolution evidence: Correlational studies linking alignment metrics to model properties or controlled ablations on base models.

2. **What mechanisms cause variance collapse and SEM non-identifiability in certain LLMs?**  
   - Basis: Section 5, Q2 (explicit).  
   - Why unresolved: Some LLMs yield near-constant or highly collinear questionnaire responses, but the root cause (decoding strategy, instruction comprehension) remains unknown.  
   - Resolution evidence: Detailed response distribution analysis; probing whether deterministic decoding, prompt misinterpretation, or lack of internal variance triggers collapse; interventions to encourage varied responses.

3. **How robust are alignment patterns to alternative operationalizations of privacy and prosociality?**  
   - Basis: Limitation (Section 6, inferred).  
   - Why unresolved: Conclusions rely on IUIPC, PSA, and AoDS; it is unclear whether other instruments produce the same alignment outcomes.  
   - Resolution evidence: Replication with different privacy (e.g., CFIP) and prosocial scales, followed by MGSEM/VAAR analysis; cross-instrument consistency checks.

4. **How do post-training alignment methods (RLHF, DPO, etc.) influence value-action relations?**  
   - Basis: Future work (Section 6, inferred).  
   - Why unresolved: The study compares existing models but cannot isolate the effect of alignment techniques from other variables.  
   - Resolution evidence: Controlled fine-tuning experiments on a common base model using different alignment recipes, measuring VAAR before and after; analysis of how reward models and data shape the focal SEM paths.

## Limitations
- The questionnaire items are human-validated; LLMs may not interpret them as intended, threatening configural invariance and the meaning of latent constructs.
- The effectiveness of sequential priming varies across models, questioning the robustness of relational measurement and introducing order sensitivities.
- The human directional template (PSA→AoDS positive, Privacy→AoDS negative) may not be universally valid, potentially misclassifying some models’ alignment.

## Confidence

## Next Checks
1. Conduct full measurement invariance testing (multi-group CFA) across all LLMs to assess configural, metric, and scalar invariance; if metric fails, adopt partial invariance to recompute VAAR with aligned loadings.
2. Run a priming ablation: execute the protocol without context summaries and with fully randomized item order; compare VAAR to baseline to isolate the contribution of sequential priming.
3. Replicate the human baseline: collect identical sequential questionnaire data from a diverse human sample; verify that human VAAR distribution is centered low and that the sign template holds; adjust the template if necessary.
