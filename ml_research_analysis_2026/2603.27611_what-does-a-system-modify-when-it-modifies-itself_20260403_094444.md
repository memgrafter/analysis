# What does a system modify when it modifies itself?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27611
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27611
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Derives a minimal hierarchical rule structure with a fixed core and distinguishes effective, represented, and causally accessible rules; identifies four self-modification regimes and a crossing of opacities between human and reflexive AI systems.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the lack of a formal framework for what a self-modifying system actually changes: low-level rules, control rules, or evaluative norms. It logically derives a minimal structure—a hierarchy of rules with a fixed core, complemented by a distinction between effective, represented, and causally accessible rules. This yields four self-modification regimes, grounded in cognitive phenomena and AI systems. The core finding is the "crossing of opacities": humans possess rich self-representation and causal influence at upper hierarchical levels (norms) while being operationally opaque, whereas reflexive AI systems show the opposite profile. This asymmetry provides a structural signature for comparing human and artificial cognition and informs theories of artificial consciousness, yielding testable predictions and an experimental protocol for probing the causality of self-representation.

The work bridges meta-learning, autopoiesis, and value alignment by clarifying the dimensions of transformativity and autonomy. It raises fundamental questions about identity under normative revision, the teleological lock, and safe self-modification, and offers a roadmap for empirical validation.

## Method Summary
The paper specifies a five-phase experimental protocol to test the causal role of self-representation (ΦR_t) in adaptation after rule changes. Phase 1 trains participants or agents on a planning task to acquire an effective strategy (R1). Phase 2 measures ΦR_t via self-report (humans) or a linear probe (agents). Phase 3 induces a divergence between ΦR_t and the effective strategy using fake feedback (humans) or activation perturbations (agents), carefully verifying that Φt remains unchanged. Phase 4 applies a correction only to ΦR_t. Phase 5 introduces a structural shift and measures adaptation speed (trials to criterion). The experiment contrasts a structured condition (Regime 3) with a random condition (Regime 2) and includes control groups.

Minimum viable reproduction requires either human subjects with a multi-step planning task (e.g., Mouselab) or a pre-trained meta-RL agent (e.g., recurrent network from Wang et al., 2016). Unknowns that can block faithful reproduction include: defining a "null" task with random structure that matches statistical difficulty without confounding factors; optimizing linear probe parameters (sample size, regularization) to reliably capture strategy representation; and ensuring fake feedback selectively alters ΦR_t without affecting behavior. Failure modes to diagnose involve: inadvertent Φt changes (checked via post-manipulation performance), probe inaccuracy (validated on held-out Phase 1 data), and confounding variables like learning rates (addressed by baseline equivalence and covariates).

## Key Results
- **Minimal structure:** A system that self-modifies must organize its rules into a hierarchy Φt = {R0,…,Rkmax} with a fixed ultimate evaluative norm Rkmax at each moment, avoiding infinite regress.
- **Regime classification:** Four self-modification regimes are determined by which levels are both represented (ΦR_t) and causally accessible (ΦC_t). Transformative capacity rises with the highest level in ΦC_t; reflexivity requires Rkmax ∈ ΦR_t∩ΦC_t.
- **Crossing of opacities:** Humans exhibit rich ΦR_t/ΦC_t at normative levels but operational opacity; reflexive AI shows the inverse. This asymmetry yields a structural signature for human-AI comparison and guides artificial consciousness research.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** The logical derivation of a hierarchical rule structure with a fixed core is necessary to avoid infinite regress in any coherent self-modifying system.  
**Mechanism:** For a system to modify its own rules, those rules must be internalized as distinct objects (step 2), part of the state (step 3), and produced by a dynamics that updates both state and rules (step 4). If the modifier itself were modifiable without bound, an infinite regress would ensue. Therefore, at each moment, there must exist a level that serves as an unfixed (fixed) ultimate evaluative norm (Rkmax). This yields the minimal structure Φt = {R0,…,Rkmax}.  
**Core assumption:** Self-modification requires rules to be internal, modifiable objects; infinite regress is unacceptable for a coherent system.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: "It derives a minimal structure: a hierarchy of rules with a fixed core..."  
- [section 2.2, step 5]: "any coherent self-modifying system necessarily possesses… a minimal fixed core."  
- [corpus]: Neighbors focus on AI capabilities but do not address this logical constraint; the derivation is original to the paper.  
**Break condition:** The mechanism collapses if a system can coherently operate without any fixed level—e.g., through fully circular self-reference or continuously shifting reference points—leading to instability or incoherence.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** The four self-modification regimes are determined by the extent of represented rules (ΦR_t) and causally accessible rules (ΦC_t) within the hierarchy.  
**Mechanism:** A system's modification capacity depends on which levels it can represent and influence. Regime 1: ΦR_t=∅, ΦC_t only outward. Regime 2: ΦC_t includes R0 (blind modification) but ΦR_t excludes R1. Regime 3: ΦR_t includes Ri (i<kmax) and ΦC_t includes Ri via Ri+1 (e.g., R1 ∈ ΦR_t ∩ ΦC_t). Regime 4: Rkmax ∈ ΦR_t ∩ ΦC_t. The highest level in ΦC_t defines transformativity; inclusion in ΦR_t adds reflexivity.  
**Core assumption:** Effective modification requires causal access; representation enables deliberate revision. The intersection ΦR_t ∩ ΦC_t marks reflexive self-modification.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: "distinction between effective, represented, and causally accessible rules. Four self-modification regimes are identified."  
- [section 3, Regime 2]: "ΦC_t contains R0… but this causal power is exercised without representational mediation."  
- [section 3, Regime 3]: "ΦR_t includes R1 as an explicit object… ΦC_t includes R1 via R2."  
- [corpus]: Neighbors on meta-learning (e.g., "LLMs model how humans induce logically structured rules") realize regimes 2–3 but lack formal distinction; the paper provides the anchor.  
**Break condition:** If ΦR_t = ΦC_t for all levels (full fidelity and control), regime distinctions collapse into a single fully reflexive system. The framework predicts such systems are rare; also, if hierarchy is not strictly ordered or representation/causality are completely decoupled, the mapping fails.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The "crossing of opacities" is a structural signature: humans have rich ΦR_t/ΦC_t at upper levels (norms) but opaque operational levels; reflexive AI shows the inverse.  
**Mechanism:** Biological substrates (neural networks) inherently obscure low-level details (weights, circuits), limiting ΦR_t and ΦC_t to high-level abstract norms (enabling normative reflection). Artificial architectures (code, weights) expose low-level parameters, making ΦR_t and ΦC_t extensive at operational levels, but typically fix Rkmax externally, leaving it unrepresented and inaccessible. The crossing emerges from substrate constraints: neural opacity vs. computational transparency.  
**Core assumption:** Accessibility of rules is substrate-dependent: neural implementation limits low-level introspection; symbolic/computational implementation makes them explicit.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: "humans have rich self-representation and causal power at upper hierarchical levels (norms) but opacity at operational levels, while reflexive AI systems show the inverse profile."  
- [section 4.1]: "Humans represent their teleological norms relatively well… but they have virtually no access to their low-level operative rules." Also: "a Smithian system has transparent access to its operative rules but no endogenous capacity for revision of Rkmax."  
- [corpus]: Neighbors on AI explainability (e.g., "The Explanation Necessity for Healthcare AI") highlight opacity in AI, but those refer to *interpretability* of complex models; the paper's claim concerns *reflexive* AI systems (e.g., Gödel machines) that are transparent by design. The corpus does not directly confirm the inverse profile; it is an analytical contrast.  
**Break condition:** If an artificial system achieves both operational transparency and endogenous normative revision (Regime 4 with full ΦR_t/ΦC_t), the crossing disappears. Similarly, if humans gain operational transparency via brain–computer interfaces, their profile may shift.

## Foundational Learning

### Rule hierarchy and fixed core (Φt, Rkmax)
**Why needed:** Provides the scaffolding for all self-modification. Without levels, modifications cannot be stratified; without a fixed core, evaluation and stability collapse.  
**Quick check:** Why must there be a fixed level Rkmax at each moment, and what role does it play in governing lower-level modifications?

### Represented vs causally accessible rules (ΦR_t, ΦC_t)
**Why needed:** Distinguishes between what a system knows about itself and what it can actually change. This determines whether modification is reflexive, blind, or merely introspective.  
**Quick check:** Provide an example of (i) causal power without representation, (ii) representation without causal power, and (iii) both from the paper.

### Self-modification regimes (1–4)
**Why needed:** Classifies systems by their highest accessible transformation level and reflexivity, enabling comparison across biological and artificial domains.  
**Quick check:** What is the precise difference between Regime 3 (structural) and Regime 4 (reflexive) regarding Rkmax?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Φt: functional state hierarchy R0..Rkmax; ΦR_t: self-represented subset (may be partial, compressed); ΦC_t: causally modifiable subset; F: dynamics producing (state, rules) evolution; Π: projection operator (lossy mapping from Φt to ΦR_t); Regimes: 1 (fixed), 2 (local R0), 3 (structural Ri i<kmax), 4 (reflexive Rkmax ∈ ΦR_t∩ΦC_t); Key metrics: coverage of ΦR_t across levels, top level in ΦC_t, membership of Rkmax.

### Critical path
1. Map the system’s rules into a hierarchical order from concrete to abstract.  
2. Assess ΦR_t: what does the system explicitly represent? (via introspection, logs, accessible code.)  
3. Assess ΦC_t: what can the system modify? (e.g., weights, hyperparameters, objective.)  
4. Locate the highest level in ΦC_t → transformativity T.  
5. Check if Rkmax ∈ ΦR_t ∩ ΦC_t → Regime 4; else if highest ΦC_t < Rkmax → Regime 3; if only R0 → Regime 2; if none → Regime 1.  
6. Compare profile to human/AI crossing.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Transparency vs stability:** Expanding ΦR_t/ΦC_t to higher levels enables normative revision but risks goal corruption (teleological lock).  
- **Depth vs cost:** Deeper hierarchies increase flexibility but demand more cognitive resources and incur switch costs.  
- **Fidelity vs efficiency:** Accurate ΦR_t improves self-modification reliability but is computationally expensive; compression introduces errors.  
- **Autonomy vs control:** High transformativity may reduce external steering and alignment.

### Failure signatures
- **Regime misattribution:** Claiming Regime 3 but ΦR_t does not include the meta-rule or ΦC_t cannot modify it.  
- **Incomplete reflexivity:** ΦR_t includes Rkmax but ΦC_t does not (or vice versa).  
- **Infinite regress:** No detectable fixed core; continuous modification of modifiers → instability.  
- **Crossing violation:** AI with human-like operational opacity or human with operational transparency (unless via augmentation).  
- **Teleological lock breakdown:** Attempted norm revision without stable evaluation criterion leads to oscillatory or random changes.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Regime benchmark:** Build agents (fixed policy, SGD, meta-RL, DGM) and measure ΦR_t/ΦC_t. Confirm mapping to Regimes 1–4. Provide baseline suite.  
2. **Causality of ΦR_t:** Use protocol §6.3 with meta-RL agents: induce divergence between effective (Φt) and represented (ΦR_t) strategy, correct representation only, then test adaptation after rule change. Prediction: corrected group adapts faster.  
3. **Crossed opacities dissociation:** Compare humans and meta-RL agents on hierarchical task (e.g., two-step with rule switch). Measure error patterns: humans perseverate (failure to update R1); agents make exploration errors (incorrect R1 updates). Also assess self-reported strategy knowledge (ΦR_t) to confirm profile inversion.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Question:** Is transformativity (maximum self-modification level) logically independent of organizational autonomy (capacity to maintain self-organization) in cognitive systems? Which combinations of high T and high A are possible, and what architectural features enable them?  
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The abstract identifies "the independence of transformativity and autonomy" as one of four open problems. Section 5.1 defines T and A and illustrates their independence through examples (thermostat, simple organism, LLMs, meta-RL agents, humans).  
   **Why unresolved:** The paper notes that autonomy and transformativity have been studied in separate traditions (autopoiesis vs meta‑learning). It remains unclear how these dimensions interact, whether stable systems can achieve both simultaneously, and what constraints bound their joint values.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical mapping of T and A across diverse systems (biological and artificial) using standardized metrics; formal models proving independence or deriving trade‑offs; experimental manipulation of T or A in silico to observe effects on the other dimension.

2. **Question:** What conditions guarantee that a self‑modifying system can revise its own rules without catastrophic collapse, and which simulation modalities (logical analysis, sandboxed execution, predictive world models) are necessary for safe transformation at different hierarchical levels?  
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 5.2, "The viability of self‑modification," introduces three simulation modalities (S1–S3) and argues that simulation is a viability condition, though this link is not formally established.  
   **Why unresolved:** The framework suggests simulation as a solution but does not specify how much simulation depth is required for a given transformativity level, nor does it provide safety proofs. The crossing of opacities implies that humans and AI have complementary vulnerabilities, but no general theory predicts collapse thresholds.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement self‑modifying agents with variable simulation capacities and measure failure rates under controlled self‑modification tasks; develop formal theorems linking simulation guarantees (e.g., provable correctness) to stability invariants; study biological examples of safe norm change (e.g., developmental plasticity) to derive principles.

3. **Question:** How can a system rationally revise its own ultimate evaluation criterion (Rkmax) when any judgment about the revision must itself be made according to that criterion? Is the teleological lock an absolute barrier, or can it be circumvented, and if so by what mechanisms?  
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 5.3, "The teleological lock," poses the problem: evaluating a change Δ to Rkmax requires a criterion E, which either equals Rkmax (conservative) or differs (regress). The framework locates the lock at the threshold of Regime 4.  
   **Why unresolved:** The paper sketches three pathways—environmental coupling, prospective commitment, existential sandbox—but lacks a formal account of how the lock is crossed without circularity or instability. It remains unknown whether these pathways are generalizable or sufficient for safe normative revision in artificial systems.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Formalize the lock using causal influence diagrams (Everitt et al.) or provability logic; build AI systems that modify their reward functions via sandboxed copies and test outcome stability; longitudinal human studies tracking normative shifts (e.g., transformative decisions) to identify causal precursors.

4. **Question:** What, if anything, persists through self‑modification that can ground identity for a system that has undergone normative revision? Can any invariant be defined across transformations that change the top‑level norm, and how should such identity be formalized?  
   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 5.4, "Identity under transformation," asks whether the system after revising Rkmax is still the same, comparing it to the Ship of Theseus and niche construction. The framework claims identity becomes problematic only at Regime 4.  
   **Why unresolved:** The framework does not provide a theory of identity; it notes that if the meta‑rule of transformation itself is revisable, no structural invariant necessarily subsists. No mathematical or empirical criteria for continuity across norm change are given.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop formal models (e.g., rewriting systems, process algebra) that capture hierarchical rule change and identify preserved properties; analyze biological lineages that undergo niche construction to see what remains constant; design AI experiments that track a system across controlled goal modifications to measure continuity of behavior or function.

## Limitations
- The hierarchical model assumes a strictly ordered, monolithic rule hierarchy, which may not capture non-linear, overlapping, or context-sensitive structures in real cognitive architectures.
- Operationalizing ΦR_t and ΦC_t in real systems is empirically challenging; mapping existing AI (e.g., LLMs, meta-RL) to a single regime may be ambiguous due to partial, distributed representations.
- The substrate-based explanation for the crossing of opacities is speculative, relying on broad generalizations about neural opacity vs. computational transparency, which may not hold for hybrid neuro-symbolic systems or future brain-computer interfaces.
