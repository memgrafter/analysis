# A Systematic Evaluation Protocol of Graph-Derived Signals for Tabular Machine Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13998
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13998
- **Reference count:** 27
- **Primary result:** Graph-augmented models yield consistent, statistically significant F1-score improvements over transaction-only baselines in cryptocurrency fraud detection, with GNN-derived, proximity-based, and community-based signals being most effective and differing in robustness.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the lack of statistical reliability in evaluating graph-derived signals for tabular learning by proposing a systematic, taxonomy-driven protocol. The protocol ensures fair comparison through automated hyperparameter optimization, multi-seed evaluation, formal significance testing (McNemar’s test), and robustness analysis under graph edge removal. Applied to cryptocurrency fraud detection, graph-augmented models show consistent and statistically significant F1-score improvements. GNN-derived, proximity-based, and community-based signals emerge as top performers, with GNNs demonstrating strong robustness while proximity signals degrade rapidly under sparsity. These findings enable principled signal selection based on performance, statistical reliability, and robustness.

## Method Summary
The protocol standardizes comparison via a unified workflow: for each classifier and graph signal configuration, hyperparameter optimization is performed once using Bayesian search (50 trials) on a fixed seed split. The optimal hyperparameters are then reused across 10 random seeds with identical data splits. Performance is aggregated using a trimmed mean (removing minimum and maximum F1-scores) to reduce outlier influence. Statistical significance is assessed via paired McNemar’s test on predictions for each seed, summarizing the count of seeds with significant improvement versus degradation. Robustness is evaluated by recomputing signals on graphs with 25% and 50% random edge removal and repeating the multi-seed evaluation with the same hyperparameters, quantifying performance degradation trends.

## Key Results
- Graph-augmented models achieve statistically significant F1-score improvements over transaction-only baselines.
- GNN-derived, proximity-based, and community-based signals are the most effective categories.
- GNN signals remain robust under edge removal, while proximity-based signals degrade rapidly.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Taxonomy-Driven Signal Organization**  
Organizing 24 graph-derived signals into 7 conceptual categories (Centrality, Cohesion, Community, Proximity, Spectral, Structural, GNN) enables interpretable, category-level performance analysis and guides signal selection. The protocol aggregates signals within a category (by concatenation) and compares against baselines, assuming within-category homogeneity. If categories are too coarse or heterogeneous, aggregation may mask outperformers.

**Multi-Seed Evaluation with Trimmed Aggregation and Paired Significance Testing**  
Running 10 independent train-eval cycles with fixed splits, using trimmed mean aggregation, and applying McNemar’s test to paired predictions yields statistically reliable conclusions, guarding against overfitting to specific splits or initializations. The approach assumes 10 seeds sufficiently capture variability and that McNemar’s assumptions (paired nominal outcomes) hold. Low seed counts or violated assumptions risk invalid conclusions.

**Robustness Analysis via Controlled Graph Perturbations**  
Evaluating signals under systematic random edge removal (25%, 50%) reveals their sensitivity to structural degradation, helping practitioners select signals suitable for noisy or incomplete graphs. The method assumes random edge deletion realistically proxies missing/corrupted relational data. Degradation trends may not generalize to other noise models (e.g., targeted attacks).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Data ingestion -> Graph signal generators -> Signal concatenation module -> Classifier wrapper -> Hyperparameter optimization engine -> Multi-seed evaluation manager -> Statistical testing module -> Robustness experiment controller -> Results aggregator

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Preprocess graph and base features; generate all graph signals on the full graph (deterministic, stored).  
  2. For each classifier-signal configuration: run HPO once using seed 42 split to obtain best hyperparameters.  
  3. For each random seed (1–10): using fixed hyperparameters, train on train split, predict on test split, store predictions and F1-scores.  
  4. Aggregate performance: compute trimmed mean F1 across seeds, compute ΔF1 vs. baseline.  
  5. For each seed, run McNemar’s test between baseline and augmented predictions; count significant improvements/degradations.  
  6. For robustness: for each perturbation level (0%, 25%, 50% edge removal), recompute graph signals, repeat steps 3–5 using same HPO-derived hyperparameters, and record ΔF1 trends.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Computational cost vs. reliability:* Reducing seeds or HPO trials saves time but may increase variance or miss optimal hyperparameters.  
  - *Trimmed aggregation vs. information loss:* Trimming reduces outlier influence but discards extreme runs that might represent valid edge cases.  
  - *Fixed hyperparameters per config vs. per seed:* HPs optimized once (seed 42) ensure fair comparison but may not be optimal for every seed.  
  - *Perturbation simplicity vs. realism:* Random edge deletion is cheap but may not capture all real-world noise patterns (e.g., targeted attacks).

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High variance across seeds (F1 standard deviation > 0.05) suggests unstable results.  
  - McNemar counts show many significant degradations, indicating graph signals often harm performance.  
  - Unexpected robustness patterns (e.g., GNN degrading severely under small edge removal) may indicate implementation errors or data leakage.  
  - HPO validation scores consistently low or hyperparameters hit search boundaries, suggesting insufficient search space.  
  - Baseline performance far below reported numbers indicates pipeline misconfiguration.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline sanity check:** Run transaction-only Random Forest with seed 42 (after HPO) and verify F1 ≈ 0.82–0.83.  
  2. **Single signal replication:** Choose a top-performing signal (e.g., GCN) and run multi-seed evaluation (seeds 1–10). Verify average ΔF1 > 0 and net McNemar improvements.  
  3. **Robustness validation:** For a proximity-based signal (e.g., node2vec-BFS), evaluate under 0% and 25% edge removal using same HPs. Expect clear drop in ΔF1 as in Figure 4.

## Limitations
- Single-dataset validation (Elliptic Bitcoin) limits generalizability to other domains or graph structures.
- Random edge removal may not reflect real-world noise (e.g., targeted attacks, temporal drift).
- Incomplete specification of some signal implementations (e.g., role2vec) risks reproduction errors.

## Confidence
- Protocol design (systematic HPO, multi-seed, significance testing): High
- Statistically significant F1 improvements on Elliptic: Medium
- Superiority of GNN, proximity, community signals: Medium
- Robustness ranking (GNN robust, proximity fragile): Medium

## Next Checks
1. Replicate on at least two additional tabular graph datasets (e.g., social networks, academic graphs) to test generalizability of signal categories.
2. Evaluate robustness under non-random edge deletions (e.g., targeted attacks, temporal sampling) to assess sensitivity to realistic noise.
3. Disaggregate category-level results to identify individual signals driving performance and test if aggregation obscures outliers.
