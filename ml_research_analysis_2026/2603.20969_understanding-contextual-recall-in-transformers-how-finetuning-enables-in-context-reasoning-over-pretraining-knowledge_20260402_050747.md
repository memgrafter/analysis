# Understanding Contextual Recall in Transformers: How Finetuning Enables In-Context Reasoning over Pretraining Knowledge

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.20969
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20969
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Finetuning on implicit-inference tasks using a subset of subjects enables transformers to generalize contextual recall to held‑out subjects and across format distribution shifts, whereas pretraining alone does not.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether transformers pretrained on open‑ended text can perform contextual recall—inferring attribute types from pairwise examples to recall facts in novel formats. Using a synthetic framework where pretraining sequences encode attribute types via grammar statistics, the authors first show that pretraining yields factual recall but models fail at contextual recall when grammar cues are removed. However, finetuning on implicit‑inference tasks using a subset of subjects enables generalization to held‑out subjects across formats, even with distribution shifts such as adding grammar tokens between subject and attribute. Further analysis reveals that finetuning induces low‑dimensional task vectors encoding attribute types, with clustering strength increasing with demonstration count and with the separation of pretraining Markov chains. Mechanistic constructions for attention‑only models show that the subject head isolates the query subject, while the relation head shifts from attending to explicit relation tokens (in pretraining) to aggregating context attributes (after finetuning).

## Method Summary
The synthetic framework defines a vocabulary of subjects (M), attribute types (L) each with its own set of values, grammar tokens (G), and a [sep] token. Pretraining (PT) sequences concatenate Ntr subject‑grammar‑attribute tuples for a single subject; each tuple’s grammar subsequence is generated from an attribute‑type‑specific first‑order Markov chain. With probability pG, a tuple is replaced by a grammar‑only segment from a separate fixed Markov chain. PT uses next‑token prediction. In‑context learning (ICL) sequences present N subject‑attribute pairs (optionally interleaved with grammar tokens) followed by a query subject, and the model predicts the missing attribute. After pretraining a 2‑layer decoder‑only transformer, the authors finetune on ICL or ICL with grammar (ICL+Grm) while exposing only a subset of subjects. Evaluation tests contextual recall on grammar‑free ICL sequences for both seen and held‑out subjects. Metrics include exact match, attribute‑type match, etc. They systematically vary pretraining hyperparameters (grammar length S, Markov chain separation Div, number of subjects M) and measure their impact on finetuning generalization. Additionally, they analyze representations by extracting last‑token, layer‑2 activations and computing clustering strength (silhouette score) across demonstration counts.

## Key Results
- Pretraining alone fails to perform contextual recall when grammar cues are removed; finetuning on ICL+Grm with only 50% of subjects yields high accuracy on held‑out subjects in the grammar‑free ICL format.
- Finetuning induces low‑dimensional task vectors encoding attribute types; the separability (clustering) of these vectors grows with more demonstrations and with higher Markov chain separation (Div) during pretraining.
- Attention heads specialize after finetuning: a subject head isolates the query subject, while the relation head shifts from attending to explicit relation tokens (pretraining) to aggregating context attributes, enabling contextual recall.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Finetuning on grammar‑augmented in‑context examples teaches the model to abstract away from surface‑level grammar tokens and instead extract the underlying attribute‑type information from the context. This abstraction is captured by low‑dimensional task vectors in the model’s representations. The increased clustering strength with more demonstrations indicates that the model aggregates evidence across examples to disambiguate the attribute type. The Markov chain separation during pretraining provides a statistical signal that helps disentangle attribute types, making it easier for finetuning to carve out separable manifolds. Mechanistically, attention heads become specialized: one head (subject head) focuses on isolating the query subject, while the relation head learns to gather attribute information from the context rather than relying on explicit relation tokens. This specialization allows the model to perform contextual recall without explicit grammatical cues.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input tokens -> Token embeddings + Position embeddings -> Layer1 (LayerNorm -> Attention (1 head) -> Residual -> LayerNorm -> MLP (1024 hidden) -> Residual) -> Layer2 (same) -> Output logits (tied to token embeddings)

**Critical path**: The input sequence is tokenized, embeddings summed, then passed sequentially through two identical transformer blocks. Each block applies pre‑attention layer normalization, then attention with a single head, adds residual, applies pre‑MLP layer normalization, passes through an MLP (Linear -> GELU -> Linear), and adds residual. After the second block, the final token’s hidden state is projected to the vocabulary via a linear layer tied to the token embedding matrix, producing logits for next‑token prediction.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Two layers and 256‑dimensional hidden size balance expressivity with training stability; more layers/heads might improve performance but increase compute and risk overfitting.
- Using 1 attention head simplifies mechanistic analysis but may limit representation power compared to multi‑head.
- Tied embeddings reduce parameters but might constrain the output space.
- Pretraining grammar length S: longer sequences provide richer statistical cues but increase memory and training difficulty.
- Markov chain separation (Div): higher separation may improve attribute‑type disentanglement but requires a larger grammar token set G to achieve.

**Failure signatures**:
- Pretraining loss remains high or plateaus far above expected levels; check data generation, learning rate, or model capacity.
- After pretraining, ICL exact‑match is non‑zero; indicates the model is not solely relying on grammar cues, possibly due to insufficient Markov chain separation or short grammar sequences.
- Finetuning loss does not decrease or ICL accuracy does not improve after finetuning; may be due to insufficient iterations, too small finetuning subset, or incompatibility of ICL+Grm sequences.
- Clustering silhouette scores do not increase with demonstration count; perhaps representational extraction is wrong or finetuning failed.

**Three first experiments**:
1. **Data generator validation**: Sample a batch of PT sequences, compute average grammar length, frequency of pG replacements, and pairwise TM ℓ₁ distances to confirm target Div (e.g., 0.5). Adjust G and α if needed.
2. **Pretraining sanity check**: Train the 2‑layer model for 20k steps on PT data. Validate that exact match on a held‑out PT set exceeds 90% and that on ICL (without grammar) is near 0%.
3. **Finetuning effectiveness**: Finetune the pretrained model on ICL+Grm (variable Sft) with 50% subjects for 2k steps. Evaluate on ICL (grammar‑free) for seen and held‑out subjects; expect accuracy to rise significantly for both, with held‑out subjects catching up progressively.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly list open questions; however, the limitations and discussion point to several undefined or under‑explored aspects that could inform future research. These include: precise data generation details (Dirichlet prior α, grammar token count G, and the construction of grammar‑only segments), the choice of Markov chain separation procedure (Div) and its sensitivity, and the optimal finetuning iteration count across various distribution shifts. Additionally, the scalability of the observed mechanisms to deeper or multi‑head architectures remains an open direction.

## Limitations
- Data generation details (Dirichlet prior α, grammar token count G, procedure for grammar‑only segments) are unspecified, affecting reproducibility of the pretraining distribution.
- Model architecture specifics (MLP activation, dropout, layer norm placement) and optimization hyperparameters (learning rate schedule, betas) for the 2‑layer transformer are not fully described.
- Control of Markov chain separation (Div) via greedy selection lacks implementation clarity; it may be sensitive to the random seed and grammar vocabulary size.
- Finetuning iteration count may not be optimal across all conditions; the paper reports results at 2k steps but convergence could vary.
