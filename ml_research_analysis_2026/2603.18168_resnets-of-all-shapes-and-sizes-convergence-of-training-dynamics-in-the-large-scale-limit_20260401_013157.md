# ResNets of All Shapes and Sizes: Convergence of Training Dynamics in the Large-scale Limit

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.18168
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18168
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** Proves quantitative convergence of ResNet training dynamics in the joint large-scale limit with error bound \(O(1/L + \sqrt{D/(LM)} + 1/\sqrt{D})\) and derives scaling law \(O(P^{-1/6})\) under shape constraints, with a conjecture of \(O(P^{-1/5})\) in output space.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the asymptotic behavior of deep ResNets when depth \(L\), width \(M\), and embedding dimension \(D\) grow simultaneously. By combining the cavity method from statistical physics with propagation of chaos on skeleton maps, the authors establish a rigorous two-stage limit: first the infinite-depth Mean ODE limit (\(L \to \infty\) for fixed \(D\)), building on [Chi25], then the large-dimension limit (\(D \to \infty\)) via dynamical mean field theory (DMFT). They prove that the error between the finite model and the infinite limit scales as \(O(1/L + \sqrt{D/(LM)} + 1/\sqrt{D})\), which matches empirical observations in the embedding space. Under a total parameter budget \(P = LMD\) and shape constraints (\(D/(ML) \to 0\), \(\log L = O(M)\), \(D = O(M)\)), the optimal scaling yields convergence rate \(O(P^{-1/6})\). The framework applies to ResNets with depth‑two blocks and smooth activations, and extends to Transformers with bounded key‑query dimension. The authors conjecture a tighter \(O(P^{-1/5})\) rate for output‑space error.

## Method Summary
The core method employs a combination of the cavity method and propagation of chaos on non‑anticipative skeleton maps. The analysis proceeds in two asymptotic stages: first, taking \(L \to \infty\) while keeping \(D\) fixed, the training dynamics converge to a Mean ODE (as shown in a companion work). Second, for this Mean ODE, letting \(D \to \infty\) yields a mean‑field limit described by a system of stochastic differential equations. The skeleton maps \(f\) and \(g\) recursively encode the weight updates in terms of past feature statistics, enabling the application of propagation of chaos and the cavity expansion to control CLT‑type terms arising from high‑dimensional inner products. This yields a quantitative error decomposition: finite‑to‑Mean‑ODE error \(O(1/L + \sqrt{D/(LM)})\) plus Mean‑ODE‑to‑limit error \(O(1/\sqrt{D})\), summed via triangle inequality to the total \(O(1/L + \sqrt{D/(LM)} + 1/\sqrt{D})\).

## Key Results
- The total error between finite ResNets and the infinite limit satisfies \(O(1/L + \sqrt{D/(LM)} + 1/\sqrt{D})\).
- Under a fixed parameter budget \(P = \Theta(LMD)\) and shape constraints, optimal scaling yields convergence rate \(O(P^{-1/6})\) in embedding space.
- The paper conjectures a tighter \(O(P^{-1/5})\) rate for output‑space error and shows the framework extends to Transformers with bounded key‑query dimension.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Sequential Limits and Error Additivity
The joint large‑scale limit is approached via two sequential limits: \(L \to \infty\) (Mean ODE) then \(D \to \infty\) (DMFT). The finite‑to‑Mean‑ODE error from [Chi25] is \(O(1/L + \sqrt{D/(LM)})\); the Mean‑ODE‑to‑limit error is \(O(1/\sqrt{D})\). By the triangle inequality, the overall finite‑to‑limit error is additive: \(O(1/L + \sqrt{D/(LM)} + 1/\sqrt{D})\). This decomposition relies on the Mean ODE accurately capturing infinite‑depth behavior (proven in the companion paper) and on ResNets having depth‑two blocks to define skeleton maps.

### Mechanism 2: Cavity Method for CLT‑Type Terms
In the high‑dimensional limit, inner products \(\langle U, h \rangle\) become Gaussian fields. The cavity method expands around a removed (“cavity”) weight coordinate, expressing terms like \(\mathbb{E}[\rho(\langle U, h \rangle)\sqrt{D} V \mid \mathcal{F}^W]\) as a series in \(1/\sqrt{D}\). The first‑order term vanishes due to the centeredness of the weights, and higher‑order terms vanish as \(D \to \infty\). This yields a deterministic limit involving derivatives of \(\rho\) evaluated at Gaussian variables. The method requires subgaussian initialization, bounded derivatives of \(\rho\) up to fifth order, and uniformly bounded hidden states.

### Mechanism 3: Skeleton Maps for Propagation of Chaos
The training dynamics can be encoded by non‑anticipative skeleton maps \(f\) and \(g\) that depend only on past feature statistics \(\Gamma^H, \Gamma^B\). These maps are Lipschitz in their auxiliary arguments with constants controlled by norms of hidden states. In the large‑\(D\) limit, inner products converge to expectations under the limit law, turning the finite‑\(D\) skeleton maps into mean‑field maps \((F, G)\). The Lipschitz properties and a priori bounds on hidden states ensure that the sequence of finite‑\(D\) systems satisfies propagation of chaos, so that the empirical distribution of neurons converges to the mean‑field limit. The depth‑two block structure and maximal local update scaling are essential for the bounds.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Mean‑Field Theory / Propagation of Chaos**  
  *Why needed:* The large‑\(D\) limit makes coordinates of hidden states conditionally independent, leading to a McKean‑Vlasov type mean‑field limit. Propagation of chaos guarantees that the finite‑\(D\) system approximates the infinite‑\(D\) limit.  
  *Quick check:* How does the mean‑field limit depend on the covariance structure of \((H, B)\)?

- **Concept: Dynamical Mean Field Theory (DMFT) / Cavity Method**  
  *Why needed:* Inner products in the \(D \to \infty\) limit exhibit CLT scaling (variance \(1/\sqrt{D}\)), requiring a cavity expansion to compute limits of nonlinear functionals beyond standard law of large numbers.  
  *Quick check:* Why can’t we simply apply the law of large numbers to \(\langle U, h \rangle / D\)? What extra structure demands the cavity method?

- **Concept: Non‑Anticipative Skeleton Maps**  
  *Why needed:* They provide a recursive, low‑dimensional representation of the high‑dimensional training dynamics, depending only on past statistics, which is crucial for proving propagation of chaos on the level of the whole process.  
  *Quick check:* How do skeleton maps differ from tracking the full \((h, b)\) process directly?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Input \(x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}\) → Embedding \(h^{(0)} = W_{\text{in}}^\top x\) (\(W_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times d_{\text{in}}}\)) → Residual blocks (\(\ell = 1,\dots,L\)): \(h^{(\ell)} = h^{(\ell-1)} + \frac{1}{M L} V_\ell \rho\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}} U_\ell^\top h^{(\ell-1)}\right)\) with \((U_\ell, V_\ell) \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times M}\) → Output \(y = \frac{1}{\sqrt{D}} W_{\text{out}}^\top h^{(L)}\) (\(W_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times d_{\text{out}}}\)) → Loss \(\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \ell_i(y(x_i))\) → Gradient updates (e.g., \(U_\ell \leftarrow U_\ell - \eta_u L M D \nabla_{U_\ell} \mathcal{L}\)).

### Critical Path
1. Finite‑\(D\) training dynamics (gradient descent)  
2. Mean ODE limit (\(L \to \infty\), fixed \(D\)) [per companion paper]  
3. Large‑\(D\) limit (\(D \to \infty\)) yielding the final mean‑field system  
4. Skeleton maps \((f, g)\) bridge finite‑\(D\) updates to Gaussian field \((S^H, S^B)\) with recurrence \(f_{k+1} = f_k - \eta_u \rho'(z_k + \langle h_k, f_k \rangle_D)(z_b^k + \langle b_k, g_k \rangle_D) h_k\).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Shape constraints:** Require \(D/(ML) \to 0\), \(\log L = O(M)\), and \(D = O(M)\). Violating these, e.g., letting \(D\) grow too fast relative to \(ML\), enlarges the error term \(\sqrt{D/(LM)}\).
- **Residual scaling:** The maximal local update (MLU) regime necessitates scaling updates by \(\Theta(\sqrt{D}/(L M))\) (embodied in the \(1/(ML)\) factor).
- **Activation \(\rho\):** Must have bounded derivatives up to 5th order and at most linear growth to prevent finite‑time blow‑up of the Mean ODE; nonsmooth activations like ReLU are excluded.

### Failure Signatures
- **Norm blow‑up:** If hidden state norms \(\|h\|_D\) or \(\|b\|_D\) become large (e.g., due to lack of residual scaling), the constants in a priori bounds (Lemma 3.1) diverge, breaking propagation of chaos.
- **Superlinear activations:** Activations with superlinear growth (e.g., \(\rho(s)=s^2\)) can cause explosion in the Mean ODE; LayerNorm or bounded inputs would be required.
- **Shape violation:** If \(D/(ML) \to \alpha > 0\), the limit dynamics changes (Section 1.2), and the proven error rates no longer hold; convergence may still occur but with different scaling.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Validate the \(O(1/L + \sqrt{D/(LM)})\) finite‑to‑Mean‑ODE error rate predicted by [Chi25]: fix \(D\) and vary \(L\), \(M\).
2. Check the \(O(1/\sqrt{D})\) Mean‑ODE‑to‑limit error: fix \(L\), \(M\), vary \(D\), compare finite‑\(D\) system to the mean‑field system using coupled initializations.
3. Test convergence of output error \(\Delta y_k\): verify conjectured \(O(\sqrt{D/(ML)})\) scaling vs. the proven \(O(\sqrt{D}/\sqrt{ML})\) (Figure 2).

## Limitations
- **Dependence on companion paper:** The Mean ODE convergence bound relies on [Chi25]; any weakness there propagates to the overall result.
- **Rigorous assumptions:** Requires shape constraints (\(D/(ML) \to 0\), \(D = O(M)\), \(\log L = O(M)\)), smooth activations with bounded derivatives up to 5th order, and subgaussian initializations, limiting direct applicability to many practical settings.
- **Unproven aspects:** Output‑space scaling law is a conjecture without formal proof; the extension to Transformers is asserted but not derived in full detail.

## Confidence
- **Main convergence bound (Theorem 2.5):** High
- **Derived scaling law \(O(P^{-1/6})\) under optimal allocation:** Medium
- **Output space conjecture \(O(P^{-1/5})\):** Low
- **Framework extension to Transformers:** Medium
