# On the Semantics of Primary Cause in Hybrid Dynamic Domains

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14994  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14994  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Proves equivalence of two formal definitions of primary cause in hybrid domains and establishes key properties (uniqueness, persistence, correct handling of preemption).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of defining actual causation in hybrid domains where change is both discrete (actions) and continuous (over time). Existing causal formalisms typically cover either discrete or continuous change, but not their interaction. The authors ground their work in the Hybrid Temporal Situation Calculus (HTSC) and propose two definitions of primary cause: a **foundational definition** that identifies causes via the enabling of contextual discrete fluents, and a **production-based definition** that uses contributions verified by a modified counterfactual "but‑for" test. Their central contribution is proving that these two definitions are equivalent. Additional properties are derived, including the **uniqueness** of the primary cause and its **persistence** under continuation of the effect. The modified but‑for test is shown to correctly handle **preemption** by removing preempted contributors. The approach is validated through a running example of a nuclear power plant where continuous core temperature changes are influenced by discrete control actions.

## Method Summary
The methodology consists of (1) formalizing hybrid scenarios in HTSC, which integrates discrete actions with continuous dynamics; (2) defining primary cause from a foundational perspective as the discrete action that makes a contextual discrete fluent true, thereby enabling the continuous process leading to the effect; (3) offering a production‑based definition where each candidate cause contributes to the effect and a modified but‑for test (which excludes preempted contributors) confirms causation; (4) proving that both definitions pick out the same cause in any linear scenario; and (5) deriving properties such as uniqueness and persistence from the logical structure. The nuclear power plant example demonstrates how the definitions handle continuous temperature changes and preemptive actions.

## Key Results
- Two definitions of primary cause in HTSC: foundational (enabling context) and production‑based (contributions + modified but‑for test).  
- **Equivalence theorem**: the two definitions are co‑extensive.  
- **Uniqueness**: every effect has exactly one primary cause.  
- **Persistence**: the primary cause remains a cause as long as the effect continues.  
- **Preemption handling**: the modified but‑for test correctly discards preempted contributors.  

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The equivalence arises because both definitions rely on the same logical core: a primary cause is the minimal action that makes a discrete fluent true, which in turn enables a continuous process that inevitably produces the effect. The foundational definition captures this directly, while the production‑based definition uses contributions and a counterfactual test. The modified but‑for test aligns with the foundational notion by ignoring actions that would have caused the effect via an alternative enabled context but were preempted. Persistence follows from the monotonicity of the enabling relation in deterministic HTSC: once the context is established, the continuous trajectory leading to the effect proceeds without further intervention. Uniqueness is guaranteed because, in a linear deterministic scenario, only one action can serve as the minimal enabler of the relevant context.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Hybrid Temporal Situation Calculus (HTSC)**  
   - *Why needed*: Provides a rigorous language to model systems with both instantaneous actions and continuous evolution.  
   - *Quick check*: Can you model a scenario where an action changes a continuous variable and then describe its dynamics in HTSC?

2. **Primary cause**  
   - *Why needed*: The central notion to identify the singular action responsible for an effect in a hybrid setting.  
   - *Quick check*: Given a simple hybrid story, can you pinpoint which discrete action is the primary cause of a later state?

3. **Enabling of contextual discrete fluents**  
   - *Why needed*: Captures the idea that a cause makes a context true that allows continuous processes to produce the effect.  
   - *Quick check*: If removing the candidate cause also removes a specific discrete fluent that precedes the effect, does that indicate it is an enabler?

4. **Contributions and modified but‑for test**  
   - *Why needed*: Provides a counterfactual criterion that handles preemption by ignoring preempted contributors.  
   - *Quick check*: In a preemption scenario, does the modified test correctly identify the cause that actually enabled the effect while discounting the one that was preempted?

5. **Equivalence of definitions**  
   - *Why needed*: Guarantees that both perspectives describe the same causal relation, strengthening the theory.  
   - *Quick check*: Can you construct a mapping from a primary cause under the foundational definition to its characterization under the production‑based definition?

6. **Uniqueness and persistence**  
   - *Why needed*: Establishes desirable, intuitive properties for a causal notion in deterministic hybrid domains.  
   - *Quick check*: In a deterministic hybrid scenario, should there be exactly one primary cause? Does it remain the cause if the effect continues without further actions?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Hybrid Domain Specifier -> HTSC Model -> Foundational Cause Identifier -> Production‑Based Cause Evaluator -> Equivalence & Property Prover

**Critical path**  
1. Encode the scenario in HTSC (actions, continuous fluents, dynamics).  
2. Apply the foundational definition to find the minimal action enabling the necessary discrete context.  
3. Use the production‑based definition to compute contributions and run the modified but‑for test.  
4. Prove that steps 2 and 3 always yield the same result; derive uniqueness and persistence.  
5. Validate on illustrative examples.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Logical vs. counterfactual**: The paper shows both perspectives can be reconciled via the modified but‑for test.  
- **Linearity**: Assumes a total order of events (no concurrency) to avoid partial‑order complications.  
- **Determinism**: Continuous evolution is deterministic; stochastic extensions would require probabilistic reasoning and may break the equivalence.

**Failure signatures**  
- The definitions cannot handle **compound effects** (e.g., conjunctions of fluents).  
- In scenarios with **over‑determination** not captured by preemption, uniqueness might be challenged.  
- **Nondeterministic dynamics** would make the but‑for condition ambiguous and the equivalence proof invalid.

**First experiments**  
1. Implement the nuclear power plant example in an HTSC‑based simulator and verify that the identified primary cause aligns with the expected intuition.  
2. Apply the definitions to a standard hybrid benchmark (e.g., a robotic pouring task) to assess scalability and robustness.  
3. Extend the framework to handle simple compound effects (e.g., “temperature above threshold AND pressure stable”) and experimentally check whether the equivalence still holds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How to extend the definitions to compound effects (conditions combining multiple fluents)?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "We did not address compound effects... In the future, we plan to study direct and indirect causes for arbitrary compound effects, both discrete and temporal."  
- **Why unresolved:** Current definitions only handle primitive temporal fluents; compound effects require deciding which combination of fluents constitutes the effect and how contexts contribute.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A formal definition for compound effects in HTSC, with proofs of key properties and validation on examples.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How to define and compute indirect causes (enabling actions) in hybrid domains?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "we did not consider indirect causes."  
- **Why unresolved:** The primary cause captures only the immediate enabler of the context; causal explanations often need a chain of enabling actions, which is nontrivial with continuous dynamics.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An inductive definition analogous to KL's Causes but for HTSC, with proofs of soundness and handling of continuous change.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the approach handle concurrent actions, where actions are not totally ordered?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper assumes linear scenarios: "no concurrent actions are allowed."  
- **Why unresolved:** Definitions rely on total order via timestamps; concurrency requires partial order reasoning and handling simultaneous contributions.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Reformulation using partial orders, demonstration that uniqueness persists or new causal notions for concurrency.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the modified but‑for test work for non‑achievement effects (e.g., prevention) and non‑linear scenarios?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors note their justification holds only for "achievement causation relative to a linear scenarios", leaving the general case open.  
- **Why unresolved:** The test was shown to hold for their restricted setting; it is unknown whether it correctly handles other effect types or branching scenarios.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Proof of soundness/completeness for broader classes, or counterexamples leading to revised test.

## Limitations
- Assumes deterministic continuous evolution; nondeterminism is not covered.  
- Validation uses only one example; lacks broader empirical evaluation.  
- No assessment of alignment with human causal intuitions, especially in over‑determination.
