# Engineering Regression Without Real-Data Training: Domain Adaptation for Tabular Foundation Models Using Multi-Dataset Embeddings

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04692
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04692
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The adapted model achieves mean multiplicative data-efficiency gains of 1.75× versus TabPFN 2.5 and 4.44× versus AutoGluon on 35 engineering regression benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Engineering regression tasks often suffer from limited real-world data and a distributional gap between synthetic pre‑training data and actual engineering problems. This paper introduces TREDBench, a collection of 83 tabular regression datasets (35 engineering) to quantify this gap, showing that standard synthetic datasets are highly distinguishable from engineering data (96.8% classifier accuracy). To bridge the gap without using real engineering samples, the authors propose embedding-guided synthetic data curation: they generate 10,000 synthetic datasets, compute TabPFN 2.5’s dataset-level embeddings, train an XGBoost classifier to identify “engineering-like” synthetic tasks, select the top 200, and continue pre‑training TabPFN 2.5 on these only. The resulting model improves over the base TabPFN 2.5 on 29 out of 35 engineering benchmarks and over AutoGluon on 27, with substantial data‑efficiency gains. The synthetic‑engineering distinguishability drops to 88.9%, demonstrating that principled synthetic curation can turn procedural generators into domain‑relevant data engines for sparse engineering domains.

## Method Summary
The adaptation pipeline consists of four steps: (1) For each of 10,000 procedurally generated synthetic regression datasets and for real engineering datasets, compute a fixed‑size dataset embedding by feeding all context points through a pre‑trained TabPFN 2.5 and averaging the per‑datapoint representations from the last hidden layer. (2) Train an XGBoost binary classifier on a balanced subset (70% of engineering datasets vs. 250 random synthetic embeddings) to predict whether a dataset is engineering. (3) Apply this classifier to all 10,000 synthetic embeddings and select the 200 with highest predicted engineering probability. (4) Continue pre‑training TabPFN 2.5 on only these 200 selected synthetic tasks for 5 epochs using the standard Prior‑Data Fitted Network objective (context‑target pairs). Evaluation on 35 held‑out engineering regression benchmarks uses 10‑fold cross‑validation with training‑size sweeps (10%–90%), comparing mean squared error and multiplicative data‑efficiency gains relative to the base TabPFN 2.5 and AutoGluon.

## Key Results
- The adapted model outperforms base TabPFN 2.5 on 29/35 engineering datasets and AutoGluon on 27/35.
- Mean multiplicative data‑efficiency gains: 1.75× vs. TabPFN 2.5 and 4.44× vs. AutoGluon.
- The synthetic‑engineering distinguishability (classifier accuracy) drops from 96.8% to 88.9% after embedding‑guided curation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Principled selection of synthetic datasets reduces the synthetic‑real engineering domain gap
**Claim:** Principled selection of synthetic datasets based on their embeddings can reduce the synthetic‑real engineering domain gap.  
**Mechanism:** The TabPFN 2.5 dataset embedding creates a representation where proximity indicates similarity in statistical structure (e.g., feature‑label relationships, noise, dimensionality). By training a classifier to identify synthetic datasets whose embeddings fall within the engineering cluster, the authors curate a subset of synthetic tasks that better match the distribution of real engineering problems.  
**Core assumption:** Proximity in the TabPFN 2.5 embedding space correlates with similarity in the underlying data‑generating distributions relevant to regression performance.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] The synthetic‑engineering distinguishability drops from 96.8% to 88.9% after curation.  
- [section 4.1] Describes the embedding‑guided selection process and the resulting reduced classifier accuracy.  
- [corpus] Related work (e.g., “Real‑TabPFN”) explores continued pre‑training but does not specifically anchor the *curation via embedding classifier* mechanism; this is the paper’s novel proposal.  
**Break condition:** If the embedding space is not well‑calibrated to task‑relevant statistical properties (e.g., it clusters by irrelevant dataset metadata), curation could select synthetics that are embedding‑near but functionally dissimilar, potentially *increasing* the effective domain gap.

### Mechanism 2: Continued pre‑training on a curated, domain‑like synthetic distribution improves in‑context learning on real engineering tasks
**Claim:** Continued pre‑training a tabular foundation model (TabPFN 2.5) on a curated, domain‑like synthetic distribution improves its in‑context learning performance on real engineering regression tasks, without using real engineering data.  
**Mechanism:** TabPFN 2.5 is a Prior‑Data Fitted Network (PFN) trained to approximate Bayesian inference across a wide synthetic prior. Continued pre‑training on a narrowed, engineering‑like subset of that prior shifts the model’s implicit learned prior distribution toward the structure of engineering problems. This adjustment refines the model’s internal representations and inference procedures to be more effective when presented with new engineering datasets in its context window.  
**Core assumption:** The model’s performance is causally influenced by the distribution of tasks seen during (continued) pre‑training, and this influence persists at inference time via in‑context learning.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] Reports mean multiplicative data‑efficiency gains of 1.75× vs. base TabPFN 2.5 and 4.44× vs. AutoGluon on 35 engineering datasets.  
- [section 4.2] Provides the experimental results table and data‑efficiency curves showing improvement on 29/35 datasets vs. base TabPFN.  
- [corpus] “Real‑TabPFN” shows similar benefits from continued pre‑training on a small *real* dataset collection, supporting the general mechanism of task‑distribution adaptation.  
**Break condition:** If the curated synthetic distribution is a poor match for the *diversity* of the target engineering domain (even if average similarity is high), the model may overfit to a narrow subset of engineering patterns, harming performance on outlier engineering tasks.

### Mechanism 3: Dataset‑level embeddings from TabPFN capture domain‑relevant properties of tabular regression datasets
**Claim:** The dataset‑level embedding derived from TabPFN 2.5’s internal representations effectively captures domain‑relevant properties of tabular regression datasets.  
**Mechanism:** During its in‑context inference pass, TabPFN processes all context points through its transformer layers. The activations at a chosen layer encode information about the dataset’s overall statistical signature (e.g., smoothness, nonlinearity, feature interactions, noise level). Averaging these datapoint embeddings produces a fixed‑size vector that summarizes this signature. This vector can then be used for meta‑level tasks like dataset classification or similarity search.  
**Core assumption:** The representations learned by TabPFN 2.5 for making per‑datapoint predictions are sufficiently rich that their aggregate (mean) reflects dataset‑level characteristics useful for domain identification.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 3.4] Explicitly defines the embedding construction method (mean of transformer representations).  
- [section 3.5] Shows this embedding yields 65.8% accuracy in classifying engineering vs. non‑engineering real datasets, and 96.8% accuracy in distinguishing synthetic from engineering data, demonstrating its discriminative power.  
- [corpus] “Does TabPFN Understand Causal Structures?” investigates what TabPFN’s embeddings capture, implying the community uses and questions this representation.  
**Break condition:** If the embedding primarily captures superficial or spurious correlations (e.g., dataset size, specific noise patterns in the synthetic generator) rather than core functional relationships, it will guide curation poorly, leading to ineffective fine‑tuning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Prior‑Data Fitted Networks (PFNs) / In‑Context Learning for Tabular Data**  
  *Why needed:* The entire method hinges on using TabPFN 2.5, a PFN. Understanding that it is *pre‑trained on synthetic tasks* and *performs inference via in‑context learning* (without weight updates) is crucial to grasping why its training distribution matters and why continued pre‑training is a viable adaptation strategy.  
  *Quick check question:* How does TabPFN make a prediction for a new dataset at test time, and what does its pre‑training objective optimize for?

- **Concept: Dataset Embeddings**  
  *Why needed:* The core adaptation method relies on representing entire datasets as vectors to compute similarity and guide synthetic selection. One must understand how these are constructed (mean of model’s internal activations) and their limitations as a summary statistic.  
  *Quick check question:* What information is lost when you average per‑datapoint embeddings into a single dataset vector? What properties might not be captured?

- **Concept: Multiplicative vs. Additive Data Efficiency**  
  *Why needed:* The primary outcome metric is “data‑efficiency gain.” Understanding the difference (relative vs. absolute samples saved) is key to interpreting the quantitative results (1.75x mean gain). The multiplicative metric is emphasized in the abstract.  
  *Quick check question:* If Model A reaches target MSE with 100 samples and Model B needs 175 samples, what are the additive and multiplicative data‑efficiency values for A vs. B?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
1. Synthetic Data Generator → 2. Dataset Embedder → 3. Engineering‑Likeness Classifier → 4. Synthetic Curation Module → 5. Continued Pre‑Training Module → 6. Evaluation Harness

### Critical Path
The process is linear but has a critical dependency: **Step 3 (Classifier) depends on the quality of Step 2 (Embeddings).** If embeddings are poor, curation fails. The final model performance (**Step 6**) depends entirely on the quality of the curated subset (**Step 4**) and the success of the fine‑tuning (**Step 5**).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Curation Scope (K):** Selecting too few synthetic datasets (small K) risks insufficient coverage of the engineering manifold. Too many may include many non‑engineering‑like synthetics, diluting the effect. K=200 was chosen empirically.
- **Class Imbalance in Classifier:** Engineering datasets are rare (35 vs. 10,000 synthetics). The classifier training uses a small, balanced subset (250 synthetics + 70% of engineering). This biases the decision boundary. More sophisticated sampling or weighting could improve selector quality.
- **Embedding Source:** Using the mean of the *last* layer’s representations is a design choice. Earlier layers might capture different properties. The paper does not ablation this choice.
- **Computational Cost:** Generating 10,000 synthetic datasets and computing 10,000 embeddings is expensive. For adaptation in a new domain, this one‑time cost must be amortized.

### Failure Signatures
- **Synthetic‑Engineering distinguishability remains high (>90%) after curation.** The classifier failed to find a good subset; the synthetic generator’s output space may not overlap sufficiently with engineering data.
- **No improvement or degradation on majority of engineering tasks after fine‑tuning.** The curated synthetic distribution, while embedding‑similar, may not be *task‑performance‑similar*. The embedding might not be a perfect proxy for learnable signal.
- **High variance in performance gains across datasets.** The method works well on some engineering problems but fails on others, indicating the curated subset matches a cluster of engineering data but not the full spectrum.
- **Overfitting to synthetic noise:** If the generator produces synthetics with specific, unrealistic artifacts (e.g., a particular synthetic correlation pattern), the model may latch onto these, harming generalization.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Embedding Sanity Check:** Compute t‑SNE/UMAP of embeddings for (a) 100 random synthetics, (b) 35 engineering datasets, (c) 100 synthetics selected by the paper’s method (if code/models available). Visually confirm the selected synthetics interleave with engineering data more than the random set.
2. **Single‑Dataset Curation Ablation:** Instead of a global classifier, for a *single* hold‑out engineering dataset, find its 200 nearest neighbor synthetic datasets (by embedding cosine distance). Fine‑tune TabPFN on this single‑dataset‑neighbor subset. Test on that same hold‑out engineering dataset. This isolates whether global curation succeeds due to capturing general engineering structure vs. overfitting to the specific curation classifier.
3. **Classifier Confidence vs. Downstream Gain Correlation:** For the 200 selected synthetics, plot the classifier’s “engineering probability” score against some proxy of their “training value” (e.g., the loss reduction they cause during the first fine‑tuning epoch on a validation set of engineering tasks). A strong positive correlation would support that the classifier’s score is predictive of useful synthetic tasks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Can a foundation model trained solely on synthetic data match or exceed the performance of models trained on real engineering data?** The paper demonstrates improvement using a specific synthetic generator and TabPFN 2.5, but it remains unknown whether these gains hold for other synthetic generation methods, other foundation models, or for different problem formulations (e.g., classification).
- **What specific aspects of distributional similarity between synthetic and engineering datasets are predictive of transfer performance?** The paper uses a single similarity measure (classifier accuracy on TabPFN embeddings) and finds that even after selection, synthetic and engineering datasets remain somewhat distinguishable (88.9% accuracy). It is unclear which distributional features (e.g., feature correlations, noise patterns, functional forms) matter most for effective transfer.
- **Why does the proposed embedding‑guided synthetic curation lead to performance degradation on some engineering datasets?** The authors do not investigate the properties of the datasets where the method fails. Without understanding the failure modes, it is impossible to know whether the issue lies with the synthetic selection criteria, the fine‑tuning process, or inherent mismatches that cannot be bridged.

## Limitations
- Synthetic generator details (TabICL) missing; fine‑tuning hyperparameters (learning rate, epochs, batch size) unspecified.
- Embedding curation assumes embedding proximity correlates with task‑performance similarity, which may not hold.
- Evaluation limited to 35 datasets; may not represent broader engineering regression.

## Confidence
- **Curation reduces domain gap:** Medium. Distinguishability drops from 96.8% to 88.9%, still high.
- **Pre‑training gains:** High. 29/35 datasets improve vs base TabPFN; 4.44× data‑efficiency vs AutoGluon. However, dataset scope limits generalization.
- **Embeddings capture domain properties:** Medium. High classification accuracies show discriminative power, but direct link to regression performance unproven.

## Next Checks
1. **Nearest‑neighbor ablation:** For a hold‑out engineering set, select its 200 nearest synthetic tasks by embedding distance, fine‑tune TabPFN, and compare to global classifier approach.
2. **Embedding overlap quantification:** Visualize embeddings (t‑SNE/UMAP) for engineering, random synthetic, and curated synthetics; compute silhouette scores to measure bridging.
3. **Selector utility correlation:** Correlate classifier’s engineering probability with each synthetic task’s effect on validation loss during early fine‑tuning; positive correlation validates the selector.
