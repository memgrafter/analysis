# Annealed Langevin Posterior Sampling (ALPS): A Rapid Algorithm for Image Restoration with Multiscale Energy Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.02594
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02594
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** ALPS achieves competitive image restoration using distilled EBMs and annealed Langevin sampling, e.g., FID=3.35 on CIFAR-10 (35 NFEs) and PSNR=35.33±1.31 dB on MRI (4x acceleration), with fewer function evaluations than diffusion baselines and support for both MMSE and MAP estimation.

## Executive Summary
Annealed Langevin Posterior Sampling (ALPS) addresses scalable Bayesian inference for imaging inverse problems by combining score distillation from pre-trained diffusion models with multi-scale Energy-Based Models (EBMs). The core idea is to transfer the generative capabilities of a diffusion teacher into an EBM student, preserving the conservative (curl-free) component of the score field and retaining the interpretable, compositional energy structure of EBMs. This enables the definition of static, well-defined posterior distributions at each noise scale by simply summing the EBM prior energy and the negative log-likelihood. ALPS then samples from these posteriors using preconditioned Langevin dynamics with an annealing schedule, avoiding the complex time-dependent guidance required by diffusion inverse solvers. The preconditioner, derived from the data-fidelity term, scales gradients and noise to align with the local posterior curvature, yielding smoother sampling trajectories and faster convergence. Empirical results on CIFAR-10 generation and MRI/inpainting tasks demonstrate competitive or superior accuracy with fewer function evaluations, while also supporting uncertainty quantification and out-of-distribution detection.

## Method Summary
ALPS operates in two phases. First, a pre-trained diffusion model (teacher) is distilled into a multi-scale EBM (student) via a score matching loss that regresses the student’s denoiser to the teacher’s denoiser. This effectively learns the conservative component of the diffusion score, yielding an EBM whose gradient provides a curl-free score field. Second, for a given inverse problem with forward model \(A\) and measurements \(y\), ALPS defines a sequence of posterior energies \(C_t(x) = \|Ax-y\|^2/(2\eta^2) + E_\theta(x;t)\), one per noise scale \(t\). An annealed Langevin sampler then iterates over decreasing \(t\): at each scale, it performs \(K\) steps of (1) denoising using the EBM score, (2) a quadratic data-consistency update that projects onto the measurement manifold, and (3) noise injection with covariance \(B = (A^H A / \eta^2 + I/t^2)^{-1}\). The preconditioner \(B\) scales updates to efficiently explore the posterior, particularly in the null-space of \(A\). Initialization at the highest scale uses the pseudo-inverse solution. The final sample from the lowest scale yields a MAP estimate if noise is omitted, or an MMSE estimate if multiple runs are averaged.

## Key Results
- **Generative quality:** Distilled EBMs achieve FID=3.35 on CIFAR-10 with 35 NFEs, comparable to the teacher diffusion model.
- **MRI reconstruction:** ALPS MMSE attains PSNR=35.33±1.31 dB (4x acceleration), matching DAPS (35.22±1.30 dB) with similar NFEs; ALPS MAP gives PSNR=35.16±1.09 dB deterministically.
- **Efficiency and versatility:** Across inpainting and MRI, ALPS matches or outperforms diffusion baselines (DPS/DAPS) with smoother sampling trajectories and fewer function evaluations; it also enables uncertainty quantification and out-of-distribution detection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The efficacy of ALPS stems from three interconnected mechanisms:

1. **Distillation of conservative score** – By training a multi-scale EBM to match the denoiser of a pre-trained diffusion model, ALPS extracts the curl-free (conservative) component of the diffusion score. This component, associated with a potential-based energy landscape, retains high generative quality while being amenable to composition with likelihood terms. Evidence: Distilled EBMs achieve FID 3.35 on CIFAR-10, comparable to the teacher, demonstrating that the conservative component suffices for high-fidelity sampling.

2. **Compositional posterior definition** – The EBM’s energy-based formulation allows a natural sum of prior energy and negative log-likelihood to define a static posterior distribution at each noise scale. Annealing over these scales gradually sharpens the posterior, avoiding the time-dependent guidance complexities of diffusion inverse solvers. Evidence: The paper shows mathematically that the composed posterior converges to the true posterior as noise vanishes, and validates empirically across multiple inverse problems.

3. **Preconditioned Langevin dynamics** – ALPS uses Langevin updates with a preconditioner derived from the data-fidelity term. This preconditioner, equivalent to the posterior covariance of the quadratic term, scales gradients and injects noise primarily in the null-space of the forward model. As a result, sampling trajectories start near the measurement manifold (via pseudo-inverse initialization) and evolve smoothly, requiring fewer steps. Evidence: Supplementary figures show ALPS trajectories are smoother and achieve higher PSNR with fewer NFEs compared to DAPS.

## Foundational Learning
- **Denoising Score Matching (DSM)**: A training objective for score-based models where the learned score matches the gradient of a denoising objective. In ALPS, DSM underlies both the teacher diffusion model and the student EBM distillation loss, tying the EBM’s score to the diffusion model’s conservative component. *Why needed*: The distillation loss (Eq. 8) regresses the EBM’s denoiser to the teacher’s, effectively learning the conservative score. *Quick check*: If an EBM is trained with DSM loss \(\|\nabla_{\tilde{x}}E_\theta(\tilde{x};t) - (x-\tilde{x})/t\|^2\), what is the implied denoiser \(\tilde{D}_\theta(x;t)\) and how does it relate to the score?

- **Langevin Dynamics**: An MCMC method for sampling from a distribution using gradient updates and injected noise. In ALPS, annealed Langevin dynamics sample from the composed posterior at each noise scale. *Why needed*: It provides a principled way to draw samples from the static posteriors defined by EBM-likelihood composition. *Quick check*: Why is a small step size or preconditioning important for Langevin dynamics when sampling from a multimodal posterior like \(p_t(x|y)\)?

- **Energy-Based Model (EBM) Compositionality**: The property that the joint energy of an EBM prior and an explicit likelihood is simply the sum of their energies. *Why needed*: This allows defining a proper posterior energy by adding the negative log-likelihood to the EBM’s prior energy, creating static posteriors amenable to Langevin sampling. *Quick check*: For an EBM prior \(p_\theta(x) \propto \exp(-E_\theta(x))\) and Gaussian likelihood \(p(y|x) \propto \exp(-\|Ax-y\|^2/(2\eta^2))\), write the unnormalized posterior energy and its gradient. Why is this “compositionality” powerful for inverse problems?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Pre-trained Diffusion Teacher → provides denoiser \(D_\text{teacher}(\tilde{x};t)\) → used in distillation loss to train Student Multi-Scale EBM (network \(D_\theta(x;t)\)) → outputs denoised estimate \(\tilde{D}_\theta(x;t)\) → score \(\nabla E_\theta(x;t) = (x-\tilde{D}_\theta(x;t))/t\) (with preconditioning correction) → combined with likelihood term to form posterior energy \(C_t(x)\) → ALPS Sampler orchestrates annealing: for each \(t\) (descending schedule), perform \(K\) iterations of: (1) compute \(d_{k,t} = \tilde{D}_\theta(x_{k,t}; t)\) (denoising), (2) solve quadratic \(\min_x \|Ax-y\|^2/(2\eta^2) + \|x-d_{k,t}\|^2/(2t^2)\) to get \(\tilde{x}_{k+1,t}\), (3) add noise \(x_{k+1,t} = \tilde{x}_{k+1,t} + B^{1/2} \xi_k\), where \(B = (A^H A / \eta^2 + I/t^2)^{-1}\) (preconditioner). Initial \(x_{0,t}\) from pseudo-inverse solution at highest \(t\).

**Critical path**:
1. **Distillation Phase**: Train \(D_\theta\) on noisy images using loss \(\|\tilde{D}_\theta(\tilde{x};t) - D_\text{teacher}(\tilde{x};t)\|^2\). Validate unconditional sample quality (FID).
2. **Single-Scale Test**: Fix \(t\) near \(\sigma_\text{min}\), run ALPS loop for a simple inverse problem (e.g., inpainting). Verify denoising and data consistency.
3. **Full ALPS Inference**: Choose noise schedule (\(\sigma_\text{max}\), \(\sigma_\text{min}\), \(\rho\)) and run Algorithm 1. Tune \(N\) (annealing steps) and \(K\) (Langevin steps) using validation PSNR/SSIM.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- NFE vs. Quality: More annealing steps \(N\) and Langevin steps \(K\) improve quality but increase NFEs linearly; \(K\) typically has more consistent impact.
- MAP vs. MMSE: Omitting noise addition yields deterministic MAP estimates (faster, single run); adding noise and averaging yields MMSE estimates (potentially higher PSNR, with uncertainty).
- Preconditioner Complexity: Exact \(B\) is efficient for diagonalizable \(A\); approximations (e.g., FFT for specific MRI) trade exactness for speed and may introduce null-space artifacts.

**Failure signatures**:
1. Poor unconditional samples (high FID) indicate failed distillation.
2. PSNR deteriorates with increased \(K\) or \(N\) suggests misspecified posterior or poor EBM prior at low \(t\).
3. Overly high uncertainty estimates everywhere imply EBM prior is too flat or noise schedule too high.
4. OOD detection failure (low NLPr separation) indicates EBM has not learned a sharp manifold.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Validate distillation on a small dataset (e.g., 10k FFHQ) with a small diffusion teacher. Generate 100 samples, compute FID; target within 2–3× of teacher’s FID.
2. Test single-scale posterior sampling at \(t=0.1\) on box inpainting. Check that samples fill the mask plausibly and data consistency plateaus.
3. Run full ALPS on one MRI slice (4x acceleration) with \(N=50\), varying \(K=1,3,5\). Plot PSNR vs. NFE. Compare MAP (no noise) vs. MMSE (5-sample average).

## Limitations
1. The distillation process discards the non-conservative component of the diffusion score without theoretical guarantees, potentially limiting scalability to complex data manifolds.
2. The quality of the EBM prior at intermediate noise scales—crucial for annealing—is not independently validated beyond final reconstruction metrics.
3. The efficient preconditioner assumes diagonalizable forward operators; its approximation for non-diagonalizable operators (e.g., multi-coil MRI) may be crude and lacks thorough evaluation.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Distillation Transferability):** Medium Confidence. Strong empirical evidence (FID 3.35) supports that the distilled conservative score retains generative quality on CIFAR-10, but theoretical justification is lacking and scalability to more complex data is unproven.
- **Claim 2 (Compositional Posteriors):** High Confidence. The mathematical framework is sound and results across inverse problems validate practical utility.
- **Claim 3 (Preconditioned Langevin Efficiency):** Medium Confidence. Visual evidence and NFE gains support smoother trajectories, but confidence is reduced by limited evaluation to diagonal operators; robustness for general \(A\) relies on unvalidated approximations.

## Next Checks
1. Test ALPS on a non-diagonalizable forward model (e.g., multi-coil MRI with SENSE reconstruction) and compare against DAPS/DPS in terms of PSNR/LPIPS to stress-test the preconditioner approximation.
2. Quantify the discarded curl component: compute the Helmholtz decomposition of a teacher diffusion’s score field, then generate samples using only the curl component via Langevin dynamics to measure its impact on sample quality.
3. Ablate EBM prior quality at intermediate noise scales: train EBMs with truncated noise schedules (different \(\sigma_\text{max}\)) and evaluate ALPS PSNR on a fixed inverse problem to isolate the contribution of the prior in the annealing regime.
