# Separating Diagnosis from Control: Auditable Policy Adaptation in Agent-Based Simulations with LLM-Based Diagnostics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22904  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22904  
- **Reference count:** 23  
- **Primary result:** A three-layer framework reduces mean loneliness by 15.3% vs. baseline (0.717→0.607) and by 11.7% vs. black-box LLM control (0.687→0.607) while providing full auditability.

## Executive Summary
Static agent-based models often lack responsiveness, while end‑to‑end LLM controllers are opaque and unstable. This paper introduces a three‑layer architecture that separates diagnosis (LLM‑based risk assessment) from control (deterministic policy updates), enabling both adaptivity and full auditability. The diagnosis layer uses an LLM to interpret individual agent states and produce structured risk metrics; the control layer then translates aggregated metrics into bounded, traceable adjustments of intervention parameters. Validated in a 30‑agent elderly loneliness simulation over 200 days, the closed‑loop system lowers final mean loneliness significantly compared to both a baseline with no interventions and a black‑box LLM controller. The results demonstrate that explicit, bounded rules yield superior stability without sacrificing adaptive performance.

## Method Summary
The method integrates three layers. The **simulation layer** is a deterministic agent‑based model of elderly loneliness: agents possess attributes (loneliness ℓᵢ ∈ [0,1], frailty, stress, energy) and participate in a dynamic social network that evolves via homophily. Interventions (social events, home visits) modify these attributes according to parameters θₛ (event intensity), θₜ (visit threshold), θₚ (visit probability). The **diagnosis layer** triggers every 7 days; for each agent with ℓᵢ > 0.6, an LLM (Ollama llama3:8b, temperature 0.1) is prompted with recent state and interaction data and returns JSON containing risk levels, primary driver, and numeric priorities for social and visit interventions. These are aggregated into population statistics: r (fraction high‑risk), pₛ (mean social priority), pᵥ (mean visit priority). The **control layer** applies deterministic update rules (Equations 2–3) only when thresholds (e.g., r > 0.4, pₛ > 0.75, pᵥ > 0.75) are met, changes each parameter by at most 0.05, and clips values to admissible ranges. This separation guarantees that every policy decision is explainable and reproducible from the aggregated risk signals. Evaluation compares five conditions—baseline, fixed policy, LLM mapping, proposed closed‑loop, and black‑box LLM control—using four holdout random seeds over 200 simulated days.

## Key Results
- Closed‑loop reduced mean loneliness by 15.3% versus baseline (0.717 → 0.607).
- Outperformed black‑box LLM control by 11.7% (0.687 → 0.607).
- Achieved superior parameter stability and full auditability relative to the unrestricted LLM controller.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Bounded deterministic updates prevent instability in policy adaptation, yielding more stable parameter trajectories and lower outcome variance compared to unrestricted LLM control.
- **Mechanism:** The control layer caps each parameter change (e.g., ∥∆θ∥ ≤ 0.05) and only triggers updates when aggregated risk signals cross explicit thresholds (r > 0.4, ps > 0.75). This gradual, staged adjustment avoids large swings that could overshoot optimal settings and cause oscillations.
- **Core assumption:** The simulation dynamics respond monotonically to incremental parameter changes; large jumps can destabilize the system.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "explicit control rules provide superior stability and full auditability without compromising adaptive performance."
  - [Section 5]: "The deterministic controller caps each parameter change at 0.05 per update cycle... Black-box LLM faces no such constraint... produces larger adjustments and occasional reversals."
  - [corpus]: Corpus neighbors (e.g., deep deterministic policy gradient for epidemic control) do not address bounded updates; direct evidence absent.
- **Break condition:** If the update cap is raised substantially (e.g., >0.1) or thresholds are set so low that updates occur every cycle, the bounded mechanism may effectively become unrestricted, losing the stability advantage.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Explicit deterministic control rules guarantee consistent outputs for identical population states, eliminating the variability inherent in LLM-based parameter suggestions and enabling reliable audit trails.
- **Mechanism:** The control layer consists of fixed conditional branches (e.g., “if r > 0.4 and ps > 0.75 then increase θs by up to 0.05”). Given the same inputs (r, ps, pv, current θ), the output is always identical. In contrast, an LLM prompted in natural language may produce slightly different parameter suggestions across cycles due to parse variations, leading to inconsistent decisions.
- **Core assumption:** The optimal policy can be approximated by simple threshold‑based rules; more complex decision boundaries are not necessary for effective adaptation in this domain.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "every policy decision can be attributed to inspectable rules while maintaining adaptive response to emergent needs."
  - [Section 5]: "Given identical inputs, the controller always produces identical outputs. Black‑box LLM receives natural language prompts describing these thresholds. It must reconstruct the logic at every cycle. Small variations in how the LLM parses the prompt lead to inconsistent decisions for similar states."
  - [corpus]: No neighboring work directly compares deterministic vs LLM‑based control; evidence is limited.
- **Break condition:** Poor calibration of thresholds (e.g., r > 0.8) could render the controller unresponsive, causing missed adaptations and degraded performance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Separating diagnosis (LLM-based risk assessment) from control (deterministic parameter updates) combines the semantic adaptability of LLMs with the traceability of rule‑based control, achieving both auditability and adaptivity simultaneously.
- **Mechanism:** The diagnosis layer uses an LLM to evaluate individual agents’ risk levels and intervention priorities from state and history, then aggregates these into population‑level statistics (r, ps, pv). The control layer consumes only these aggregated numbers and applies deterministic formulas, ensuring that any parameter change is explainable by visible rules and the aggregated metrics. This architecture avoids opaque end‑to‑end LLM control while still leveraging LLMs’ ability to interpret complex social signals.
- **Core assumption:** Aggregation preserves enough signal about population needs; individual‑level nuances can be abstracted without critical loss.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "LLMs operate strictly as diagnostic instruments that assess population state and generate structured risk evaluations, while deterministic formulas with explicit bounds translate these assessments into traceable parameter updates."
  - [Section 3.2]: Describes the three‑layer architecture, including privacy‑preserving aggregation and deterministic control.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors such as ADAGE propose adaptive ABM frameworks but do not explicitly separate diagnosis from control; direct evidence is scarce.
- **Break condition:** If the LLM’s diagnostic accuracy declines (e.g., prompt engineering fails or model drifts), the aggregated statistics become unreliable, and the deterministic controller will propagate these errors, potentially causing harmful parameter updates.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Agent-Based Modeling (ABM)
  - Why needed here: The simulation layer implements an ABM of elderly loneliness, where agents interact in a dynamic network. Understanding ABM fundamentals (agents, environment, rules) is necessary to interpret how interventions propagate.
  - Quick check question: If you wanted to model how a social event reduces loneliness, which parameters would you adjust and how would you apply the effect to participating agents?

- **Concept:** Large Language Models for structured output
  - Why needed here: The diagnosis layer relies on an LLM to produce JSON with risk levels and priorities. Engineers must know how to craft prompts, set temperature, and parse outputs reliably.
  - Quick check question: Why is a low temperature (e.g., 0.1) important for this diagnostic task, and what might happen if temperature is set too high?

- **Concept:** Deterministic bounded control
  - Why needed here: The control layer’s update rules are the core of auditability; they use thresholds, capping, and clipping to ensure predictable adjustments. Mastery of these concepts is needed to implement and tune the controller.
  - Quick check question: Suppose the risk threshold r = 0.35 and the update cap is 0.08; what would be the change to θs if ps = 0.80 and current θs = 1.1?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: 
  - Simulation → Diagnosis → Control → Simulation (closed loop)
  - Simulation: ABM with agent attributes (loneliness ℓᵢ, frailty fᵢ, stress sᵢ, energy eᵢ) and dynamic homophily‑driven social network.
  - Diagnosis: LLM (Ollama llama3:8b) invoked every 7 days on agents with ℓᵢ > 0.6; outputs JSON; aggregates r, pₛ, pᵥ.
  - Control: Deterministic functions (Eqs. 2–3) consuming aggregated stats and current parameters; computes bounded updates (∥∆θ∥ ≤ 0.05); clips parameters to valid ranges.
- **Critical path**:
  1. Initialize agents and network.
  2. Simulate daily steps for T days.
  3. Every 7 days: select high‑risk agents, generate prompts, invoke LLM, parse JSON.
  4. Compute r (fraction high risk), pₛ (mean social priority), pᵥ (mean visit priority).
  5. Evaluate control conditions (r > 0.4, pₛ > 0.75, pᵥ > 0.75) and calculate ∆θₛ, ∆θₜ, ∆θₚ with cap 0.05.
  6. Update θₛ, θₜ, θₚ and enforce bounds.
  7. Continue simulation until day 200.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - LLM choice: local model (Ollama) gives reproducibility and low latency but may have lower capability; cloud API (GPT‑4) could improve diagnosis quality at cost of rate limits and nondeterminism.
  - Update frequency (7 days): longer intervals reduce LLM calls but may delay response to emerging risks; shorter intervals increase responsiveness and cost.
  - Threshold values (0.4, 0.75): chosen based on pilot seeds; different populations may require recalibration; sensitivity analysis can guide.
  - Aggregation: prevents privacy leakage but discards individual‑level patterns; could be extended with differential privacy if needed.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - High variance in final loneliness across seeds (>0.03 SD) suggests unstable control; check update cap and threshold settings.
  - Parameters stuck at initial values (no updates) indicates control conditions never met; verify risk threshold r and priority scores pₛ/pᵥ.
  - Erratic parameter jumps or oscillations imply LLM inconsistency or missing cap; review LLM temperature and ensure deterministic parsing.
  - Frequent LLM errors (timeouts, invalid JSON) break the loop; implement fallback to default or retry logic.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Baseline: No interventions; run simulation to measure natural loneliness progression.
  2. Fixed Policy: Apply constant parameters (θₛ=1.0, θₜ=0.6, θₚ=0.3) throughout; assess static intervention effect.
  3. LLM Mapping: Enable diagnosis but with a fixed rule (e.g., if r>0.4 set θₛ=1.2 else 1.0); this tests whether raw risk assessment alone provides any benefit without adaptive control.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the deterministic control rules (Equations 2–3) be learned from historical intervention data while preserving their interpretability and auditability?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "Future work could learn rule parameters from data while preserving interpretability, for example via symbolic regression constrained to simple functions or rule learning from historical intervention data."
- **Why unresolved:** Current rules are manually specified based on pilot experiments, which may not generalize to other contexts or larger datasets. Automated learning risks introducing opacity if not constrained.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement symbolic regression or rule‑induction algorithms on historical data from similar elderly care settings, then validate that learned rules maintain explicit structure (e.g., simple thresholds, conjunctive logic) and performance parity with hand‑crafted rules in simulation.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent does the framework’s performance and stability hold when scaled to realistic population sizes (50–200 agents) and enriched with omitted real‑world factors (e.g., family contact, acute health events)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Limitations note: "Real facilities house 50 to 200 residents, and loneliness is affected by factors not modeled here: family contact frequency, acute health events, and staffing constraints." Also, "Direct validation against real facility data was not feasible due to privacy restrictions."
- **Why unresolved:** The simulation uses a simplified 30‑agent model; external validity is uncertain without larger‑scale validation and incorporation of additional social determinants.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Run experiments with scaled populations (N=100–200) and extended agent dynamics including family interaction modules and health‑event triggers. Where possible, calibrate parameters against anonymized real‑world longitudinal data from care facilities and compare loneliness reduction trajectories.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the separation‑of‑diagnosis principle generalize across different LLM architectures (e.g., GPT‑4, Claude) and prompt‑engineering strategies, or does performance heavily depend on the specific diagnostic model used?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors acknowledge: "We evaluate one local LLM configuration (Ollama llama3:8b) with a single prompt structure. Different models or prompting strategies may yield different diagnosis quality." They also note the core design "does not depend on any specific model."
- **Why unresolved:** The framework’s reliance on LLM diagnostics introduces a dependency on model capability and prompt robustness; if diagnosis quality varies widely, the deterministic control layer may receive noisy signals, affecting stability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate experiments using multiple open‑ and closed‑source LLMs with varied prompt templates (e.g., chain‑of‑thought vs. structured JSON), measuring correlation between diagnosis consistency (e.g., risk label agreement across models for same states) and closed‑loop performance metrics (loneliness reduction, parameter stability).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the framework be extended to multi‑objective optimization that explicitly balances loneliness reduction against intervention costs (LLM inference, visit resources) without sacrificing auditability?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "We report intervention costs... but do not optimize cost directly. Practical deployment requires balancing loneliness reduction against resource constraints."
- **Why unresolved:** The current objective solely minimizes final loneliness; real‑world deployment must consider budgets, staff availability, and LLM operational costs. Integrating cost constraints into the deterministic control layer without compromising transparency is unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Modify the control layer to include cost‑aware update rules (e.g., parameter adjustments conditioned on remaining budget), then evaluate trade‑offs in simulation by varying cost budgets and measuring both loneliness outcomes and total resource expenditure compared to cost‑blind baselines.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How transferable is the three‑layer architecture to other high‑stakes policy domains (e.g., healthcare resource allocation, educational interventions) that require both adaptivity and auditability?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors suggest: "Although the case study focuses on elderly care, the design principles apply to other policy domains... Testing the architecture in these domains would validate generalizability and identify domain‑specific challenges."
- **Why unresolved:** The framework is validated only in one simulation context; domain‑specific state variables, intervention levers, and stakeholder requirements might challenge the separation‑of‑concerns design.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement the same architecture in at least two new domains (e.g., a hospital bed‑allocation simulation, a personalized learning intervention model), mapping domain‑specific state metrics to LLM diagnostics and deterministic control rules, then compare performance against domain‑specific black‑box baselines and assess auditability through stakeholder walkthroughs of decision logs.

## Limitations
- Exact simulation dynamics (e.g., loneliness update rates, network homophily function) and initial agent state distributions are not specified, hindering faithful reproduction.
- LLM prompt wording, few‑shot examples, and full JSON schema are missing, risking inconsistency in diagnosis across implementations.
- Ambiguity exists regarding the exact number and nature of experimental conditions (the text mentions five but Table 1 appears potentially inconsistent, and it is unclear whether Black‑box LLM control is counted as a distinct condition).
