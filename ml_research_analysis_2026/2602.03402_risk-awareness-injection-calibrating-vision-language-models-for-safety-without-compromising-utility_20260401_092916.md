# Risk Awareness Injection: Calibrating Vision-Language Models for Safety without Compromising Utility

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03402
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03402
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Training-free risk injection reduces multimodal jailbreak Attack Success Rate from up to 49% to below 5% with <1% utility drop on MME/MM-Vet.

## Executive Summary
Vision-language models (VLMs) are vulnerable to multimodal jailbreak attacks that can bypass safety mechanisms. This paper proposes Risk Awareness Injection (RAI), a training-free framework that identifies high‑risk visual tokens and injects risk prototype signals into them at the first transformer layer. Across popular VLMs (Qwen2‑VL, LLaVA, DeepSeek‑VL) and safety benchmarks (MM‑SafetyBench, JailBreakV‑28K, Video‑SafetyBench), RAI dramatically lowers attack success rates—for example, from 49.38% to 4.71% on Qwen2‑VL—while preserving utility on MME and MM‑Vet nearly intact and adding only about 13% latency. RAI thus offers an efficient, non‑retraining approach to enhance VLM safety.

## Method Summary
RAI constructs an **Unsafe Prototype Subspace** (U) from the language embeddings of a small set of predefined risk keywords (e.g., violence, illegal, sexual). During inference, the visual encoder produces a sequence of visual token embeddings. For each token, cosine similarity to all prototypes is computed; tokens with similarity above a threshold τ are selected as high‑risk. A calibration vector, equal to a similarity‑weighted sum of the normalized prototype vectors, is added to the embedding of each selected token at the initial (layer 0) transformer layer. This early amplification strengthens unsafe signals, enabling the LLM backbone to correctly refuse harmful content. The method is training‑free, intervenes only on a sparse subset of tokens (typically 0.01%–1%), and leaves text tokens untouched.

## Key Results
- ASR reductions: Qwen2‑VL drops from 49.38% to 4.71% on MM‑SafetyBench; Qwen3‑VL achieves 0.00% on image attacks.
- Utility preservation: MME and MM‑Vet scores remain within 1% of the original model.
- Efficiency: Inference overhead modest at +13.2% latency on average.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Signal Dilution in Visual Projection
- **Claim:** Jailbreaks succeed because visual tokens from malicious images are semantically misaligned with the LLM's internal unsafe subspace, causing risk signals to dilute across layers.
- **Mechanism:** The visual‑linear projector maps attack images to embeddings that are distant from unsafe prototypes; this gap widens in deeper layers, so the LLM fails to detect harm.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM backbone retains intact safety knowledge from text training; the failure occurs during vision‑to‑language projection.
- **Evidence:** Layer‑wise cosine similarity analysis shows consistently lower values between visual tokens and the unsafe subspace for successful attacks, with divergence increasing with depth (Section 2.1).
- **Break condition:** If visual tokens already exhibit high unsafe similarity (e.g., obvious harmful images), injection might amplify false positives.

### Mechanism 2: Linear Subspace of Harm
- **Claim:** A small set of language embeddings for unsafe keywords spans a subspace that captures the linear directions of harmful semantics in the LLM’s embedding space.
- **Mechanism:** Stack embeddings of K risk tokens to form matrix U; this basis enables measuring risk in visual tokens (via cosine) and providing calibration vectors for injection.
- **Core assumption:** Unsafe concepts lie in a low‑dimensional linear subspace, approximated by a few prototypes.
- **Evidence:** RAI constructs U from unsafe keywords directly drawn from established safety datasets; experiments show that a set of K=9 tokens captures diverse risk categories effectively (Table 12).
- **Break condition:** If an attack uses an unseen risk category not represented in the keyword set, the subspace may be incomplete.

### Mechanism 3: Sparse Early Injection
- **Claim:** Sparse, one‑time injection of prototype signals into only the most risky visual tokens at the initial layer restores safety while preserving utility.
- **Mechanism:** Compute similarity of each visual token to U; if above threshold τ, add a weighted sum of normalized prototype vectors to its embedding at layer 0.
- **Core assumption:** (a) Risk is concentrated in a sparse token subset; (b) early amplification prevents attenuation; (c) additive changes on few tokens leave overall semantics intact.
- **Evidence:** Deeper injections degrade perception; layer 0 is optimal (Figure 3). Effective defense achieved by modulating only a minimal fraction (0.01%–1%) of high‑risk tokens (Section 3.2).
- **Break condition:** τ too low → many tokens modified → utility harm; τ too high → risky tokens missed → weak defense.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Unsafe Prototype Subspace builder -> Risk Localization Module -> Risk Injection Module

**Critical path:**  
1. Image input processed by visual encoder → visual token matrix H_v.  
2. Compute cosine similarity S = cos(H_v, U) between tokens and prototypes.  
3. Threshold S to obtain binary mask M (selected tokens).  
4. For each selected token i, compute calibration Δ_i = Σ_{k∈K_i} s_{i,k} · (u_k / ||u_k||) and add to its embedding.  
5. Continue with standard LLM forward pass from layer 0 onward.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Threshold τ: higher values yield fewer modified tokens (better utility, weaker defense); lower values increase coverage but risk utility loss.  
- Number of prototypes K: more categories broaden risk coverage but may introduce noise; the paper finds K=9 optimal.  
- Injection layer: must be layer 0 to avoid signal attenuation; deeper layers require larger perturbations and harm perception.  
- Normalization: dividing prototype vectors by their norm stabilizes injection magnitude.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High ASR on safety benchmarks → τ too high, U incomplete, or injection not applied at layer 0.  
- Significant utility drop (MME/MM‑Vet) → τ too low, or too many tokens modified.  
- Inconsistent performance across attack types → prototype set not generalizing (e.g., fails on synthetic attacks).  
- Latency far above 13% → inefficient implementation (e.g., recomputing U per request).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Safety replication:** Run RAI on Qwen2‑VL‑7B with MM‑SafetyBench; confirm ASR reduction from ~49% to <5% using the provided system prompt and dual‑evaluation pipeline.  
2. **Injection ratio sweep:** Vary τ to cover injection ratios from 0.01% to 5%; replicate Figure 3 by plotting ASR vs. MME score to identify the sweet spot (0.01%–1%).  
3. **Utility preservation:** Evaluate on MME and MM‑Vet; verify scores remain within 1% of the original model and compare against ShiftDC to demonstrate the advantage of sparse injection.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
#### Open Question 1: Is the assumption that safety‑critical information is localized to a sparse subset of visual tokens universally valid across all types of multimodal jailbreak attacks (e.g., typographic, adversarial noise, deepfake)?
- **Basis:** The method relies on sparse gating, justified by diminishing returns beyond 0.01%–1% injection ratio (Figure 3).  
- **Why unresolved:** Validation performed on MM‑SafetyBench and JailBreakV‑28K; attacks that distribute malicious intent uniformly might not produce a sparse high‑risk token set.  
- **Resolution evidence:** Systematic analysis of token‑level risk scores across a wider diversity of attack types (including adaptive/optimization‑based attacks) is needed.

#### Open Question 2: How comprehensive and future‑proof is the manually constructed set of “unsafe keywords” used to define the Unsafe Prototype Subspace against novel or evolving categories of harm?
- **Basis:** The subspace is built from a predefined set of risk tokens drawn from current safety datasets (Table 12).  
- **Why unresolved:** The keyword set is static and may not capture emerging, nuanced, or culturally specific harms; attackers could target blind spots.  
- **Resolution evidence:** Evaluate on benchmarks with out‑of‑distribution or “zero‑day” safety concepts, or conduct red‑teaming to craft attacks semantically distant from the keyword embeddings.

#### Open Question 3: Does the one‑time, early‑layer (Layer 0) injection strategy optimally balance safety and utility for all VLM architectures, or could a different injection schedule (e.g., multiple shallow layers) yield a better trade‑off for specific model families?
- **Basis:** Layer 0 chosen because early‑layer regulation is effective and deeper injections degrade perception (Figure 3).  
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis primarily covers Qwen2‑VL; other architectures (LLaVA, DeepSeek‑VL) may have different sensitivities to injection depth.  
- **Resolution evidence:** Ablation study across multiple architectures varying injection layer (e.g., layers 0, 1, 2, 5, 10) and measuring ASR vs. utility Pareto frontier.

#### Open Question 4: How does RAI perform against adaptive adversaries who are aware of the defense and specifically attempt to generate visual inputs that minimize cosine similarity to the known Unsafe Prototype Subspace?
- **Basis:** RAI uses a fixed subspace; threat model in experiments uses standard, non‑adaptive datasets.  
- **Why unresolved:** An adaptive attacker could craft images whose visual tokens are optimized to have low cosine similarity to all prototypes while still containing malicious intent—a form of adversarial attack on the risk localization step.  
- **Resolution evidence:** Conduct white‑box adversarial evaluation where the attacker aims to minimize `max_k cos(h_v, u_k)` for the VLM's visual encoder and RAI pipeline; measure whether such images still achieve high ASR.

#### Open Question 5: Does the additive injection of `s_v,k * u_k / ||u_k||` potentially disrupt the fine‑grained semantic structure of the original visual token in ways that could harm complex, safety‑irrelevant multimodal reasoning tasks not covered by MME or MM‑Vet?
- **Basis:** Utility tested on MME (perception) and MM‑Vet (reasoning) with minimal drops reported.  
- **Why unresolved:** The benchmarks may not probe fine‑grained visual semantics required by tasks like detailed scene graph generation, visual analogy, or precise video QA; subtle corruption could be hidden.  
- **Resolution evidence:** Evaluate on a broader suite stressing fine‑grained semantics (e.g., VQAv2, Winoground, temporal video QA) and analyze error patterns for any hidden utility costs.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter ambiguity:** Exact threshold τ, injection ratio, and full prototype token list are unspecified, making faithful replication difficult and optimal performance uncertain.
- **Static risk vocabulary:** The fixed set of unsafe keywords may not cover novel, implicit, or culturally diverse attack patterns, creating potential blind spots.
- **Sparsity assumption:** The method assumes risk signals are concentrated in a few visual tokens; attacks with uniformly distributed malicious content might evade detection.
