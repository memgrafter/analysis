# HiVAE: Hierarchical Latent Variables for Scalable Theory of Mind
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16826  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16826  
- **Reference count:** 5  
- **Primary result:** HiVAE achieves a Brier score of 0.104 at 25% observation, significantly outperforming the next best baseline (0.890) on a 3,185‑node campus graph with 100,000 synthetic pedestrian trajectories.

## Executive Summary
Theory of Mind (ToM) inference in large, realistic spatiotemporal domains remains a challenge, as most existing methods are limited to small gridworlds. HiVAE introduces a three‑level hierarchical variational autoencoder inspired by the belief‑desire‑intention (BDI) cognitive structure to address this scaling gap. The model jointly encodes partial agent trajectories and the environment graph, infers hierarchical latent mental states, and predicts future goals. Evaluated on a large campus graph with 100,000 synthetic trajectories, HiVAE dramatically reduces goal prediction error (Brier score 0.104 vs. 0.890 at 25% observation) and demonstrates robustness to misleading spatial cues and agent preference drift. The work provides a promising direction for scalable ToM in complex, real‑world settings.

## Method Summary
HiVAE tackles goal inference from partial trajectories in a large environment graph. The architecture comprises:
- A Transformer encoder processes the trajectory node sequence with temporal positional encoding to produce *h_traj*.
- A Graph Attention Network (GAT) encodes the environment graph into node embeddings, from which a graph‑level representation *h_graph* is derived.
- A fusion module (MLP) combines *h_traj* and *h_graph* into a unified spatiotemporal representation *h_fused*.
- A hierarchical VAE infers three sequential latent variables: belief (*z_b*), desire (*z_d*), and intention (*z_i*). Each level’s posterior *q_φ(z_l | h_fused, z_<l)* is Gaussian; the prior is a standard Gaussian at each level (or hierarchical).
- A goal predictor (MLP + Softmax) takes the concatenation *[z_b ∥ z_d ∥ z_i]* and outputs a distribution over all nodes in the graph.
The model is trained with a total loss *L_total = L_goal + β_KL * L_KL + β_recon * L_recon*, where *L_goal* is cross‑entropy against the true goal, *L_KL* is the sum of KL divergences between posteriors and priors across levels, and *L_recon* is a reconstruction loss (target unspecified) encouraging the latents to capture generative information.

The dataset consists of 100 agents, each with 1,000 synthetic episodes on a 3,185‑node campus graph. Goals are points of interest, and trajectories are generated via shortest‑path routing. A 70/30 train/test split per agent is used. The model is evaluated on Brier score at various prefix lengths, resistance to false goals, and robustness under preference drift.

## Key Results
- **Substantial performance gain:** At 25% observation, HiVAE achieves a Brier score of 0.104, far below the next best baseline (0.890), with statistical significance (*p*<0.01). The advantage persists across all observation fractions.
- **Robustness to misleading cues:** When agents pass near a location they strongly dislike, HiVAE keeps the predicted probability for that false goal low, whereas baselines increasingly associate the nearby location with the goal as the agent approaches.
- **Stability under preference drift:** After significant perturbation of agent goal distributions, HiVAE’s Brier score changes by only ~0.03, while other models exhibit larger shifts, indicating better generalization to non‑stationary preferences.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Hierarchical Latent Structure (BDI)
- **Claim:** The three‑level hierarchical latent structure (belief → desire → intention) enables more accurate and robust goal prediction by segregating and conditioning increasingly abstract mental states.
- **Mechanism:** Each latent variable level *z_l* (for *l* ∈ {b, d, i}) is generated by a network *f_φ* that conditions on the fused spatiotemporal representation *h_fused* and all previously inferred latents *z_<l*. This creates a Markov chain of latent dependencies where higher‑level states (intentions) integrate information from lower‑level states (beliefs, desires), mirroring the BDI cognitive model.
- **Core assumption:** Mental‑state reasoning for goal inference can be decomposed into a temporal hierarchy of latent variables where each level captures a distinct abstraction (e.g., beliefs about the environment, desires/goals, immediate intentions), and this decomposition improves predictive performance relative to a flat latent structure.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “Inspired by the belief‑desire‑intention (BDI) structure of human cognition, our three‑level VAE hierarchy achieves substantial performance improvements.”  
  - [section] “*q_φ(z_l|h_fused,z_<l) = N(z_l; µ_l, diag(σ²_l))* … where *z_<l* denotes concatenation of previous levels.”
- **Break condition:** If the conditioning *z_<l* fails to provide meaningful, discriminative information to subsequent levels (e.g., due to posterior collapse or uninformative latents), the hierarchy offers no advantage and may harm performance by introducing unnecessary complexity.

### Mechanism 2: Joint Spatiotemporal Fusion
- **Claim:** Parallel encoding and fusion of trajectory (temporal) and graph (spatial) data into a unified *h_fused* representation allows the model to jointly reason about movement patterns and environmental layout, which is critical for disambiguating goals in large graphs.
- **Mechanism:** A Transformer processes the node sequence *{v_t}* with temporal positional encodings to capture dynamics (*h_traj*). A Graph Attention Network (GAT) processes the environment graph *G* to capture spatial connectivity and relative node importance (*h_graph*). These are concatenated and passed through an MLP to produce *h_fused*, which serves as the conditioning base for the latent hierarchy.
- **Core assumption:** Goal inference in realistic spatiotemporal domains requires an integrated representation that simultaneously accounts for an agent’s observed motion history *and* the static/dynamic constraints of the environment graph; neither source alone is sufficient.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “It jointly encodes partial trajectories and environment graphs…”  
  - [section] “We fuse encodings via: *h_fused = MLP([h_traj ∥ h_graph])*, creating a unified spatiotemporal representation.”
- **Break condition:** If the environment graph is poorly defined (e.g., disconnected, inaccurate edges) or if trajectories are extremely noisy, the fused representation may become ambiguous or dominated by one modality, leading to poor latent conditioning and goal prediction.

### Mechanism 3: Robust Latent Preference Modeling
- **Claim:** The hierarchical VAE’s probabilistic latent space, trained with reconstruction and prediction objectives, inherently models agent‑specific preference distributions, making the system robust to misleading spatial cues and non‑stationary preference drift.
- **Mechanism:** The latent variables *z_b, z_d, z_i* are regularized by a hierarchical prior and trained to reconstruct parts of the input (via *L_recon*) and predict the final goal (*L_goal*). This forces the latents to encode information that is both generative of the trajectory and predictive of the goal, capturing stable behavioral priors (preferences) rather than transient path details.
- **Core assumption:** An agent’s long‑term goals/preferences are relatively stable latent causes of their observed trajectories; a model that learns to capture these latent causes will be less susceptible to being misled by short‑term, locally consistent but globally misleading path segments (e.g., passing near a non‑preferred location).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “It also shows robustness by maintaining low false‑goal probability when agents pass near misleading locations and demonstrates stable performance under significant agent preference drift.”  
  - [section] Experiment 2 (False Goal Test) and Experiment 3 (Generalizability) results in Figures 3 & 4 and Tables 1 & 2.
- **Break condition:** If the agent’s preferences are extremely volatile or if the training data lacks sufficient examples per agent to learn a stable prior, the latent variables may fail to capture meaningful preferences, and the model’s robustness will degrade to that of non‑hierarchical baselines.

  - *Quick check question:* “What is the key difference between a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) and a Graph Attention Network (GAT) in how they aggregate information from neighbors?”
- **Belief‑Desire‑Intention (BDI) Cognitive Architecture**  
  - *Why needed here:* HiVAE’s three‑level hierarchy is explicitly inspired by BDI. Understanding this cognitive model provides the conceptual rationale for separating mental states into beliefs (about the world), desires (goals), and intentions (plans to act).  
  - *Quick check question:* “In a BDI model, how does an agent’s set of beliefs typically influence its set of desires, and how do desires then generate intentions?”

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** `(T, G) → (Transformer, GAT) → (h_traj, h_graph) → MLP → h_fused → (z_b → z_d → z_i) → MLP + Softmax → goal distribution → L_total`
- **Critical path:** The core flow goes: (T, G) → h_traj/h_graph → h_fused → hierarchical latent inference (*z_b* first, then *z_d* conditioned on *h_fused* and *z_b*, then *z_i* conditioned on *h_fused* and previous latents) → concatenated latents → goal predictor → *L_goal*. The sequential latent sampling and conditioning are crucial for both training (reparameterization) and inference.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Hierarchy depth vs. training stability:* Adding more BDI levels improves performance but increases risk of posterior collapse and training instability, requiring careful KL annealing and initialization.  
  - *Fusion simplicity vs. information loss:* Simple concatenation of *h_traj* and *h_graph* may not optimally integrate modalities; more complex fusion (e.g., cross‑attention) could help but adds computational overhead.  
  - *Latent dimensionality:* Larger latent dimensions can encode richer abstractions but demand more training data and increase computational cost for the goal predictor.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *High Brier score early in trajectories:* Could indicate poor *h_fused* (check GAT/Transformer outputs), latent collapse (monitor KL term), or non‑discriminative latents (check variance).  
  - *Susceptibility to false goals:* Suggests latents are not capturing stable preferences; check if *L_recon* forces memorization of paths; verify utilization of *z_d*.  
  - *Large performance drop under preference drift:* Indicates overfitting to static training priors; latents may be encoding agent‑ID shortcuts rather than generalizable preferences. Consider adding agent embeddings.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. *Goal inference over time:* Train HiVAE and all baselines on the 70% train split. Evaluate Brier at 25%, 50%, 75%, 95% on the 30% test set. Confirm HiVAE’s significant outperformance (*p*<0.01) and stable early inference (flatter Brier curve).  
  2. *False goal test:* For each agent, identify the most and least preferred goals from the training distribution. Generate a trajectory that passes within one hop of the least‑preferred goal en route to the most‑preferred. Plot the predicted probability of the false goal across the prefix lengths. HiVAE should keep this probability low, while baselines rise as the agent approaches the false location.  
  3. *Preference drift test:* For each agent, perturb the goal distribution to create a new distribution with KL divergence >1. Sample new test trajectories from the perturbed distribution. Compute Brier scores on both the original and drifted test sets, and report the difference. HiVAE should exhibit minimal change (~0.03) compared to larger shifts for baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Q1: How can the latent variables be explicitly grounded to cognitive mental states?** The learned representations lack explicit alignment with beliefs, desires, or intentions. Self‑supervised strategies (contrastive learning, auxiliary prediction, intention consistency) are proposed but not yet implemented.
- **Q2: How to incorporate individual agent preferences via meta‑learning?** The current architecture treats all agents identically; future work will add agent‑specific embeddings (à la ToMNet) to capture personal preferences and enable few‑shot adaptation.
- **Q3: Do alternative latent architectures (normalizing flows, diffusion models) outperform VAEs?** The paper uses a hierarchical VAE; other generative models may offer better disentanglement or expressiveness for mental state representation but remain unexplored.
- **Q4: How does HiVAE scale to real‑world noisy trajectories and non‑spatial domains?** Evaluation used synthetic, noise‑free shortest‑path data on a static graph. Performance on real‑world data (e.g., GPS traces, healthcare sensors) with missing observations and dynamic environments is unknown.

## Limitations
- **Critical hyperparameter and training details missing:** The exact learning rate, β‑annealing schedules, latent dimensions, GAT/Transformer configurations, and the precise reconstruction target (*L_recon*) are unreported, making faithful replication difficult.
- **Baseline implementations not provided:** All compared methods (e.g., BToM, ToMNet) lack architectural and training specifications, so the reported performance gap may be due to suboptimal baselines rather than inherent superiority.
- **Evaluation restricted to synthetic, noise‑free data:** The experiments assume perfect shortest‑path trajectories on a static graph; real‑world trajectories contain noise, missing points, and dynamic obstacles, which could degrade performance significantly.

## Confidence
- **Hierarchical latent structure improves goal inference:** Medium confidence (internal ablations suggest benefit but lack external validation or detailed failure analysis of flat models).
- **Joint spatiotemporal representation is essential:** Medium confidence (plausible and aligned with multimodal ToM trends, but the paper does not ablate either modality to isolate its unique contribution).
- **Robustness to false goals and preference drift:** Low confidence (robustness tested only on synthetic, controlled perturbations; real‑world misleading cues and non‑stationarity are likely more challenging).
