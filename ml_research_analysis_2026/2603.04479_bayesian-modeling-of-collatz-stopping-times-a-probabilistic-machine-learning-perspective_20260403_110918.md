# Bayesian Modeling of Collatz Stopping Times: A Probabilistic Machine Learning Perspective

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.04479  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04479  
- **Reference count**: 37  
- **Primary result**: A Bayesian hierarchical Negative Binomial regression (NB2‑GLM) using log n and n mod 8 predicts the Collatz total stopping time τ(n) with log predictive score –272,912 (average –5.46) and 1‑Wasserstein distance 3.20 on 50,000 test points, far outperforming stochastic odd‑block generators.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates probabilistic modeling of the Collatz total stopping time τ(n) for n ≤ 10⁷. Two complementary approaches are developed: (1) a Bayesian hierarchical Negative Binomial regression (NB2‑GLM) that models τ(n) as a function of log n and the residue class n mod 8, with partial pooling across the eight classes; (2) a stochastic odd‑block generator that approximates the Collatz dynamics by randomizing the number of halvings after each odd update, calibrated via a Dirichlet‑multinomial update on observed block lengths. On a held‑out test set of 50,000 numbers, the NB2‑GLM achieves a log predictive score of –272,912 (average –5.46 per observation) and a 1‑Wasserstein distance of 3.20, substantially beating both the global generator (score –1,165,983, W1=17.59) and the mod 8‑conditioned generator (score –1,079,087, W1=5.43). The dramatic improvement from conditioning the generator on m mod 8 highlights low‑order modular structure as a primary source of heterogeneity in τ(n).

## Method Summary
The study leverages a dataset of total stopping times computed for all integers n ≤ 10⁷. After randomly splitting this dataset into 50 000 training and 50 000 test points, two models are constructed. The NB2‑GLM assumes τ(n) follows a Negative Binomial distribution with mean µ_n = exp(β₀ + β_log log n + u_r), where r = n mod 8, and dispersion α. The random intercepts u_r share a hierarchical prior N(0, σ_u²) enabling partial pooling. The model is fit via NUTS MCMC. The odd‑block generator decomposes the stopping time as τ(n) = v₂(n) + Σ_{j=0}^{J-1} (1 + K(m_j)), where m_j are successive odd states and K(m) = v₂(3m+1). The block lengths K are modeled as i.i.d. draws from a categorical distribution p_k, estimated globally (G2) or conditionally on m mod 8 (G3) using a symmetric Dirichlet(1) prior. Given fitted p_k (or p_k^{(r)}), τ for a test n is simulated by generating a sequence of random K's and summing contributions. Both predictive distributions are evaluated on the test set via log predictive score (with ε=10⁻¹² for numerical stability) and 1‑Wasserstein distance against the empirical τ distribution.

## Key Results
- **NB2‑GLM**: log predictive score –272,912 (avg –5.46), 1‑Wasserstein distance 3.20.  
- **Global odd‑block generator (G2)**: log score –1,165,983, W1=17.59.  
- **Mod 8‑conditioned generator (G3)**: log score –1,079,087, W1=5.43.  
- Conditioning the generator on m mod 8 yields a large improvement, confirming the importance of low‑order modular effects.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Overdispersed count modeling with NB2‑GLM**  
The Negative Binomial (NB2) likelihood accommodates the extreme overdispersion in τ(n) (empirical variance/mean ≈24.6) by expressing the variance as Var(Y|µ,α)=µ+αµ². The log‑link connects the conditional mean µ_n to log n, capturing the slow increase of average stopping time, while a random intercept u_r for r=n mod 8 accounts for systematic differences among residue classes. Partial pooling stabilizes estimates for classes with few observations, preventing overfitting. Posterior predictive checks show the NB2‑GLM reproduces the empirical distribution’s bulk and tail behavior.

**Mechanism 2: Odd‑block decomposition with stochastic block lengths**  
The Collatz trajectory can be reduced to alternating odd numbers; each odd update 3m+1 = 2^{K(m)} m' contributes 1+K(m) to τ. Replacing the deterministic K(m) with i.i.d. draws from a calibrated categorical distribution (derived from observed K values in the training data) yields a stochastic process that approximates the true τ distribution. This abstraction ignores within‑trajectory autocorrelations but retains the heavy‑tailed nature via the block‑length distribution.

**Mechanism 3: Modular dependence of block lengths on m mod 8**  
The block length K(m) exhibits a clear dependence on m mod 8 because 3m+1’s 2‑adic valuation is partly determined by m modulo low powers of 2. Estimating separate categorical probabilities p_k^{(r)} for each residue class r (using a Dirichlet prior) captures this effect. The resulting conditioned generator (G3) achieves a log score of –1,079,087 versus –1,165,983 for the global generator (G2), and reduces W1 from 17.59 to 5.43. This improvement demonstrates that low‑order modular structure is a dominant source of heterogeneity in τ(n).

## Foundational Learning
- **Overdispersed count models (Negative Binomial)**  
  *Why needed*: τ(n) is an integer count with variance far exceeding the mean, ruling out Poisson; NB2 accommodates overdispersion with a dispersion parameter α.  
  *Quick check*: In the NB2 parameterization with mean µ and α, what is the variance? (Answer: µ + αµ²)

- **Bayesian hierarchical modeling and partial pooling**  
  *Why needed*: The eight residue classes share information via a common prior on their intercepts, stabilizing estimates for small classes and quantifying uncertainty.  
  *Quick check*: How does increasing the hyperparameter σ_u affect the degree of partial pooling? (Answer: Larger σ_u reduces pooling, making class effects behave more like fixed effects.)

- **Proper scoring rules for distributional evaluation**  
  *Why needed*: Log predictive score and Wasserstein distance assess the quality of full predictive distributions, not just point estimates.  
  *Quick check*: What does it mean for a scoring rule to be "proper"? (Answer: It incentivizes the forecaster to report their true predictive distribution; the expected score is minimized when the reported distribution equals the true one.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Data generation (compute τ(n) for n ≤ 10⁷) → Feature engineering (log n, n mod 8, odd states, K(m)) → Branch: (NB2‑GLM trainer: NUTS MCMC) and (Generator calibrator: Dirichlet‑multinomial fits) → Predictive engine (NB2 posterior predictive averaging; Generator Monte Carlo simulation) → Evaluation (log score, Wasserstein distance) → Diagnostics (posterior predictive checks, block‑length posteriors).

**Critical path**  
1. Compute τ(n) for all n ≤ 10⁷ using dynamic programming with caching.  
2. Split dataset into fixed training (50 000) and test (50 000) sets using RNG seed 123.  
3. Fit NB2‑GLM with NUTS (2 chains, 1000 tuning, 1000 draws) and check convergence (R̂, ESS).  
4. Extract all odd states from training trajectories, compute K(m), and fit per‑class Dirichlet posteriors (K_max=30).  
5. For each test n, evaluate NB2‑GLM posterior predictive pmf and simulate 40 trajectories for the generator.  
6. Aggregate log predictive densities and compute 1‑Wasserstein distances.  
7. Generate diagnostic figures (posterior predictive checks, block‑length distributions, distribution overlays).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Covariates*: log n is used over raw n to better capture the slowly increasing mean; n mod 8 as a random effect balances flexibility and parsimony.  
- *Generator’s K_max*: Truncating K at 30 reduces state space but discards rare long blocks, potentially biasing τ downward.  
- *Monte Carlo replication (SMC)*: SMC=40 offers a practical trade‑off between noise and compute; higher counts improve log score stability at linear cost.  
- *Partial pooling strength*: Prior σ_u ~ HalfNormal(1) is weakly informative; too tight underfits class differences, too loose overfits.  
- *Scoring metrics*: Log score is sensitive to tails; Wasserstein provides a robust global measure; both are used.

**Failure signatures**  
- MCMC diagnostics: R̂ > 1.1 or ESS < 100 indicates poor mixing; divergences suggest model misspecification or need for reparameterization.  
- Generator non‑absorption: A non‑negligible fraction of simulated trajectories exceed MAX_STEPS=200,000 signals issues with block‑length calibration or odd‑projection implementation.  
- Log score discrepancy: A result far worse than reported (e.g., > –300,000) often points to feature mismatches (using n instead of log n), incorrect mod 8 assignment, or NB2 likelihood bugs.  
- Conditional generator no improvement: If G3’s score is close to G2’s, verify that the conditional p_k^{(r)} are correctly applied (i.e., conditioning on the first odd iterate’s residue).

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Dataset sanity check**: For a small n (e.g., 27), compute τ via the dynamic programming cache and via a naive step‑by‑step simulation; they must agree (τ(27) is known). Also verify the dispersion ratio on a subset matches ~24.6.  
2. **Regression baseline comparison**: Fit a Poisson GLM with only log n. Compute Pearson’s chi‑squared statistic; it should indicate severe overdispersion, justifying NB2.  
3. **Generator validation**: For a fixed residue class r, simulate many K from the global calibrated distribution and compare to the observed K(m) for m ≡ r (mod 8) in training data; the conditional generator should match class‑specific histograms while the global one should not.

## Limitations
- The odd‑block generator assumes i.i.d. block lengths given residue, ignoring potential within‑trajectory autocorrelation.  
- The NB2‑GLM uses only log n and mod 8; omitted variables like v₂(n) or higher‑order residues may further explain residual variation.  
- Exact tie‑breaking in the rounding step of the odd projection and certain RNG seeds beyond the train/test split are unspecified, which could cause minor numerical discrepancies upon reproduction.

## Confidence
- NB2‑GLM’s superiority: **High** (empirically validated, dispersion matches NB2, posterior predictive checks confirm).  
- Odd‑block generator’s distributional fit: **Medium** (improved by mod 8 conditioning but still far behind regression; i.i.d. assumption not fully verified).  
- Modular dependence (effect of m mod 8): **High** (large, robust improvement in scores and clear visual patterns in posterior p_k^{(r)}).

## Next Checks
1. Test whether conditioning the block‑length distribution on higher moduli (e.g., mod 16 or mod 32) yields further improvement over mod 8, which would indicate additional modular structure.  
2. Compute the autocorrelation function of K(m_j) within deterministic Collatz trajectories to assess the validity of the i.i.d. assumption in the generator.  
3. Perform a prior sensitivity analysis for the NB2‑GLM: vary σ_u (e.g., HalfNormal(0.5) and HalfNormal(2)) and α (e.g., HalfNormal(2) and HalfNormal(10)) to confirm that the main conclusions are not prior‑driven.
