# EARTalking: End-to-end GPT-style Autoregressive Talking Head Synthesis with Frame-wise Control

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20307  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20307  
- **Reference Count:** 40  
- **Primary Result:** EARTalking achieves state-of-the-art performance in streaming talking head synthesis with frame-wise control, outperforming prior AR methods and matching diffusion-based ones on HDTF and MEAD datasets.

## Executive Summary
EARTalking addresses the limitations of existing autoregressive talking head methods (intermediate representations limiting expressiveness) and diffusion-based approaches (high latency and coarse control). It introduces an end-to-end GPT-style autoregressive framework that generates video frames sequentially while accepting audio and motion intensity as streaming, in-context conditions. Two core innovations enable this: Sink Frame Window Attention (SFA) maintains identity consistency across variable-length sequences via a permanent reference “sink” frame and KV‑cache; Frame Condition In-Context (FCIC) injects diverse control signals without separate networks. Evaluations on HDTF and MEAD datasets show superior lip‑sync accuracy, expression fidelity, and video quality, matching diffusion systems while enabling real‑time‑adjacent streaming generation.

## Method Summary
The system employs a pretrained 3D VAE (Wan2.1) to compress videos into latent sequences. Given a reference portrait image and an audio track, the model predicts future latents autoregressively. Training proceeds in two stages: first aligns audio and visual latents and trains the backbone; second adds cyclic positional encoding and frame‑wise motion control. Fixed‑length windows (e.g., 37 frames) are used with noise perturbation on input latents for robustness, and inference supports variable lengths via a sliding window.

Core mechanisms are Sink Frame Window Attention (SFA) and Frame Condition In‑Context (FCIC). SFA introduces an adaLN module conditioned on the reference frame’s latent and a permanent KV‑cache entry for that frame, ensuring identity constancy while allowing arbitrary stream lengths. FCIC treats audio, motion intensity, and future conditions as tokens added to the input sequence; the transformer processes them in a single pass without extra networks. A diffusion head refines each predicted latent over K spatial steps, balancing quality and speed.

## Key Results
- Best overall average ranking: 1.66 on HDTF, 1.50 on MEAD.
- Top lip‑sync scores: Sync‑C 5.707, Sync‑D 8.903 on HDTF; best expression fidelity (E‑FID 1.326 HDTF, 0.872 MEAD).
- Lowest video distortion: FVD 363.909 HDTF, 316.275 MEAD.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
EARTalking succeeds by unifying identity, audio, and motion within a single autoregressive backbone. The adaLN sink in SFA directly injects the reference identity into every generation step, preventing drift that typical AR models suffer over long horizons. The KV cache permanently stores the sink frame’s attention keys/values, guaranteeing stable identity regardless of sequence length. FCIC’s in‑context control eliminates separate conditioning networks; by appending control tokens alongside audio and visual tokens, the model learns to modulate generation on a per‑frame basis, enabling fine‑grained adjustments like varying motion intensity. The two‑stage training first establishes robust audio‑visual alignment and then refines motion control, reducing interference. Combined with diffusion‑based spatial refinement, the system produces high‑fidelity frames, while the causal backbone allows efficient streaming, avoiding the high latency of full diffusion models. These design choices bridge the gap between expressiveness and real‑time control.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
z_ref -> adaLN Sink -> FCA Transformer (with Audio A_t, Motion M_t, KV Cache) -> Diffusion Head -> z_t -> VAE Decoder -> F_t

**Critical Path**  
1. Encode all training videos into latent sequences using the pretrained 3D VAE.  
2. Extract audio features with WavLM‑Large and compute frame‑wise motion intensity from facial landmarks.  
3. Train the FCA backbone in two stages: first stage learns audio alignment and backbone; second stage adds cyclic positional encoding and motion control. Use noise perturbation on input latents.  
4. During inference, initialize KV cache with the sink frame’s latent and maintain it throughout, applying sliding window attention and cyclic position embeddings. Generate each frame autoregressively, refining the latent via the diffusion head.  
5. Decode each latent to a video frame with the VAE decoder.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Fixed‑length training vs. variable‑length streaming necessitates cyclic positional encoding and a sliding window; may cause identity drift over very long sequences.  
- Diffusion head for spatial AR (K steps) improves frame quality but adds sequential cost per frame.  
- KV cache with permanent sink frame ensures identity stability but increases memory linearly; sliding window mitigates this.  
- FCIC’s single‑network control reduces complexity but requires careful embedding design to avoid interference between conditions.

**Failure Signatures**  
- *Identity fade* after a few seconds → likely missing/incorrect adaLN sink or KV cache maintenance.  
- *Lip‑sync mismatch* → examine audio embedding injection; verify bidirectional audio‑visual attention and audio cache.  
- *Frame distortion or jitter* → may result from insufficient noise perturbation during training.  
- *Performance drop when extending sequence beyond training window* → cyclic positional encoding becomes ambiguous; consider hierarchical memory.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. Baseline replication: Train on HDTF and report metrics from Table 1 to confirm implementation correctness.  
2. Ablate SFA: Remove the sink frame’s KV cache and compare identity preservation (E‑FID) and overall quality (FVD) on sequences of 2× training length.  
3. Test FCIC sensitivity: Generate with motion intensity scaled to 0.5×, 1.0×, and 2.0×; quantify landmark displacement and lip‑sync to verify control fidelity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
*Question:* How to achieve real‑time inference in EARTalking?  
*Basis:* The paper states it still struggles to reach real‑time targets due to its masked autoregressive inference.  
*Why unresolved:* Autoregressive decoding and spatial masked diffusion are sequentially heavy.  
*What would resolve:* Provide FPS measurements on RTX 3090 with optimizations (e.g., fewer diffusion steps, KV‑cache reuse) achieving >25 FPS with <5% drop in key metrics.

**Open Question 2**  
*Question:* How can FCIC be extended to new control signal types (e.g., emotion, gaze) without separate networks?  
*Basis:* Future work: integrating additional control conditions into the streaming framework.  
*Why unresolved:* Adding modalities might require distinct representations and could interfere with existing controls.  
*What would resolve:* Append new control embeddings to in‑context sequence, validate via metrics or user studies, and show no degradation on audio and motion controls.

**Open Question 3**  
*Question:* For videos much longer than the 37‑frame training window, does EARTalking preserve identity and expression fidelity?  
*Basis:* Figure 5 shows degradation at 3× length, implying limited extrapolation of cyclic positional encoding.  
*Why unresolved:* Attention window size and cyclic positions may lose reference influence and cause ambiguity over many steps.  
*What would resolve:* Evaluate metrics (E‑FID, Sync‑C, FVD) across lengths up to 10×; if performance drops, test improvements like hierarchical memory.
