# Learning to Staff: Offline Reinforcement Learning and Fine-Tuned LLMs for Warehouse Staffing Optimization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.24883
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24883
- Reference count: 5
- Primary result: Offline RL with Transformer-GNN yields 2.4% throughput improvement; fine-tuned LLMs with iterative DPO achieve +0.6% over human baseline in separate simulators.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses real-time worker reallocation in semi-automated warehouses to maximize throughput. It presents two distinct approaches: first, a custom Transformer-GNN (TGNN) trained with offline reinforcement learning on detailed historical state representations; second, a fine-tuned large language model (LLM) that processes human-readable state summaries. In learned simulators, the offline RL policy improves throughput by 2.4% over historical human decisions. The LLM, after supervised fine-tuning and iterative Direct Preference Optimization using simulator-generated preferences, matches and slightly exceeds the human baseline by 0.6% in a hand-crafted simulator. The work demonstrates the viability of both neural and LLM-based methods for offline staffing optimization, highlighting trade-offs in state representation, simulator dependence, and interpretability.

## Method Summary
The authors develop two parallel pipelines. The offline RL pipeline uses a Transformer-GNN encoder that processes spatial (lines/stages) and temporal dependencies from historical trajectories (31,366 shifts, 501,856 transitions). The policy employs an action factorization head where each worker independently decides to stay or move based on dot-product similarities, with a movement threshold (stay probability <0.1) to limit simultaneous moves. Training combines actor-critic updates with advantage-weighted regression and behavioral regularization to mitigate distribution shift. Evaluation occurs in a learned simulator that approximates real dynamics. The LLM pipeline abstracts raw metrics into structured text. It begins with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on historical decisions using Qwen2.5 models. Then, iterative Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is applied: preference pairs are generated by rolling out two candidate actions in a hand-crafted simulator and labeling the higher-throughput action as preferred. The final policy is evaluated in the hand-crafted simulator.

## Key Results
- Transformer-GNN with offline RL yields a 2.4% throughput improvement over replayed human decisions in learned simulators.
- Fine-tuned LLM with SFT + iterative DPO achieves +0.6% improvement over historical baseline in a hand-crafted simulator.
- Action factorization reduces computational complexity by 95% with no performance loss.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Offline RL with Detailed State Representations
- Claim: Using a Transformer-GNN on fine-grained historical states and training via offline actor-critic with behavioral regularization produces policies that exceed replayed human decisions in learned simulators.
- Mechanism: The TGNN encodes spatial (lines/stages) and temporal dependencies; the actor-critic updates the policy using advantages computed from a state-value critic, while a regularization term keeps the policy close to demonstrated behavior to avoid distribution shift.
- Core assumption: The learned simulator faithfully approximates real system dynamics, and the conditional independence in the reallocation head does not critically harm joint optimality.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "custom Transformer-based policies using offline reinforcement learning on detailed historical state representations, achieving a 2.4% throughput improvement"
  - [section 3] details the TGNN architecture, actor-critic implementation, and behavioral regularization (pages 4–5; Table 2).
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence for this exact combination is weak; neighboring papers (e.g., 37257 on RL for warehouse orchestration) apply RL to different warehouse tasks.
- Break condition: If the simulator’s buffer or throughput predictions diverge significantly from reality, the policy may overfit to simulated dynamics and underperform live.

### Mechanism 2: Action Factorization via Reallocation Head
- Claim: Decomposing the combinatorial worker reassignment into independent per-worker decisions with a movement threshold reduces computational complexity and enables tractable training without sacrificing performance.
- Mechanism: The reallocation head computes attention-like similarities between position features; each occupied position samples a destination (including staying). A threshold (stay probability <0.1) limits simultaneous moves, approximating the full joint action space.
- Core assumption: Worker move decisions are conditionally independent given the system state, and the threshold effectively controls the frequency of reallocations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 3] "We address this through factorization... each worker independently decides... We apply a threshold: workers only move if the probability of staying is below 0.1."
  - [section 3] "This reduces computational complexity substantially, with all models fitting on a single GPU and training time being reduced by 95% without a loss in performance."
  - [corpus] Limited direct evidence; related graph-based spatial-temporal models (e.g., Zheng et al., 2020 cited) inspire the architecture but are not validated for staffing.
- Break condition: If coordinated multi-worker moves are necessary for optimal performance, the independent assumption will degrade solution quality.

### Mechanism 3: Iterative DPO with Simulator-Generated Preferences
- Claim: Starting from SFT on historical decisions, iterative DPO using preferences computed from short simulator rollouts teaches the LLM to avoid unnecessary moves and slightly exceeds human baseline throughput.
- Mechanism: Pairs of candidate actions for the same state are evaluated by rolling them out in the simulator; the higher-throughput action is labeled preferred. DPO updates the policy to increase the likelihood of preferred actions relative to non-preferred ones. Iterating regenerates preferences with the improved policy, creating a feedback loop.
- Core assumption: Simulator-based preferences reliably reflect manager priorities (e.g., throughput maximization) and the LLM can iteratively refine from synthetic feedback.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "supervised fine-tuning combined with Direct Preference Optimization on simulator-generated preferences achieves performance that matches or slightly exceeds historical baselines."
  - [section 4] describes preference generation, DPO training, and iterative refinement; Table 4 shows SFT+DPO (2 iterations) at +0.6% (95% CI [+0.5%, +0.7%]).
  - [corpus] Corpus shows LLMs used for warehouse routing and bottleneck identification but not staffing; evidence for this application is still emerging.
- Break condition: If simulator dynamics are inaccurate or the preference signal is noisy, DPO may overfit to simulator-specific quirks rather than true operational goals.

## Foundational Learning
- Concept: Offline Reinforcement Learning
  - Why needed here: Real warehouses cannot host online exploration; we must learn exclusively from historical trajectories. Offline RL enables policy improvement without live interaction, but requires handling distribution shift and overestimation.
  - Quick check question: "How does behavioral regularization (e.g., Fujimoto & Gu, 2021) mitigate the risk of the policy exploiting out-of-distribution actions in offline RL?"
- Concept: Behavioral Cloning and Fine-Tuning
  - Why needed here: Imitating human decisions provides a strong initialization. Fine-tuning on a subset of high-reward trajectories further focuses learning on successful patterns, as shown by the 2.1% gain from BC-FT vs 1.6% from BC.
  - Quick check question: "Why might fine-tuning on only the top-performing demonstrations (BC-FT) yield higher returns than training on all demonstrations, even though the dataset is smaller?"
- Concept: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
  - Why needed here: Standard SFT cannot distinguish between good and bad actions; DPO learns from pairwise comparisons, teaching the model which actions to avoid (e.g., unnecessary moves). This is vital for correcting over-generation tendencies observed in early experiments.
  - Quick check question: "What is the key objective function in DPO, and how does it implicitly define a reward model without explicit reinforcement learning?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map:
  - Offline RL pipeline: Data loader (historical transitions) → Encoder (embeddings) → TGNN (spatial-temporal blocks) → Reallocation head (per-worker move probabilities) + Value head (state value). Training loop computes advantages and applies actor-critic + behavioral regularization losses.
  - LLM pipeline: State abstractor (converts raw metrics to text) → SFT model (initial imitation) → DPO trainer (uses simulator to generate preference pairs). Inference: prompt with abstracted state → JSON action.
- Critical path:
  - Offline RL: Preprocess dataset → train TGNN → freeze encoder, tune reallocation head? → evaluate in learned simulator.
  - LLM: Build SFT dataset (historical decisions + text states) → train SFT → generate preference pairs via simulator rollouts → iterative DPO → evaluate in hand-crafted simulator.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Detailed vs. abstracted state: Detailed states capture more dynamics but require custom neural architecture; abstracted states align with manager intuition but rely on a hand-crafted simulator.
  - Model size vs. latency: Offline RL uses a compact TGNN enabling fast inference; LLM approach, especially with large models, may have higher latency but offers natural language interfaces.
- Failure signatures:
  - Offline RL: Large gap between two learned simulators’ evaluation results; policy makes too few moves (threshold too high) or too many (threshold too low); value loss increasing.
  - LLM: Zero-shot performance below no-move baseline; SFT overfits (loss decreases but simulator performance plateaus); DPO iteration reduces performance or collapses diversity (all actions become “no move”).
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Train a behavior cloning baseline (no advantage weighting) to validate data pipeline; expect ~1.6% improvement (Table 2).
  2. Run zero-shot prompting with base LLM on abstracted states; expect performance worse than human baseline (e.g., -3.5%, Table 3), justifying fine-tuning.
  3. Implement SFT followed by one DPO iteration; expect near human parity (0% gain) and then a second iteration to achieve modest exceedance (+0.6%, Table 4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Does iterative DPO with real manager feedback outperform synthetic-preference DPO and historical baselines in live warehouse settings?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The future work section identifies replacing simulator-generated preferences with real manager feedback as a critical next step for deployment.
- Why unresolved: Synthetic preferences may not capture the nuance of human priorities (e.g., worker safety, fatigue) and the dynamics of real manager-AI interaction remain untested.
- What evidence would resolve it: A/B testing in operational warehouses, comparing policies trained on real pairwise preferences or alternative actions against synthetic-preference policies, measuring throughput and manager satisfaction.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does continuous online learning from real-time manager feedback affect policy stability and performance over extended deployments?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors propose an online learning setting with periodic updates as a promising direction for post-deployment improvement.
- Why unresolved: The current iterative DPO is simulated and static; real-time feedback may introduce non-stationarity and distribution shift that destabilize learning.
- What evidence would resolve it: Longitudinal deployment studies tracking policy performance, feedback quality, and update frequency, with controlled experiments varying update schedules and feedback granularity.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What operational factors beyond throughput (e.g., worker safety, fatigue, fairness) do managers implicitly consider, and how can these be integrated into LLM-based decision policies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The limitations note that real manager preferences include factors not captured by the throughput-only simulator.
- Why unresolved: The current reward model is single-objective (throughput), omitting multi-dimensional human judgment that may trade off throughput against other concerns.
- What evidence would resolve it: Qualitative studies (e.g., manager interviews, think-aloud protocols) to identify key non-throughput factors, followed by extending the preference framework to multi-objective DPO that incorporates these factors.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Can scaling LLM size beyond 32B parameters reduce or eliminate the need for fine-tuning in this staffing task?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Future work suggests scaling model size as a potential improvement, and the study finds prompting insufficient with 14B/32B models.
- Why unresolved: Larger models may possess more latent operational reasoning knowledge, enabling effective zero-shot or few-shot decision-making without task-specific adaptation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluate state-of-the-art large LLMs (e.g., >100B parameters) using identical prompting strategies in the hand-crafted simulator, comparing performance to fine-tuned smaller models and historical baselines.

### Open Question 5
- Question: How robust are offline RL policies to inaccuracies in the learned simulator, and do they transfer to real warehouse dynamics?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The limitations acknowledge that some improvement may stem from policy adaptation to simulator divergence rather than real-world efficacy.
- Why unresolved: Policies are evaluated in simulators with known prediction errors (e.g., buffer state inaccuracies), and their sensitivity to dynamics mismatch or facility-to-facility variation is unknown.
- What evidence would resolve it: Stress-test policies across multiple simulators with varying fidelity and in pilot real deployments, measuring performance degradation and identifying failure modes due to simulator-reality gaps.

## Limitations
- Simulator fidelity: Both results depend on unvalidated simulators; real-world transfer is unknown.
- Data and implementation gaps: Full historical dataset, exact feature engineering, and critical hyperparameters (e.g., TGNN dimensions, DPO β) are unreleased.
- Generalization constraints: The TGNN’s conditional independence assumption for worker moves and the LLM’s reliance on abstracted states may not capture coordination needs or subtle dynamics in a live warehouse.

## Confidence
- Offline RL throughput gain (2.4%): Medium (internal simulator evaluation; no real-world A/B test).
- LLM parity/exceedance (+0.6%): Medium (hand-crafted simulator; synthetic preferences may misalign with true priorities).
- Mechanistic claims (factorization, iterative DPO): Low to Medium (limited ablation studies; break conditions not experimentally ruled out).

## Next Checks
1. Simulator sanity check: Rebuild the hand-crafted simulator using only the paper’s verbal description; validate expected nonlinear buffer effects (e.g., throughput drop when buffer >90% full).
2. Synthetic data benchmark: Generate a small synthetic warehouse dataset with a known optimal policy (e.g., via MDP enumeration). Train the TGNN and LLM pipelines; they should recover near-optimal performance.
3. Architecture replication stress test: Implement the TGNN exactly as described. Train on a held-out historical segment without the reallocation head’s threshold (0.1). If performance degrades severely vs. thresholded version, it supports the factorization claim; if not, the threshold may be masking a suboptimal design.
