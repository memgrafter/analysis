# COOL-MC: Verifying and Explaining RL Policies for Multi-bridge Network Maintenance

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07546  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07546  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** COOL-MC provides formal safety guarantees for an RL bridge maintenance policy, showing a 3.5% failure probability over 20 years and revealing a systematic bias toward bridge 1.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces COOL-MC, a framework that combines reinforcement learning with formal verification to provide safety guarantees and interpretability for policies that maintain aging multi-bridge networks. The authors encode a network of three heterogeneous bridges with a shared periodic budget as an MDP in PRISM, train a PPO agent, and then use COOL-MC to construct the induced DTMC from the learned policy. Probabilistic model checking on this DTMC yields a bridge failure probability of 3.5% over a 20-year horizon, near-zero budget exhaustion probability (<2.1×10⁻⁶), and reveals horizon-gaming behavior. Explainability analyses uncover a systematic bias: the policy over-weights the condition of bridge 1 relative to bridges 2 and 3 regardless of actual deterioration. Feature lumping shows that for bridge 1, a coarse three-level condition assessment suffices without measurable safety loss.

## Method Summary
The COOL-MC pipeline begins with a formal MDP model of the bridge maintenance domain specified in PRISM, capturing state variables (three NBI condition ratings, budget, cycle year, time, flags) and stochastic transitions for deterioration and maintenance effects. A PPO neural network with four hidden layers of 512 units is trained on this model to produce a policy that maps observations to feasible joint maintenance actions under budget constraints. After training, COOL-MC constructs the reachable induced DTMC by performing a guided exploration of the MDP: starting from the initial state, it queries the deterministic policy (argmax) to select an action, then follows all probabilistic successors, recursively building the subgraph reachable under the fixed policy. The resulting DTMC, which resolves all nondeterminism, is passed to the Storm model checker to verify PCTL properties such as the probability of bridge failure within a given horizon. For explainability, COOL-MC annotates DTMC states with derived labels (e.g., chosen action, coarsened features) and supports analyses including feature lumping (coarsening a feature and re-verifying), gradient-based saliency ranking (computing input gradients across visited states), action labeling (frequency of actions), and counterfactual action replacement (globally swapping actions and re-verifying). This combination of formal verification and perturbation-based explanation quantifies safety risks and uncovers policy biases.

## Key Results
- The trained PPO policy yields a bridge failure probability of 3.5% over a 20-year horizon.
- Budget exhaustion probability is negligible (<2.1×10⁻⁶), but the policy exhibits horizon-gaming by reducing maintenance near the planning horizon.
- The policy shows a systematic attention bias, consistently over-weighting bridge 1's condition irrespective of which bridge is most deteriorated.
- For bridge 1, using only a coarse three-level condition assessment (instead of the full NBI scale) does not measurably increase the failure probability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Induced DTMC Construction
- **Claim:** COOL-MC enables formal verification of an RL policy by resolving the MDP's nondeterminism into a reachable DTMC, avoiding full state explosion.
- **Mechanism:** After RL training, the tool traverses the MDP starting from the initial state. At each step it queries the memoryless policy to select a deterministic action. Only successors reachable under this fixed policy are explored and added to the induced DTMC. This constructs a substantially smaller, fully probabilistic model.
- **Core assumption:** The learned policy is memoryless (depends only on current observation) and the reachable state space under that policy is tractably smaller than the full MDP.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states that COOL-MC "constructs only the reachable state space induced by that policy, resolving all nondeterminism and yielding a discrete-time Markov chain (DTMC) amenable to probabilistic model checking". Section 3.1 defines the induced DTMC. Section 5.2 reports that |Dπ| (27,033) is smaller than the full MDP (156,579 states).
- **Break condition:** The policy is non-memoryless, or the reachable subgraph under the fixed policy remains combinatorially large.

### Mechanism 2: Exact Probabilistic Model Checking
- **Claim:** Probabilistic model checking on the induced DTMC provides exact, formal guarantees on safety properties, which are infeasible to compute on the original MDP.
- **Mechanism:** The DTMC, being a standard probabilistic model without nondeterminism, is passed to a model checker (Storm). Properties specified in PCTL (e.g., `P=?(♢"failed")`) are evaluated exactly over the finite state-transition graph, yielding precise probabilities rather than Monte Carlo estimates.
- **Core assumption:** The induced DTMC accurately captures the policy's behavior on the underlying MDP; i.e., the policy's action selection and the MDP's transition probabilities are correctly modeled and faithfully translated.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract reports "probabilistic model checking reveals that the trained policy has a safety-violation probability of 3.5%". Section 3.2 describes Storm. Section 4.3 details passing the DTMC to Storm.
- **Break condition:** Incorrect PRISM encoding or a mismatch between the training environment and the formal MDP model.

### Mechanism 3: Explainability via Perturbation and Re-verification
- **Claim:** Explainability analyses (feature lumping, saliency, action labeling, counterfactuals) are performed on the induced DTMC to formally quantify policy behavior and biases.
- **Mechanism:** The DTMC's states are annotated with derived labels (e.g., which action was selected, coarse feature bins). These labels become atomic propositions in PCTL queries. By reconstructing the DTMC under modified labeling (e.g., coarsened features, globally swapped actions) and re-running model checking, the tool measures the quantitative impact of those modifications on safety properties.
- **Core assumption:** The policy's reasoning can be interrogated by systematically perturbing its input space (features) or action space and observing the resulting change in verified outcomes on the same underlying MDP dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.4 explains feature lumping: "the lumped policy is re-evaluated on the full, unmodified MDP". Section 4.4 describes counterfactual action replacement: "we globally replace actions... and reconstruct and re-verify the induced DTMC". Section 5.2.7 reports labeling results showing the policy overwhelmingly selects a specific action for bridge 1.
- **Break condition:** The perturbation probes a dimension the policy is insensitive to, or alters the reachable state set so drastically that comparisons become invalid.

## Foundational Learning

### Markov Decision Process (MDP) & Discrete-Time Markov Chain (DTMC)
- **Why needed:** The entire verification pipeline starts from a formal MDP model. Understanding states, actions, transition probabilities, and the distinction between an MDP (with nondeterminism/policy) and its induced DTMC (fully probabilistic under a fixed policy) is fundamental.
- **Quick check:** Given an MDP and a memoryless policy, what is the mathematical object that represents the evolution of the system under that policy? (Answer: The induced DTMC.)

### Probabilistic Temporal Logic (PCTL)
- **Why needed:** Safety requirements must be formalized as PCTL formulas for the model checker to verify them (e.g., probability of failure below a threshold).
- **Quick check:** What does the PCTL property `P=?(♢≤100 "budget_empty")` compute? (Answer: The exact probability of reaching a state where the budget is empty within at most 100 steps.)

### Reinforcement Learning (PPO) & Policy Representation
- **Why needed:** The tool's input is a trained RL policy (PPO neural network). Understanding that the policy is a function from observations to actions, and that it is typically stochastic, is key. COOL-MC resolves this by using the policy's deterministic mode (argmax) for DTMC construction.
- **Quick check:** If an RL policy outputs a probability distribution over actions, how does COOL-MC use it to construct a deterministic transition system? (Answer: By selecting the action with the highest probability at each state/observation.)

### Explainability Methods (Saliency, Lumping, Counterfactuals)
- **Why needed:** The paper uses gradient-based saliency (local feature influence), feature lumping (global sensitivity), action labeling (behavioral profiling), and counterfactual replacement (what-if analysis). Each method requires understanding how to perturb the policy's input or decision and measure the effect via formal queries.
- **Quick check:** What is the difference between gradient-based saliency and feature lumping as used in this paper? (Answer: Saliency computes per-state input gradients; lumping coarsens the feature space globally and re-verifies the entire DTMC.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** PRISM MDP Model → RL Agent (PPO) → DTMC Constructor (COOL-MC Core) → Storm Model Checker; Explainability Preprocessors modify state labeling; Property/Query Harness orchestrates the workflow.

**Critical path:** PRISM Model → Train PPO Policy → DTMC Constructor (with optional label preprocessing) → Storm Verification → Result Interpretation.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Completeness vs. Tractability:** The reachable DTMC is incomplete (only states visited under the trained policy) but this yields scalability. Forgetting to explore a relevant state leads to unsound results.
- **Deterministic Policy Assumption:** The tool uses a deterministic (argmax) policy for DTMC construction. A highly stochastic policy might require sampling or considering multiple actions, breaking the simple construction.
- **PRISM Fidelity:** The analysis is only as good as the PRISM model. Simplifications must be validated against reality.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Construction fails/hangs:** The reachable state space is too large (policy explores too much) or the PRISM model has errors.
- **Verification times out or OOM:** The induced DTMC, while smaller than the full MDP, is still too large for Storm.
- **Contradictory results:** A property passes but a counterfactual with a minor change drastically increases it, indicating sensitivity or modeling error.
- **Zero visited states for a label:** In action labeling, if an action is never chosen, the probability of reaching a state with that action label is zero.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline Verification:** Run `P=?(♢"failed")` on the trained policy's DTMC to establish the core safety metric (failure probability).
2. **Feature Lumping:** Coarsen a key feature (e.g., `cond_b1` into three bins) and re-verify `P=?(♢"failed")`. Compare to baseline to assess if coarse assessment is safe.
3. **Gradient-based Saliency Ranking:** Compute mean absolute gradients of the policy output w.r.t. each input feature across all visited states. This produces a global importance ranking (e.g., `year`, `cond_b1` dominate).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Open Question 1:** How do verified safety properties change when MDP transition probabilities and reward structure are derived from real bridge inspection and monitoring data instead of synthetic values?  
  *Basis:* Abstract: "these results are based on artificially constructed transition probabilities and deterioration rates rather than real-world data, so absolute performance figures should be interpreted with caution."  
  *Why unresolved:* Synthetic model may not reflect actual deterioration patterns or inspection accuracy; thus numerical guarantees lack external validity.  
  *Evidence:* Integrate real datasets (e.g., NBI, state DOT inspections) to estimate transition probabilities and reward costs; re-run COOL-MC pipeline; compare verification results and assess sensitivity.

- **Open Question 2:** Can COOL-MC be extended to verify and explain RL policies for partially observable bridge maintenance problems that account for inspection uncertainty and hidden deterioration states?  
  *Basis:* Section 4.5: "First, the MDP assumes full observability of NBI condition ratings, whereas real inspection data are subject to inter-inspector variability and subsurface deterioration that is difficult to detect... A POMDP extension would be warranted once reliable observation models become available."  
  *Why unresolved:* Real-world inspections are imperfect, so a POMDP model would be more realistic; however, COOL-MC currently only supports MDPs.  
  *Evidence:* Develop a POMDP extension of COOL-MC, e.g., using belief MDPs or directly operating on belief states; evaluate on a case study with simulated observation noise; demonstrate verification of safety properties under partial observability.

- **Open Question 3:** Does using a permutation-invariant policy architecture (e.g., graph neural network with shared weights across bridges) eliminate the systematic attention bias toward bridge 1 and ensure that the policy focuses on the most deteriorated bridge in all states?  
  *Basis:* Section 5.2.6 (Worst bridge focus analysis): "This pattern confirms the asymmetry... reveals a potential coverage gap in the learned policy for bridge 3." Discussion: "The conditional saliency bias toward bridge 1 motivates either a worst-bridge penalty reward or a permutation-invariant policy architecture, with the desired outcome that cond_bi ranks first in saliency whenever bridge i is worst."  
  *Why unresolved:* The current feedforward network processes bridge conditions in a fixed order, leading to bias; a permutation-invariant architecture might remove that.  
  *Evidence:* Retrain the RL policy using a permutation-invariant architecture (e.g., treating bridge conditions as an unordered set via shared MLP or attention), then apply COOL-MC's feature importance analysis to verify that conditional rankings become symmetric: when bridge i is worst, cond_bi should rank highest.

- **Open Question 4:** What additional insights into policy behavior can be gained by integrating more advanced explainability methods (e.g., causal analysis, concept activation vectors) into COOL-MC, and how do they complement existing verification and explanation results?  
  *Basis:* Conclusion: "Beyond the analyses presented here, COOL-MC provides additional capabilities [48, 47, 49, 51, 50], which could yield further insight into maintenance behavior and safety margins in infrastructure maintenance settings."  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper demonstrates only four methods; other XRL techniques might reveal different aspects of policy logic, such as causal relationships or higher-level concepts.  
  *Evidence:* Implement and integrate one or more additional XRL methods (e.g., SHAP values, causal influence analysis) into COOL-MC; apply to the trained policy; contrast findings with those from feature lumping, saliency, and action labeling; assess practical utility for infrastructure managers.

## Limitations
- External dependency on PRISM model from GitHub; any code mismatch invalidates analysis.
- Missing PPO hyperparameters (e.g., clip, entropy) hinder exact replication of the trained policy.
- Assumes memoryless policies and full observability, which may not hold in complex or partially observable real-world settings.
