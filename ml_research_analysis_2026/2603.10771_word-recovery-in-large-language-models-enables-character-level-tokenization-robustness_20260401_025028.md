# Word Recovery in Large Language Models Enables Character-Level Tokenization Robustness

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.10771
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10771
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: LLMs trained with canonical subword tokenization can process character-level inputs robustly by internally reconstructing word-level token representations.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) are typically trained using subword tokenization, but they can also handle inputs broken down into individual characters, a non-canonical form. This paper uncovers the internal mechanism behind this robustness: word recovery, the process by which the model reconstructs the original word-level token representations from the character sequence. The authors introduce a decoding-based probe to measure recovery, finding it consistently high across models. Causal interventions show that the recovered representations are necessary for downstream task performance. Furthermore, masking in-group attention (attention among characters of the same word) in early layers reduces both recovery and performance, indicating that early-layer aggregation via in-group attention is a key enabling factor. Together, these results explain why LLMs are robust to non-canonical tokenization.

## Method Summary
The study employs three complementary methodologies. First, a decoding-based probe (LogitLens) is used: for each layer, the hidden states of character positions are decoded via the output embedding matrix, and a recovery score is computed as the proportion of canonical tokens (the tokens that would have been used in standard tokenization) whose IDs appear in the top-K predictions aggregated over the character positions belonging to that token. Second, causal interventions remove the subspace aligned with a canonical token’s output embedding vector from all character positions of that token, starting from a given layer onward; the resulting drop in task accuracy demonstrates the causal necessity of the recovered representation. Third, attention masking ablates connections between characters of the same canonical token in early layers to test the role of intra-group attention in enabling recovery.

## Key Results
- Recovery scores are high across models: Gemma-2-9B-It reaches up to 96.8%, while Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct achieve around 75%.
- Removing the recovered token subspace causes significant accuracy degradation (e.g., up to -35.8% on ARC-E for Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct).
- Masking in-group attention in early layers substantially reduces both recovery scores and downstream task performance, confirming the mechanism’s critical role.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Hidden state reconstruction of canonical tokens
**Claim:** Hidden states reconstruct canonical word-level token identities from character-level inputs.  
**Mechanism:** A decoding-based probe (LogitLens) decodes each character-level hidden state via the output embedding matrix, aggregates top-K predictions across the sequence for each canonical token, and checks if the token ID appears in the union. The recovery score is the proportion of unique canonical tokens recovered.  
**Core assumption:** The model’s output embedding matrix provides a linear readout of lexical identity in the residual stream, and canonical token identities are present as directions in hidden states even when inputs are character-level.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section 3 (decoding procedure and layerwise scores); supporting figures (2,8,9,10,11).  
**Break condition:** If canonical tokens are not decodable from any layer, the mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 2: Causal necessity of recovered representations
**Claim:** Recovered word-level representations are causally necessary for downstream task performance.  
**Mechanism:** A targeted subspace intervention removes the component of hidden states aligned with a canonical token’s output embedding vector (`w_t`) from all character positions belonging to that token, starting from a given layer through the final layer. Degradation in accuracy demonstrates causal reliance.  
**Core assumption:** Removing only the `w_t`-aligned component specifically disrupts lexical identity without globally scrambling the residual stream.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section 4 (intervention details, layerwise performance drops, correlation with recovery score).  
**Break condition:** If task accuracy is unaffected by the subspace removal (even with high recovery), the recovered representation is epiphenomenal.

### Mechanism 3: In-group attention enables aggregation
**Claim:** In-group attention (attention among characters of the same canonical token) in early layers enables word recovery.  
**Mechanism:** Masking pre-softmax attention scores within each canonical token’s character span (`G(t_i)`) prevents intra-group information flow. Early-layer masking reduces both subsequent recovery scores and task performance.  
**Core assumption:** In-group attention is the primary mechanism aggregating distributed character information into a coherent word-level representation; other aggregation mechanisms (e.g., MLPs) cannot compensate when removed.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section 5 (attention masking procedure and results).  
**Break condition:** If masking early in-group attention does not reduce later recovery scores or task accuracy, in-group attention is not the critical aggregator.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Subword Tokenization & Canonical Tokenization**  
  Why needed: The analysis contrasts the model’s canonical training tokenization (BPE) with character-level input. Understanding canonical tokens and character spans (`G(t_i)`) is foundational.  
  Quick check: Given “natural” tokenized as [“n”, “atural”], what are the canonical tokens and `G(t_i)` for each?

- **Concept: Probing with the LogitLens**  
  Why needed: The detection mechanism relies on decoding hidden states with `W_out` to see if lexical identities emerge, assuming linear decodability.  
  Quick check: If a hidden state at layer 5 has high probability for “ing” when decoded, what does that suggest about what that state represents?

- **Concept: Causal Mediation Analysis via Subspace Intervention**  
  Why needed: The claim of causal necessity depends on surgically removing a hypothesized direction (`w_t`) and measuring effect.  
  Quick check: Why subtract `<h, w_t> w_t` instead of zeroing out `h`? What might zeroing destroy besides the `w_t` direction?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
1. Input embedding layer: Embeds each character token into the model dimension.  
2. Transformer layers (residual stream + attention + MLP): Process the character sequence; includes multi-head attention matrices `A^(ℓ,h)` and residual stream activations `h_j^(ℓ)`.  
3. Output embedding matrix `W_out`: Used as a linear probe (`W_out^T h`) to decode hidden states into token distributions `p_j^(ℓ)`.  
4. LM head: Softmax over `W_out^T h` for final prediction (not directly used in recovery scoring).

**Critical path:**  
1. Early aggregation (layers 0–~5): In-group attention among characters of the same canonical token (`A_{jk}` for `j,k ∈ G(t_i)`) aggregates character features into a unified span representation.  
2. Lexical alignment (mid layers): The aggregated span representation aligns with the `w_t` direction for the canonical token.  
3. Downstream consumption (later layers): Subsequent layers and the LM head leverage these recovered token representations for task reasoning. Intervention after recovery is complete has less effect.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Top-K choice in decoding: Higher K yields higher recall but may include spurious tokens; lower K is stricter.  
- Span-localized vs. global decoding: Set-based decoding may overestimate recovery; in-group decoding is stricter but may miss tokens correctly represented but not decoded in-span.  
- Intervention scope: Removing `w_t` from all positions in `G(t_i)` isolates the token’s contribution; removing only from the first position might be insufficient if representation is distributed.

**Failure signatures:**  
- No recovery: Recovery score `R(ℓ)` remains ~0 across all layers.  
- Recovery without causal effect: High `R(ℓ)` but no accuracy drop from subspace removal.  
- In-group attention masking ineffective: Masking early in-group attention does not lower `R(ℓ)` or accuracy.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline recovery profiling: Compute layerwise `R(ℓ)` under character-level tokenization with K=5 for a model/dataset. Verify the recovery pattern matches paper figures.  
2. Causal intervention test: For a layer where recovery first becomes high, apply the `w_t` removal intervention to all tokens in a batch. Measure accuracy drop on the same examples.  
3. In-group attention masking: Mask in-group attention in the first N layers (e.g., 5). Recompute `R(ℓ)` and measure task accuracy. Expect both recovery and accuracy to drop significantly, with recovery reduced throughout the network.

## Limitations
- The decoding probe assumes linear decodability of lexical identity; it may capture semantic correlates rather than exact token IDs.
- Subspace removal might affect entangled features beyond the targeted direction.
- In-group attention masking relies on canonical token spans and may not rule out alternative aggregation pathways (e.g., cross-token attention or MLP-based pooling).

## Confidence
- Word recovery detection: Medium  
- Causal necessity of recovered representations: Medium  
- In-group attention as the aggregation mechanism: Medium  

## Next Checks
1. **Probe robustness**: Replace the decoding-based probe with a supervised linear classifier and a nonlinear probe; compare recovery patterns.
2. **Intervention specificity**: Apply the subspace removal to a task where canonical token identity is decoupled from the correct answer; a null performance drop would isolate the effect.
3. **Head-level in-group analysis**: Compute an in-group attention ratio per head, identify top-contributing heads, and ablate them selectively in early layers.
