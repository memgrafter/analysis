# Inference-Time Alignment for Diffusion Models via Doob's Matching

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.06514
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06514
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Inference-time alignment via Doob's matching provides a consistent plug-in guidance estimator and non-asymptotic convergence guarantees in 2-Wasserstein distance, with rates depending only on the intrinsic dimension under a low-dimensional subspace assumption.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles inference-time alignment for diffusion models: adapting a pre-trained reference diffusion to sample from a target distribution \(q_0 \propto w p_0\) without retraining. The authors introduce **Doob's matching**, where guidance is defined as the gradient of the log-Doob \(h\)-function, estimated via gradient-regularized regression. The method yields a consistent estimator without requiring target samples or gradients of \(w\). Theoretically, non-asymptotic convergence rates are established for both the guidance estimator (in \(L^2\)) and the resulting controllable diffusion process (in 2-Wasserstein distance). Crucially, under the assumption that the reference distribution lies on a low-dimensional linear subspace, the rates depend only on that intrinsic dimension, mitigating the curse of dimensionality. The approach combines stochastic calculus (Doob's h-transform), variational stability (Sobolev regularization), and careful analysis of neural network approximations.

## Method Summary
Given a pre-trained reference diffusion model with score estimate \(\hat{s}(t,x) \approx \nabla \log p_{T-t}(x)\) and a known weight function \(w(x)\) defining the target \(q_0(x) \propto w(x)p_0(x)\), the goal is to sample from \(q_0\) at inference time. The guidance term is derived from Doob's \(h\)-function \(h^*(t,x) = \mathbb{E}_P[w(X_T^{\leftarrow}) \mid X_t^{\leftarrow}=x]\). The authors propose to estimate \(h^*\) by minimizing a regularized least-squares objective on samples from the reference distribution: \(\min_h \mathbb{E}[(h - w)^2] + \lambda \mathbb{E}[\|\nabla h\|^2]\). The resulting estimator \(\hat{h}_\lambda\) is used to compute guidance \(g(t,x) = \nabla \log \hat{h}_\lambda(t,x)\). During sampling, an exponential integrator combines the frozen reference score \(\hat{s}\) and the guidance \(g\) to evolve the reverse-time SDE. Post-processing includes truncation of particle norms and a scaling step to correct early-stopping drift. No retraining of the reference model is required; only the guidance network is trained on data drawn from the reference distribution.

## Key Results
- The guidance term \(2\nabla \log h^*\) is exactly the drift correction that transforms the reference diffusion path measure into the target path measure (via Doob's h-transform).
- Gradient-regularized regression yields a consistent plug-in estimator \(\hat{h}_\lambda\) with non-asymptotic \(L^2\) bounds for both \(h\) and \(\nabla h\) (oracle inequalities).
- Under a low-intrinsic-dimensional subspace assumption on \(p_0\), the convergence rate of the generated distribution in 2-Wasserstein distance depends only on the intrinsic dimension \(d^* \ll d\), avoiding the curse of dimensionality.
- Theoretical guarantees are non-asymptotic and account for reference score error, estimation error of \(h\), and discretization error.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach rests on three pillars:

1. **Doob’s h-transform** provides a mathematically rigorous way to derive the guidance drift. The target distribution \(q_0 = w p_0 / Z\) corresponds to a change of measure from the reference diffusion path measure \(P\) to \(Q\). The Radon-Nikodym derivative satisfies \(L_t = h^*(t,X_t^{\leftarrow}) / \mathbb{E}[w(X_T^{\leftarrow})]\) where \(h^*(t,x) = \mathbb{E}[w(X_T^{\leftarrow}) \mid X_t^{\leftarrow}=x]\). Applying Girsanov’s theorem yields the SDE under \(Q\) with drift \(2\nabla \log h^*\). (Evidence: Section 3.2, Props 3.1–3.2)

2. **Gradient-regularized regression** ensures that minimizing a least-squares objective for \(h\) also yields a reliable estimate of its gradient, which is the guidance. The penalized objective \(J_t^\lambda(h)=J_t(h)+\lambda\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla h\|^2]\) is coercive in the \(H^1\) norm, so low empirical risk simultaneously controls function and gradient errors. As \(\lambda\to0\), the minimizer converges to \(h^*\) in both \(L^2\) and \(H^1\) under smoothness assumptions. (Evidence: Props 4.2–4.3, Lemma 5.2)

3. **Low intrinsic dimensionality** reduces sample complexity. If the reference distribution \(p_0\) is supported on a \(d^*\)-dimensional linear subspace (i.e., \(p_0 = P\sharp \bar{p}_0\) with \(P \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d^*}\) orthonormal), then \(h^*(t,x)\) reduces to a function of the projected coordinates \(P^\top x\). Consequently, the estimation problem effectively has dimension \(d^*\), and the convergence rates feature \(d^*\) instead of ambient \(d\). (Evidence: Prop 5.7, Thm 5.9)

## Foundational Learning
- **Doob’s h-transform**: Why needed: It mathematically justifies how to modify a reference diffusion to sample from a tilted target distribution via a drift correction. Quick check: How does the conditional expectation \(h^*(t,x) = \mathbb{E}[w(X_T^{\leftarrow})|X_t^{\leftarrow}=x]\) lead to the target SDE?
- **Variational stability (Sobolev regularization)**: Why needed: Guarantees that the estimated \(h\) has a well-behaved gradient, preventing oscillatory guidance. Quick check: Why does adding \(\lambda\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla h\|^2]\) to the loss encourage simultaneous approximation of \(h\) and \(\nabla h\)?
- **VC dimension and Rademacher complexity**: Why needed: Generalization bounds in the oracle inequality depend on the complexity of the hypothesis class and its derivatives, guiding architecture choices. Quick check: How does the VC dimension of the gradient class \(\nabla\mathcal{H}_t\) affect the convergence rate compared to that of \(\mathcal{H}_t\) itself?
- **Wasserstein distance**: Why needed: The final guarantee is in 2-Wasserstein distance between generated and target distributions, which respects the geometry of the sampling process. Quick check: Why is KL divergence insufficient for the final error bound, and how is \(W_2\) obtained from it?
- **Exponential integrator**: Why needed: The controllable diffusion’s semi-linear drift makes the exponential integrator a natural and accurate numerical scheme; its discretization error is a key term in the analysis. Quick check: What property of the SDE makes the exponential integrator suitable, and what role do truncation and scaling play?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Reference score network \(\hat{s}(t,x)\) (frozen) → Guidance estimator \(\hat{h}_\lambda(t,x)\) (neural net trained via gradient-regularized regression) → Controllable diffusion solver (exponential integrator combining \(\hat{s}\) and \(\nabla \log \hat{h}_\lambda\)) → Post-processing (truncation \(T_R\) and scaling \(M\))

**Critical path**  
- *Training*: Sample \(\{X_i^0\} \sim p_0\). For each time \(t\), generate pairs \((t, x = \mu_{T-t} X_i^0 + \sigma_{T-t} \varepsilon_i, y = w(X_i^0))\). Minimize empirical loss plus \(\lambda\) gradient penalty to obtain \(\hat{h}_\lambda\).  
- *Inference*: Initialize \(x_0 \sim \mathcal N(0,I)\). For each step \(k=0,\dots,K-1\): compute \(\hat{s}(kh, x_k)\) and \(g_\lambda(kh, x_k) = \nabla \log \hat{h}_\lambda(kh, x_k)\); update \(x_{k+1}\) via exponential integrator (4.8). After \(K\) steps, apply \(M \circ T_R\) to final particles.

**Design tradeoffs**
- **Regularization \(\lambda\)**: Too small → noisy gradients; too large → biased \(h\). Theory suggests \(\lambda = O(n^{-2/(d^*+8)})\).
- **Network size**: Depth \(L = O(\log n)\), number of non-zero weights \(S = O(n^{d^*/(d^*+8)})\) balance approximation and generalization.
- **Timesteps \(K\) and early-stopping \(T_0\)**: More steps reduce discretization error but increase cost; \(T_0\) trades off initialization bias vs. guidance error due to \(\sigma_{T-t}^{-8}\) blowup near \(t=T\).

**Failure signatures**
- **Exploding guidance**: If \(\hat{h}_\lambda\) becomes too small or its gradients blow up, diffusion diverges. Monitor \(\min \hat{h}_\lambda\) and \(\|\nabla \hat{h}_\lambda\|\) during training.
- **Slow convergence**: If the intrinsic dimension of data is higher than assumed, sample complexity grows exponentially. Verify that the reference distribution concentrates on a low-dimensional manifold.
- **Mode collapse**: The exponential integrator combined with early stopping may bias samples toward the prior. Adjust truncation radius \(R\) and scaling factor \(\mu_{T_0}^{-1}\).

**First 3 experiments**
1. **1D Gaussian unit test**: Use \(p_0 = \mathcal N(0,1)\), \(w(x)=\exp(-x^2/2)\) so that \(q_0 = \mathcal N(0,1/2)\). Derive analytical \(h^*\), train \(\hat{h}_\lambda\) on generated samples, and compare \(\|\nabla \log \hat{h}_\lambda - \nabla \log h^*\|_{L^2}\) as a function of sample size.
2. **Gradient stability sweep**: For a fixed moderate \(n\), vary \(\lambda\) across a wide range (e.g., \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^2\)). Record the \(L^2\) errors of both \(\hat{h}_\lambda\) and its gradient. Identify the \(\lambda\) region that yields the smallest gradient error without exploding function error.
3. **Low-dimensional sanity check**: Create a 2D dataset where all points lie on the line \(x_2=0\) (so intrinsic dimension \(d^*=1\)). Train two guidance networks: one with architecture scaled to \(d=2\) (default) and one scaled to \(d^*=1\) (e.g., by projecting inputs). Compare the sample efficiency (error vs. \(n\)) and verify that the \(d^*\)-scaled network matches the \(d=2\) network’s performance with far fewer samples, consistent with the theory.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Density ratio estimation**: Can the method be extended when \(w(x) = q_0(x)/p_0(x)\) is unknown and must be estimated from limited or no target samples? The paper assumes the ratio is exactly known; follow-up work exists but the interplay of estimation error is unanalyzed.
2. **Nonlinear manifolds**: Does Doob’s matching adapt to low intrinsic dimensionality for general nonlinear \(d^*\)-dimensional manifolds in \(\mathbb R^d\)? The current analysis relies on a linear push-forward structure (assumption 4); real data often lie on curved manifolds.
3. **Unbounded distributions**: What happens if the compact support (assumption 1) and bounded weight (assumption 2) are violated, e.g., heavy-tailed data or unbounded rewards? The proofs require boundedness for uniform control; removing these assumptions would need new concentration inequalities.
4. **Regularization parameter scheduling**: The theoretical choice \(\lambda = O(n^{-2/(d+8)})\) is asymptotic and depends on unknown constants. Can \(\lambda\) be optimally scheduled (e.g., adaptive, decreasing) to improve finite-sample performance? A data-driven selection method is lacking.
5. **Discretization beyond exponential integrator**: The current analysis ties the discretization error to the exponential integrator. Could higher-order or adaptive-step solvers achieve the same Wasserstein guarantee with fewer steps? Extending the error analysis to other schemes is an open problem.

## Limitations
- **Strong distributional assumptions**: The theory requires compact support of \(p_0\), bounded weight function \(w\), and exact low-dimensional linear subspace structure. These are often violated in real-world applications (e.g., heavy tails, nonlinear manifolds).
- **Dependence on reference model quality**: The method assumes a well-trained reference diffusion with small score error. A poor reference model cannot be fully corrected by guidance.
- **Gradient estimator stability**: The plug-in guidance \(\nabla \log \hat{h}_\lambda\) can become unstable if \(\hat{h}_\lambda\) approaches zero or if the regularization \(\lambda\) is misspecified. No robustness guarantees are provided.
- **Constant factors and early-stopping bias**: Theoretical rates contain large constants and logarithmic factors; the early-stopping and truncation steps introduce additional biases not fully captured by the Wasserstein bound.

## Confidence
- Doob’s h-transform derivation: **High**
- Gradient-regularized regression consistency: **Medium**
- Non-asymptotic convergence (L2, W2): **Medium**
- Intrinsic dimension mitigation (linear subspace): **Medium–Low**
- Assumptions (compact support, bounded w): **Medium** (plausible for synthetic or constrained data but not general)
