# Reinforcing the World's Edge: A Continual Learning Problem in the Multi-Agent-World Boundary

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.06813  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06813  
- Reference count: 12  
- Primary result: Invariant cores (maximal common subsequences of successful trajectories) degrade in decentralized MARL due to endogenous world boundary drift, quantified by a variation budget linking boundary instability to invariant loss.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why reusable decision structures (invariants) degrade in multi‑agent reinforcement learning. For single‑agent stationary MDPs, the authors define an “invariant core” as the set of maximal common subsequences (optionally under abstraction) across all successful trajectories and prove its existence under mild goal‑conditioned assumptions. In decentralized MARL, when a focal agent treats a peer as part of the environment, each peer policy update changes the induced MDP, causing the invariant core to shrink or vanish entirely—even for small changes—because shared prototypes disappear. This drift is quantified by a variation budget that sums L₁,∞ and Linf norms of differences in transition and reward kernels across episodes. The work reframes decentralized MARL as a continual learning problem driven by endogenous agent‑world boundary drift rather than exogenous task switches.

## Method Summary
The paper employs theoretical analysis rather than an empirical algorithm. It first formalizes the invariant core for finite‑horizon goal‑conditioned MDPs, proving existence by representing trajectories in a trie and showing that a longest common subsequence exists under a common goal or abstract symbol. For decentralized MARL, the focal agent models the peer as part of the world, generating a sequence of effective MDPs as the peer policy evolves. A variation budget V_E is defined to accumulate normed differences between successive induced transition and reward kernels. The authors show that even minor peer updates can alter the set of successful trajectories, thereby shrinking or eliminating the invariant core. The analysis is supported by propositional arguments and illustrative examples.

## Key Results
- In stationary single‑agent MDPs, an invariant core exists and is policy‑independent, capturing reusable prototypes shared by all successes.
- In decentralized MARL, peer policy updates induce MDP drift, which can cause the invariant core to shrink or vanish entirely as shared prototypes disappear.
- The drift is quantified by a variation budget V_E; V_E = 0 iff the induced MDPs are identical, and positive values correlate with loss of core overlap.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Invariant core existence in single‑agent MDPs
- **Claim:** Under a stationary finite‑horizon MDP with a unique absorbing goal or a suitable abstraction, a non‑empty invariant core (maximal common subsequences of all successful trajectories) exists and is policy‑independent.
- **How it works:** Fixed agent–world boundary; all successful trajectories reach a common goal (or abstract symbol). Finite horizon yields finitely many subsequences; a longest common subsequence exists and is maximal.
- **Core assumptions:** (1) MDP stationary; (2) unique absorbing goal or abstraction ϕ mapping successes to a common symbol; (3) exhaustive enumeration of successful trajectories (complete trie).
- **Evidence:** Theorem 2.1 formalizes and proves the claim; the abstraction relaxes the need for exact symbol matches.
- **Break condition:** Core empty if no common symbol exists across all successes (e.g., disjoint solution paths) or if the trajectory set is incomplete.

### Mechanism 2: Peer folding causes MDP drift and core shrinkage
- **Claim:** In decentralized MARL, folding a peer agent into the world makes the effective MDP depend on the peer policy; as the peer updates between episodes, the invariant core per episode can shrink or vanish.
- **How it works:** From the focal agent’s view, the peer policy π² defines the induced transition P^e and reward R^e. Updating π² modifies the set of successful trajectories S^e. A prototype u ∈ Core(S^e) is lost if any successful trajectory in S^{e+1} does not contain u as a subsequence under ϕ. Thus Core(S^e) ∩ Core(S^{e+1}) can shrink to the trivial terminal symbol or become empty.
- **Core assumptions:** (1) Decentralized two‑agent Markov game; (2) focal treats peer as part of the world; (3) peer policy changes between episodes; (4) underlying task fixed.
- **Evidence:** Proposition 2.1 formalizes induced MDP sequence and shows vanishing prototypes; simple example: peer learns a skill that obviates a previously required prototype.
- **Break condition:** If peer policy is stationary, induced MDPs constant → cores identical. Even with drift, a policy‑independent motif (e.g., terminal goal symbol) may persist.

### Mechanism 3: Variation budget quantifies drift
- **Claim:** The drift between successive induced MDPs is quantified by a variation budget V_E = Σ_{e=2}^E (||P^e - P^{e-1}||_{1,∞} + ||R^e - R^{e-1}||_∞), linking boundary instability to loss of invariant overlap.
- **How it works:** V_E sums per‑episode changes in transition and reward kernels. If V_E = 0, the induced MDPs are identical → cores equal. Positive V_E indicates dynamics changed, potentially altering S^e and thus the core.
- **Core assumptions:** (1) Finite‑horizon MDPs; (2) norms defined over state‑action pairs; (3) induced kernels known or estimable.
- **Evidence:** Section 2.3 defines V_E and notes V_E=0 iff (P^e,R^e) stationary; positive changes can add/remove successes.
- **Break condition:** Budget assumes additive, independent changes; it may underestimate non‑additive accumulation (e.g., phase transitions) and requires oracle knowledge of kernels, making practical estimation challenging.

## Foundational Learning

- **Finite‑horizon Goal‑conditioned MDP**  
  *Why needed:* Formalizes the single‑agent stationary interaction; defines the success set S used to construct the invariant core.  
  *Quick check:* What MDP components must be fixed for the core to be policy‑independent? (Answer: P, R, G, H; the policy only affects which successes are sampled.)

- **Decision Trie (Prefix Tree over Trajectories)**  
  *Why needed:* Representational tool to enumerate all observed (or possible) trajectories and label whether a prefix leads to success; enables searching for common subsequences.  
  *Quick check:* How is a trie node’s success label y(u) determined? (Answer: y(u)=1 if some extension of prefix u reaches G within H steps; else 0.)

- **Abstraction (ϕ: S×A → Σ)**  
  *Why needed:* Reduces alphabet size by aggregating semantically equivalent actions/states, making common prototypes shorter and more meaningful; relaxes need for exact symbol matches.  
  *Quick check:* Provide an example where ϕ helps a core emerge even if raw trajectories share no exact symbols. (Answer: In a key–door task, ϕ maps “pick up key”, “hold key” to σ_carry and “open door” to σ_open; all successes contain σ_carry → σ_open.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Rollouts → Trajectory Trie Builder → Invariant Core Extractor → Core_e  
Peer policies → Induced MDP Simulator → (P^e, R^e) → Drift Quantifier → V_E  
Core_{e-1}, Core_e, V_E → Core Stability Monitor  

**Critical path:**  
1. Start with single‑agent MDP → compute Core_φ(S) once (stable).  
2. Introduce peer agent. For each episode e:  
   a. Sample interactions using peer policy π²_e.  
   b. Build trajectory trie T_e from rollouts.  
   c. Extract invariant core Core_φ(S^e) from successful leaves.  
   d. Compute induced MDP (P^e, R^e) by marginalizing over peer actions.  
   e. Compute drift V_E relative to previous episode (if e>1).  
   f. Core Stability Monitor compares Core_φ(S^e) with Core_φ(S^{e-1}) and logs overlap changes.  
   The loop reveals shrinking or vanishing prototypes.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Abstraction ϕ:* Simpler ϕ (small Σ) increases chance of common symbols and reduces computational cost but may overgeneralize and include spurious prototypes. Complex ϕ (e.g., options) preserves semantics but requires prior knowledge or learning.  
- *Drift metric:* L₁,∞ norm is sensitive to sparse large changes; Linf captures immediate reward shifts. Using only one may miss important dynamics. Exact norms require model access; in model‑free settings, estimation via visitation counts introduces error.  
- *Core computation:* Exact longest common subsequence across k sequences is NP‑hard in k; practical systems use approximate or sampling‑based methods (e.g., frequent pattern mining on trie paths), trading optimality for scalability.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Core collapse:** Core_φ(S^e) becomes empty (except possibly terminal symbol) after a peer update, indicating total loss of reusable structure.  
- **High variation budget:** V_E spikes after a peer update, predicting imminent core change or collapse.  
- **Negative transfer:** Executing a prototype from Core_φ(S^{e-1}) in episode e fails (does not reach goal), confirming invalid reuse.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Single‑agent baseline:** In a gridworld key–door task, compute Core_φ(S) with and without abstraction. Verify non‑emptiness and that core contains expected sequence (e.g., “find key → open door”).  
2. **Induced drift:** Implement a cooperative two‑agent key–door where peer can pick up the key. Define two peer policies: one that picks up the key, one that does not. For each, compute induced MDPs and extract cores. Observe that the core from the first episode (e.g., containing “drop key for peer”) disappears in the second.  
3. **Variation budget correlation:** Over many incremental peer policy updates (e.g., peer gradually learns to pick up the key), compute V_E between consecutive episodes and the intersection size of cores. Expect negative correlation: higher V_E leads to smaller core overlap; a V_E threshold may predict core vanishing.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Preserving invariants under drift:** How can invariant cores be made robust against small boundary drift (low V_E) in decentralized MARL? (Future work suggests using options or deviation mechanisms.)  
- **Predicting boundary shifts via opponent modeling:** Can opponent models forecast peer policy updates to anticipate core changes? (Authors propose predictability through modeling.)  
- **Regret/sample complexity in drifting MDPs:** What are theoretical guarantees for RL algorithms as explicit functions of V_E? (Algorithms with V_E‑dependent bounds are needed.)  
- **Online estimation of V_E:** Can the variation budget be estimated from agent interactions without knowing peer policies? (Estimating V_E from rollouts is open.)  
- **Benchmarks for controlled drift:** What environments can systematically vary V_E to study core stability? (Need benchmarks with parameterized peer updates.)

## Limitations
- **Abstraction φ selection:** Conditions guaranteeing a common abstract symbol are informal; choosing φ for arbitrary tasks may require domain insight.  
- **Proof completeness:** Proof sketches are incomplete (e.g., Proposition 2.1), making it ambiguous whether the claimed core emptiness can be rigorously instantiated.  
- **Variation budget expressiveness:** V_E’s additive norm‑based measure may underestimate non‑linear drift and requires oracle knowledge of induced kernels, hindering practical use.

## Confidence
- **Invariant core existence (single‑agent):** Medium – rigorous but untested empirically and relies on exhaustive trajectory enumeration.  
- **Core drift in MARL:** Medium – plausible argument, illustrative examples, but lacks experimental validation.  
- **Variation budget link:** Low‑Medium – conceptually appealing but may not fully capture fragile prototypes and is not empirically correlated.

## Next Checks

2. Build a cooperative two‑agent Markov game with two distinct peer policies; compute induced MDPs and cores; confirm that the core from the first episode shrinks or vanishes in the second.
