# ODySSeI: An Open-Source End-to-End Framework for Automated Detection, Segmentation, and Severity Estimation of Lesions in Invasive Coronary Angiography Images

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20021
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20021
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** A 2.5-fold increase in lesion detection mAP@0.50 over the baseline via the Pyramidal Augmentation Scheme (PAS), with severity estimation achieving ±2-3 pixel accuracy against ground truth.

## Executive Summary
ODySSeI is an open-source framework that automates the analysis of invasive coronary angiography (ICA) images to reduce subjectivity in clinical interpretation. It integrates a YOLO11-based detector and a DeepLabv3+ segmenter, both enhanced by a novel Pyramidal Augmentation Scheme (PAS) that improves robustness. A rule-based algorithm then computes quantitative coronary angiography (QCA)-free severity metrics (minimum lumen diameter, diameter stenosis) directly from the predicted lesion geometry. The system processes images in seconds on a CPU and demonstrates strong generalizability across multiple international cohorts.

## Method Summary
The framework uses a two-stage pipeline. First, a YOLO11m detector localizes lesions, trained with PAS—a combination of static (domain-specific like CLAHE, blur), dynamic (probabilistic like scaling), and composite (Mosaic) augmentations. Second, detected regions are cropped and resized for segmentation by a DeepLabv3+ model. Finally, a post-processing step skeletonizes the binary mask, computes a Euclidean distance transform to derive radius profiles, identifies healthy reference peaks, and calculates minimum lumen diameter and stenosis percentage. All models are pre-trained on large generic datasets (COCO, ImageNet) and fine-tuned on annotated ICA data.

## Key Results
- Lesion detection mAP@0.50 improved 2.5-fold with full PAS (from 0.175 to 0.433 on the lesion level).
- Severity estimation (MLD) achieves high accuracy, with mean absolute differences of ~2.4 pixels against QCA ground truth.
- The pipeline is computationally efficient, running in seconds on CPU and sub-second on GPU.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The Pyramidal Augmentation Scheme (PAS) substantially improves lesion detection performance by exposing the model to clinically plausible variations through hierarchical augmentation.
- **Mechanism:** Static augmentations (domain-specific transforms like CLAHE, blur, noise) increase dataset size and force the model to learn arterial geometry/topology rather than shortcuts. Dynamic augmentations (probabilistic transforms like scaling, flipping) improve robustness to configurational changes during training. Composite augmentation (Mosaic) promotes compositional learning and precise localization by simulating occlusions.
- **Core assumption:** The selected augmentations adequately represent the dominant sources of variability in ICA imaging (contrast, noise, focal blur, rotation, occlusion) without creating anatomically impossible samples.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Our PAS yields large performance gains in highly complex tasks as compared to relatively simpler ones, notably, a 2.5-fold increase in lesion detection performance versus a 1-3% increase in lesion segmentation performance over their respective baselines."
  - [section 2.2.1.1] Table 1 shows mAP@0.50 increases from 0.175 (no augmentation) to 0.433 (full PAS) at lesion-level total.
  - [section 2] Figure 2 illustrates that static augmentations encourage structured overfitting while dynamic augmentations stabilize performance.
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers (CathAI, DeepCoro) use simpler or no augmentation strategies, suggesting PAS addresses a gap.
- **Break condition:** If clinical variability exceeds augmentation coverage (e.g., novel imaging protocols, severe motion artifacts) or if augmentations introduce unrealistic geometries that mislead learning.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A two-stage pipeline (YOLO11m detector → DeepLabv3+ segmenter) enables accurate, real-time lesion analysis while keeping computational demands low.
- **Mechanism:** The detector produces candidate bounding boxes that crop the image to a small region containing the lesion. The segmenter then operates on this cropped, resized patch, reducing spatial complexity and allowing a high-capacity model to focus on fine details. Both models are pre-trained (COCO, ImageNet) and separately tuned with PAS, avoiding the optimization difficulties of a single end-to-end model.
- **Core assumption:** The detector’s bounding boxes reliably contain the full lesion extent so the cropped region provides sufficient context for accurate segmentation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "ODySSeI processes a raw ICA image within only a few seconds on a CPU and in a fraction of a second on a GPU."
  - [section 1] "ODySSeI adopts a substantially simpler architecture, requiring only two deep learning models instead of five [CathAI] and twelve [DeepCoro]."
  - [section 2.2] Detection mAP@0.50 reaches 0.438 on FAME2 validation with full PAS and ARCADE data, indicating sufficient localization.
  - [section 2.3] Segmentation Dice scores >0.89 on test sets show that cropped inputs yield accurate masks.
  - [corpus] Prior frameworks (CathAI, DeepCoro) use 5–12 models, implying higher latency; ODySSeI’s 2-model design directly contrasts.
- **Break condition:** If detection recall drops (<0.7), lesions are missed entirely; if detection boxes are too tight or mislocalized, segmentation receives incomplete or misleading inputs, degrading mask quality.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The QCA-free severity estimation algorithm accurately derives MLD and diameter stenosis directly from the segmented lesion geometry, avoiding training bias and achieving ±2–3 pixel agreement with ground truth.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm skeletonizes the binary segmentation mask, computes the Euclidean distance transform (EDT) from every skeleton pixel to the nearest background pixel (giving lumen radius at each point), identifies two prominent radius peaks corresponding to healthy arterial segments, and takes twice the minimum radius between them as MLD. The highest peak’s diameter is the maximal (healthy) arterial diameter (MAD); DS = (1−MLD/MAD)×100%.
- **Core assumption:** (1) The segmentation mask accurately represents the true lumen boundary; (2) the skeleton is a faithful centerline; (3) MLD occurs between two locally healthy peaks rather than at the global minimum, which may be an artifact of tapering or cropping.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Our LSE technique achieves high accuracy, with predicted MLD values differing by only ±2–3 pixels from the corresponding ground truths."
  - [section 2.4] Table 7 shows mean absolute difference (MAD) of 2.378–2.877 pixels and balanced accuracy >0.82.
  - [section 2.4] Figure 8 (Bland-Altman plots) shows tight agreement across datasets.
  - [section 4.2.3] Details the stepwise rule-based computation; no learned parameters.
  - [corpus] CathAI and DeepCoro predict severity via trained models with higher MAD; ODySSeI’s rule-based approach is distinctive.
- **Break condition:** If segmentation masks contain holes, spurious branches, or rough boundaries, skeletonization and EDT become unreliable; if the lesion is extremely long with no healthy reference peaks, peak detection fails.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Domain-specific medical image augmentation**
  - *Why needed here:* PAS distinguishes itself from generic augmentations by using transforms that mimic ICA-specific artifacts (speckle noise, defocus blur, contrast variation). Understanding the rationale for each augmentation tier is key to reproducing or extending ODySSeI.
  - *Quick check:* Why might "Local Pixel Shuffling" be more suitable than random cropping for forcing the model to learn global arterial topology?

- **Concept: Two-stage detection-then-segmentation pipeline**
  - *Why needed here:* The architecture choice (YOLO for detection, DeepLab for segmentation) is central to ODySSeI’s efficiency. One must understand the tradeoffs between single-stage instance segmentation (Mask R-CNN) vs. two-stage vs. separate models.
  - *Quick check:* What failure modes are unique to a detection→segmentation pipeline that would not occur in an end-to-end segmentation model?

- **Concept: Morphological analysis for geometric measurement**
  - *Why needed here:* The severity estimation relies on skeletonization and distance transforms, not learned regressors. This requires understanding how binary morphological operations relate to physical measurements (lumen diameter).
  - *Quick check:* How does the Euclidean distance transform on a binary lesion mask yield the arterial radius at each skeleton point, and why is the "minimum between two peaks" preferred over the global minimum?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  DICOM frame → 512×512 grayscale → YOLO11m (detector) → bounding boxes → ROI cropping/resizing (256×256) → DeepLabv3+ (segmenter) → binary mask → skeletonize + EDT + peak detection → MLD & DS → output (boxes, masks, metrics)

- **Critical path:**
  1. Prepare FAME2 dataset with bounding box annotations; split train/val/test.
  2. Implement PAS: static (Albumentations, fixed before training), dynamic (PyTorch/Kornia, per-batch), composite (Mosaic, detection only, last 10 epochs).
  3. Train YOLO11m on augmented FAME2 (+ARCADE if desired) using Ultralytics; monitor mAP@0.50 on validation.
  4. Generate cropped lesion patches from training set (using ground truth boxes or detector outputs) for segmentation training.
  5. Train DeepLabv3+ on cropped patches with PAS (static+dynamic only); monitor Dice on validation.
  6. Implement severity estimation in Python (OpenCV/scikit-image): `skeletonize`, `distance_transform_edt`, peak finding on radius profile.
  7. Assemble inference script: detector → cropper → segmenter → severity estimator; benchmark on CPU/GPU.
  8. (Optional) Deploy to web framework (Flask/FastAPI) with UI for image upload and result visualization.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Detection model size:** YOLO11m vs. larger/smaller variants; larger improves accuracy but increases latency.
  - **Augmentation scope:** More static transforms increase dataset diversity but risk unrealistic samples; Mosaic helps detection but harms segmentation if used.
  - **Severity estimation:** Rule-based avoids training data bias but depends entirely on segmentation quality; a learned regressor might be more robust to segmentation errors but would require labeled MLD data.
  - **Open-source vs. closed:** Releasing code and web interface improves reproducibility but limits control over deployment environment; CPU speed (seconds) is acceptable for non-real-time use.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Detection:** Validation mAP@0.50 < 0.4; precision-recall curve shows many FPs in anatomically implausible regions (e.g., outside coronary tree).
  - **Segmentation:** clDice < 0.9; MHD > 2 pixels; masks show fragmented or overly smooth boundaries.
  - **Severity:** MAD > 5 pixels; failure to find two clear peaks in radius profile; DS > 100% or < 0%.
  - **Pipeline:** Stage 1 recall < 0.7 → missed lesions; Stage 2 IoU between predicted box and ground truth < 0.6 → poor crop; high variance in CPU processing time (>10s/image) indicates inefficient augmentation or I/O.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline replication:** Train detector and segmenter with no augmentations on FAME2 train; evaluate on val. Expect mAP@0.50 ~0.175 and Dice ~0.883 per Tables 1 and 5. Confirms need for PAS.
  2. **PAS ablation:** Train four detector variants: (a) static only, (b) dynamic only, (c) static+dynamic, (d) full PAS. Measure mAP@0.50 on validation. Expect (d) best, (a) high precision/low recall, (b) better recall, demonstrating complementary roles.
  3. **Out-of-distribution test:** Run best full PAS detector+segmenter on FC dataset (no training). Compare detection mAP@0.50 and segmentation Dice to FAME2 test. Expect drop, especially in detection (FC: 0.132 vs FAME2: 0.406), highlighting cohort shift; then train with ARCADE added and re-evaluate to show improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Can the selection of an end-diastolic ICA frame be fully automated without cardiologist input?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state ODySSeI "still requires a cardiologist to select a high resolution, end-diastolic ICA frame" and list automating this as future work in the Discussion (Section 3).
  - **Why unresolved:** Manual frame selection introduces variability and hinders end-to-end automation, limiting real-time clinical deployment.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Integration and prospective validation of an automated frame-selection module within ODySSeI, benchmarked against expert annotations on diverse ICA video sequences.

- **Question:** How does limited training data size affect ODySSeI's generalizability to unseen patient populations and lesion types?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors acknowledge the "size of our training sets is limited" as a limitation in Section 3.
  - **Why unresolved:** Small, site-specific datasets may not capture the full spectrum of anatomical variations, imaging protocols, and rare lesion presentations.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Training and testing ODySSeI on significantly larger, multi-center datasets with comprehensive annotation of demographic and acquisition variables, followed by rigorous subgroup analysis.

- **Question:** Does ODySSeI's rule-based severity estimation accurately quantify stenosis for very wide lesions, which were occasionally missed in the OOD evaluation?
  - **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Table 6 and Figure 7 show higher segmentation performance on the OOD FC dataset, but the Discussion notes the model "occasionally fails to correctly identify wide lesions" (Section 2.2.1.2), implying potential severity estimation bias.
  - **Why unresolved:** The severity algorithm relies on clean segmentation of the lesion's minimal lumen; missed or inaccurate segmentation of wide lesions could lead to unreliable MLD/DS estimates.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Targeted evaluation of severity metrics on a dedicated dataset rich in wide lesions, comparing automated MLD against QCA and expert interpretations, including analysis of failure cases.

- **Question:** To what extent does inter-observer variability in manual ICA annotations affect the training and reported performance of ODySSeI?
  - **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors introduce MLD-based metrics precisely because "bounding box overlap-based metrics... are highly sensitive to changes in bounding box coordinates" due to "inconsistencies in lesion annotation lengths" and inherent interpretation variability (Sections 2.2.2, 3). They note MLD-Recall is low partly due to FNs from this variability.
  - **Why unresolved:** The ground truth used for training and testing likely reflects a single or small set of expert opinions, which may not represent the clinical consensus, thus questioning the model's true clinical reliability.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Collecting multi-expert annotations for a common test set to quantify annotation dispersion, then re-evaluating ODySSeI's performance against the consensus distribution and examining model sensitivity to annotation noise.

- **Question:** How transferable is the Pyramidal Augmentation Scheme (PAS) to other medical image analysis tasks beyond ICA, particularly for segmentation of fine, tubular structures?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors position PAS as a key contributor to performance, with distinct benefits for detection (2.5x gain) versus segmentation (1-3% gain). They theorize this is because detection is "more complex and ambiguous" and benefits from "denser supervision" via augmentations (Section 3).
  - **Why unresolved:** The design of PAS—especially the composite Mosaic augmentation—is tailored to object detection in 2D angiograms. Its efficacy for other tasks (e.g., 3D volumes, different anatomies) with varying degrees of label sparsity is unknown.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:**Applying the identical PAS configuration (or a systematically adapted variant) to train models for other dense-prediction tasks (e.g., vessel segmentation in OCT, polyp detection in endoscopy) and comparing performance against task-agnostic and traditional augmentation baselines.

## Limitations
- Segmentation-dependent severity estimation: The rule-based algorithm is highly sensitive to mask quality; errors propagate directly to MLD/DS calculations.
- Augmentation realism: PAS includes domain-specific transforms, but the paper does not explicitly validate that augmented samples remain anatomically plausible to clinicians.
- Generalizability ceiling: Robustness to severely degraded images (motion artifacts, low contrast, rare anatomies) remains unquantified.

## Confidence
- Lesion detection performance gains from PAS: **High** (supported by ablation tables and clear mAP improvements).
- Two-stage pipeline efficiency: **High** (runtime benchmarks and model count comparisons provided).
- Severity estimation accuracy (±2-3 pixels): **Medium** (Bland-Altman plots show tight agreement, but depends entirely on segmentation quality; no external validation beyond the reported cohorts).

## Next Checks
1. **Holdout cohort stress test:** Run the complete pipeline on a completely independent, geographically distinct dataset with no fine-tuning (e.g., images from a different continent/scanner vendor). Measure drop in detection mAP and severity MAD vs. reported values.
2. **Augmentation plausibility audit:** Have interventional cardiologists blindly rate a sample of PAS-augmented images vs. real images for anatomical realism. Quantify the rate of implausible samples that could mislead learning.
3. **Segmentation error propagation analysis:** Systematically corrupt predicted segmentation masks (introduce holes, boundary noise) and re-run severity estimation. Map how mask quality metrics (e.g., Hausdorff distance) correlate with MLD error to define the segmentation accuracy threshold required for clinically acceptable severity estimates.
