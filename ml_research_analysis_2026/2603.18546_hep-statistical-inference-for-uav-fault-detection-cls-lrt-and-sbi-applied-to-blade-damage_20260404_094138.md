# HEP Statistical Inference for UAV Fault Detection: CLs, LRT, and SBI Applied to Blade Damage

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18546  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18546  
- **Reference count:** 5  
- **Primary result:** Transferred High Energy Physics statistical methods (LRT, CLs, SNPE) for UAV propeller fault detection using spectral rotor-harmonic features, achieving AUC 0.862±0.007 on the UAV-FD hexarotor dataset and 0.986 on the PADRE quadrotor after refitting generative models, with SBI providing calibrated severity posteriors (90% CI coverage 92–100%, MAE 0.012).  

## Executive Summary
This work adapts statistical inference techniques from High Energy Physics (HEP)—specifically the likelihood ratio test (LRT), the CLs method for false alarm control, and simulation-based inference (SBI) via sequential neural posterior estimation (SNPE)—to the problem of multirotor propeller fault detection. The core innovation lies in using spectral features concentrated in the rotor harmonic band (80–150 Hz) derived from a central IMU, combined with a composite LRT that scans over per‑motor alternative hypotheses. This approach yields strong detection performance: on a hexarotor dataset with subtle blade damage (5%/10%), leave‑one‑flight‑out cross‑validation gives AUC 0.862, significantly outperforming CUSUM, autoencoder, and LSTM‑A baselines. At a controlled 5% false alarm rate, the system detects 93% of severe (10%) and 81% of mild (5%) damage. Moreover, SNPE provides calibrated posterior distributions over fault severity, enabling uncertainty‑quantified estimates rather than binary flags. The methods transfer reasonably well to a quadrotor dataset (PADRE) after refitting only the generative models, achieving AUC 0.986, demonstrating the robustness of the statistical framework.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins by sliding a 500‑sample window (stride 250) over the raw 6‑channel IMU stream (≈376–500 Hz). For each window, 90 features are extracted: 4 time‑domain statistics (mean, std, RMS, kurtosis) and 11 spectral statistics per channel, computed from the Welch PSD in predefined frequency bands (5–30, 30–80, 80–150, 150–250 Hz), including band power fractions, centroid, dominant frequency, and entropy. During training, multivariate Gaussian models are fitted with Ledoit‑Wolf shrinkage: a null model H₀ from all healthy windows, and separate alternative models H₁⁽ᵐ⁾ for each motor *m* from windows where that motor is damaged. For a test window *x*, the composite LRT score is *q(x) = maxₘ* [log *p(x|H₁⁽ᵐ⁾) – log *p(x|H₀)*]. This score is smoothed via exponential moving average (α=0.3) across windows in a flight. Detection decisions use the CLs statistic: *CLs_det = P(q≥q_obs|H₀) / P(q≥q_obs|H₁)*, where the two distributions are estimated by generating 10⁴ synthetic scores from the fitted Gaussians. A fault is declared if *CLs_det* < α (e.g., 0.05 for a 5% false alarm rate). Optionally, an SNPE‑C model (from the *sbi* library) is trained on (θ, x) pairs, with θ = (severity, motor one‑hot), to produce a posterior *p(θ|x)* that yields calibrated severity estimates and motor probabilities. Final flight‑level decisions aggregate window‑level outputs via majority vote.

## Key Results
- **UAV‑FD (hexarotor) LOFO AUC:** 0.862 ± 0.007, surpassing CUSUM (0.708), autoencoder (0.753), and LSTM autoencoder (0.551).  
- **Controlled 5% false alarm rate:** 93% detection of 10% blade damage, 81% detection of 5% damage.  
- **Per‑flight sequential detection:** 100% fault recall with 94% overall accuracy across all flights.  
- **Cross‑dataset transfer (PADRE quadrotor):** AUC 0.986 after refitting only the generative models (H₀ and H₁⁽ᵐ⁾).  
- **SBI (SNPE) calibration:** 90% credible interval coverage 92–100%, mean absolute error on severity 0.012, delivering uncertainty‑quantified severity estimates.  

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Spectral features in the rotor harmonic band (80–150 Hz) provide the primary discriminative signal for detecting subtle blade damage.  
- **Mechanism:** A blade fault introduces a rotational imbalance, generating a persistent once‑per‑revolution force asymmetry that manifests as elevated energy at the blade‑pass frequency and its harmonics. For typical multirotor motor speeds (6,000–9,000 RPM), these harmonics fall within 80–150 Hz. By extracting PSD features in this band via Welch’s method, the fault signature is isolated from broadband turbulence and other noise sources.  
- **Break condition:** If flight conditions dramatically alter vibration sources (e.g., aggressive aerobatics, extreme wind) or the airframe integrity changes (e.g., loose components), the 80–150 Hz band may contain confounding energy, breaking the assumption of a stable vibration transfer function from the faulted motor to the central IMU.  

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The composite likelihood ratio test with per‑motor alternative hypotheses improves sensitivity by avoiding dilution of motor‑specific spectral signatures.  
- **Mechanism:** Instead of fitting a single pooled faulty distribution (H₁) across all fault locations and severities, separate generative models H₁⁽ᵐ⁾ are fitted for each motor. For a given data window, the test statistic is the maximum log‑likelihood ratio across these per‑motor models. This “scan” strategy, analogous to scanning over mass hypotheses in HEP resonance searches, selects the alternative that best matches the observed spectral pattern, yielding a tighter covariance and sharper discrimination than a pooled model.  
- **Break condition:** The mechanism fails if the IMU’s vibration response is symmetric across all motors (as noted for the hexarotor geometry). In such cases, spectral signatures from different faulted motors are statistically indistinguishable, making the per‑motor maximization no better than random choice and limiting motor identification accuracy.  

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The CLs statistic (adapted for detection) controls false alarm rates in low‑sensitivity regimes (e.g., 5% damage) by suppressing detection claims when statistical power is low.  
- **Mechanism:** CLs_det = *P(q≥q_obs | H₀)* / *P(q≥q_obs | H₁)* = *p_b* / (*p_s* + *p_b*). The numerator *p_b* is the p‑value under H₀; the denominator is the test’s power. When H₀ and H₁ distributions overlap heavily (low power), *p_s* + *p_b* ≈ *p_b*, so CLs_det ≈ 1, preventing the statistic from crossing a fixed threshold α. This corrects for the fact that a raw p‑value threshold would frequently flag healthy windows that happen to fall in the overlap region.  
- **Break condition:** The power correction becomes maladaptive if the H₁ model is misspecified. If the fitted H₁ distribution does not accurately represent the true fault distribution, the estimated power (*p_s* + *p_b*) will be incorrect, leading to either over‑conservative (missed detections) or under‑controlled (excess false alarms) performance. The method also assumes a fixed, known threshold α; an inappropriate choice negates the control.  

## Foundational Learning
- **Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT) & Neyman‑Pearson Lemma**  
  *Why needed here:* The entire detection framework is built on the LRT as the optimal test for simple hypotheses. Understanding that *q(x) = log[p(x|H₁)/p(x|H₀)]* is the fundamental decision metric is crucial. The composite scan is a direct generalization.  
  *Quick check:* Given two Gaussian distributions for healthy (H₀) and faulty (H₁) features, write the expression for the LRT statistic *q(x)*. What does a larger *q* value indicate?  

- **Spectral Analysis & Rotor Harmonic Physics**  
  *Why needed here:* Feature extraction is the most critical engineering choice. One must understand why the 80–150 Hz band is chosen (blade‑pass frequency harmonics) and how Welch’s method estimates PSD from short IMU windows.  
  *Quick check:* If a hexarotor motor spins at 7,200 RPM, what is the fundamental blade‑pass frequency (Hz) for a 2‑blade propeller? Into which harmonic band (80–150 Hz) would its 2nd or 3rd harmonic fall?  

- **Simulation‑Based Inference (SBI) / SNPE**  
  *Why needed here:* SNPE replaces an intractable likelihood with a learned conditional density estimator *p(θ|x)*. This provides calibrated posteriors over continuous fault parameters (severity, motor) instead of a point classification, enabling uncertainty‑quantified severity estimates.  
  *Quick check:* What is the key difference between training a standard neural network classifier (output: discrete class) and SNPE (output: approximate posterior distribution *p(θ|x)*)? What does “amortized inference” mean in this context?  

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input Stream (raw 6‑channel IMU) → Windowing (500‑sample, stride 250) → Feature Extraction (4 time + 11 spectral stats × 6 channels = 90‑D) → Generative Model Fitting (H₀ ~ N(µ₀,Σ₀), H₁⁽ᵐ⁾ ~ N(µ₁⁽ᵐ⁾,Σ₁⁽ᵐ⁾) with Ledoit‑Wolf) → Composite LRT Engine (*q(x)=maxₘ log p(x|H₁⁽ᵐ⁾) – log p(x|H₀)*) → Temporal Smoothing (EMA, α=0.3) → CLs Calibration (toy MC → *CLs_det* threshold) → [Optional] SBI Posterior (SNPE‑C: *p(θ|x)*) → Sequential Aggregation (per‑flight majority vote).  

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Feature extraction correctness (Welch PSD bands).  
  2. Generative model stability (Ledoit‑Wolf covariance well‑conditioned; check condition numbers).  
  3. CLs threshold selection (accurate toy MC estimation of H₀/H₁ *q* distributions; verify histograms).  
  4. SNPE training convergence (loss decrease; posterior calibration on validation).  

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Parametric (Gaussian LRT) vs. Deep (SNPE):** LRT simpler, more robust on small *N* (~900/fold); SNPE flexible for severity but needs careful regularization.  
  - **Composite scan vs. pooled H₁:** Composite (per‑motor) costs *M*× likelihood evals but gains +0.046 AUC; pooled cheaper but loses sensitivity.  
  - **EMA smoothing strength (α):** High α (0.5) faster reaction, more noise; low α (0.1) smoother but higher latency; paper uses α=0.3.  

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Symptom:** AUC on LOFO folds drops <0.75 or CLs FAR much higher than nominal (e.g., 15% vs 5%).  
  - **Likely cause:**  
    1. **Feature shift:** New flight conditions (high wind, maneuvers) cause spectral drift; check feature PCA by flight.  
    2. **Model mismatch:** H₁ model not representative (e.g., train only 10% damage, test has 5%); check H₁ covariance condition numbers and KL divergence.  
    3. **CLs threshold invalid:** Toy MC sample too small (10⁴) for accurate tail estimation; increase to 10⁵ and re‑evaluate.  

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Sanity‑check feature extraction:** On a single healthy window, manually compute Welch PSD (e.g., with `scipy.signal.welch`) and verify the 80–150 Hz band power matches the extracted feature vector.  
  2. **Validate CLs thresholding:** Using fitted H₀/H₁ Gaussians, generate 10⁵ synthetic *q* values from each. Plot cumulative distributions. For target 5% FAR, find the empirical 95th percentile of *q* under H₀ as a naive threshold. Compute CLs_det for candidate thresholds and confirm the selected threshold yields CLs_det < α and that a raw p‑value threshold would give much higher FAR on the H₀ synthetic set. Re‑evaluate with 10⁵ toy MC samples to ensure stability.  
  3. **Test cross‑platform transfer (minimal):** Apply the trained H₀ and H₁⁽ᵐ⁾ models from UAV‑FD to PADRE feature vectors (average the 4 arm IMUs). Compute AUC; expect ~0.98. Failure indicates feature scaling mismatch or fundamental physics difference.  

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can a physics‑informed neural network (PINN) encoding rotor aerodynamics replace the empirical healthy model (H₀) to improve cross‑condition generalization without refitting?  
- **Basis in paper:** Section 6.1 lists as a direct extension: “a physics‑informed neural network (PINN) encoding rotor aerodynamics could replace the empirical H₀ model with a physics‑grounded nominal model, improving generalization across flight conditions.”  
- **Why unresolved:** The current H₀ is a data‑driven Gaussian fitted per dataset; a PINN incorporating fluid‑structure interaction could maintain accuracy across diverse maneuvers or wind conditions without retraining, but this has not been implemented or evaluated.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train a PINN‑based H₀ on a subset of UAV‑FD flights including varied maneuvers; test on held‑out flights with different dynamics and compare AUC and false alarm stability against the empirical H₀ approach.  

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does integrating the fault detector’s quantitative output (severity posterior, confidence) into an adaptive flight controller measurably improve fault tolerance or safety margins compared to passive monitoring?  
- **Basis in paper:** Section 6.1 proposes: “the fault detector’s output... can serve as an explicit input to an adaptive flight controller, enabling fault‑aware trajectory planning rather than passive monitoring.”  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper stops at detection and characterization; the impact on closed‑loop control performance, stability under damage, and safety‑critical decision‑making (e.g., landing vs. continue) is unexplored.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Hardware‑in‑the‑loop or simulation experiments where the severity posterior modulates controller gains or triggers contingency maneuvers; compare metrics like trajectory deviation, control effort, and safe landing success against a nominal controller.  

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the discrete per‑motor composite scan be extended to a continuous severity parameter via a parameterized likelihood family, unifying the LRT and SBI into a single profiled likelihood framework with improved statistical efficiency?  
- **Basis in paper:** Section 6.1 suggests: “extending the composite hypothesis scan to a continuous severity parameter (replacing the discrete per‑motor models with a parameterized family) would unify the LRT and SBI into a single profiled likelihood framework, directly analogous to the profile likelihood ratio used for Higgs boson property measurements.”  
- **Why unresolved:** Currently, LRT uses discrete motor‑specific H₁ models while SBI separately infers continuous severity; a unified profiled likelihood could jointly optimize detection and estimation but has not been derived or tested.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive a profiled likelihood for severity as a nuisance parameter; evaluate on UAV‑FD whether detection AUC or posterior calibration improves compared to the two‑stage LRT + SNPE pipeline.  

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the HEP statistical transfer (LRT, CLs, SBI) generalize to other vibrational fault detection domains (e.g., structural health monitoring, industrial rotating machinery) where weak signatures in spectral features must be detected with controlled false alarms?  
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states: “Whether the same transfer works in other domains (structural health monitoring, industrial process control) remains to be tested, but the inferential structure is shared wherever small signals must be detected in noisy data with controlled error rates.”  
- **Why unresolved:** Evaluation is limited to UAV propellers; other domains have different signal‑to‑noise ratios, feature spaces, and operational constraints, so the method’s robustness and need for adaptation are unknown.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the same pipeline (spectral features + composite LRT + CLs + SNPE) to benchmark datasets from bridge monitoring or bearing fault detection; compare detection performance and uncertainty calibration against domain‑specific methods.  

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Would per‑arm IMU placement (as in PADRE) combined with the current method significantly improve motor localization accuracy beyond the ~70% limit observed with a single central IMU?  
- **Basis in paper:** Section 5.2 identifies the single central IMU’s mechanical symmetry as a fundamental limit: “Localization accuracy... is limited by the single centrally‑mounted IMU... per‑arm IMU placement, as in PADRE, would enable significantly better localization, but PADRE lacks severity labels.” The authors imply but do not test this hypothesis.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper speculates that per‑arm sensors would help, but PADRE’s lack of severity labels prevents a direct quantitative comparison using the full pipeline; the trade‑off between sensor cost and localization gain is unknown.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Collect a dataset with both central and per‑arm IMUs on the same platform with labeled faults; measure motor identification accuracy for the composite LRT’s argmax under both sensor configurations, controlling for flight conditions.  

## Limitations
- **LOFO fold assignments unspecified:** The exact leave‑one‑flight‑out splits are not provided; result variability may be affected.  
- **SNPE hyperparameters omitted:** Architecture details (layers, hidden units) and training settings (learning rate, epochs) are missing; posterior quality may be sensitive.  
- **CLs toy Monte Carlo sample size:** Using only 10⁴ pseudo‑experiments may yield unstable tail estimates, especially in low‑power regimes.  
- **PADRE preprocessing ambiguities:** The specific healthy configuration used to fit H₀ and the exact IMU averaging procedure are unclear.  
- **Assumption of stable vibration transfer function:** This may break under aggressive maneuvers or wind, degrading performance on out‑of‑distribution flights.
