# ClinAlign: Scaling Healthcare Alignment from Clinician Preference
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09653
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09653
- **Reference count:** 18
- **Primary result:** A 30B-A3B model achieves 33.4% on HealthBench-Hard, outperforming larger models like Deepseek-R1 (15.1%) and o3 (31.6%).

## Executive Summary
Aligning large language models (LLMs) with nuanced clinician preferences in healthcare is challenging due to the limitations of coarse reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and unreliable automated judges. This paper introduces ClinAlign, a two-stage framework that first builds a dataset of physician-verified rubrics (HealthRubrics) and then distills them into reusable clinical principles (HealthPrinciples). These principles enable scalable rubric generation for unlabeled queries and power an inference-time tool for guided self-revision. A 30B-parameter model trained with this approach significantly surpasses larger baselines on a hard medical benchmark and shows strong general evaluation transfer.

## Method Summary
The framework operates in two stages. Stage one constructs **HealthRubrics**: clinicians iteratively revise LLM-drafted, per-instance evaluation rubrics for 7,034 medical queries to ensure clinical validity and fine-grained alignment. Stage two distills these rubrics into **119 reusable HealthPrinciples** via a four-axis taxonomy (urgency, uncertainty, user expertise, 21 task types). These principles allow automatic synthesis of context-specific rubrics for 16,872 additional queries, massively expanding training coverage. The model is trained with GRPO reinforcement learning using rubrics as dense rewards. An inference-time tool uses the same principles to generate temporary rubrics, enabling the frozen model to self-revise its responses.

## Key Results
- The 30B-A3B model attains **33.4%** on HealthBench-Hard, exceeding Deepseek-R1 (15.1%), o3 (31.6%), and other larger models.
- Gains transfer to general evaluation, with strong performance on Arena-Hard-v2.
- Inference-time self-revision guided by principles yields consistent improvements, though with diminishing returns after several calls.
- Principle-scaled training (using 23,906 total rubrics) outperforms training on physician-only rubrics, demonstrating the value of scalable supervision.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: High-Fidelity Physician Rubrics
- **Claim:** Physician-refined rubrics provide precise, fine-grained supervision that generalizes better than auto-drafted rubrics or naive SFT.
- **Mechanism:** Clinicians edit LLM-drafted rubrics to resolve ambiguity, add scenario-specific safety checks, and ensure clinical validity. These validated rubrics serve as dense, multi-criteria reward signals during GRPO RL, teaching the model explicit clinician priorities.
- **Core assumption:** Physician consensus edits represent a stable, reproducible gold standard for clinical response quality.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "clinicians refine LLM-drafted rubrics"; [section 4.1] physician revision loop; [section 5.2] improved safety-critical behaviors.
- **Break condition:** Low inter-rater reliability or failure to cover long-tail scenarios could cause overfitting to the annotated subset.

### Mechanism 2: Scalable Principle Distillation
- **Claim:** Distilling recurring rubric patterns into reusable HealthPrinciples enables supervision beyond manually verified examples.
- **Mechanism:** A four-dimensional taxonomy routes rubrics to subcategories; clustered rubric items are compressed into principles. At scale, principles are retrieved and converted into instance-specific rubrics for unlabeled queries, expanding training diversity.
- **Core assumption:** Clinically meaningful evaluation patterns are reusable across similar scenarios without losing safety-critical nuance.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "119 broadly reusable, clinically grounded principles"; [section 4.2] taxonomy and extraction; [section 5.2] Principle Rubrics match Doctor Rubrics; more Query Rubrics yield gains.
- **Break condition:** Overly generic principles (e.g., "be safe") produce low-signal rubrics that may not improve policy learning.

### Mechanism 3: Inference-Time Guided Self-Revision
- **Claim:** A tool that classifies scenarios, retrieves principles, and generates context-specific rubrics improves response quality at test time.
- **Mechanism:** For a new query, the tool identifies relevant taxonomy axes, retrieves matched principles, synthesizes temporary rubric items, and feeds them back to the model for revision. This provides dynamic, post-hoc feedback.
- **Core assumption:** The same principles that generate training rubrics provide actionable revision signals for a frozen model.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "inference-time tool for guided self-revision"; [section 5.1] tool integration; [section 5.2] Figure 8 shows monotonic improvement with multiple calls.
- **Break condition:** Misclassification or irrelevant principle retrieval leads to misleading guidance; performance saturation suggests limited fixable errors remain.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Rubric-based Reinforcement Learning (RL)**  
  *Why needed:* Replaces scalar preferences with dense, multi-criteria rewards, essential for fine-grained clinical alignment where "helpful" is context-dependent (e.g., urgency).  
  *Quick check:* How does a rubric-derived reward signal differ from a pairwise preference label in guiding model behavior during RL?

- **Concept: Taxonomy-driven Principle Generalization**  
  *Why needed:* Scaling requires abstracting from instance-specific rubrics to reusable knowledge; the four-axis taxonomy is the schema that makes principles portable.  
  *Quick check:* Why might a principle like "communicate potential seriousness using calm, non-alarmist language" be assigned to the "Conditionally emergent" subcategory?

- **Concept: Inference-time Tooling for Self-Revision**  
  *Why needed:* Packages clinician knowledge as a test-time tool, separating knowledge representation (principles) from enforcement (rubric generation).  
  *Quick check:* What are the two-step actions the tool performs for a given query, and how do they differ from the offline training pipeline?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Data Curation Pipeline (medical query filtering, physician rubric revision) → Principle Distillation Engine (taxonomy mapping, clustering, physician consolidation) → Training Module (principle-based rubric synthesis, GRPO RL with Qwen3-32B scorer) → Inference-Time Tool (scenario classification, principle retrieval, rubric generation, self-revision).

- **Critical path:** Physician rubric creation (7,034 examples) → Taxonomy definition → Principle extraction → Validate principle utility → Scale to 16,872 new queries → Train final model → Package inference tool.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Physician cost vs. coverage:* HealthRubrics are high-fidelity but expensive; HealthPrinciples trade specificity for massive scale.  
  - *Automation fidelity:* GPT-5.1 used for classification/rubric synthesis; physician validation only in distillation phase, relying on GPT-5.1 at inference.  
  - *Rubric scorer choice:* Qwen3-32B improves accuracy but adds compute cost during RL.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Low physician agreement (kappa < 0.4) invalidates HealthRubrics as a gold standard.  
  - Principle retrieval returns irrelevant items (e.g., "emergency" principles for routine medication questions).  
  - Rubric scorer (Qwen3-32B) systematically disagrees with GPT-4.1 reference, causing reward mis-specification.  
  - Inference tool misclassifies task type (e.g., "insurance query" as "symptom check").

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Rubric validation:** Measure inter-physician agreement on held-out rubric edits; require κ≥0.4.  
  2. **Principle coverage test:** For 500 new medical queries, verify retrieved principles span all four taxonomy axes and generated rubrics are clinically plausible (physician audit).  
  3. **Inference tool ablation:** On 100 HealthBench-Hard questions, run self-revision with 0, 1, 3, 5 tool calls; confirm monotonic improvement and identify saturation point.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can rubric-based RL be effectively combined with objectives that directly train multi-step reasoning to improve intrinsic reasoning capabilities in healthcare LLMs? The current framework improves alignment but not underlying reasoning processes.
- **Open Question 2:** How can inference-time scaling with rubric guidance avoid diminishing returns, and what architectural modifications are needed for more efficient tool coordination and planning? Current tool invocation is passive and brute-force.
- **Open Question 3:** To what extent do HealthPrinciples generalize across rare diseases, complex comorbidities, and culturally diverse contexts not represented in the current taxonomy? Principles are distilled from a finite dataset; gaps could cause misalignment in edge cases.

## Limitations
- **Physician annotation details incomplete:** Inter-rater reliability (kappa) not quantified; cohort specialties and qualification criteria summarized but not fully specified.
- **Dependence on proprietary models:** Use of GPT-5.1 and Qwen3 models limits reproducibility and may embed hidden biases.
- **Benchmark validity uncertain:** Correlation between HealthBench scores and real-world clinical safety is unverified; out-of-distribution robustness of principles and tool is unknown.

## Confidence
- **A (Framework):** High (consistent gains on HealthBench-Hard and transfer).
- **B (Rubrics):** Medium (physician edits plausible but κ not quantified).
- **C (Principles):** Medium (scaling benefits shown, but risk of over-genericization).
- **D (Inference):** Medium (improvement monotonic, but tool reliability uncertain).

## Next Checks
1. Measure inter-physician agreement (Cohen’s κ) on held-out rubric edits; require κ≥0.4.
2. Clinician audit of principle-generated rubrics on out-of-distribution queries (e.g., rare conditions) to assess clinical validity.
3. Evaluate reward judge bias: compare Qwen3-32B scores to physician consensus on 500 stratified samples; analyze per-taxaxis disagreement.
