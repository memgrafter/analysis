# Human or Machine? A Preliminary Turing Test for Speech-to-Speech Interaction
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.24080
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24080
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: No S2S system passed the Turing test; success rates 7–31% vs. 50% threshold; AI judge achieved 96.05% accuracy.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether modern speech-to-speech (S2S) systems can pass a Turing test by conversing indistinguishably from humans. The authors construct a large-scale dataset of 1,486 dialogues (human-human, human-machine from nine S2S systems, and pseudo-human from TTS) covering ten topics in Chinese and English, and conduct a gamified Turing test with 2,968 human judgments. They also develop an 18-dimensional taxonomy for fine-grained human-likeness annotation and train an interpretable AI judge using an Ordinal Discretization Layer and a regularized linear classifier. Results show that no S2S system passes the Turing test, with success rates at most 31%, far below the 50% threshold. The proposed AI judge attains 96.05% accuracy, far surpassing off-the-shelf models. Analysis reveals that the primary bottleneck lies in paralinguistic features, emotional expressivity, and conversational persona rather than semantic understanding.

## Method Summary
The study comprises three main phases: dataset creation, human Turing test, and AI judge development. The dataset includes human–human dialogues (673) and human–machine dialogues from nine S2S systems (669) across ten daily topics in both Chinese and English, plus 144 pseudo‑human dialogues generated by TTS. Dialogues were collected via controlled role‑play and human‑guided initiation to ensure balanced interaction. A gamified web platform gathered 2,968 binary judgments (human vs. machine) from participants, alongside demographic information. For fine‑grained analysis, 36 bilingual annotators rated dialogues on 18 dimensions (e.g., prosody, disfluency, emotional tone) using a 1–5 scale; expert review refined 29.44% of labels. The AI judge leverages Qwen2.5‑Omni to extract audio embeddings; a fused pooling operation combines the first‑token mean and last‑token representations. These embeddings are fed into an Ordinal Discretization Layer that predicts ordered scores for each of the 18 dimensions, followed by a regularized linear classifier for binary discrimination. The model is trained end‑to‑end with a two‑stage procedure and validated on both in‑distribution and out‑of‑distribution test sets.

## Key Results
- No S2S system achieved a success rate above 31% in the Turing test; the 50% threshold for indistinguishability was not met.
- Human participants correctly identified machine vs. human with 72.84% accuracy, while off‑the‑shelf AI judges averaged only 45.27%.
- The proposed interpretable AI judge reached 96.05% in‑distribution accuracy and 97.40% out‑of‑distribution accuracy, demonstrating strong generalization.
- Detailed analysis pinpoints deficits in paralinguistic features (e.g., prosody, fillers), emotional expressivity (both textual and acoustic), and conversational persona (e.g., sycophancy, formality) as the primary bottlenecks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Paralinguistic Deficit as Primary Bottleneck**  
Current S2S systems fail the Turing test primarily due to rigid prosody, absence of disfluencies, and unnatural rhythm rather than semantic flaws. Human listeners detect machine-like voices through subtle acoustic cues (e.g., monotonous intonation, lack of fillers like “um”, overly perfect pronunciation) that convey spontaneity and cognitive effort. The paper shows that S2S outputs score significantly lower than humans in dimensions like Rhythm, Intonation, Stress, Use of Fillers, and Auxiliary Vocalizations, indicating a deficit in paralinguistic expressiveness.

**Mechanism 2: Emotional Expressivity Mismatch**  
S2S systems often generate neutral or misaligned emotional tones. While the textual sentiment may be appropriate, the acoustic emotion (prosodic modulation) fails to reinforce it. This multimodal mismatch—vocal affect not cohering with content—triggers a “robotic” perception. The study finds both textual sentiment and acoustic emotion scores are well below human baselines for S2S systems.

**Mechanism 3: Mechanical Persona via Sycophancy and Formality**  
S2S systems exhibit overly agreeable, apologetic, and formal language due to safety‑driven optimization (RLHF). Humans modulate agreement and formality based on relational context; this predictability signals artificiality. The taxonomy reveals large gaps in “Sycophant Behavior” and “Written‑style Expression,” showing that systems default to excessive affirmation and written‑style syntax.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Dataset module (human‑machine, human‑human, pseudo‑human) → Turing test platform (gamified web interface collecting binary judgments) → Annotation framework (18‑dim taxonomy, crowdsourcing + expert review) → AI judge backbone (Qwen2.5‑Omni → fused pooling → Ordinal Discretization Layer → regularized linear classifier).

**Critical path:**  
1. Build balanced, bias‑corrected dialogue dataset (adjust timing, volume).  
2. Deploy gamified platform and collect large‑scale human Turing test judgments.  
3. Conduct fine‑grained annotation with expert refinement.  
4. Train interpretable judge: extract audio embeddings, train Ordinal Discretization Layer on ordinal scores, freeze it, then train linear classifier.  
5. Evaluate on in‑distribution and out‑of‑distribution test sets (CosyVoice, Fisher, MultiDialog).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Human‑machine dialogue collection used controlled prompts (role‑play, human‑guided initiation) to improve realism but may restrict natural topic emergence compared to free conversation.  
- The interpretable linear head provides transparency but might underfit non‑linear patterns; a deeper head could capture more complexity.  
- Expert review improves label quality but increases time and cost; crowdsourcing alone risks noise.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low human accuracy (<60%) in Turing test suggests dataset too easy or participants inattentive.  
- Ordinal Discretization Layer loss not decreasing indicates label inconsistency or hyperparameter issues.  
- Large drop in OOD accuracy suggests the fused pooling embedding does not generalize across acoustic conditions.  

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce the main Turing test result by re‑evaluating the dataset; expect success rates 7–31% for S2S vs. ~70% for humans.  
2. Validate the 18‑dimension bottleneck by training an ablation model without the Ordinal Discretization Layer and measure performance drop.  
3. Test judge generalization on the three OOD datasets; expect >95% accuracy if the architecture is robust.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. To what extent can AI models generalize as reliable judges for Turing tests across diverse S2S systems, languages, and interaction contexts? The paper shows off‑the‑shelf models perform poorly and the proposed judge, while accurate, may not adapt without retraining.
2. How can S2S systems be enhanced to produce natural paralinguistic features—such as context‑sensitive prosody, disfluencies, and vocal dynamics—that are critical for human‑likeness? The paper identifies these as primary bottlenecks but offers no mitigation strategies.
3. What architectural and training modifications are needed to align emotional expressivity (both textual and acoustic) with conversational context in S2S systems? Current systems show limited emotional expressivity and misalignment.
4. Is the proposed 18‑dimension human‑likeness taxonomy exhaustive and culturally universal, or are there missing dimensions—particularly in non‑Western or multilingual contexts—that affect perceived human‑likeness? The taxonomy was evaluated only in Chinese and English.
5. How do human detection thresholds in Turing tests shift as S2S systems improve and as users gain more experience with AI, and can evaluation platforms adapt to these changes? The study is a single snapshot; longitudinal dynamics remain unknown.

## Limitations
- Dataset scope limited to 9 S2S systems, 10 topics, and 2 languages; findings may not generalize to newer models or other languages.
- Inter‑annotator agreement for the 18‑dimension scores is not reported; the 29.44% expert revision rate suggests potential label noise.
- Prompt control (role‑play, human‑guided initiation) may artificially constrain spontaneity and influence Turing‑test outcomes.
- Causal attribution of the paralinguistic bottleneck relies on correlations; unmeasured confounders (e.g., acoustic quality) could also play a role.
- The AI judge’s linear head and fused pooling may not fully capture non‑linear patterns or generalize to all acoustic conditions.

## Confidence
- Claim: No S2S system passes the Turing test – Confidence: **High** (large effect, 2,968 judgments).
- Claim: Bottleneck lies in paralinguistics, emotional expressivity, and persona – Confidence: **Medium** (corroborated by taxonomy scores but lacking causal experiments).
- Claim: Proposed AI judge achieves >96% accuracy – Confidence: **High** (validated on multiple test sets).
- Claim: Off‑the‑shelf AI judges perform poorly – Confidence: **Medium** (only three baselines tested).
