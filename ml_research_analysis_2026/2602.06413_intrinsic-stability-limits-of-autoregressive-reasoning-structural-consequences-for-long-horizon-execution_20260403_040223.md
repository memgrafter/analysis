# Intrinsic Stability Limits of Autoregressive Reasoning: Structural Consequences for Long-Horizon Execution

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06413
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06413
- **Reference count:** 27
- **Primary result:** Decision advantage in autoregressive reasoning decays exponentially with execution length, defining a critical stability horizon L*; segmentation mitigates decay by enforcing bounded edges, naturally inducing DAG-like topologies.

## Executive Summary
This paper reveals an intrinsic instability in autoregressive reasoning for long-horizon tasks. Using an information-theoretic framework, the authors prove that the decision advantage—the model’s ability to distinguish correct from incorrect reasoning states—decays exponentially with the number of reasoning steps. This decay defines a finite stability horizon L*, beyond which reasoning becomes unreliable. They further show that introducing discrete segmentation, where each autoregressive segment is kept shorter than L* and includes stabilization nodes, can suppress the exponential decay. Such segmented executions naturally form directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). The theory is validated across synthetic experiments, controlled chain analyses, and real-world TextWorld tasks, confirming both the predicted decay and the effectiveness of segmentation.

## Method Summary
The authors develop an information-theoretic framework to analyze autoregressive reasoning stability. They define decision advantage ρ_t and assume the transition kernel satisfies a contraction property (Assumption 1) with coefficient η < 1, meaning each step reduces the distinguishability between correct and incorrect reasoning state distributions by a fixed factor. Under this assumption, Theorem A establishes an exponential bound: ρ(L) ≤ ρ₀ e^{-γL}, where γ depends on η. The stability horizon L* is derived as the length at which ρ falls below a threshold τ. Experimental validation comprises three tracks: Track B (synthetic TW-HSF) demonstrates that segmentation shifts the bottleneck from global task length to the longest segment; Track B also includes a branch‑free chain‑sensitivity analysis isolating process instability; Track A evaluates a Landmarks agent on TextWorld, showing reduced oscillatory backtracking and increased coverage compared to monolithic baselines, thereby supporting the segmentation hypothesis.

## Key Results
- Decision advantage decays exponentially with reasoning length: ρ(L) ≤ ρ₀ e^{-γL}.
- The stability horizon L* = (1/γ) ln(ρ₀/τ) marks the length beyond which reasoning becomes unreliable.
- Structural segmentation with bounded segments (ℓ_i < L*) and stabilization nodes suppresses decay, yielding DAG‑like topologies and empirically improving performance (e.g., increased room coverage, reduced backtracking in TextWorld; shifted sample complexity in synthetic tasks).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Decision advantage (ρ_t) decays exponentially with reasoning length L, imposing a fundamental stability horizon L*.
- **Mechanism:** Persistent stochastic perturbations during autoregressive updates cause a contraction in the distinguishability between correct/incorrect reasoning state distributions (measured via total variation distance). This per‑step information loss accumulates geometrically.
- **Core assumption:** The induced transition kernel K exhibits a contraction coefficient η < 1 (Assumption 1 in Appendix B), meaning each step reduces goal‑relevant distinguishability by a fixed fraction.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Explicitly states "decision advantage in single‑path autoregressive reasoning decays exponentially with execution length" and defines L*.
  - [section 3.3] Theorem A formalizes ρ(L) ≤ ρ₀ e^{-γL} and L* = (1/γ) ln(ρ₀/τ).
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers like "TRUE" and "HiMAC" investigate reasoning stability and long‑horizon challenges, providing contextual relevance, but do not directly anchor this specific contraction mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If η ≥ 1 (no contraction), the exponential decay guarantee fails; ρ_t may not converge to zero, but stability is not ensured and could even amplify noise under different dynamics.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Stable long‑horizon reasoning requires discrete segmentation, where each continuous autoregressive segment (edge) must satisfy ℓ_i < L*.
- **Mechanism:** Segmentation introduces interruptions (stabilization nodes) that reset or consolidate state, preventing continuous noise accumulation beyond the critical length. This bounds the effective edge length, keeping each segment within the stable dynamical regime.
- **Core assumption:** L* is finite and can be estimated (via γ and ρ₀); segmentation nodes can effectively compress/perturb state to approximate a reset.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Notes "structural segmentation (Landmarks agent) significantly improves exploration coverage" and that DAG‑like topologies emerge.
  - [section 3.5] States "stable long‑horizon reasoning requires discrete segmentation" and links it to the constraint ℓ_i < L*.
  - [section 5.1] Track B shows sample complexity scaling shifts from global L to longest segment h_max under segmentation.
- **Break condition:** If segmentation is absent or segments exceed L* (ℓ_i ≥ L*), the exponential decay regime reasserts, and performance cliffs appear as predicted.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** DAG‑like reasoning topologies are a canonical structural consequence of stability constraints, not merely a heuristic choice.
- **Mechanism:** Bounded edges (ℓ_i < L*) and stabilization nodes naturally form a directed acyclic graph. Nodes act as informational anchors (bottlenecks) that compress history and filter noise, while acyclicity prevents recursive amplification of accumulated uncertainty through closed loops.
- **Core assumption:** Nodes can perform effective state consolidation (compression + task‑relevant information preservation) sufficient to reset the effective decision advantage to near ρ₀ for the next segment.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Mentions "naturally inducing graph‑like execution structures such as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)."
  - [section 4.2] Explicitly derives DAGs as canonical realizations under edge‑length constraints, defining edges as finite segments and vertices as stabilization points.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "SAGE" and "HiMAC" use hierarchical/graph structures for long‑horizon tasks, aligning with the DAG motivation, though they do not anchor the specific stability‑driven derivation.
- **Break condition:** If nodes fail to reset effectively (compression loses too much goal‑relevant information), the initial advantage ρ₀ for subsequent segments degrades, reducing overall horizon. Cyclic topologies reintroduce unbounded historical dependence, violating the single‑path decay assumption and potentially causing divergent instability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Information‑theoretic decision advantage (ρ_t)**
  - *Why needed:* It is the core measurable quantity predicted to decay; defines the stability threshold.
  - *Quick check:* If ρ_t = 0.2 at step t, what does that imply about the model's directional certainty toward the correct conclusion?
- **Contraction coefficient (η) and strong data processing inequality (SDPI)**
  - *Why needed:* They formalize the per‑step information loss assumption that leads to exponential decay. Understanding this bridges abstract dynamics to testable predictions.
  - *Quick check:* If a transition kernel has η = 0.95, what is the expected multiplicative change in distinguishability (Δ_t) after 10 steps?
- **Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) topology in reasoning**
  - *Why needed:* It is the prescribed structural output of the stability constraint; understanding edges (bounded segments) vs. nodes (stabilization) is key to architectural design.
  - *Quick check:* In a reasoning DAG, what is the functional role of a vertex node compared to an edge?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Segmentation Module → Node Stabilizer → Bounded Executor
- **Critical path:** Input → [Segment 1: Executor → Stabilizer] → [Segment 2: Executor → Stabilizer] → ... → Output. Each segment must complete within ℓ_max steps.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Segment length vs. overhead: Shorter segments (ℓ << L*) are safer but increase node operation frequency and potential context fragmentation.
  - Reset aggressiveness: Aggressive compression at nodes reduces state size but risks losing subtle, long‑term relevant information.
  - Boundary detection: Predefined fixed intervals are simple but suboptimal; learned detection (e.g., based on entropy spikes ΔH_t) adapts but adds complexity.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Oscillatory backtracking: Baseline agent repeatedly revisits states (Track A), indicating failure to suppress historical inertia—symptom of missing/ineffective resets.
  - Coherence decay within segment: Logical drift or contradiction within a single long segment (ℓ ≥ L*), even with semantic guidance.
  - Node overload: Stabilizer outputs become noisy or irrelevant, causing subsequent segments to start with ρ₀ ≈ 0.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Synthetic TW-HSF (Track B): Validate that segmentation shifts the exponential bottleneck from global L to longest segment h_max. Metric: episodes‑to‑success vs. L.
  2. Chain Sensitivity (Branch‑Free): Isolate process instability. Compare decay of decision advantage (ρ_t) in long unbranched chains with and without periodic resets.
  3. TextWorld with Paired Agents (Track A): Test structural governance in a realistic environment. Metric: distinct rooms visited, backtrack count. Compare baseline (monolithic) vs. Landmarks agent (segmented) under identical model outputs.

## Limitations
- The contraction coefficient η is assumed constant across steps; in practice, LLM transition kernels may exhibit step‑dependent η, potentially breaking the exponential guarantee.
- The mitigation mechanism presumes node stabilizers can reset decision advantage effectively, but minimal information‑preservation requirements for a "sufficient" reset are not quantified.
- Real segmentation policies may not align segment boundaries with the critical horizon L*, leading to residual instability even with segmentation.

## Confidence
- Exponential decay claim (Theorem A): **High**
- Segmentation mitigation (structural governance): **Medium**
- DAG topology as canonical outcome: **Medium‑Low**
