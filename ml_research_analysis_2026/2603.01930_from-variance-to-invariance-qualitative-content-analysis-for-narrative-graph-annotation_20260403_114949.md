# From Variance to Invariance: Qualitative Content Analysis for Narrative Graph Annotation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.01930
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01930
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Adjacent Story yields the most stable agreement across lenient, moderate, and strict metrics, making it a reliable narrative graph representation.

## Executive Summary
Annotating narrative graphs as directed acyclic graphs is challenged by human label variation, where annotators produce plausible but non-identical structures. This paper introduces a framework integrating Qualitative Content Analysis (QCA) principles to improve annotation quality through iterative refinement of guidelines and category systems. A 6×3 factorial experiment evaluates six narrative representations (e.g., All Events, Full Story, Adjacent Story) under three graph distance metrics (lenient, moderate, strict) using Krippendorff’s α. Results reveal that overlap-based lenient metrics substantially inflate reliability estimates, while locally-constrained representations—especially Adjacent Story—exhibit stable agreement across all metric strictness levels. The study concludes that Adjacent Story effectively balances contextual completeness with annotation consistency, offering a robust foundation for narrative graph annotation.

## Method Summary
No reproduction notes or artifact URLs were provided by the authors. The reported methodology is derived solely from the paper's description of its 6×3 factorial design, QCA-based iterative annotation process, and the computation of Krippendorff’s α using custom graph distance metrics for six narrative representations.

## Key Results
- Lenient overlap-based metrics overestimate reliability; for All Events, α drops from 0.868 (lenient) to 0.244 (strict).
- Locally-constrained representations maintain stable agreement: Adjacent Story (α=0.702/0.441/0.202) and Relations (α=0.642/0.513/0.483).
- Adjacent Story is identified as the optimal representation, balancing contextual completeness and annotation consistency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Iterative QCA-based refinement of annotation guidelines and category system reduces errors and builds shared understanding, thereby improving inter-annotator agreement.
- **Mechanism**: Begin with a deductive category system; conduct pilot annotations; hold group discussions to resolve ambiguities; refine guidelines and categories; repeat. This directly addresses under-specification, misunderstandings, and ambiguous definitions.
- **Core assumption**: Annotators can engage in reflective negotiation and the category system is adaptable based on empirical feedback.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - (Abstract): "we introduce a narrative graph annotation framework that integrates principles from qualitative content analysis (QCA) to prioritize annotation quality by reducing annotation errors."
  - (Section 3.4): Details the pilot study where QCA-based group discussions identified three error sources and led to iterative improvements in sampling, workflow, categories, and span annotation.
  - (Corpus context): The corpus indicates limited prior work integrating QCA into NLP annotation, so this mechanism is novel but not widely substantiated externally.
- **Break condition**: If iterative discussions are infeasible (e.g., time constraints, remote annotators) or annotators resist negotiation, the process fails to align interpretations.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Locally-constrained narrative representations (e.g., one-hop subgraphs) yield more stable agreement across distance metrics than multi-hop representations.
- **Mechanism**: By restricting annotation to events directly causing the target (Inflation), the scope is limited, reducing cognitive load and boundary ambiguity. Annotators converge on direct causal links, which are more objectively identifiable.
- **Core assumption**: Core narrative elements are captured by immediate causal relationships; multi-hop connections introduce subjectivity and variability.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - (Abstract): "locally-constrained representations, particularly Adjacent Story (α=0.702/0.441/0.202) and Relations (α=0.642/0.513/0.483), achieve the most stable agreement across metric strictness."
  - (Section 4.2): Shows Adjacent Story has the highest α across all metrics and the smallest gaps (Δ) between lenient and strict metrics. It also exhibits lower standard deviation in annotation cardinality compared to Full Story and Extended Story.
  - (Corpus context): The corpus contains papers on narrative extraction but not direct comparisons of local vs. multi-hop constraints; evidence is primarily from this study.
- **Break condition**: If narratives require multi-hop reasoning to capture essential plot elements (e.g., indirect but critical causes), local constraints may omit valid information, reducing downstream utility.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Employing a suite of distance metrics (lenient, moderate, strict) prevents overestimation of reliability and reveals the structure of human label variation.
- **Mechanism**: Lenient metrics (overlap) inflate agreement when annotations share any element; moderate (Jaccard) penalize partial overlap; strict (exact) require full correspondence. Comparing across metrics shows how much consensus exists at different granularities.
- **Core assumption**: Annotators will always share some elements by chance or partial overlap; lenient metrics mask true divergence, while stricter metrics expose it.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - (Abstract): "lenient, overlap-based metrics substantially overestimate reliability (e.g., All Events α drops from 0.868 to 0.244)."
  - (Section 4.2): Presents α values across all representations and metrics, demonstrating dramatic drops from lenient to strict, especially for dense representations like All Events and Full Story. Relations shows the smallest drop, indicating higher consistency in directional judgments.
  - (Corpus context): The corpus includes related work on metrics (e.g., MASI, LAS/UAS) but the tripartite framework for narrative graphs is novel; external validation is limited.
- **Break condition**: If the task definition inherently values presence/absence over exact structure (e.g., “does this narrative mention any supply shock?”), then lenient metrics may be appropriate and strict ones unnecessarily punitive.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Data Ingestion -> Task Design -> Annotator Management -> Pre-annotation -> Annotation Interface -> Evaluation Engine -> Analysis

**Critical path**
1. Sample documents from inflation-peak years.
2. Apply LLM filter to enrich cause-dominant documents.
3. Task 1 classification by multiple annotators.
4. Select documents with unanimous/majority cause-dominant labels.
5. Task 2 extraction on selected documents using pre-annotation.
6. Compute α for each of the six representations under three metrics.
7. Identify reliable representation via stability (smallest Δ between metric extremes).

**Design tradeoffs**
- Coverage vs. consistency: Full Story maximizes narrative completeness but sacrifices strict agreement; Adjacent Story optimizes reliability at the cost of multi-hop context.
- Metric strictness: Lenient metrics are easier to satisfy but obscure meaningful disagreement; strict metrics are conservative but may undervalue partial consensus.
- Pre-annotation bias: Gliner reduces cognitive effort but may skew span selection toward named entities; annotators must actively correct omissions.

**Failure signatures**
- Consistently low α across all metrics: Category system too ambiguous or annotators inadequately trained.
- High lenient α but low moderate/strict α: Annotators agree only on a small common core; consider adopting a more constrained representation like Adjacent Story.
- Large variance in annotation cardinality (number of nodes/edges): Unclear annotation boundaries; revisit guidelines on graph size granularity.

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Pilot validation**: Run the full pipeline on a small sample (e.g., 10 documents). Compute α for all representations/metrics. Use group discussions to refine categories based on observed disagreements.
2. **Stability test**: On a held-out set, calculate α for each representation. Select the one with the smallest drop from lenient to strict α (i.e., most stable) as the primary representation.
3. **Disagreement localization**: For the chosen representation, identify the least-frequently annotated triples. Perform qualitative analysis to pinpoint problematic categories or relation types; iterate on guidelines accordingly.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can graph distance metrics for narrative annotation be designed to incorporate semantic similarity between node/edge labels, thereby more accurately reflecting annotator consensus on conceptually equivalent events?
- **Basis in paper**: Section 6.3 identifies that current evaluations "do not consider the semantic similarity of node labels," treating different labels for the same concept as completely dissimilar and potentially underestimating true agreement.
- **Why unresolved**: Existing metrics (lenient, moderate, strict) rely on exact or set-based matches, failing to capture cases where annotators use synonymous but differently phrased labels (e.g., "Energy Prices" vs. "Oil Costs"), a common source of human label variation.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Development and validation of semantics-aware distance metrics (e.g., using label embeddings from pretrained models), evaluated through correlation with human judgments of event similarity and their impact on Krippendorff’s α across new narrative datasets.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What operational criteria and annotation protocol most effectively distinguish "core" narrative elements—those essential to plot development—from supplementary or peripheral details in structured graph representations?
- **Basis in paper**: Section 6.4 states that while representations like *Adjacent Story* achieve reliability, they "do not provide a direct measure of core narrative elements," leaving unclear whether consistent annotations capture truly central causal events.
- **Why unresolved**: Core elements are defined theoretically (e.g., Abbott, 2002) but not empirically isolated in the annotation process; the paper’s focus on local consistency does not guarantee that the most narratively crucial events are identified.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Creation of annotation guidelines explicitly labeling core vs. supplementary events based on narrative theory, followed by studies measuring annotator agreement on this distinction and testing whether core-element annotations better predict human understanding or downstream task performance (e.g., summarization).

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: To what extent do the reliability patterns observed for locally-constrained representations (e.g., *Adjacent Story*) generalize across diverse narrative domains (beyond economic news) and larger, demographically varied annotator pools?
- **Basis in paper**: Section 6.1 acknowledges limitations: the dataset is small, license-restricted, and annotators are a homogenous group of economics students, restricting generalizability of findings on representation stability.
- **Why unresolved**: The study’s context—inflation narratives annotated by domain-trained students—may produce reliability effects specific to that domain or population; it is unknown if *Adjacent Story* remains optimal for political, literary, or social narratives with different annotator backgrounds.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replication of the 6×3 factorial design across multiple narrative domains (e.g., political scandals, social movements) with larger, diverse annotator cohorts (varying expertise, demographics), comparing representation reliability metrics and testing for interaction effects between domain/annotator traits and representation type.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability**: Findings are based on economics narratives (inflation); unknown if Adjacent Story’s stability holds for other domains (e.g., social media disinformation, literary plots).
- **QCA Scalability**: The iterative, discussion-based guideline refinement is resource-intensive; feasibility for large-scale or remote annotation teams is untested.
- **Metric-Goal Alignment**: The tripartite metric suite exposes HLV but may not correlate with downstream utility (e.g., graph-based QA accuracy); strict agreement may not be necessary for all applications.
