# Submodular Evaluation Subset Selection in Automatic Prompt Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03493
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03493
- **Reference count:** 10
- **Primary result:** SESS, a submodular subset selection method, consistently outperforms random and heuristic baselines in APO, especially under small evaluation budgets, achieving up to 67.8% average test accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Automatic Prompt Optimization (APO) iteratively improves prompts using an LLM scorer, but the choice of evaluation subset is a critical, often overlooked factor. This paper introduces SESS, which frames the selection of a small, fixed evaluation subset as maximizing a monotone submodular set function. This enables a greedy algorithm with a (1-1/e) approximation guarantee. SESS instantiates three objectives: representative coverage (F<sub>rep</sub>), least-confidence (F<sub>lc</sub>), and confidence-weighted coverage (F<sub>wrep</sub>). Integrated into the OPRO framework across GSM8K, MATH, and GPQA-Diamond, SESS variants significantly outperform random sampling and prior heuristic methods (IPOMP, Anchor-Points), demonstrating that a carefully chosen small subset provides a more efficient and effective optimization signal than a larger random one.

## Method Summary
SESS is a pre-processing module for APO frameworks like OPRO. Given a pool of candidate evaluation examples and a budget *k*, it selects a static subset *S* of size *k* by maximizing a submodular objective. For F<sub>rep</sub> (facility location), it uses a combination of semantic (normalized cosine) and lexical (TF-IDF) similarity to maximize coverage of the data distribution. F<sub>lc</sub> selects examples with the lowest model confidence (via likelihood or verbalized confidence) to focus on discriminative, difficult cases. F<sub>wrep</sub> combines both by weighting the coverage contribution of each example by its importance, derived from confidence. The greedy algorithm iteratively adds the example with the highest marginal gain. This selected subset *S* is then frozen and used for all subsequent prompt scoring during the OPRO optimization loop, replacing default random or dynamic selection strategies.

## Key Results
- SESS variants (especially SESS-wrep and SESS-vlc) achieve the highest final test accuracy across GSM8K, MATH, and GPQA-Diamond under small evaluation budgets (1-10% of pool).
- SESS-wrep leads in the most challenging small-budget setting (GPQA-Diamond 10%, 37.4% accuracy), while SESS-vlc achieves the best overall average (67.6%).
- Carefully selected subsets can match or exceed the performance of subsets 3-4x larger chosen randomly, proving the efficiency of the submodular approach.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Submodular objectives enable greedy selection with a theoretical (1-1/e) approximation guarantee, making subset selection computationally tractable and provably near-optimal.
- **Mechanism:** The paper proves the proposed objectives (F<sub>rep</sub>, F<sub>wrep</sub>) are monotone and submodular under mild conditions (non-negative similarity and weights). This mathematical property allows the simple greedy algorithm to achieve a bounded worst-case performance relative to the optimal solution, avoiding intractable exhaustive search.
- **Core assumption:** The similarity measure `sim(i,j)` is non-negative (achieved via normalized cosine similarity) and importance weights `w(j)` are non-negative.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We frame selection as maximizing an objective set function and show that, under mild conditions, it is monotone and submodular, enabling greedy selection with theoretical guarantees."
  - [section 3.2] Provides definitions and states "since F<sub>wrep</sub> is a nonnegative weighted sum of facility-location terms, it remains monotone submodular when sim≥0 and w(j)≥0; we prove this in Appendix C."
- **Break condition:** The guarantee collapses if the similarity measure becomes negative or if the weighting scheme introduces negative contributions, violating the non-negativity precondition for submodularity.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Prioritizing difficult examples (low model confidence) in the evaluation subset yields a more informative optimization signal for comparing candidate prompts, especially under small budgets.
- **Mechanism:** The least-confidence objective (F<sub>lc</sub>) is modular and selects examples where the scorer model's likelihood or verbalized confidence is lowest. These "hard" examples are more likely to discriminate between prompt qualities because high-performing prompts should resolve them correctly, while weaker prompts will fail. This concentrates the feedback signal on examples with higher expected gradient information for optimization.
- **Core assumption:** Model confidence (likelihood or verbalized) is a reliable proxy for example difficulty and informativeness for prompt comparison.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] Defines F<sub>lc</sub> as summing `(1−˜c(j))` over selected examples, explicitly framing it as a difficulty-aware selection.
  - [section 5] Reports: "SESS-vlc achieves the best Avg(All) (67.6)" and "SESS-wrep achieves the best Avg(Small) (67.8)", with the discussion noting strong performance on GPQA-Diamond 10% (37.4%). This supports the value of difficulty-awareness.
- **Break condition:** If confidence estimates are miscalibrated or uninformative (e.g., the model is overconfident on hard examples), the selected subset may not actually be difficult, reducing the discriminative power of the feedback signal.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Combining representativeness with difficulty-awareness via a weighted coverage objective (F<sub>wrep</sub>) optimally balances covering the data distribution while focusing on informative, hard examples, leading to strong performance particularly in computationally constrained (small budget) regimes.
- **Mechanism:** F<sub>wrep</sub> weights the coverage contribution of each pool example `j` by its importance `w(j)`, derived from confidence. This biases the greedy facility-location selection to "cover" more weight (i.e., include representatives for) the high-importance (hard) regions of the data distribution. The result is a subset that is both broadly representative and concentrated on the most challenging cases, maximizing the expected utility of each evaluation slot.
- **Core assumption:** A weighted sum of representativeness (coverage) and importance (difficulty) yields a subset that better optimizes the final prompt's *test distribution* performance than either criterion alone.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] Explicitly defines F<sub>wrep</sub> and the weighting scheme `w(j) = (1−λ) + λ*(1−˜c(j))`, stating the goal is to "combine representativeness and difficulty awareness."
  - [section 5] Shows "SESS-wrep achieves the best Avg(Small) (67.8)" and specifically excels on GPQA-Diamond 10% (37.4%), indicating the weighted approach excels where budget is most tight.
- **Break condition:** The optimal trade-off parameter λ is dataset- and model-dependent. An inappropriate λ (e.g., too high) could overfit to a narrow set of hard examples, losing broad representativeness and harming generalization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Submodular Set Function**
  - **Why needed here:** The entire SESS method rests on the mathematical property of submodularity (diminishing returns), which justifies the use of the greedy algorithm and its (1-1/e) guarantee. Without this, the theoretical contribution is opaque.
  - **Quick check question:** What is the formal diminishing returns property, and why does satisfying it allow a simple greedy algorithm to be near-optimal?
- **Concept: Automatic Prompt Optimization (APO) Loop**
  - **Why needed here:** SESS is an intervention in the APO feedback loop. Understanding that the optimizer proposes prompts and the evaluator scores them on a fixed subset is crucial to see why the subset choice is a *signal* that shapes the search process.
  - **Quick check question:** In a typical APO framework like OPRO, what is the role of the evaluation subset, and how could its composition bias the optimization trajectory?
- **Concept: Greedy Algorithm for Submodular Maximization**
  - **Why needed here:** This is the workhorse algorithm that makes SESS practical. Knowing how it iteratively adds the element with the largest marginal gain connects the abstract objective function to a concrete, implementable procedure.
  - **Quick check question:** Describe the standard greedy algorithm for maximizing a monotone submodular function under a cardinality constraint.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Pool (D) -> Representation/Confidence Computation -> Objective Construction (F_rep/F_lc/F_wrep) -> Greedy Selection -> Fixed Subset (S) -> APO Framework (OPRO) -> Optimized Prompt.
- **Critical path:** 1) **Representation/Confidence Computation**: Embed all examples (for similarity) or score them with the fixed scorer LLM (for confidence). 2) **Objective Construction**: Build the appropriate set function matrix (similarity matrix for F_rep/F_wrep, or sorted list for F_lc). 3) **Greedy Selection**: Iterate *k* times, each time finding the example with max marginal gain to the current set *S*. 4) **Freezing**: *S* is fixed and used consistently throughout all APRO optimization steps.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Static vs. Dynamic Selection:** SESS uses a static subset chosen once before optimization. This is efficient and stable but cannot adapt if the optimizer's focus shifts dramatically. Dynamic re-selection (not studied) could be more accurate but adds overhead and noise.
  - **Similarity Measure:** Choice of embedding model (e.g., Qwen3-Embedding-8B) and combination with lexical similarity (TF-IDF) affects coverage. Dense embeddings capture semantics; lexical captures surface form. The convex combination parameter α=0.7 is a tunable design choice.
  - **Confidence Source:** For F_lc/F_wrep, the trade-off is between computational cost and calibration. Likelihood-based confidence is cheap but may not reflect true difficulty; verbalized confidence is more expensive via API calls but aims for better calibration.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Poor submodularity:** If the similarity matrix contains negative values (from unnormalized cosine) or the weighting `w(j)` becomes negative, the theoretical guarantees and greedy performance may degrade significantly.
  - **Miscalibrated confidence:** If the scorer model's confidence scores are anti-correlated with true difficulty (e.g., it is more confident on harder examples), the difficulty-aware objectives will select misleading, easy examples, harming the optimization signal.
  - **Overfitting to seed set:** If the initial pool `D` is not a reasonable sample of the true task distribution, the selected subset `S` will inherit this bias, leading to an optimized prompt that performs poorly on the actual target distribution.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Validate Submodularity:** On a small synthetic dataset with known properties, compute `F(S)` for random subsets and verify the diminishing returns property `F(S ∪ {x}) - F(S) ≥ F(T ∪ {x}) - F(T)` for all `S⊆T`. This confirms the objective is well-formed.
  2. **Objective Sensitivity Test:** On a single dataset (e.g., GSM8K) with a fixed small budget (1%), run SESS with λ=0 (F_rep), λ=1 (pure F_lc), and λ=0.5 (F_wrep). Compare the selected subsets' diversity (e.g., average pairwise similarity) and average difficulty. Observe the trade-off in the final OPRO prompt's test accuracy to see which objective aligns best with the task's needs.
  3. **End-to-End Sanity Check:** Run the full OPRO pipeline twice on a small dataset: once with `S=Random` (baseline) and once with `S=SESS-wrep` (best small-budget performer from paper). Use the same optimizer/scorer LLMs, seeds, and steps. Verify that the SESS run yields a higher-scoring prompt on the *full* test set within the same optimization budget, confirming the paper's core claim.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How robust are the performance gains of SESS to the choice of optimizer and scorer LLMs?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Our experiments focus on a single prompt optimization pipeline OPRO with one optimizer LLM and one scorer LLM. While this setting allows controlled comparisons between subset selection methods, broader experiments across additional optimizer and scorer models would help clarify how sensitive the conclusions are to these settings."
  - **Why unresolved:** The study only used one optimizer (`gpt-oss-120B`) and one scorer (`Qwen2.5-7B`), so it is unclear whether the observed advantages generalize to other model architectures, sizes, or capabilities.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments varying both the optimizer (e.g., GPT-4, Claude, Llama) and scorer (e.g., PaLM, Mistral) across multiple model scales, measuring final prompt accuracy. Additionally, analyze interaction effects between optimizer/scorer quality and SESS objective effectiveness.

- **Question 2:** What are the optimization dynamics of prompt optimization when using submodularly selected evaluation subsets (e.g., convergence speed, discovery rate of strong prompts)?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "We also primarily report final test performance. A more detailed study of optimization dynamics, such as convergence speed and how quickly strong prompts are discovered, would provide a fuller picture of how subset selection shapes the optimization process."
  - **Why unresolved:** The paper only reports the final performance of the best prompt after optimization, leaving unknown how different evaluation subsets influence the trajectory of prompt improvement over time.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Run OPRO with SESS and baseline subset selection methods while logging validation scores at each optimization step. Analyze metrics: number of steps to reach target accuracy, rate of improvement, diversity of prompts explored, and stability across random seeds.

- **Question 3:** Would dynamic re-selection of the evaluation subset during the APO process outperform static selection?
  - **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors used a one-time static selection and hypothesized that "optimization feedback is primarily determined by intrinsic instance properties (e.g., representativeness or difficulty), allowing a fixed subset to provide a stable and efficient signal without the overhead of dynamic re-selection." However, this hypothesis was not empirically validated against dynamic strategies.
  - **Why unresolved:** It remains unknown whether periodically updating the evaluation subset to reflect the current state of optimization could yield better prompts or faster convergence.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement dynamic SESS variants that re-select or refine the evaluation subset at predefined intervals or based on performance signals. Compare against static SESS on final prompt accuracy, convergence speed, and computational cost. Investigate trade-offs between overhead and benefits.

## Limitations
- The evaluation is confined to a single APO framework (OPRO) and a specific pair of optimizer/scorer models, limiting generalizability.
- Subset selection is static; potential benefits of dynamic re-selection during optimization are unexplored.
- Reproduction is hindered by unclear access to the `gpt-oss-120B` optimizer and unspecified details of baseline implementations (IPOMP, Anchor-Points) and OPRO prompt templates.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical guarantees (submodularity):** **High** – Proofs are provided and follow standard submodular analysis.
- **Empirical superiority in small budgets:** **Medium** – Results are consistent across datasets but depend on unreported seeds and specific model implementations.
- **General applicability to other APO frameworks:** **Low** – Only tested on OPRO; the static vs. dynamic selection trade-off is unexplored.
