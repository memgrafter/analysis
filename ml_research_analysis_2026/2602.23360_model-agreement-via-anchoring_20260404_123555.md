# Model Agreement via Anchoring
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23360
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23360
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: The paper shows that for a variety of learning setups (stacking, gradient boosting, neural net architecture search, regression trees), increasing the natural complexity parameter forces the expected squared difference between independently trained models to zero. The core technique uses a midpoint anchoring identity that bounds disagreement by the gap between the ensemble midpoint’s error and the best achievable error in a class containing the midpoint.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the fundamental problem of model disagreement: independently trained models often produce divergent predictions even on the same data. The authors introduce a simple algebraic identity—the midpoint anchoring formula—which relates the expected squared difference between two predictors to their individual mean squared errors (MSE) and the MSE of their average. By showing that under mild conditions the average (midpoint) lies in a class with a known optimal risk, they bound disagreement by the sum of the models’ excess risks relative to that class. This framework yields clean, interpretable bounds for several common learning paradigms: stacking, gradient boosting, neural architecture search, and regression trees. In each case, as the complexity parameter grows (number of base models, boosting iterations, network size, or tree depth), the disagreement decays to zero. The results offer a theoretical foundation for understanding why and when independent training yields consistent predictions, with practical implications for ensembling, fairness, and uncertainty estimation.

## Method Summary
The method centers on the midpoint identity: for any two predictors f₁, f₂, D(f₁,f₂)=E[(f₁−f₂)²] = 2(MSE(f₁)+MSE(f₂)−2MSE( (f₁+f₂)/2 ). This reframes disagreement as twice the loss gap between the individuals and their average. If the average lies within a hypothesis class H for which we can bound the optimal risk R(H), and if each model’s risk is close to that of the best in its own class, then D ≤ 2[(MSE(f₁)−R(H))+(MSE(f₂)−R(H))] + 4ε. The authors apply this template to four settings:

- For stacking: f₁,f₂ are linear combinations of k base models drawn from a distribution Q; the relevant anchor class is the span of 2k base models, leading to E[D] ≤ 4(¯R_k−¯R_{2k}).
- For gradient boosting: models are linear combinations of weak learners from a class C, which is convex; the span itself serves as the anchor, and under a weak learning condition the error gap is O(τ*²/k) where τ* is the atomic norm of the optimal predictor in the weak‑learner span. A Frank‑Wolfe variant gives D ≤ O(τ²/k) with user‑set τ.
- For neural nets and regression trees: the model class Fₙ (net size ≤ n or tree depth ≤ d) is not convex but has hierarchical midpoint closure—averaging two nets in Fₙ can be represented in F₂ₙ by weight duplication or tree grafting. Using the local learning curve gap R(Fₙ)−R(F₂ₙ) yields D ≤ 4(R(Fₙ)−R(F₂ₙ)+ε). Assuming power‑law scaling R(Fₙ)=R*+c n^{-γ}, disagreement decays as O(n^{-γ}).

The approach reduces the problem of bounding disagreement to controlling the rate at which the risk of the larger class exceeds that of the smaller class—a classic learning‑curve analysis.

## Key Results
- **Stacking**: The expected squared difference between two independently trained stacked predictors satisfies E[D] ≤ 4(¯R_k−¯R_{2k}), where ¯R_k is the average risk of the best linear combination of k base models. The factor 4 is tight.
- **Gradient boosting**: After k iterations, disagreement between two independent runs is D = O(τ*²/k), where τ* is the atomic norm of the optimal predictor in the weak‑learner span. For a Frank‑Wolfe variant with user parameter τ, D ≤ O(τ²/k).
- **Neural nets & regression trees**: If f₁,f₂ are approximately optimal in Fₙ (net size n or depth d), then D ≤ 4(R(Fₙ)−R(F₂ₙ)+ε). Under power‑law scaling R(Fₙ)=R*+c n^{-γ}, disagreement decays as O(n^{-γ}). The bound extends to multi‑dimensional strongly convex losses with a factor 8/µ.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Disagreement as excess risk relative to an anchor class**
The midpoint identity transforms the expected squared difference into twice the sum of the individual excess risks relative to the MSE of the average. If we can ensure that the average predictor belongs to some class H with known optimal risk R(H), and if each model’s risk is within ε of the best achievable in its own class, then D ≤ 2[(MSE(f₁)−R(H))+(MSE(f₂)−R(H))] + 4ε. The core assumption is that the midpoint is representable in H; otherwise the inequality may not hold. Empirical evidence comes from the formal Lemmas 2.2 and 2.3 in the paper. A break condition occurs when the average lies outside any class with a useful risk bound (e.g., for non‑closed model families).

**Mechanism 2: Hierarchical midpoint closure**
For non‑convex classes like neural nets (size n) and regression trees (depth d), the authors prove that the average of two predictors from Fₙ is contained in F₂ₙ—by doubling network width/number of nodes or grafting one tree onto another. This closure property, combined with near‑optimality of training (MSE(fᵢ) ≤ R(Fₙ)+ε), yields D ≤ 4(R(Fₙ)−R(F₂ₙ)+ε). The gap shrinks as complexity grows because the learning curve is monotonically decreasing and eventually flattens. The mechanism is validated by constructive proofs (Lemmas 5.1 and 5.3). It breaks if the local learning curve does not flatten or if near‑optimality is unattainable.

**Mechanism 3: Gradient boosting via weak learning**
In gradient boosting, models are linear combinations of weak learners from class C, so the span V(C) is convex and contains the midpoint. The disagreement bound from Mechanism 1 reduces to bounding the error of each boosted model relative to the best predictor in V(C). The analysis shows that under a weak learning condition (each weak learner has near‑maximal correlation with residuals) and with a subgradient oracle, the excess risk after k iterations is O(τ*²/k) where τ* is the atomic norm of the optimal predictor. This yields D = O(τ*²/k). The Frank‑Wolfe variant with a user‑specified norm constraint gives D ≤ O(τ²/k). Evidence is provided by Lemmas 4.1–4.2 and Theorems 4.4–4.5. The mechanism fails if the weak learner class is too weak or the weak learning condition is violated.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Mean Squared Error (MSE) Decomposition**
- Why needed: The anchoring identity is purely algebraic: D(f₁,f₂)=2(MSE(f₁)+MSE(f₂)−2MSE(¯f)). This is the bridge between disagreement and optimization error.
- Quick check: If MSE(f₁)=0.4, MSE(f₂)=0.5, and MSE(¯f)=0.3, then D=2*(0.4+0.5−2*0.3)=2*(0.9−0.6)=0.6.

**Concept: Hypothesis Class Closure Properties (Midpoint Closure)**
- Why needed: To apply Lemma 2.3 we need the midpoint to belong to a class H with a known risk bound. For hierarchical settings we require F₂ₙ to contain the average of any two Fₙ predictors.
- Quick check: If f₁ and f₂ are ReLU networks with at most 5 hidden layers, can (f₁+f₂)/2 be represented by a ReLU network? Yes, by doubling the width (or number of nodes) of the hidden layers, the average lies in F₂ₙ.

**Concept: Strongly Convex Loss Functions**
- Why needed: Extending the results to multi‑dimensional outputs and arbitrary losses requires the loss to be µ‑strongly convex to control the relationship between prediction distance and loss difference. This yields a factor 8/µ in the bound (Lemma 6.3).
- Quick check: For a µ‑strongly convex loss L, we have L(y,p₁)−L(y,p₂) ≤ ∇L(y,p₂)·(p₁−p₂) and by the definition of strong convexity, L(y,p₁) ≥ L(y,p₂) + ∇L(y,p₂)·(p₁−p₂) + (µ/2)||p₁−p₂||², which links loss difference to squared distance.

## Architecture Onboarding
The paper’s framework applies to several training algorithms by identifying the appropriate anchor class and verifying midpoint closure.

**Component map:**
Stacking: Distribution Q over base models → sample two sets of k models (G, G') from Q → train linear regressions on their predictions within spans V(G) and V(G') → outputs f₁, f₂.
Gradient boosting: Weak learner class C → SQ-oracle returns g_t ≈ argmax⟨∇L, g⟩ with bounded errors → add g_t to current model with step size to form f_t → after k iterations output f_k.
Neural net architecture search: Model family Fₙ = ReLU networks with ≤ n internal nodes → empirical risk minimization yields f₁∈Fₙ, f₂∈Fₙ (independently trained) → outputs.
Regression trees: Family F_d = axis‑aligned trees of depth ≤ d → empirical risk minimization yields two independently trained trees → outputs.

**Critical path:**
1. Identify the prediction space hierarchy: for stacking/boosting it’s the convex span (V(G) for 2k models or V(C)); for nets/trees it’s F₂ₙ/F₂d.
2. Verify midpoint closure: ensure the average of two predictors from the smaller class lies in the larger anchor class (by construction for convex spans, by doubling resources for nets/trees).
3. Obtain a bound on each model’s risk relative to the anchor class’s optimal risk. For stacking/boosting this comes from learning‑curve or convergence analysis; for nets/trees it requires R(Fₙ)→R(F₂ₙ) to shrink.
4. Apply Lemma 2.3 (or Lemma 2.4 when ε=0) to translate risk gaps into a disagreement bound.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Stacking is model‑agnostic but requires many base models and storage for their predictions; the bound depends on the local learning‑curve gap between k and 2k models.
- Gradient boosting often achieves low error quickly, but the O(1/k) rate hinges on a persistent weak learning signal and can degrade if residuals become hard to approximate.
- Neural net/tree training is directly compatible with standard pipelines, but proving near‑optimality within Fₙ is challenging due to non‑convexity; the closure argument doubles resources, which may be computationally expensive.

**Failure signatures:**
- Midpoint not contained in the proposed anchor class → bound invalid. E.g., if averaging two small nets cannot be exactly represented by twice‑as‑large nets due to activation constraints (though ReLU closure holds).
- Local learning gap R(Fₙ)−R(F₂ₙ) remains large at feasible n → disagreement persists. This indicates the model class is still under‑fitting relative to the problem complexity.
- Weak learning condition fails (correlation with residuals near zero) → boosting progress stalls and error gap does not shrink as O(1/k).
- Strong convexity assumption violated → the 4/µ factor in Lemma 6.3 does not apply; the anchoring technique may not yield a meaningful bound.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Midpoint closure verification**: Construct tiny neural nets (e.g., width 10) and shallow trees (depth 3) with random weights. Explicitly compute their average and then build a net of double width (or graft a tree) to represent it. Check if the construction succeeds without error.
2. **Local learning curve flattening**: On a regression dataset (e.g., California housing), train models across increasing complexities: stacking with k=1,2,4,8,...; neural nets of size n=2⁰,…,2⁶; trees of depth d=1,...,8. Estimate population risks (via large validation set) and compute Δₙ = R(Fₙ)−R(F₂ₙ). Observe how rapidly Δₙ decays and at which n it becomes negligible.
3. **Weak learning condition test**: Fix a weak‑learner class (e.g., decision stumps). Perform one iteration of gradient boosting on a held‑out dataset. Compute the maximum absolute correlation ⟨∇L, g⟩ over all stumps g∈C. Repeat for several datasets and early iterations. If the median correlation is significantly positive (e.g., >10⁻³), the condition is plausible; if it drops to near zero quickly, the O(1/k) bound may not hold in practice.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**
- Question: Can the anchoring technique apply to model classes where the average of two predictors may not lie in the same or a related class (e.g., SVMs, random forests)?
- Basis: The proof of Lemma 2.4 uses the property that the midpoint of two Fₙ models lies in F₂ₙ. This is verified for neural nets and regression trees but not for other families.
- Why unresolved: Without such closure, the anchor predictor might not be contained in any class with a known convergence guarantee, preventing the analysis from being used.
- What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical relaxation that bounds the distance of the midpoint to a larger class, or an empirical demonstration that independent training still yields agreement for a non‑closed class.

**Open Question 2**
- Question: How robust are the agreement bounds to finite‑sample effects and imperfect optimization (e.g., stochastic gradient descent, early stopping)?
- Basis: The results assume the learned models achieve near‑optimal population risk within their class: MSE(fᵢ) ≤ R(Fₙ) + ε. The paper does not connect ε to sample size or optimization degrees of freedom.
- Why unresolved: In practice ε may be large or decay slowly, potentially undermining the predicted agreement as complexity increases.
- What evidence would resolve it: Derive explicit sample‑complexity and optimization‑error rates for the algorithms studied, then verify end‑to‑end agreement via experiments that vary data size and compute budget.

**Open Question 3**
- Question: Do analogous agreement guarantees exist for losses that are not strongly convex, such as 0‑1 loss or log loss?
- Basis: The extension to multi‑dimensional losses is restricted to strongly convex losses (Section 6). No other loss families are considered.
- Why unresolved: The anchoring inequality (Lemma 6.3) relies on strong convexity; without it, the link between disagreement and risk differences may break, leaving the behavior of independent training unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop new anchoring techniques for broader loss classes and prove corresponding agreement bounds; back them with experiments on real classification tasks using such losses.

## Limitations
- **τ* estimation**: The boosting bound depends on the atomic norm τ* of the optimal predictor in the weak‑learner span, which is problem‑specific and not directly observable, making the bound hard to verify empirically.
- **Near‑optimality assumption**: Theory requires models to be ε‑close to the best in their class. In practice, especially for non‑convex problems (neural nets, trees), achieving sufficiently small ε is not guaranteed and may require disproportionate resources.
- **Weak learning condition**: The boosting analysis relies on each weak learner having non‑trivial correlation with residuals. This may fail for unsuitable weak‑learner classes or after many iterations, limiting the applicability of the O(1/k) rate.

## Next Checks
1. **Empirical midpoint closure**: For small neural nets (e.g., width 10) and shallow trees (depth 3), explicitly construct f₁, f₂ ∈ Fₙ and verify (f₁+f₂)/2 can be represented in F₂ₙ (by building the doubled net/grafted tree). Measure any representational gap.
2. **Local learning curve flattening**: On multiple datasets, train models at increasing complexities (k, n, d). Plot R(Fₙ) vs. n and compute Δₙ = R(Fₙ)−R(F₂ₙ). Check if Δₙ decays rapidly (<1% of initial risk) at computationally feasible n; if not, disagreement may not vanish in practice.
3. **Weak learning condition test**: For a fixed weak‑learner class C (e.g., stumps), run one iteration of gradient boosting on held‑out data. Compute maximum residual correlation over g∈C. Repeat across datasets/iterations. If median correlation drops near zero quickly, the O(1/k) rate is not achievable.
