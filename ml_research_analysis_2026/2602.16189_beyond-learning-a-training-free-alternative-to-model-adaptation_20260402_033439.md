# Beyond Learning: A Training-Free Alternative to Model Adaptation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16189  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16189  
- Reference count: 3  
- Primary result: Model transplantation achieves up to twice the baseline accuracy on mathematical reasoning benchmarks without retraining.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces model transplantation, a training‑free method to boost underperforming language models by replacing selected modules from a high‑performing source model. Evaluated on Phi‑3/Phi‑3.5 and Gemma‑2‑2B/IT pairs on the MATH‑500 benchmark, the approach yields gains of up to 2× (or 2.33×) over the target baseline and exhibits over 100% gap‑based recovery. These results demonstrate that localized modularity in LLMs can be leveraged for immediate capability transfer without any gradient‑based adaptation.

## Method Summary
Model transplantation requires no retraining. First, the target model processes a dataset (often the target task) to collect activation statistics for each module (e.g., attention heads, feed‑forward layers). Modules are ranked by a selection criterion, typically the magnitude of activations. The top K modules are then taken from a source model that already excels at the task and directly transplanted into the target, assuming one‑to‑one module alignment (identical architecture and tensor shapes). The modified target is evaluated using Task Improvement Rate (TIR) and Recovery metrics to measure the fraction of the performance gap closed by transplantation.

## Key Results
- Cross‑generation transplantation (Phi‑3 → Phi‑3.5) yields up to 2× the target baseline accuracy and >100% gap recovery.  
- Base‑to‑instruction‑tuned transfer (Gemma‑2‑2B → Gemma‑2‑2B‑IT) achieves up to 2.33× improvement with 100% recovery in optimal cases.  
- TIR curves are non‑monotonic with respect to K, peaking at intermediate transplant scales and declining when too many modules are replaced.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Language models exhibit localized modularity: individual components specialise in distinct computational functions. Activation‑based selection pinpoints modules most engaged on the target task. Transplanting these pre‑optimised sub‑routines from a proficient source model into the target network immediately transfers the specialised capability while the remainder of the target model retains its general knowledge. Because the operation is purely a weight substitution without any gradient updates, performance improvements are instantaneous.

## Foundational Learning
- **Activation‑Based Module Ranking**  
  *Why needed*: Identifies which modules contribute most to the target task, ensuring the transplanted components are relevant.  
  *Quick check*: Compute mean absolute activation per module on a validation set and sort descending.
- **Module Transplantation without Retraining**  
  *Why needed*: Central claim of the paper – that capability transfer can occur via direct weight replacement, avoiding costly fine‑tuning.  
  *Quick check*: Replace one layer’s weights in a small transformer with another checkpoint’s and confirm inference runs.
- **Task Improvement Rate (TIR) & Recovery**  
  *Why needed*: Normalise performance gains relative to the source–target gap, allowing comparison across transplant conditions.  
  *Quick check*: Given target (T), source (S), and transplanted (P) accuracies, TIR = (P‑T)/(S‑T); Recovery = TIR × 100%.
- **Architectural Compatibility**  
  *Why needed*: Transplantation demands identical module shapes and naming; mismatches break the one‑to‑one replacement.  
  *Quick check*: Verify that the selected modules’ parameter tensors have exactly the same dimensions in source and target checkpoints.
- **Non‑Monotonic Scaling (K)**  
  *Why needed*: Guides the practical choice of how many modules to transplant; too few or too many harms performance.  
  *Quick check*: Sweep K and plot TIR; expect a peak region rather than monotonic increase.
- **Over‑Recovery Phenomenon**  
  *Why needed*: When Recovery > 100%, transplanted models exceed the source baseline, hinting at emergent synergy.  
  *Quick check*: Flag any result where P > S as over‑recovery and investigate further.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Source Model -> Activation Extraction -> Module Ranking -> Top‑K Selection -> Target Model (with replaced modules)

**Critical path:**  
1. Choose source and target models of identical architecture.  
2. Pick a dataset representing the target task.  
3. Run the target model to record activations for every module.  
4. Compute a ranking score (e.g., mean activation) for each module.  
5. Select the top K modules based on the score.  
6. Copy the chosen modules from the source checkpoint into the target.  
7. Evaluate the transplanted model on the target benchmark.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Number of modules (K)*: Small K gives modest gains; large K risks breaking network coordination, often causing a performance drop after a peak.  
- *Activation dataset*: In‑task data maximises relevance but may leak evaluation data; out‑of‑distribution datasets reduce leakage risk but might misrank modules.  
- *Module type*: The paper does not differentiate between replacing feed‑forward versus attention layers; this could affect efficiency and efficacy.  
- *Source‑target similarity*: Even with matching architectures, the magnitude of gain varies across model families and generations.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Negative TIR (transplanted worse than target) signals poor selection or subtle incompatibility.  
- Sharp TIR decline after a certain K indicates over‑replacement disrupts residual network dynamics.  
- High variance across random seeds or different activation datasets reveals instability in the ranking criterion.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Replicate the Phi‑3↔Phi‑3.5 and Gemma transplant experiments exactly as described to confirm reported TIR and Recovery values.  
2. Vary the activation‑collection dataset (e.g., in‑distribution vs. random text) to assess robustness of module ranking.  
3. Perform an ablation separating feed‑forward and attention layer transplants to determine which module type contributes more to performance gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can model transplantation be extended to architecturally heterogeneous models without structural compatibility?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The conclusion states: "We plan to extend transplantation to heterogeneous model pairs with architectural mismatches by developing compatibility mappings between non-identical modules."
- Why unresolved: The current method relies on one-to-one module alignment (shape and name matching). Heterogeneous models (e.g., differing layer counts, dimensions, or types) cannot be directly addressed, limiting applicability across model families.
- What evidence would resolve it: Demonstrate successful transplantation between models with different architectures (e.g., different hidden sizes or layer counts) by learning or defining functional equivalence mappings (e.g., via linear projections) without retraining, and show consistent accuracy gains on benchmarks.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Beyond mathematical reasoning, in which diverse tasks and domains does activation-selected transplantation yield reliable improvements?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The conclusion notes: "Finally, we will broaden the evaluation beyond mathematical reasoning to diverse domains and tasks to better characterize when and how localized module replacement yields reliable capability transfer."
- Why unresolved: The study only uses the HuggingFaceH4/MATH-500 dataset. Task-specific activation patterns may differ greatly (e.g., code generation vs. creative writing), and module transferability might not generalize.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments across multiple task families (e.g., coding, commonsense QA, multilingual tasks) with varied model pairs, measuring TIR/Recovery to identify conditions (task type, model similarity) under which transplantation is consistently effective.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Why does transplantation performance (TIR) often peak at intermediate transplant scales K and decline with larger K, even under high structural compatibility?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Figures 2 and 3 show non-monotonic TIR vs. K curves for both Phi and Gemma settings. The discussion notes: "Increasing K does not yield monotonic gains..." but offers no mechanistic explanation.
- Why unresolved: The paper speculates that broader replacement disrupts coordination but lacks analysis. The interaction dynamics between transplanted and residual modules are unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies tracking output logit or representation similarity as K grows; visualize activation distributions in downstream layers post-transplant to detect interference or saturation effects.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What mechanisms underlie "over-recovery" (Recovery >100%) where transplanted models surpass the source baseline?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Table 2 shows Recovery = 300% at 32 tokens (Phi-3 ↔ Phi-3.5). The text suggests "synergistic improvement" but does not explain how residual target modules interact with transplanted ones to exceed source performance.
- Why unresolved: Over-recovery implies emergent compositional effects, but the paper only reports metrics without analyzing module interactions or whether certain module combinations induce novel capabilities.
- What evidence would resolve it: Isolate and recombine subsets of transplanted modules (e.g., only FFN vs. only attention) to test for synergy; compare internal representations (e.g., probing for task-relevant features) between source, target, and transplanted models to identify complementary computations.

## Limitations
- The evaluation is narrow: only two model families (Phi‑3/Phi‑3.5 and Gemma‑2‑2B/IT) on a single mathematical reasoning dataset (MATH‑500).  
- Key methodological details (exact module selection, transplantation mechanics, any implicit tuning) are underspecified, hindering reproducibility.  
- Non‑standard metrics (TIR, Recovery) obscure comparability with other adaptation approaches and lack statistical significance tests.

## Confidence
- **Performance gains (~2× improvement)**: Medium  
- **Training‑free assertion**: Medium  
- **Localized modularity inference**: Low  
- **Cross‑generation transfer**: Medium
