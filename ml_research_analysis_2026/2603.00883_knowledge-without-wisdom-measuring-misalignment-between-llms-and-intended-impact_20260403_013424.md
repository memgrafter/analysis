# Knowledge without Wisdom: Measuring Misalignment between LLMs and Intended Impact
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.00883
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00883
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: LLMs show strong inter-model correlation but often negative alignment with student learning gains (VAMs); misalignment is largely systemic (~50% shared variance).

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether large language models (LLMs) performing educational evaluation tasks align with real student learning outcomes. Using elementary math classroom transcripts, 16 LLMs rated teaching quality across seven dimensions. Alignment was measured via pairwise Kendall’s τ concordance with expert human ratings and with value‑added measures (VAMs) of student learning gains. Key findings: (1) LLMs exhibit higher inter‑model agreement than with humans, indicating shared pretraining biases; (2) alignment with expert ratings does not translate to alignment with learning outcomes—many models show significantly negative τ with VAMs; (3) ensembling (expert‑weighted or unanimous) exacerbates misalignment; (4) variance decomposition attributes only ~6% of misalignment error to model choice and prompts, with ~50% shared across models, implicating systemic pretraining biases. The results reveal a critical risk: LLMs can appear authoritative yet be detrimentally misaligned with the true goal of education—student learning.

## Method Summary
The authors used the NCTE dataset containing 479 elementary math lesson transcripts, expert ratings on MQI/CLASS instruments, and teacher‑year value‑added measures (VAMs) controlling for confounders. Sixteen foundation models (GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, etc.) generated zero‑shot ordinal ratings under three prompt families (base, chain‑of‑thought, pseudo‑RAG) for seven evaluation dimensions. Pairwise Kendall’s τ, computed via antisymmetric matrices, quantified alignment between LLM ratings and (a) experts and (b) VAMs. Misalignment error was defined as residuals from regressing VAMs on LLM ratings. A fully crossed random effects model (items × models × prompts × transcripts) partitioned error variance. Ensembling strategies (pedagogy‑weighted, unanimous) were also evaluated. Authentic classroom transcripts are out‑of‑distribution relative to standard pretraining corpora, a key design premise.

## Key Results
- LLMs show stronger inter‑model agreement than agreement with human experts, revealing a shared latent heuristic of “good teaching” from pretraining.
- Alignment with expert rubrics fails to predict alignment with learning outcomes; many models have significantly negative Kendall’s τ with VAMs, and ensembling further degrades alignment.
- Variance decomposition shows only 4.8% of misalignment variance due to model choice and 1.0% to prompts, with ~50% shared across models, indicating systemic pretraining bias.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**1. Behavioral homogeneity from shared pretraining** – LLMs rely on convergent heuristics derived from autoregressive pretraining on internet text, which override task‑specific reasoning when faced with authentic K‑12 discourse absent from training. Evidence: inter‑model dCor² > model‑human dCor²; high consensus across architectures. Break condition: if classroom transcripts were well‑represented in pretraining or if fine‑tuning on educational data reduced convergence.

**2. Proxy‑impact divergence** – Models replicate the language of effective pedagogy (rubric terminology) without capturing the causal features that drive achievement gains. Expert observation instruments themselves correlate only faintly with VAMs, and models amplify this gap by focusing on surface patterns aligned with rubric wording but opposite to learning impact. Evidence: many models have positive τ with experts but negative τ with VAMs; ensembling exacerbates the negative shift. Break condition: VAMs would need systematic invalidity or expert ratings would need perfect convergent validity with VAMs.

**3. Systemic error dominance** – Misalignment stems from common training artifacts rather than controllable factors. Variance decomposition attributes <7% of error to model choice and prompts, while ~50% is shared across all foundation models. Evidence: model effect 4.8%, prompt effect 1.0%, shared component ~50%. Break condition: major methodological flaws in the decomposition or emergence of a new model class with fundamentally different bias patterns.

## Foundational Learning
**Value‑Added Measures (VAMs)**  
*Why needed*: VAMs operationalize the intended impact—actual student learning gains. LLMs' negative τ with VAMs reveals a catastrophic mismatch.  
*Quick check*: Why might expert human raters show only weak correlation with VAMs, and what does that imply for LLMs trying to mimic human rubrics?

**Out‑of‑Distribution (OOD) Generalization**  
*Why needed*: Authentic classroom transcripts are likely absent from internet‑scale pretraining, causing models to fall back on heuristic “good teaching” proxies.  
*Quick check*: If LLMs have been trained on billions of educational tokens, why would real classroom transcripts be considered OOD?

**Kendall’s τ**  
*Why needed*: A rank‑correlation metric robust to ordinal scales and rating distribution differences; used to compare LLM outputs to experts and VAMs via antisymmetric matrices.  
*Quick check*: How does Kendall’s τ differ from traditional accuracy, and why is it better for evaluating LLMs on tasks with no “correct” answer?

**Generalizability Theory (G‑Theory)**  
*Why needed*: A framework for partitioning measurement error into facets (items, models, prompts, transcripts). Used in the fully crossed random effects design to quantify controllable vs. systemic error.  
*Quick check*: What does it mean when main effects of model and prompt are small but their interactions with items/transcripts are also small? What does that imply for prompt engineering as a solution?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: NCTE transcripts (with VAM metadata) -> segmentation -> LLM inference (16 models × 3 prompts × 7 tasks) -> ordinal ratings -> alignment metrics (Kendall’s τ, dCor²) -> ensembling -> regression residuals -> variance decomposition (brms/lme4) -> results and visualizations.

**Critical path**:
1. Prepare data: load NCTE test set, extract VAMs, fit teacher‑skill BLUP model to get segment‑level residuals.
2. Score models: run zero‑shot inference on 479 transcripts for each model‑prompt‑task; store ratings.
3. Compute alignment: build pairwise antisymmetric matrices; calculate τ against experts and VAMs with Fieller confidence intervals.
4. Test ensembling: compute pedagogy‑weighted and unanimous ensembles; recompute τ_VAM.
5. Decompose variance: fit mixed model `residVAM ~ (1|ITEM) + (1|LLM) + (1|PROMPT) + (1|LLM:PROMPT) + ...`; extract variance proportions.
6. Sensitivity checks: alternate estimators (Thiel‑Sen), quartile analyses.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **Transcript granularity**: Finer segmentation (7.5 min) increases sample size but may dilute holistic teaching signal; coarser segments (15 min) capture more context but reduce power.
- **Zero‑shot vs few‑shot**: Zero‑shot avoids contamination; few‑shot might reveal learning capacity but is unlikely to succeed given OOD nature.
- **Ensemble design**: Tested extreme aggregation methods; correlated models in ensembles likely worsen misalignment.
- **VAM residualization**: Complex multi‑stage model controls confounders but adds assumptions; simpler VAMs may yield noisier results.
- **Model selection**: 16 models from HELM and pedagogy leaderboards provide diversity; not exhaustive but sufficient to reveal convergence.

**Failure signatures**:
- **Pattern A**: High inter‑model dCor² (>0.3) with low human‑model dCor² (<0.2) → systemic bias.
- **Pattern B**: τ with experts positive but τ with VAMs negative → proxy misalignment.
- **Pattern C**: Ensembling shifts τ_VAM further negative → correlated errors reinforced.
- **Pattern D**: Variance decomposition shows >40% “shared” component → pretraining root cause.
- **Pattern E**: No improvement with CoT/RAG prompts → surface‑level prompting insufficient.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Human‑VAM alignment verification**: Compute Kendall’s τ, Thiel‑Sen slopes, and quartile differences between expert ratings and VAMs to confirm a faint positive signal, validating VAM as the intended impact metric.
2. **Inter‑model convergence test**: Calculate bias‑corrected squared distance correlation (dCor²) among all model pairs, within‑model across tasks, and versus human raters to quantify behavioral homogeneity.
3. **Alignment scatterplot analysis**: For each model‑task point, plot τ_expert vs. τ_VAM; include ensemble points; visualize proxy‑impact disconnect and ensemble degradation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Mitigating systemic pretraining biases** – How can deep structural biases inherited from autoregressive pretraining be reduced? Current levers (model selection, prompting) explain <7% of misalignment. Evidence needed: train models on curated educational corpora and measure VAM alignment.
2. **Evaluating pedagogical impact vs. proxy alignment** – What frameworks can reliably assess whether an LLM’s judgments improve student learning rather than merely replicating expert rubrics? Proposed: benchmarks requiring prediction or influence of simulated/real learning gains with careful confound control.
3. **Generalizability across educational contexts** – Do findings hold beyond U.S. elementary math classrooms? The NCTE dataset limits external validity. Evidence needed: replication with diverse cultural, grade, and subject transcripts, or synthetic role‑plays preserving structural features.
4. **Addressing the “Paradox of Free Advice”** – How to prevent users needing guidance from over‑trusting low‑quality AI? Solutions may include calibrated confidence scores, uncertainty rationales, mandatory human‑in‑the‑loop, and curricula teaching critical evaluation of AI content.
5. **Can smaller specialized models beat foundation models?** – Might expertly curated, education‑focused models trained on limited high‑quality data better align with learning outcomes while avoiding web‑scale biases? Evidence needed: comparative training and deployment studies measuring actual student outcomes.

## Limitations
- The assumption that classroom transcripts are OOD relative to pretraining is inferential; direct verification is lacking.
- VAM validity as the gold standard for learning gains depends on correct residualization of confounders; measurement noise could bias alignment estimates.
- Human rating aggregation method (BLUPs vs. raw averages) may shift expert benchmarks and affect the measured proxy‑impact gap.
- The variance decomposition’s “shared” component could conflate unmeasured item effects with true pretraining biases.

## Confidence
- **Pretraining bias convergence**: High  
- **Proxy‑impact misalignment**: Medium‑High  
- **Systemic error dominance**: Medium
