# Multi UAVs Preflight Planning in a Shared and Dynamic Airspace

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.12055
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12055
- **Reference count:** 39
- **Primary result:** Near-100% success for fleets up to 1,000 UAVs, up to 50% runtime reduction from pruning, and superior scalability compared to batch Enhanced Conflict-Based Search.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the challenge of scalable preflight planning for large-scale unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) fleets operating in dynamic urban airspace. It considers temporal no-fly zones, heterogeneous vehicle capabilities, and delivery deadlines. The proposed framework, DTAPP-IICR, integrates urgency-aware prioritized planning with a novel 4D single-agent planner (SFIPP-ST) that handles temporal constraints and models inter-agent conflicts as soft costs. An iterative large neighborhood search (LNS) guided by a geometric conflict graph resolves residual conflicts, while a directional pruning technique accelerates the underlying search. Empirical results demonstrate near-100% success rates for fleets up to 1,000 UAVs, significant runtime reductions (up to 50%) from pruning, and superior scalability over batch CBS/ECBS, enabling realistic city-scale operations where other priority-based methods fail.

## Method Summary
DTAPP-IICR is a centralized preflight planner for heterogeneous UAVs performing roundtrip deliveries in a 4D grid (3D space + time) that must avoid temporal no-fly zones and static obstacles. The method operates in two phases. First, an initial solution is built by scheduling UAVs in order of increasing departure time (urgency). Each UAV is planned sequentially using SFIPP-ST, a single-agent A*-based planner that searches over safe flight intervals (time windows where a voxel is free from static obstacles and NFZs). Previously planned trajectories are treated as soft obstacles: overlapping incurs a penalty cost that influences the search order but does not block the path. SFIPP-ST incorporates a directional pruning heuristic that prioritizes neighbor expansions aligning with the goal direction, reducing branching while preserving completeness via a fallback to full expansion when needed. Second, after all UAVs have an initial path, a geometric conflict graph is constructed: nodes are UAVs, edges indicate a separation violation (minimum distance < sum of radii + buffer). An iterative LNS loop then refines the solution: a neighborhood of conflicting agents (size N=10) is selected via a heuristic on the conflict graph; their paths are removed and each is replanned sequentially with SFIPP-ST (using remaining paths as soft obstacles). If any replan fails, the neighborhood is rolled back. This repeats until no conflicts remain or a time limit is reached.

## Key Results
- Near-100% success rate for fleets up to 1,000 UAVs in Monte Carlo simulations.
- Directional pruning yields up to 50% runtime reduction without sacrificing solution quality.
- DTAPP-IICR outperforms batch CBS/ECBS and priority-based baselines, especially in the presence of temporal no-fly zones, and successfully handles city-scale scenarios with up to 500 UAVs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Urgency-based prioritization
- **Claim:** Urgency-based prioritization reduces the complexity of conflict resolution by scheduling high-urgency missions first, minimizing later disruptions.
- **Mechanism:** Missions are ordered by earliest departure time (\(t_{init}\)). Each UAV plans its roundtrip with SFIPP-ST, treating previously planned paths as soft obstacles. Early high-urgency paths become fixed constraints for later planners, reducing the need for extensive backtracking.
- **Core assumption:** Missions with earlier start times are less likely to be invalidated by subsequent plans, as they already occupy space-time resources during peak congestion periods.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "Our framework first generates an initial solution by prioritizing missions based on urgency."
  - [section 4.4] "DTAPP-IICR begins by sorting UAVs based on \(t_{init}\), ensuring urgent deliveries are planned first."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; neighbor papers focus on different UAV coordination approaches (e.g., reinforcement learning, swarm optimization) and do not address urgency-aware prioritization in MAPF.
- **Break condition:** If many missions share similar urgency values, the ordering loses discriminative power, potentially leading to similar conflict densities as unordered planning.

### Mechanism 2: Soft-cost iterative repair
- **Claim:** Modeling inter-agent conflicts as soft costs in single-agent planning, followed by iterative resolution, avoids over-constraining early plans and enables scalability in dense airspace.
- **Mechanism:** SFIPP-ST assigns a penalty cost to trajectories that geometrically overlap with others (soft constraints), rather than rejecting them outright. A subsequent Large Neighborhood Search (LNS) iteratively removes and replans agents involved in conflicts, guided by a geometric conflict graph, until a conflict-free solution emerges.
- **Core assumption:** Temporarily allowing conflicts during individual planning yields feasible initial solutions that can be efficiently repaired, whereas treating all conflicts as hard constraints leads to early failures in crowded scenarios.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "SFIPP-ST ... models inter-agent conflicts as soft constraints."
  - [section 4.1] "SFIPP-ST penalizes potential conflicts with \(O_s\) but does not strictly forbid them, allowing iterative conflict resolution to refine the solution."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; neighboring works use hard vertex/edge conflicts (e.g., CBS variants) or do not employ iterative soft-constraint repair.
- **Break condition:** In extremely dense fleets with intricate conflict graphs, iterative repair may exceed time limits or cycle without convergence, especially if the LNS neighborhood selection fails to identify critical conflicts.

### Mechanism 3: Directional pruning
- **Claim:** Directional pruning in 3D grid neighbor expansion reduces the branching factor by 30–50% while preserving search completeness, accelerating pathfinding without sacrificing solution quality.
- **Mechanism:** During graph search, only neighbors whose displacement direction aligns with the goal vector (in XY-plane and Z-axis) are expanded first. If no such neighbors exist, the algorithm falls back to full 26-neighbor expansion, guaranteeing completeness.
- **Core assumption:** Direct path segments toward the goal are more likely to belong to optimal or near-optimal solutions, making nondirectional moves low-probability early in the search.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "A completeness-preserving directional pruning technique further accelerates the 3D search."
  - [section 4.2] Algorithm 2 details the pruning heuristic with fallback to all neighbors.
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; neighboring UAV planning papers (e.g., Lazy A*, DRL-based methods) do not implement this specific geometric pruning.
- **Break condition:** In environments requiring detours (e.g., around obstacles or NFZs) or during waiting actions, the goal direction may not align with any neighbor, triggering full expansion and negating speedups.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input Parser → Urgency Sorter → (SFIPP-ST Planner with integrated Pruning Module) → Conflict Graph Builder → LNS Resolver (loop: neighborhood selection → remove paths → replan with SFIPP-ST) → final conflict-free paths.

**Critical path:**
1. Sort UAVs by \(t_{init}\).
2. For each UAV in order, call PLANROUNDTRIP: use SFIPP-ST to plan outbound, fixed wait, and return, with previously planned paths as soft obstacles.
3. Build geometric conflict graph from all trajectories.
4. While conflicts exist and within time limit: select a neighborhood of size \(N=10\) via conflict graph heuristic; remove those UAVs' paths; replan each sequentially with PLANROUNDTRIP using current paths as soft obstacles; if any fails, rollback neighborhood and end iteration.
5. Return conflict-free paths.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Soft vs. hard constraints: Soft constraints improve initial success rates but require iterative repair; hard constraints (as in PP) simplify but cause early failures in dense fleets.
- Pruning aggressiveness: More pruning speeds up search but risks missing valid paths if fallback logic is inefficient; completeness is preserved but may incur overhead.
- Neighborhood size (\(N\)): Larger \(N\) captures more systemic conflicts but increases per-iteration cost; \(N=10\) was fixed in experiments.

**Failure signatures:**
- Repeated rollbacks during LNS iterations, indicating persistent conflicts.
- Rapid conflict count reduction that plateaus above zero.
- Timeouts before reaching conflict-free solution, especially with >500 UAVs or many NFZs.
- Success rate drop when increasing NFZ count or obstacle density.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Evaluate SFIPP-ST with/without directional pruning on small fleets (10–100 UAVs), measuring runtime reduction and success rate.
2. Scale to large fleets (up to 1,000 UAVs) with varying obstacle densities, comparing DTAPP-IICR against batch CBS/ECBS on success rate and runtime.
3. Introduce temporal NFZs (1, 2, 4 zones) and measure robustness; inspect runtime growth and success rate degradation versus NFZ count.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can multi-armed bandit (MAB) learning be integrated into the Large Neighborhood Search (LNS) to dynamically select the most effective neighborhood heuristics for 4D UAV path planning?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state: "future works will explore advanced neighborhood selection strategies... implementing an adaptive learning mechanism, such as a multi-armed bandit (MAB), to dynamically choose the most effective heuristic during the search."
- **Why unresolved:** The current DTAPP-IICR uses fixed or simple heuristics for selecting conflicting agents to replan. An adaptive MAB approach could significantly improve efficiency by learning which operators yield faster conflict resolution in different scenarios.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Integrating a contextual bandit algorithm into the LNS loop and measuring performance improvements against the baseline on Monte Carlo (up to 1000 UAVs) and city-scale benchmarks, with statistical significance tests.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent does the fixed neighborhood size parameter (N=10) in the geometric conflict graph heuristic limit DTAPP-IICR’s scalability and solution quality for ultra-dense fleets (e.g., >2000 UAVs)?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper’s experiments fix N=10 and test up to 1000 UAVs. The success rate drops at 500+ agents with 4 NFZs, suggesting N may be insufficient for very large, tightly coupled conflict graphs.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze sensitivity to N. A fixed N may be too small to resolve large conflict clusters or unnecessarily large for sparse conflicts.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study varying N (e.g., {5, 10, 20, 50}) on stress-test scenarios with 2000+ UAVs in the city-scale map, measuring success rate, runtime per iteration, and iterations to convergence.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the completeness-preserving directional pruning ever cause significant performance degradation in environments where the goal direction is constantly changing (e.g., due to asymmetric roundtrips or winding no-fly zones)?
- **Basis in paper:** The pruning prioritizes neighbors aligning with the goal vector; a fallback ensures completeness. The paper only tests on grids with straight-line biases.
- **Why unresolved:** The empirical cost of fallback (enabling full 26-neighbor expansion) is not measured. Frequent fallback could erase the claimed 50% runtime benefit.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Instrument the planner to log fallback frequency. Run controlled experiments on synthetic 3D mazes (e.g., spiral, cave-like) where straight-line direction is a poor heuristic, comparing runtime and node expansions with and without pruning.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the soft geometric conflict model in SFIPP-ST be enriched to differentiate conflict severity (e.g., near-miss vs. prolonged close proximity), and would this improve solution quality (e.g., lower cumulative risk) without sacrificing success rate?
- **Basis in paper:** Conflicts are modeled as a cumulative geometric cost, treating all violations of distance ≤ r_i + r_j + γ uniformly. The objective is flowtime, not risk.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not consider factors like time of conflict, relative velocity, or duration; a solution might minimize conflict count but still contain high-risk encounters.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Redefine `GeoConflict` to return a continuous risk score (e.g., based on miss distance, duration, closing speed). Run DTAPP-IICR with different risk-weighting parameters and evaluate using a separate risk metric, checking for Pareto improvements.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How does DTAPP-IICR’s performance compare to learned or sampling-based planners (e.g., RL-based or decentralized methods) in scenarios with partial observability or dynamic mission insertions during execution?
- **Basis in paper:** The framework assumes centralized, preflight batch planning with all missions known in advance and static NFZ schedules. Real UTM involves continuous request streams.
- **Why unresolved:** DTAPP-IICR’s iterative conflict resolution might be too slow for real-time replanning. Its scalability relative to decentralized or learning-based approaches under dynamic arrivals is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend the experimental setup: missions arrive according to a Poisson process. Compare DTAPP-IICR (replanned from scratch or incrementally) against a fast decentralized policy or a learned policy. Metrics: average mission delay, success rate under dynamic load, computational latency per arrival.

## Limitations
- Random seeds for benchmark generation are not disclosed, preventing exact numeric replication.
- The soft conflict cost formulation in SFIPP-ST lacks full specification (e.g., continuous-time geometric overlap details), which could affect conflict resolution and success rates.
- The CBS/ECBS baseline code is unavailable; any reimplementation may diverge from the original, weakening comparative claims.

## Confidence
- **Scalability to 1000 UAVs:** Medium (results rely on unreproducible benchmarks and a reimplemented baseline).
- **Pruning yields 30–50% runtime reduction:** Medium (heuristic is well-described; empirical verification needed across diverse scenarios).
- **Soft-cost iterative repair outperforms hard constraints:** Low–Medium (evidence limited to authors' comparisons; sensitivity unknown).
- **Directional pruning preserves completeness:** Medium (theoretical fallback ensures completeness; practical behavior on complex maps uncertain).
