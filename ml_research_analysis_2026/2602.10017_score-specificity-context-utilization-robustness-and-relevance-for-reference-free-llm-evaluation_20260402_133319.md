# SCORE: Specificity, Context Utilization, Robustness, and Relevance for Reference-Free LLM Evaluation

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.10017
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10017
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** SCORE introduces a multi-dimensional reference-free evaluation framework for LLMs in high-stakes domain-specific settings, demonstrating that no single metric suffices and revealing trade-offs between specificity, robustness, relevance, and context utilization.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents SCORE, a novel framework for evaluating large language models (LLMs) in high-stakes, domain-specific scenarios without relying on gold-standard references. SCORE assesses four critical dimensions: **specificity** (detail and verifiability), **robustness** (consistency under paraphrasing and semantic perturbations), **answer relevance** (semantic alignment with the query after masking domain terms), and **context utilization** (dependence on retrieved evidence). The authors compile a dataset of 1,412 question–answer pairs spanning 40 professional roles and 7 natural hazards, and evaluate GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5 using both LLM-as-a-judge and human annotators. Key findings include: GPT-4o produces more specific answers, models are robust to paraphrasing but sensitive to hazard/location changes, masked relevance scores range between 0.69 and 0.83, and context utilization shows modest positive contributions. Human agreement is high for hazard identification (92%) but low for timeline (52%), highlighting inherent subjectivity in fine-grained domain evaluation.

## Method Summary
SCORE operates within a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline. For each query (defined by profession, hazard, location, timeline), a retriever (FAISS with sentence‑transformer embeddings) fetches the top‑5 documents from a knowledge base of ~600k climate abstracts. A generator (GPT‑4o or Gemini‑2.5) produces an answer conditioned on these documents. The answer is then scored on four dimensions:

- **Specificity:** The answer is decomposed into atomic claims. Multiple LLM judges label each claim for four dimensions (hazard, location, timeline, intensity). Labels are aggregated via majority vote and a weighted sum (weights prioritize hazard and location).
- **Robustness:** A paraphrased question and a semantically perturbed question (hazard/location swapped) are generated. RAG is rerun for each; semantic consistency between the original answer and the perturbed answers is measured using embedding similarity.
- **Answer relevance:** Domain entities (hazard, profession, infrastructure, concern) in the answer are masked. Five candidate questions are generated from the masked answer; average cosine similarity to the original query yields a masked relevance score. An optional BGE reranker computes marginal contribution of answer segments.
- **Context utilization:** Unique claims are extracted from retrieved documents. For each claim, a proxy model (LLaMA/Qwen) estimates answer confidence (geometric mean of token probabilities) with the full claim set and with that claim removed. The leave‑one‑out confidence drop yields CU and CU_rel.

Readability (Flesch Reading Ease, Flesch‑Kincaid Grade Level) is also computed.

## Key Results
- Models exhibit trade‑offs: GPT‑4o yields significantly higher specificity, while Gemini shows greater robustness to certain perturbations.
- Masked relevance scores range from 0.69 to 0.83; Gemini typically scores higher under BGE reranking.
- Context utilization scores are modestly positive; positive claim contributions appear in ~85% of cases, but negative contributions occur rarely (~0.4%).
- Human inter‑annotator agreement varies: 92% for hazard identification but only 52% for timeline, underscoring subjectivity in fine‑grained evaluation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Multi‑Agent Specificity Evaluation
The specificity metric captures fine‑grained, verifiable details by decomposing answers into atomic claims and having multiple LLM judges label each claim for hazard, location, timeline, and intensity. Majority voting and a weighted average (with hazard and location dominating) reduce individual judge bias. Evidence: Section 3.1 details the pipeline; human validation shows reasonable agreement for hazard/location but lower for timeline. Break condition: If inter‑annotator agreement falls below ~60% (e.g., timeline 52%) or majority voting yields contradictory labels, the aggregated score becomes unstable.

### Answer Relevance via Semantic Masking and BGE Reranking
Relevance is measured by masking domain‑specific entities in the answer, generating candidate questions from the masked text, and comparing them to the original query via cosine similarity. This mitigates lexical overlap bias. BGE reranking further identifies the marginal contribution of answer segments. Evidence: Section 3.3 describes the masking, question generation, and reranking steps. Break condition: If masking removes too much semantic content (candidate questions become incoherent) or if scores correlate strongly with answer length, the metric fails to reflect true alignment.

### Context Utilization via Leave‑One‑Out Confidence Drop
Context utilization estimates how much the generator depends on each retrieved claim by measuring the drop in answer confidence when that claim is omitted. A lightweight proxy model (e.g., LLaMA, Qwen) provides token‑level probabilities via teacher forcing, and the geometric mean yields confidence. Evidence: Section 3.4 defines CU and CU_rel; Section 4.1 selects proxy models with high confidence consistency. Break condition: If the proxy model’s confidence estimates are poorly correlated with the generator’s (e.g., ρ < 0.5 for Gemma), CU scores become noisy and potentially misleading.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reference‑free evaluation:** Avoids reliance on gold answers, which are scarce in high‑stakes domains. *Why needed here:* SCORE must evaluate without reference answers. *Quick check:* Why do BLEU or ROUGE fail to assess whether an LLM’s advice is actionable for a wastewater engineer during an ice storm?
- **Retrieval‑Augmented Generation (RAG):** Assumes answers are grounded in retrieved literature; context utilization directly measures evidence use. *Why needed here:* The framework evaluates how well the model leverages external knowledge. *Quick check:* How does retrieved context differ from the LLM’s parametric knowledge, and why is this distinction critical for hazard response?
- **Multi‑agent (ensemble) judgment:** Uses several LLM judges to reduce individual biases and variance. *Why needed here:* Specificity assessment requires consistent labeling of fine‑grained details. *Quick check:* What are the risks of using a single LLM as a judge for fine‑grained domain specificity, and how does majority voting mitigate them?
- **Confidence estimation via token probabilities:** Normalized per‑token likelihoods approximate the generator’s reliance on context. *Why needed here:* CU relies on confidence estimates from a proxy model. *Quick check:* Why is length normalization (geometric mean vs. raw product) important when comparing confidence across answers of different lengths?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
User query (profession, hazard, location, timeline) -> Retriever (FAISS + all‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2) -> Top‑5 documents -> Generator (GPT‑4o / Gemini‑2.5) -> Answer -> Evaluator (Specificity, Robustness, Answer Relevance, Context Utilization modules) -> Scores + Readability metrics.

### Critical path
1. Question ingestion  
2. Document retrieval  
3. Answer generation  
4. Parallel computation of Specificity, Robustness, Relevance, and Context Utilization  
5. Aggregation and reporting

### Design tradeoffs
- **LLM‑as‑judge vs. human:** Scalable and reproducible but may harbor model biases; multiple judges partially offset.
- **Proxy models for CU:** Necessary for closed generators; introduces approximation error – select models with high confidence consistency (LLaMA, Qwen) and reject Gemma/Ministral.
- **Weighted specificity:** Prioritizing hazard and location (α=[0.6,0.2,0.1,0.1]) reflects domain importance but may under‑represent intensity; validated by human agreement patterns.
- **Masking vs. nuance:** Masking reduces lexical bias but risks losing subtle meaning; kept minimal by replacing only domain‑specific nouns.

### Failure signatures
- Low inter‑annotator agreement on specificity dimensions (≤60%) signals ambiguous criteria.
- Poor proxy consistency (ρ < 0.5 between *fθ(a|q∪C)* and *fθ(a|q∪D)*) invalidates CU estimates.
- Unusually high robustness to perturbation (perturbation score < 0.1) may indicate overly generic answers.
- Masked relevance remains close to unmasked score, suggesting masking failed to remove lexical shortcuts.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Specificity comparison:** Run GPT‑4o and Gemini generators, evaluate with both GPT‑4o and Qwen judges; show GPT‑4o consistently yields higher specificity.
2. **Robustness test:** Generate paraphrased questions (preserving semantics) and hazard/location‑perturbed questions; measure answer similarity to confirm models are paraphrase‑robust but perturbation‑sensitive.
3. **CU validation:** Compute CU for multiple proxy models, assess confidence consistency; select LLaMA/Qwen as reliable proxies; report modest but positive CU means and rare negative claims.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can we develop a unified, interpretable scoring system that effectively aggregates multiple, often conflicting, evaluation dimensions (specificity, robustness, relevance, context utilization) without arbitrary weighting?**
2. **What is the most reliable and model‑agnostic method for estimating answer confidence to measure context utilization (CU), especially when evaluated against proprietary models?**
3. **How can evaluation frameworks be designed to mitigate the inherent subjectivity and low inter‑annotator agreement in domain‑specific, open‑ended tasks (e.g., timeline and intensity assessment)?**
4. **To what extent are current LLM‑as‑a‑judge systems (like those used for SCORE) reliable proxies for expert human judgment in high‑stakes, nuanced domains, and what causes their failures?**
5. **How does the performance and failure mode of evaluation metrics change when applied to responses grounded in *insufficient or low‑quality* retrieved context, and how should this be measured?**
6. **Do the proposed metrics (especially context utilization via leave‑one‑out confidence) truly measure *utilization* versus mere *compatibility* or *paraphrasing* of retrieved content?**
7. **How generalizable are the findings (e.g., GPT‑4o's higher specificity, robustness patterns) across different high‑stakes domains beyond natural hazards (e.g., healthcare, law, finance) and different model architectures?**
8. **What is the optimal granularity (atomic claim vs. sentence vs. paragraph) for evaluating dimensions like specificity and context utilization in long, complex answers?**
9. **Can the "readability" metrics (FRE, FKGL) used in SCORE meaningfully assess suitability for *professional* users, or do they better reflect general literacy? How should readability be defined for expert decision‑support?**
10. **How do the evaluated models' performances (specificity, robustness) correlate with actual decision‑making outcomes in simulated or real high‑stakes scenarios?**

## Limitations
- Dataset and retrieval corpus are not fully public; missing key prompts impede exact replication.
- Human inter‑annotator agreement on timeline specificity is only 52%, revealing inherent subjectivity in fine‑grained evaluation.
- Context utilization depends critically on proxy model consistency; Gemma shows poor correlation (ρ < 0.5) with generator outputs.
- Generalizability beyond the 7 hazards and 40 professions remains untested.
